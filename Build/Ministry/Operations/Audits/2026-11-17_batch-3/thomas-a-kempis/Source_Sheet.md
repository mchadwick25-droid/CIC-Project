## 1. Setting

**Tradition, spoken name.** Census: `"informalName": "The Brethren of the Common Life"`, `"shortName": "Devotio Moderna"`. Thomas's own name for it, per his translator: Founders 104–107 (Arthur, editor), "since that Brotherhood is referred to by the author himself as " The New Devotion,"". Plainest spoken forms: "the New Devotion", "the Brothers of the Common Life". Note: Thomas was schooled among the Brothers at Deventer, but he himself became a canon regular (a priest-monk under the Rule of St Augustine) of Mount St Agnes, a house of the Windesheim order (Chronicle 1045–1048, 1189–1195). Documented.

**Place and birth.** Chronicle 1189–1193, own words: "Thomas Hemerken of the city of Kempen in the diocese of Cologne, and own brother to John of Kempen the first Prior." Kempen is in the diocese of Cologne (modern Germany). Mount St Agnes is near Zwolle (Chronicle title and 3413–3415; Neale 4704). Documented.

**Birth year.** Not stated anywhere in his own words. Derived: Chronicle 2381, 1448 entry, "being sixty-seven years of age"; Chronicle 3415, death notice 1471, "He was in the ninety-second year of his age". Benham 490–491 (editor): "born at Kempen, near Koln, about the year 1380." Ingram 261 reports a 17th-century note in a Dublin manuscript: "Tho. e Kempis natus 1380 obyt 25 Julij 1471." Say "about 1380". Widely Accepted.

**Century.** 1399 (arrival) to 1471 (death): the fifteenth century, in the Low Countries (modern Netherlands).

## 2. Events, in order

**E1. School at Deventer under Florens Radewijns.** Own words, Founders 8990–9016:
> "When I had come to study in Deventer, in the days of my youth, I sought the way to the Regulars at Windesheim, and having found there the Canons Regular, amongst whom was my brother, I was led by his advice to approach that most reverend man Master Florentius, who was Vicar of the Church of Deventer"

> "he placed me in the School, and besides this gave me the books which he thought I needed. Afterwards he obtained for me a lodging, at no cost to myself, with a certain honourable and devout matron"

> "Never before could I recollect to have seen such men, so devout and fervent were they in the love of God and of their neighbour." (9014–9016)

What he saw them do, Founders 9031–9033: "Remaining at home they laboured carefully in copying books, being instant continually in sacred study and devout meditation." And 9052–9055: "brought every man what was his own into the common stock, and receiving simple food and clothing avoided taking thought for the morrow." Documented (own words, written decades later).

His service in Florens's house, own words: Founders 6506–6510, "I myself, unworthy as I am, often made ready his table at his request, and brought from the buttery that modest draught which he desired"; 6752–6754, "I myself also by his direction brought to his house at dinner time certain poor persons whom he named to me". Documented.

**E2. 1399: he comes to Mount St Agnes.** Own words, Chronicle 1100–1106:
> "In the year 1399, Indulgences were granted to the people of Zwolle by the Apostolic See ... In this same year, I, Thomas of Kempen, a scholar at Deventer and a native of the diocese of Cologne, came to Zwolle to gain indulgences. Then I went on, glad at heart, to Mount St. Agnes, and was instant to be allowed there to abide, and I was received with mercy."

An indulgence: a pardon of the penance owed for sins, granted here by Pope Boniface IX to those "truly penitent" at St Michael's church (Chronicle 1100–1102). His brother John had been chosen first prior that same year (Chronicle 1047–1048). Documented.

Variant, death notice by another brother (Chronicle 3419–3421): "he was an hearer of Florentius at Deventer, by whom also he was sent, when twenty years old, to his own brother, who at that time was Prior of Mount St. Agnes." Neale 4704–4706 gives 1400. See section 5.

**E3. A poor house, built by hand; books written for sale.** Chronicle 1089–1096, own words, of Prior John (his brother): "he himself was the first among them that laboured, and would carry the hod of mortar, and dig with the spade ... When he had leisure he was instant in reading holy books, and often worked at writing or illuminating ... because they were poor he appointed certain Brothers to write for sale, as was the custom from old time." Documented.

**E4. 1406: clothed as a canon.** Own words, Chronicle 1189–1195: "In the year of the Lord 1406, on the Feast of Corpus Christi ... two brothers that were Clerks, and one that was a Convert, were invested. These were Thomas Hemerken of the city of Kempen ... The father of these was called John and their mother Gertrude." ("Invested" = clothed in the habit, the start of life as a canon.) Death notice, Chronicle 3421–3424: "From this same brother he received his investiture after six years of probation, and from the early days of the monastery he endured great poverty and many labours and temptations." Grube, editor (Busch 615–617), explains the long wait by the rule against entering where a brother is prior ("dieses Hinderniss ... da sein Bruder Prior auf dem Agnetenberge war"): editor's inference. Documented (date); the reason for the wait is Inferential-Thin.

**E5. 1429–1432: exile for obedience to the pope.** Own words, Chronicle 1819–1829: the States of Overijssel rejected the pope's bishop of Utrecht and were placed under an interdict (a ban on church services). "Alas! Holy God! on the day before the Feast of St. Lambert we ceased from our singing by reason of the Interdict ... we suffered many insults, and at last we were driven to go forth from our country and our monasteries in order to observe the Interdict." Chronicle 1887–1896: the houses "chose rather to give place to the people that were enraged against them, and to be exiles for justice' sake than to consent to such commandments". Chronicle 1908–1915: they sailed to "their Brothers at Lunenkerc" in Frisia, "and these abode three years in Lunenkerc". Chronicle 1923–1924, list of exiles: "the second was Brother Thomas of Kempen, the Sub-Prior". Return: Chronicle 1981–2001, dated "1422 (1432)" in Arthur. Documented. **What he defended:** obedience to the pope's interdict, at the cost of exile.

**E6. 1432: his brother's death.** Own words, Chronicle 2010–2026: John of Kempen died "at midnight ... in Bethany ... I was with him and I closed his eyes, for I had been sent by the Visitors to bear him company, and I abode with him for a year and two months." Documented.

**E7. Teacher of novices; sub-prior.** Sub-prior (the prior's deputy) in 1429 (Chronicle 1924). Elected again in 1448, Chronicle 2379–2386: "Brother Thomas of Kempen was nominated and elected after a brief scrutiny. He was one of the elders, being sixty-seven years of age, and in past times had been appointed to this office, and albeit he knew himself to be insufficient and would have made excuse, yet he did submit him humbly". Documented.

Scully, editor (Scully 241–251): "For many years the Venerable Thomas à Kempis had charge of the young Religious at Mount St. Agnes. The office of training the Novices was with the Windesheimers usually attached to the sub-priorship". Editor's claim (Dominant Modern Reconstruction); the prologue confirms he gathered sermons for the novices ("pro noviciis nostris ... in unum collegi", Pohl VI 255–257; Scully's "given to our Novices" is looser than the Latin):

Own words, Sermons to the Novices, prologue. Latin, Pohl VI 252–261: "Sermones quos per modum dulcis collationis pro noviciis nostris diversis quidem temporibus in unum collegi ... Quamvis igitur incultus sit sermo, et materia non ardua; tamen simplicibus placere cupiens, quod Dominus inspiravit et dedit: libenter piis et devotis offero ad legendum." Scully 1011–1020: "The Sermons, which although given to our Novices by way of sweet conference at different times, I have gathered into one ... Albeit, therefore, the discourse be uncouth and the matter not subtle : nevertheless, wishing to please simple minds, that which the Lord has inspired and bestowed: I freely offer to the pious and devout to read." He signs himself "Frater peregrinus in valle lacrimarum" (Pohl VI 248–249), "a Brother Pilgrim dwelling in the vale of tears" (Scully 1003–1004). Documented.

**E8. Writing the house's memory.** Chronicle preface, own words, 292–300: "The pious desire of certain of our Brothers hath constrained me to put together a short chronicle ... that the said chronicle may be a comfort to them that are now alive, and a memorial for them that come after ... these I have seen with mine own eyes, or have heard from the Elders of our House, or else have gathered from the writings of others." Founders preface, own words, 2063–2069: "since many Fathers and devout brethren, who were of old well known to me, are fallen asleep, I fear not to speak of their virtues, that by so doing I may leave a good memorial of them to those that come after". Arthur, editor (Founders 1541–1544): "The Lives are written in the form of a dialogue between an elder Brother of the house and a Novice". Documented.

**E9. 1471: death.** Death notice by another brother of the house, Chronicle 3409–3437 (contemporary):
> "SO FAR THE CHRONICLE WAS WRITTEN BY THOMAS OF KEMPEN; THE RESIDUE THEREOF WAS DONE BY ANOTHER." (3409–3410)

> "on the Feast of St. James the Less, and after Compline, died our most beloved Brother Thomas Hemerken ... He was in the ninety-second year of his age, and this was the sixty-third year after his investiture; likewise he had been a Priest for above fifty-seven years." (3413–3417)

> "Moreover, he wrote that complete copy of the Bible which we use, and also many other books for the use of the House, and for sale. Likewise he composed divers little books for the edification of the young, which books were plain and simple in style, but mighty in the matter thereof and in their effectual operation." (3426–3430)

> "The thought of the Lord's passion filled his heart with love, and he was wondrous comfortable to the troubled and the tempted; but as age grew upon him he was vexed with a dropsy in the legs, and so fell asleep in the Lord" (3432–3435)

Pohl quotes the Latin of one phrase, Pohl I 18033–18034: "Thomae tractatuli appellantur «praegrandes in sententia et operis efficacia»". Documented. Note: the notice does **not** name the Imitation.

Arthur, editor (Chronicle 236–241): the last pages were "added by an anonymous writer, and carry on the chronicle from the year 1471 ... to 1477 ... and contains a notice of the author written by a contemporary member of the community".

## 3. The Imitation of Christ: who wrote it

**A1. Busch names him (contemporary, a canon of Windesheim).** Busch 5410–5416, Latin, in the story of Prior John Vos's death in 1424:
> "Contigit ante paucos dies sui obitus, ut duo fratres notabiles de Monte sancte Agnetis prope ZuoUis ordinis nostri dictum priorem nostrum super certis rebus consulturi in Windesem advenirent, quorum unus frator Thomas de Kerapis vir probate vite, qui plures devotos tractatulos** composuit, videlicet „qui sequitur me" de imitacione Christi cum aliis, nocte insecuta sompnium vidit presagium futuronim."

("Kerapis" and "frator" are OCR for Kempis, frater.) Sense: "one of them, Brother Thomas of Kempis, a man of proven life, who wrote several devout little treatises, namely 'He who follows me', on the imitation of Christ, with others, saw a dream that night foretelling what was to come." Variant note 5423: three manuscripts read "libros" for "tractatulos". Busch's birth 1399/1400 (Busch file header line 2; Grube 1546–1554). Grube, editor (Busch 1664–1667), dates Busch's completed chronicle to 1464, in Thomas's lifetime. Busch is the earliest held witness naming Thomas as author. Contested (the authorship as a whole).

**A2. Thomas's own Chronicle tells the same dream without naming the dreamer.** Chronicle 1728–1734: "two Brothers came from Mount St. Agnes to Windesem to commune with the Prior. And one of them had a dream after this wise, which vision did foretell the Prior's death". Grube, editor (Busch 5428): "Die folgende Stelle hat das Chron. Montis St. Agnetis S. 50 entlehnt" (the Chronicle borrowed this passage). That the Chronicle borrowed from Busch is the editor's judgment.

**A3. The 1441 copy in his hand.** Neale 4731–4736 (1858, later): "the only reason for ascribing it to him consists in the epigraph, " Finished and completed in the year of our Lord 1441, by the hands of brother Thomas a Kempis, of the monastery of Mount Saint Agnes, near ZwoUe." The same reason might make him author of the Bible itself." Ingram 172 calls it "the autograph of 1441". Neale does not mention Busch.

**A4. Against Thomas.** Neale 4716–4722: "It is now, however, almost a settled point among ecclesiastical scholars, that Thomas was not its author." Benham 487–488 (1886): "There seems no doubt that this must be a mistake"; Benham 518–521 says the 1441-style closing note gave rise to "the erroneous notion that he was its author"; Benham 606–661 argues for John Gersen, an abbot of Vercelli, but grants (635–636) "our ground becomes somewhat uncertain". Arthur, Founders 1519–1524: "the present writer has seen a MS. of the first book of the " Imitatio," whose date would seem to be about 1423, in which that work is assigned to a Carthusian Monk."

**A5. Early copies under Gerson's name.** Ingram 1176–1182: Atkynson's English (printed 1504) speaks of the work "as ' compyled in Latin by the right worshypfulle doctor master lohn Gerson,'" Exercises 3101–3105, editors: the Spanish edition printed at Burgos in 1516 is titled "Contemptus mundi, compuesto por el famoso maestro en sancta theologia Juan Gerson, chanceller de París."

**A6. For Thomas.** Ingram 1183–1186: "I believe neither in the authorship of the Chancellor Gerson, nor in that of Gersen (who, indeed, appears to be an apocryphal person) ; I am convinced that, in the words of Hirsche, ' Thomas und kein Anderer der Verfasser der Imitatio ist,'" Pohl I 18010–18013, Latin: "suum loannem Gersen, quem ne fuisse quidem umquam persuasum habemus" (his John Gersen, who we are persuaded never even existed). Pohl I 17511–17518 and 17560–17583 (editor): a list "Tituli librorum et tractatuum fratris thome kempis", by an unknown author made "intra septendecim post mortem Thomae annos", lists his works in the same order as the Brussels autograph, with the fourth book of the Imitation before the usual third. That list is not held; it rests on Pohl. Arthur (Chronicle 252–256) holds that Thomas wrote "the first part at least of the great work with which his name is associated" in the early years of his priesthood: editor's view.

**Confidence on authorship:** Contested. Plain form: a canon of Windesheim in Thomas's own lifetime named him as the author; early copies and printings gave it to John Gerson; later scholars split.

**The book's own words.** Imitation I.1. Latin, Pohl II 699–706: "Quid prodest tibi alta de Trinitate disputare ; si careas humilitate unde displiceas Trinitati? Vere alta verba non faciunt sanctum et iustum : sed virtuosa vita efficit Deo carum. Opto magis sentire compunctionem : quam scire eius definitionem." Pohl's volume II contents list (Pohl II 574–579) begins "Qui sequitur me non ambulat in tenebris" (Busch's title) and puts "De sacramento" (the usual Book IV) third, before "Audiam quid loquatur in me Dominus Deus" (the usual Book III): the 1441 autograph's order. Book I's title (598–605) reads "[DE IMITATIONE CHRISTI QVAE VVLGO DICITVR LIBER PRIMVS]". Also quoted in a footnote, Ingram 1297: "'Opto magis sentire compunctionem quam scire ejus definitionem.'" Benham 1146–1149: "I had rather feel contrition than be skilful in the definition thereof." Old English Version, Ingram 1470–1471: "I desire more to knowe compunccion þen his diffinycion." Surrounding passage, Benham 1141–1146: "What doth it profit thee to enter into deep discussion concerning the Holy Trinity, if thou lack humility ... verily it is not deep words that make a man holy and upright ; it is a good life which maketh a man dear to God." Opening, Benham 1120–1123: "He that followeth me shall not walk in darkness, saith the Lord." Busch's title "qui sequitur me" is these opening words.

## 4. Impact, ranked heaviest first

**I1. Ignatius of Loyola and the Jesuits (heaviest: carried into the founding text of a religious order).**
- Câmara's Memoriale, from what Ignatius told him (1555; contemporary), Scripta 9403–9408, Spanish: "97. Item dixo mas: que en Manrresa auia visto primero el Gerçonzito, y nunca más auia querido leer otro libro de deuoción; y este encomendaua á todos los que trataua, y leya cada dia vn capitulo por orden; y despues de comer y otras horas lo abria assy sin orden, y siempre topaua lo que en aquella hora tenia en el coraçon, y lo de que tenia necessidad." (OCR marks cleaned only of the diacritic garble; the file reads "Ger^onzito", "m^", "A", "cora^^n".) Editors' note, Scripta 9431: "Libellus De imitatione Christi, Joanni Gersoni adscriptus." Câmara, Portuguese, Scripta 9409–9414: Ignatius was so familiar with the book that his conversation was "huma ligão uiua de Gersam" (a living reading of Gerson). Documented (Ignatius's reading); he knew it as Gerson's.
- Ignatius's own Spiritual Exercises, Spanish autograph, Exercises 24995–25000: "Para la segunda semana y así para adelante, mucho aprouecha el leer algunos ratos en los libros de ymitacione Xpi. o de los euangelios y de vidas de sanctos." Latin Versio prima, Exercises 25089–25093: "multum expediet interdum legere libellos aliquot deuotos, ut librum de Imitatione Christi vel euangelia vel uitas sanctorum." Documented (own words).
- Jesuit editors, Exercises 2643–2644: "Libellum Gerconzito notum est illum esse, quem De imitationc Christi Thomas a Kempis conscripsit." Editors' identification (1919).
- Oliver Manare, Jesuit, reply to Lancicius, Scripta 24338–24347, Latin: "in cubiculo suo secretiori ordinarie super mensam alios libros non habebat, quam nouum Testamentum et Thomam de Kempis, quem solebat appellare perdicem librorum spiritualium." Later witness (Manare entered 1551; Scripta 10067–10068). Widely Accepted. "Partridge" is Ignatius's nickname as Manare reports it; its sense is not explained in the source.

**I2. John Wesley and the Methodists.**
- Wesley's own words (written 1738, of 1725), Wesley 960–968: "When I was about twenty-two, my father pressed me to enter into holy orders. At the same time, the providence of God directing me to Kempis's Christian Pattern, I began to see that true religion was seated in the heart, and that God's law extended to all our thoughts as well as words and actions. I was, however, very angry at Kempis for being too strict ... Yet I had frequently much sensible comfort in reading him". Documented.
- Methodist Conference, 1746, Methodist minutes (`methodist-conference_minutes-v1-1744-1798_1862.txt`) 2643–2653: preachers are advised "Always to rise at four. 2. From four to five in the morning ... partly some close, practical book of divinity. In particular, The Life of God in the Soul of Man, Kempis, the Pilgrim's Progress". Documented.
- Curnock, editor (Wesley 9161): Rivington had "published the Wesley edition of Thomas a Kempis's De Imitatione Christi". Editor's note.

**I3. The first English, for the king's mother.** Ingram 168–178: Atkynson's version "was executed at the desire of Margaret, Countess of Richmond and Derby, the mother of King Henry the Seventh"; Book IV "was made by Margaret herself from the French, and was printed, along with Atkynson's version of the three preceding Books, by Wynkyn de Worde in 1504." Under Gerson's name (A5). Documented.

**I4. Across Protestant lines.**
- Philipp Jakob Spener, Lutheran Pietist, Pia Desideria (`spener_pia-desideria-deu_zunner1706.txt`) 5855–5857: "Zu den beyden ist auch zu setzen Thomæ à Kempis Nachfolgung Christi" (beside the two, the Theologia Germanica and Tauler, set Thomas à Kempis's Imitation of Christ). Documented.
- John Woolman, Quaker, 1757 (`woolman_journal-and-essays_gummere1922.txt`) 12189–12192: "I have been informed that Thomas a Kempis lived & died in the profession of the Roman Catholick Religion, and in reading his writings, I have believed him to be a man of a true Christian spirit, as fully so as many who died Martyrs". And 12219–12223: "Thomas a Kempis, without disputing against the Articles then generally agreed to, appears to have laboured, by a Pious Example as well as by Preaching & writing to promote Virtue and the Inward Spiritual Religion". Documented.
- Robert Barclay, Quaker (`barclay_apology-for-the-true-christian-divinity_baskerville1765.txt`) 12338–12340: "Bernard and Bonaventure, Taulerus, Thomas à Kempis, and divers others have both known and tasted of the Love of God". Documented.

**I5. Reach, general claims (editors).** Neale 4716–4720: the Imitation "next to the Holy Scriptures, has probably been read more widely than any other in the Western Church". Benham 663–664: "Whoever the author may be, he has produced the most popular religious work in Christendom." Editors' judgments; keep in their voices.

**I6. The house's memory carried on.** The Chronicle continued after his death by another brother (Chronicle 3409–3410; Arthur 236–241). Grube's notes trace Busch and Thomas's Chronicle sharing passages (Busch 3322, 5428, 19294). Documented.

## 5. Where sources disagree or come late

- **How he came, 1399.** Own words: came for indulgences and asked to stay (Chronicle 1103–1106). Death notice: Florens sent him at twenty to his brother (Chronicle 3419–3421). Both can be true; do not merge them into one motive.
- **Year he came.** Chronicle: 1399. Neale 4705: 1400.
- **Birth year.** Derived only (see section 1).
- **Day of death.** Notice: St James the Less, after Compline (Chronicle 3413). Dublin note: 25 July (Ingram 261).
- **The Bible copy.** Notice: "that complete copy of the Bible which we use" (Chronicle 3426). Benham 504–510: fifteen years, closing note dated "MCCCCXXXix" (1439), seen by Rosweyde at Cologne. Neale 4708–4711: "begun in 1417, and finished in 1459." Keep to the notice.
- **Authorship of the Imitation.** Section 3. Contested.
- **Parents.** Chronicle names "John" and "Gertrude" only (1193–1194). Benham 491–492: father "a labouring man", mother "the village schoolmistress", with no source. Leave out.
- **Was he a Brother of the Common Life?** He studied and lodged among them (Founders 8990–9016) but became a canon regular of the Windesheim order (Chronicle 1189–1195). Say "schooled by the Brothers", not "a Brother".
