Thomas was a young scholar at Deventer when he walked to Zwolle, in what is now the Netherlands, to gain the pope's pardons at St Michael's church. The year was 1399, and he went on, glad at heart, to a poor, half-built house of canons called Mount St Agnes, and asked to stay.

Thomas was born about 1380 in Kempen, near Cologne. At Deventer he had been schooled and lodged by the priest Florens Radewijns, who led the Brothers of the Common Life in a movement called the New Devotion. There he had seen men who shared all they had and spent their days copying books.

After six years of testing, Thomas was clothed in 1406 as a canon, a priest living in community under the Rule of St Augustine. In 1429 a disputed election for bishop of Utrecht brought a papal ban on church services. The canons kept it, and chose exile in Frisia over breaking it.

One little book credited to him travelled much further. It is the Imitation of Christ, and it says, "I would rather feel sorrow for sin than know how to define it." Johannes Busch, a canon of Thomas's own order writing in his lifetime, names Thomas as its author. But early copies gave it to John Gerson, a Paris theologian, and scholars still disagree.

Ignatius of Loyola, who founded the Jesuits, knew it by that other name. He told a fellow Jesuit, Luis Gonçalves da Câmara, who wrote it down in 1555: "At Manresa he had first seen the little Gerson. After that he never wanted to read any other book of devotion. He commended it to everyone he dealt with. He read one chapter a day, in order." Ignatius set it as reading in his Spiritual Exercises, the retreat he wrote.

Centuries later the young John Wesley, reading it in English, began to see that true religion was seated in the heart. His Methodists later set it for their preachers' early mornings. The Quaker John Woolman, told that Thomas had lived and died a Roman Catholic, still judged him a man of a true Christian spirit.

Back at Mount St Agnes, Thomas gathered into one book the talks made for the novices, the men in their trial year, in plain speech meant for simple minds. He wrote his house's Chronicle as a memorial for those who came after.

He died there after the last prayer of the day. It was 1471, and he was in his ninety-second year.
