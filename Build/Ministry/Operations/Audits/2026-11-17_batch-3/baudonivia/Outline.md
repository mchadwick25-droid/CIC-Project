# Baudonivia — Outline

File keys and line numbers are those of `Source_Sheet.md` (SRM2 = Krusch, MGH SRM 2; AA4 = Fortunatus, MGH AA 4; SRM3 = MGH SRM 3; GregLat = Gregory, Arndt-Krusch; Dalton = Gregory in English). Row numbers (5.21 and so on) point to the sheet.

- **The heart of her life:** Baudonivia is on the tree for one book, and the heart of her life is what that book holds. A nun of the convent of the Holy Cross at Poitiers wrote a second Life of the woman who founded it, Radegund, once a Frankish queen. She wrote it on the order of her abbess and the whole community, who knew that life "best" (SRM2 29440, row 5.19), and she wrote it to be heard by Radegund's "flock" (SRM2 29451–29452, row 5.20). Her stated aim was to tell what the poet Fortunatus, who wrote the first Life, had passed over (SRM2 29493–29496, row 4.1). *Documented.*
  What the book tells, as the house remembered its founder:
  - **The queen who would not give way.** Still a queen, on her way to a dinner, Radegund had a pagan shrine of the Franks burned, and sat her horse unmoved while Franks came with swords and clubs, until it burned and peace was made (SRM2 29663–29675, row 5.1). *Documented as her report* ("what we heard we tell", SRM2 29664–29665).
  - **The peacemaker.** When the Frankish kingdoms moved against each other, Radegund wrote the same letters to each rival king, that they not make war, "and that the homeland not perish"; she wrote to their nobles too, and taught the nuns to pray and keep vigils for the kings (SRM2 29987–29995, row 5.21). *Documented as her report.*
  - **The Cross.** She sought a piece of the wood of Christ's cross from the emperor, with King Sigibert's leave, "for the safety of the whole homeland" (SRM2 30246–30249, row 5.22). It was kept out of Poitiers; the whole community fasted and wept every day, until the king had the bishop of Tours bring it into the convent (SRM2 30256–30312, rows 5.9, 5.23). Baudonivia sets Radegund beside the empress Helena, who in the old story found the Cross (SRM2 30238–30245). She says Radegund left it "for the honour of the place and the saving of the people" (SRM2 30313–30322, row 5.10). *Documented* (her account); who kept it out is *Contested* (row 7.1).
  - **The death seen from inside.** The nuns round the bed, crying that their light was taken; "her eyes were closed, and ours were darkened"; the nuns weeping from the wall, since none of them went out of the gate (SRM2 30514–30522, 30581–30584, rows 5.13, 5.16, 5.25). *Documented.*
  What it added: Fortunatus's book has no relic of the Cross and no death scene (Krusch, SRM2 28175–28180), and a search of his book finds no shrine burning and no letters for peace (row 5.27). *Documented* (as far as the search reaches).
  Why it matters that a woman of the house wrote it: no held source says what it means. What the sources hold is plain, and the writer tells that. She writes as one of Radegund's "daughters" and in "we": the words Radegund spoke to "us", the prayers "she taught us", the grief "we" felt (SRM2 29900–29903, 29988–29989, 30522). So her book gives Radegund as the women she gathered saw her, where Fortunatus, a friend of the house, saw her from outside it. A later bishop weighed the two and followed Fortunatus for his "rank as bishop" (SRM2 28229–28231, row 5.28). *Documented* (her stance and Hildebert's choice); any larger claim about her as a woman writer is *Inferential-Thin* and is not made (see Do not say).

- **Length:** 2 minutes (about 300 words, Neo's voice). Her own life is a blank beyond her book: no family, age, entry or death is held. The book is rich enough for 2 minutes, not 3, and she is not a major figure on the tree. Do not stretch. The heart is told by about word 150.

- **Setting:**
  - Time: she wrote soon after 600 (Krusch's reasoning, SRM2 28186–28197; AA4 41899). Spoken: "a little after the year 600". Radegund died on 13 August (SRM2 30521–30522); the year 587 is Krusch's (SRM2 28171). Spoken: "in 587", with Radegund named in that sentence.
  - Place: the convent of the Holy Cross at Poitiers, in Gaul, which Radegund, once the wife of the Frankish king Chlothar, had built (SRM2 29743–29751; 28153). Spoken: "Poitiers, in Frankish Gaul"; "the convent of the Holy Cross".
  - The house: about two hundred nuns stood round Radegund's bier (GregLat 61577–61578), and no nun went out of its gate (SRM2 30581–30582).
  - Tradition: The Church of Merovingian Gaul (census informalName).

- **Shape:** Founder or leader, told through the one who knew the founder from inside. The founder is Radegund; Baudonivia is the daughter who set down how the house remembered her. The impact sits in what the book kept: the house's own memory of its founder. Trap to avoid: titles in place of acts, and its mirror here, a story about how the book was written. Tell what Radegund did, as Baudonivia tells it ("she tells how..."), so the listener meets both women at once.

- **Openings:**
  1. Radegund's words to her nuns, as Baudonivia keeps them: "I chose you, daughters... You are the new planting" (SRM2 29900–29903). Then: one of those daughters wrote her life. This makes plain sense heard once, and leads straight into the heart: a woman of the house writing the founder's life.
  2. The funeral seen from inside: the nuns, who never went out of the gate, weeping from the wall as the body passed below (SRM2 30581–30584). Then: one of them wrote it down. Strong, but it starts at the end; use it only if the story is told as a look back.

- **Story beats** (the heart is in beats 1 to 3, inside the first minute):
  1. **The daughter and her book.** Radegund, a queen who left her husband, King Chlothar, built a convent at Poitiers and called its nuns her daughters (SRM2 29677 "a rege terreno discessit", she left the earthly king; 29743–29751; 29900–29903). A little after 600 one of them, Baudonivia, was told by her abbess and the community to write Radegund's life (SRM2 29433–29440). The poet Fortunatus had already written one; she meant to tell what he had passed over (SRM2 29493–29496). *Documented;* the date is Krusch's, *Dominant Modern Reconstruction*.
  2. **The queen who would not give way.** The shrine burned, the Franks with swords and clubs, the queen unmoved on her horse (SRM2 29663–29675). A "shrine" here is a pagan holy place of the Franks. *Documented as her report.*
  3. **The peacemaker.** The same letters to each rival king, the letters to their nobles, the nuns set to pray and keep vigils for the kings (SRM2 29987–29995). The reason she gives: that the kings make peace and "the homeland not perish". *Documented as her report.*
  4. **The Cross.** Sought for the safety of the homeland, with Sigibert's leave (SRM2 30246–30249); kept out of the city; the community fasting and weeping; brought in at last by Eufronius, bishop of Tours, on the king's order (SRM2 30256–30312). She blames "the enemy of mankind, through his agents" and names no one (SRM2 30258–30261). Radegund set beside Helena (SRM2 30238–30245). Left "for the honour of the place and the saving of the people"; cures there, in her voice (SRM2 30313–30322). *Documented* (her account); who blocked it, *Contested*.
  5. **The house at home** (optional, if time). Meaning to call the portress, the nun who kept the gate, Radegund cried "alleluia" instead (SRM2 29891–29894). *Documented as her report.*
  6. **The death.** The nuns round the bed crying that their light was taken (SRM2 30517–30518); "her eyes were closed, and ours were darkened" (SRM2 30522). The bishop of Poitiers was away, and Gregory of Tours buried her (SRM2 30536–30579). The nuns wept from the wall as the body passed (SRM2 30581–30584). *Documented.*
  7. **How she wrote** (one sentence, two at most). She called her Latin "rustic", plain and unpolished, and herself "less learned, more devoted" (SRM2 29492–29499). *Documented.* The borrowed phrases and her editor's pardon go to the card, not the story; if one is used, it is this sentence.

- **What came of her** (about a third of the story):
  1. **The house's memory reached later readers beside the poet's.** In most manuscripts her book follows Fortunatus's as the second book of Radegund's Life (Krusch: AA4 41918–41919; SRM2 28239–28339). Readers who met the queen through the poet met her also as her daughters remembered her: the peacemaker, the Cross, the death. *Documented.*
  2. **What she defended:** the Cross as Radegund's gift to the city, set equal to Helena's finding of it (SRM2 30245, 30322–30323), and Radegund as a maker of peace between kings (SRM2 29987–29995). *Documented.*
  3. **A later bishop used it.** Hildebert, bishop of Le Mans, rewriting the Life in the Middle Ages, went back to "Fortunatus and Baudonivia the nun", followed the bishop, and added some miracles the nun had recorded, "not all" (his words, quoted by Krusch, SRM2 28227–28233). *Documented as quoted by Krusch; Hildebert's text not held.*
  4. **Later scholars took her as the house's contemporary witness:** for who built the convent, for Fortunatus as a bishop of Poitiers (AASS-Mar2 136834–136841; PL72 50773–50777; PL88 2212–2219). The abbess Dedimia is known only from her (SRM2 29481). *Documented.* For the card more than the story.

- **Quotes** (rendered from Krusch's Latin, SRM2):
  1. "Vos elegi filias, vos, mea lumina, vos, mea vita, vos, mea requies totaque felicitas, vos, novella plantatio." (SRM2 29901–29903; OCR "raea" = mea)
     Rendering: **"I chose you, daughters. You are my light, my life, my rest and all my happiness. You are the new planting."**
     Job: Radegund's own words to the community, kept by one of the daughters she named. Opens the heart: who the book was written by and for.
  2. "Quod fecit illa in orientali patria, hoc fecit beata Radegundis in Gallia." (SRM2 30245)
     Rendering: **"What she did in the land of the East, blessed Radegund did in Gaul."** ("She" is Helena, named in the sentence before, SRM2 30239.)
     Job: what she claimed for the Cross and for her founder.
  3. "clausi sunt eius oculi, et obscurati sunt nostri." (SRM2 30522)
     Rendering: **"Her eyes were closed, and ours were darkened."**
     Job: the community's grief, in the voice of one who was there.
  ("Less learned, more devoted", SRM2 29492–29493, is no longer a quote; the writer may paraphrase it in beat 7.)

- **Connection to carry into the story:** From Radegund to her daughters, and through Baudonivia's book to every later reader of the Life. Radegund taught the nuns to pray for the kings' peace (SRM2 29988–29989, 29993–29995), and she left the Cross in their house "for the honour of the place and the saving of the people" (SRM2 30313–30322). Baudonivia set down that inheritance as the house's own memory, and claimed for its founder a place beside Helena (SRM2 30245). The listener needs to understand three things the story must say outright. Radegund founded the house and called its nuns her daughters. Baudonivia was one of them, writing for the others. What she added to Fortunatus is what only the house had: the founder's words to them, her prayers for peace, the gift she left them, and her death among them. Let it come through these acts. The book's handing-on (beside Fortunatus in the manuscripts) is one sentence in What came of her, not the connection.

- **Links:**
  - Radegund — lived under her; wrote her Life (Book II).
  - Venantius Fortunatus — continued his book; quoted his book; borrowed his prologues.
  - Gregory of Tours — quoted his book (Glory of the Confessors 104); in her story he buries Radegund.
  - Caesarius of Arles — her house kept his rule; she copied from his Life.

- **Who tells it:**
  - **Baudonivia** herself, a little after 600 (Krusch's date), some years after Radegund's death. She writes as one of the community ("we"), on her abbess's order, so her praise is a daughter's. The listener should know this once, early; it is part of the heart, not a footnote.
  - **Gregory of Tours**, a bishop and eyewitness at the burial, writing in the 590s. He alone names the bishop of Poitiers, Maroveus, as the man who would not receive the Cross. For the card unless the Cross beat needs him.
  - **Bruno Krusch**, her 19th-century editor, who dates the book and traced her borrowings. His dates and judgements are his. The story need not name him; if the date needs a witness, "by her editor's dating" once.
  - **Hildebert of Le Mans**, a medieval bishop, known here only through Krusch's quotation; his own text is not held.
  - How-we-know gets a sentence or two. The doubt is not the story here.

- **Do not say:**
  - "Radegund raised her from the cradle" or "she grew up at Radegund's side" → the phrase is borrowed word for word from Fortunatus, who used it of a bishop raised by Hilary's monastery (AA4 43008–43009, 41118–41122). Say nothing of her upbringing.
  - Any birth, family, age, office or death for her → none is held. "We know her only from her book."
  - "Touching the sky with a finger" as her own invention → it is Fortunatus's phrase (AA4 43014–43015). Best left out now; if used, say she borrowed it.
  - "The first woman to write a saint's life", "the only woman", "a woman's voice the church had never heard" → no held source says so. Say what she was: a nun writing the founder's life for her sisters.
  - "Fortunatus wrote nothing of her as a queen" or "he left out her whole life" → he wrote of her as queen and as nun. Say only what is checked: his book has no relic of the Cross and no death scene, and no shrine burning or peace letters (rows 4.3, 5.27).
  - "Fortunatus, a man, could not see what she saw" or any motive for his omissions → he says he kept it short "lest plenty bore you" (SRM2 29385–29386). Say no more.
  - "Baudonivia wrote to the kings" → it is Radegund who wrote; Baudonivia tells it.
  - "The Cross brought peace" or "the letters stopped the war" → no outcome is given. She gives Radegund's aim: that they make peace and the homeland not perish.
  - "She could not write" (from Krusch's "scribere nesciens") → Krusch means she lacked skill in composition. Say "plain Latin".
  - "The bishop Maroveus kept the Cross out, she says" → she names no one; Gregory names Maroveus. Keep the two witnesses apart.
  - "Emperor Justin and Empress Sophia", "a gold triptych", "Constantinople" → not in her text. Say "the emperor" and "the wood of the Cross, adorned with gold and gems" (SRM2 30253–30254).
  - "The nuns were angry that Fortunatus left out the Cross" → Krusch's guess. Say she wrote what he passed over, as she herself says.
  - "Gather, gather the Lord's wheat", "what a face, good God", "reading never stopped before her", "she never spoke ill of anyone" as her words or memories → copied from the Life of Caesarius (SRM3 39195, 39266, 37535–37545).
  - The vision of the tribune Domolenus on the day of death → it is in Fortunatus's book (SRM2 29380–29384), not hers.
  - "She wrote to heal the house after the revolt" → her book never mentions the revolt; no source gives this motive.
  - "Eyewitness to everything" → for the shrine burning and the king's pursuit she says "what we heard we tell". Only the death, the burial and her later witnesses are told in "we".
  - Wonders (the cures at the Cross, the angel the stonecutters heard, the finger of St Mammes) → if used, keep them in her voice: "she tells that...".
  - 587 as her date, or any 587 sentence where "her" could be heard as Baudonivia → name Radegund in the sentence that gives 587. Her book is "a little after 600", and that date is Krusch's.
  - "She revered him as 'that apostolic man'" → "apostolic man" is her standard title for any bishop (SRM2 29745, 29806, 30309, 30537, 30652).
  - Any year for Hildebert → the OCR "1197" (SRM2 28228) cannot be trusted. Say "in the Middle Ages".
  - "Hildebert praised her" or "preferred her" → he followed the bishop and took some of her miracles.
  - "Fortunatus, a bishop by the time Baudonivia wrote" as a passing aside → filler (Substance_Read, 2026-11-18). Call him "the poet Fortunatus".

- **Leave out:**
  - The making of the book beyond one sentence: the borrowed prologue, the sky-and-finger phrase, Krusch's verdict on her Latin and his pardon. They tell how we know her, not what she gave; they belong on the card.
  - The revolt of Clotild and Basina against the abbess Leubovera (Dalton 19405ff.): the house's later story, not her book, and it invites a motive no source gives.
  - The king's attempt to take Radegund back and Germanus at Martin's tomb (SRM2 29799–29822): a strong scene, but it crowds the peace and the Cross out of two minutes.
  - Reovalis at the nuns' trial (Dalton 21329ff.), the relics of St Mammes and the doves' feathers at sea (SRM2 30119–30169, 30328–30377): side stories and wonders; one wonder at most, in her voice.
  - Her phrase "in the Jewish manner" about those who blocked the Cross (SRM2 30260): it needs more context than a two-minute piece can give fairly. Leaving it out does not soften the conflict, which stays: the relic was refused entry, and she says so.
  - The Wednesday and the Lord's birth (SRM2 30519–30522): needs a gloss the story cannot carry.
  - Fortunatus's hymns for the Cross (Dalton 29581–29583): his story, not hers.
