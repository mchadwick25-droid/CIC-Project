# Baudonivia — Outline

File keys and line numbers are those of `Source_Sheet.md` (SRM2 = Krusch, MGH SRM 2; AA4 = Fortunatus, MGH AA 4; SRM3 = MGH SRM 3; GregLat = Gregory, Arndt-Krusch; Dalton = Gregory in English).

- **Length:** 2 minutes (about 300 words). Her own life is a blank beyond her book: no family, age, entry or death is held. The book itself is rich enough for 2 minutes, not 3. Do not stretch.

- **Setting:**
  - Time: she wrote soon after 600 (Krusch's reasoning, SRM2 28186–28197; AA4 41899). Spoken: "a little after the year 600". Radegund died on 13 August, a Wednesday (SRM2 30521–30522); the year 587 is Krusch's (SRM2 28171). Spoken: "in 587".
  - Place: the monastery of the Holy Cross at Poitiers, in Gaul, which Radegund, once a queen of the Frankish king Chlothar, had built (SRM2 29743–29751; 28153). Spoken: "Poitiers, in Frankish Gaul"; "the convent of the Holy Cross".
  - The house: it kept the rule of Caesarius of Arles (Dalton 19626–19629), and its nuns did not go out of its gate (SRM2 30581–30582). About two hundred nuns stood round Radegund's bier (GregLat 61577–61578).
  - Tradition: The Church of Merovingian Gaul (census informalName).

- **Thread:** A nun told what the bishop-poet had left out: the community's own memory of its founder, in plain Latin she herself called "rustic", so the church received a second Life of Radegund, written from inside her house.

- **Shape:** Teacher or thinker: the question she answered (what did the first book leave out?), what she wrote, and who read it after her. The fit is partial: she is a writer and witness, not a teacher. The trap to avoid is the same: do not turn her into a summary of Radegund's life. Show her choices. Tell each Radegund event as her choice ("she put in what he left out: ..."), never as the queen's biography.

- **Openings:**
  1. Her prologue to her abbess, in a phrase she took from Fortunatus: being told to write this is "as impossible as touching the sky with a finger" (SRM2 29439–29441). Then the turn: she wrote it anyway (the phrase is borrowed from Fortunatus; see Do not say).
  2. The funeral seen from inside: the nuns, who could not leave the house, weeping from its wall as the body passed below, "tears for a psalm, groaning for alleluia" (SRM2 30581–30584).

- **Story beats:**
  1. **The first book.** Venantius Fortunatus, a poet and friend of the house, a bishop by the time she wrote (SRM2 29493), wrote Radegund's Life after her death. He ended it saying a little would do, "lest plenty bore you" (SRM2 29385–29386). His book has no Cross relic and no death scene (Krusch, SRM2 28175–28180). *Documented* (the gap); that the nuns were displeased is Krusch's guess (*Inferential-Thin*).
  2. **The order.** Some time after 600 (Krusch's date, SRM2 28186–28197: *Dominant Modern Reconstruction*), the abbess Dedimia and the whole community told Baudonivia to write. She calls herself "less learned, more devoted", and her style "rustic, not polished" (SRM2 29433–29437, 29492–29499). Her aim was not to repeat Fortunatus but to tell what he passed over for length (SRM2 29493–29496). "Rustic" here means plain, unpolished Latin, a standard way for a writer of the time to show humility. *Documented* (the order, her words and her aim).
  3. **How she gathered it.** She says "what we heard we tell, and what we saw we bear witness to" (SRM2 29664–29665). She names living witnesses: Leo, a man of rank, who told "us with his own mouth" that he was cured (SRM2 30181–30191), and a woman "giving thanks to this day" (SRM2 30055–30056). She points readers to Gregory of Tours's book for what he saw (SRM2 30537–30539). She also borrowed whole passages from the Life of Caesarius of Arles (Krusch, SRM2 28222–28223; verified SRM3, see Source Sheet 4.7). *Documented.*
  4. **The queen Fortunatus did not show.** She chose to put in that Radegund, still a queen, had a Frankish shrine burned on her way to a dinner, and sat her horse while Franks came with swords and clubs (SRM2 29663–29675). A "shrine" here is a pagan holy place of the Franks. She chose to put in Radegund writing the same letters to rival kings, begging them not to make war (SRM2 29987–29995). Optional, if time: the king trying to take Radegund back, and Germanus, bishop of Paris, falling at the king's feet at Martin's tomb in Tours (SRM2 29799–29822). *Documented as her report.*
  5. **The daily life.** Once, meaning to call the portress, the nun who kept the gate, she cried "alleluia" instead of her name (SRM2 29891–29894). Baudonivia keeps her words to the nuns: "I chose you, daughters... you, the new planting" (SRM2 29900–29903). *Documented as her report.*
  6. **The Cross.** Radegund sought a piece of the wood of Christ's cross from the emperor, with King Sigibert's leave (SRM2 30246–30250). When it reached Poitiers it was kept out of the city. She says the bishop of the place and all the people wished to receive it devoutly, but blames "the enemy of mankind, through his agents", names no one, and adds: "Let them see to it; the Lord knows who are his" (SRM2 30256–30261). It waited at Tours until the king had the bishop of Tours, Eufronius, bring it in (SRM2 30263–30312). She sets it beside the empress Helena's finding of the Cross (SRM2 30238–30245). *Documented* (her account); who blocked it is *Contested*.
  7. **The death.** The nuns beat their breasts round the bed; "her eyes were closed, and ours were darkened" (SRM2 30514–30522). The bishop of Poitiers was away; Gregory of Tours buried her after three days' wait (SRM2 30536–30579). The body passed under the wall, and the nuns wept from above (SRM2 30581–30584). *Documented.*

- **What came of her** (about a third of the story):
  1. Her book was copied as the second book of Radegund's Life, after Fortunatus's, in most manuscripts (Krusch: AA4 41918–41919; SRM2 28239–28339). The poet's Life and the nun's Life reached later readers together. *Documented.*
  2. When Hildebert, bishop of Le Mans, rewrote Radegund's Life in the Middle Ages, he went back to the writings "of Fortunatus and of Baudonivia the nun". He chose to follow Fortunatus, whose authority came "no less from his life than from his rank as bishop". He added some miracles that Fortunatus never touched and the nun had recorded, "not all" of them (his words, quoted by Krusch, SRM2 28227–28233). *Documented as quoted by Krusch; Hildebert's text not held.*
  3. Later scholars (the Bollandists, Migne's notes, Luchi's edition of Fortunatus) used her as the contemporary record of the Poitiers church: for who built the convent, for Fortunatus as a bishop, for dating a bishop of Autun (AASS-Mar2 136834–136841; PL72 50773–50777, 51728–51735; PL88 2212–2219). The abbess Dedimia is known only from her (SRM2 29481). *Documented.*
  4. What she defended: the Cross as Radegund's gift to the city, equal to Helena's (SRM2 30245, 30322–30323); and Radegund as a maker of peace between kings (SRM2 29987–29995). *Documented.*
  5. Her editor Krusch found her Latin very rough and her borrowing heavy, but wrote that "you will pardon good Baudonivia, who confesses her weakness" (SRM2 28219–28225). *Documented as Krusch's view.*

- **Quotes** (rendered from Krusch's Latin, SRM2):
  1. "quae sum minus docta, plus devota" (SRM2 29492–29493, with "vestra me oratione iuvari peto").
     Rendering: **"I ask your prayer to help me. I am less learned, more devoted."**
     Job: her own self-portrait, in her own words, at the start.
  2. "Quod fecit illa in orientali patria, hoc fecit beata Radegundis in Gallia." (SRM2 30245)
     Rendering: **"What she did in the land of the East, blessed Radegund did in Gaul."** ("She" is Helena, named in the sentence before, SRM2 30239.)
     Job: shows what she claimed for the Cross and for her founder.
  3. "clausi sunt eius oculi, et obscurati sunt nostri." (SRM2 30522)
     Rendering: **"Her eyes were closed, and ours were darkened."**
     Job: the community's grief, in the voice of one who was there.

- **Connection to carry into the story:** Baudonivia's book passed Radegund to later readers as her own community remembered her, not only as the poet-bishop saw her. The supporting passages: her stated aim (SRM2 29493–29496), the manuscripts that join her book to Fortunatus's (AA4 41918–41919), and Hildebert's choice to follow the bishop but add some miracles the nun had recorded (SRM2 28227–28233). The listener needs to understand that Fortunatus had already written the official Life, and that she wrote alongside it, not against it: she names him with the title she gives every bishop, "the apostolic man, Bishop Fortunatus", and quotes his own closing line. Her additions are the Cross, the daily life, the queen's quarrels with the king and the death. Hildebert shows both halves of what came of it: her book was kept and used, but followed second to the bishop's.

- **Links:**
  - Radegund — lived under her; wrote her life (Book II).
  - Venantius Fortunatus — continued his book; quoted his book; borrowed his prologues.
  - Gregory of Tours — quoted his book (Glory of the Confessors 104); in her story he buries Radegund.
  - Caesarius of Arles — her house kept his rule; she copied from his Life.

- **Who tells it:**
  - **Baudonivia** herself, Book II, a little after 600 (Krusch's date), some years after Radegund's death. She writes as one of the community ("we"). The listener should know she is a nun writing about her own founder, on her abbess's orders, so her praise is a daughter's.
  - **Gregory of Tours**, a bishop and eyewitness at the burial, writing in the 590s. He alone names the bishop of Poitiers, Maroveus, as the man who would not receive the Cross.
  - **Bruno Krusch**, her 19th-century editor, who dates the book and traced her borrowings. His dates and judgements are his.
  - **Hildebert of Le Mans**, a medieval bishop, known here only through Krusch's quotation; his own text is not held.

- **Do not say:**
  - "Radegund raised her from the cradle" or "she grew up at Radegund's side" → The phrase is borrowed word for word from Fortunatus, who used it of a bishop raised by Hilary's monastery (AA4 43008–43009, 41118–41122). Say nothing of her upbringing.
  - Any birth, family, age, office or death for her → none is held. "We know her only from her book."
  - "Touching the sky with a finger" as her own invention → it is Fortunatus's phrase (AA4 43014–43015). If used, say she borrowed it, or use it without implying it is original.
  - "The first woman to write a saint's life" or "the only woman" → no held source says so.
  - "She could not write" (from Krusch's "scribere nesciens") → Krusch means she lacked skill in composition. Say "her editor found her Latin rough".
  - "The bishop Maroveus kept the Cross out, she says" → she names no one; Gregory names Maroveus. Keep the two witnesses apart.
  - "Emperor Justin and Empress Sophia", "a gold triptych" → not in her text (Krusch's and Dalton's notes). Say "the emperor" and "the wood of the Cross, adorned with gold and gems" (SRM2 30253–30254).
  - "The nuns were angry that Fortunatus left out the Cross" → Krusch's guess. Say she wrote what he passed over, as she herself says.
  - "Gather, gather the Lord's wheat" or "what a face, good God" as her words or Radegund's → copied from the Life of Caesarius (SRM3 39195, 39266).
  - The vision of the tribune Domolenus on the day of death → it is in Fortunatus's book (SRM2 29380–29384), not hers.
  - "She wrote to heal the house after the revolt" → her book never mentions the revolt; no source gives this motive.
  - "Eyewitness to everything" → for the shrine burning and the king's pursuit, she says "what we heard we tell". Only the death, the burial and her later witnesses are told in "we".
  - Wonders (the angel the stonecutters heard, the finger of St Mammes) → if used, keep them in her voice: "she tells that..."
  - 587 as her date → that is Radegund's death. Her book is "a little after 600", and that date is Krusch's.
  - "Reading never stopped before her" / "she never spoke ill of anyone" as Baudonivia's memories of Radegund → copied from the Life of Caesarius I.45 (SRM2 29887–29897 = SRM3 37535–37545). Leave them out.
  - "She revered him as 'that apostolic man'" → "apostolic man" is her standard title for any bishop (Pientius 29745, Germanus 29806, Eufronius 30309, Gregory 30537, Leifastus 30652).
  - Any year for Hildebert → the OCR "1197" (SRM2 28228) cannot be trusted. Say "in the Middle Ages".
  - "Hildebert praised her" or "preferred her" → he followed the bishop and took some of her miracles.
  - "Constantinople" → not in her text; she writes "the emperor" (SRM2 30250).

- **Leave out:**
  - The revolt of Clotild and Basina against the abbess Leubovera (Gregory, Dalton 19405ff.): it belongs to the house's story, not to her book, and drawing it in invites a motive no source gives.
  - Reovalis at the nuns' trial and the surgery he describes (Dalton 21329ff.): a side story.
  - The relics of St Mammes and the doves' feathers at sea (SRM2 30119–30169, 30328–30377): miracle stories that would crowd the time; one wonder at most.
  - Her phrase "in the Jewish manner" about those who blocked the Cross (SRM2 30260): it needs more context than a two-minute piece can give fairly. Leaving it out does not soften the conflict, which stays: the relic was refused entry, and she says so.
  - The Wednesday and the Lord's birth (SRM2 30519–30522; Krusch's note 30562): needs a gloss the story cannot carry.
  - Fortunatus's hymns for the Cross (Dalton 29581–29583): his story, not hers.
