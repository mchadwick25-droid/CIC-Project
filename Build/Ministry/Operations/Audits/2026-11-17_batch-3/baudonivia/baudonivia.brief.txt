Writing this, she told her abbess, was as impossible as touching the sky with a finger. She had borrowed the phrase from the poet Venantius Fortunatus, and then she wrote anyway.

We know Baudonivia only from her book. She was a nun of the convent of the Holy Cross at Poitiers, in Frankish Gaul, founded by Radegund, once a Frankish queen. After Radegund died in 587, Fortunatus, a bishop by the time Baudonivia wrote, told her life, but said nothing of the relic of the Cross, and nothing of her death.

A little after the year 600, by the dating of her nineteenth-century editor, the abbess and the whole community told Baudonivia to write. She called her Latin rustic, meaning plain and unpolished, and wrote: "I ask your prayer to help me. I am less learned, more devoted." She meant not to repeat the poet, but to tell what he had passed over.

In most copies her book followed Fortunatus's as the second book of Radegund's Life, so later readers met Radegund as her own house remembered her. A medieval bishop who rewrote the Life followed Fortunatus, but took some miracles the nun had recorded. Her editor found her Latin rough, yet he asked readers to pardon good Baudonivia, who confessed her weakness.

In her book she told how the queen had a pagan shrine of the Franks burned, and sat her horse as they came with swords and clubs. She told of the queen's letters begging rival kings not to make war. Radegund sought a piece of Christ's cross from the emperor, but it was kept outside the city until the king had it brought in. Baudonivia blamed the enemy of mankind and his agents, naming no one. She set her founder beside Helena, the empress who found the Cross. "What she did in the land of the East, blessed Radegund did in Gaul."

Of the death she wrote as one of the community: "Her eyes were closed, and ours were darkened." Kept inside the house, the nuns wept from its wall as the body passed below.
