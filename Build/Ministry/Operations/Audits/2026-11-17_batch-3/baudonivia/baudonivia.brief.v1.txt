Writing this, she told her abbess, was as impossible as touching the sky with a finger. She had borrowed the phrase from the poet Venantius Fortunatus, and then she wrote anyway.

We know Baudonivia only from her book. She was a nun of the convent of the Holy Cross at Poitiers, in Frankish Gaul, which Radegund, once a Frankish queen, had founded and where she died in 587. Fortunatus had already written Radegund's Life, but it held no relic of the Cross and no death scene.

A little after the year 600, by her editor's dating, the abbess and the whole community told Baudonivia to write. She called her Latin rustic, meaning plain and unpolished, and wrote: "I ask your prayer to help me. I am less learned, more devoted." She meant not to repeat the poet, but to tell what he had passed over.

So she told of a queen who had a pagan shrine of the Franks burned, and who wrote to rival kings, begging them not to make war. Radegund sought a piece of Christ's cross from the emperor, but when it came it was kept outside the city. Baudonivia blamed the enemy of mankind and his agents, and named no one. She set her founder beside the empress Helena, who had found the Cross. "What she did in the land of the East, blessed Radegund did in Gaul."

Of the death she wrote as one of the community: "Her eyes were closed, and ours were darkened." Kept inside the house, the nuns wept from its wall as the body passed below.

Copied after Fortunatus's work as the second book of Radegund's Life, her work let later readers meet Radegund as her own house remembered her. A medieval bishop who rewrote the Life followed Fortunatus, but took some miracles that the poet never touched and the nun had recorded. Her editor found her Latin rough, yet he asked readers to pardon good Baudonivia, who confessed her weakness.
