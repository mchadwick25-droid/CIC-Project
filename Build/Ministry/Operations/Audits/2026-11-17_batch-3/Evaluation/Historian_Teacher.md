# Batch 3: historian and teacher read

Read 2026-11-18. Twelve briefs (`brief.txt`) and cards (`card.json`) as listed in `Pieces.txt`, read against `Brief-Kit/Standard.md`. Suspected errors were checked in the held files under `cic/texts/` where the card points to them.

## Historian: overall judgement

The batch is accurate and unusually honest about its witnesses. Hostile and partisan sources are named (the Carmelite record of Wyclif, Lazar and Elishe on Vardan, Athanasius on Arius's death, the "winners' record" at Chalcedon). Coercion done by the church's own side is told plainly in most pieces: Radegund burning the shrine, the Armenian princes stoning a prince and killing Magi, Constantine's death penalty for keeping Arius's books, the Toledo canons on Jews and idolaters, Justin telling Trypho that the Jews suffered justly. I found no outright invented fact.

The failures are failures of selection. In three places a cause or a witness is left out, and what the listener infers runs against the held source:
- Sunna's exile is told without the plot that preceded it in the only source.
- Moses's violent death is credited to "a later Ethiopian tradition", though an early desert saying in the held collection tells it.
- The Wyclif piece ends before the burnings the council's verdict led to.

A second, quieter pattern: the "scholars" behind the cards are mostly nineteenth- and early twentieth-century editors. When a brief speaks of the state of the question in the present tense, it reports Victorian scholarship as current (Kempis, and to a lesser degree Wyclif and Paul of Mérida).

## Teacher: overall judgement

Yes, I would assign most of these, as a pair: brief for orientation, card for the seminar. The cards are the real teaching asset. They give line-referenced originals and set out where the witnesses disagree (the "uncertain" entries on Chalcedon, Arius, Moses and Kempis are good seminar prompts), and they separate the source's words from the editor's gloss. Before assigning, I would tell students that the cited modern scholarship is old and is not today's consensus.

Things students could get wrong from what goes unsaid:
- Hus was not burned, or Wyclif's followers were not persecuted.
- Nicaea only cursed phrases; it affirmed nothing.
- Holy Cross at Poitiers was a peaceful house.
- Sunna was exiled for his faith alone.
- Who wrote the Imitation is an open fifty-fifty question.

Alopen is too thin, and too out of order, to teach from without the card.

## Per piece

**Justin Martyr** — SHOULD FIX.
- The Dialogue is a literary work written decades after the meeting it describes. The conversion by the sea and the friendly parting are told as plain events, so a short marker ("as he tells it in the Dialogue") would help.
- "Tertullian gave him the name" claims that Tertullian coined the title. The held source shows only the earliest use of it.

**Vardan Mamikonian** — SHOULD FIX.
- "In 450" for the court scene has no held source. The card says Elishe and Lazar give no year, and Ormanian's 450 is the synod of Artashat. Source the year or drop it.
- The Vahan section (about 484) comes before Avarayr ("But first came Avarayr"). Students will lose the order of events.

**Moses the Black** — MUST FIX.
- "a later Ethiopian tradition remembers raiders killing him" misrepresents the witnesses. The held Paradise vol. II, saying 45 (`cic/texts/anan-isho_paradise-v2-sayings_budge1907.txt` line 1464), has Abba Moses at Scete foreseeing the barbarians, quoting "those who take the sword shall perish by the sword", and being killed with his brothers. That is an early desert witness, not only a late Ethiopian one. The card's caveat (the saying does not name him "the Ethiopian") belongs in the brief as a hedge on an early saying.
- SHOULD FIX: "A slave" goes beyond Clarke's "house-servant" (line 332). Both sayings on contempt are framed in their sources as tests of his humility (477 explicitly, "wishing to try him"). Saying so would keep the racial insult without turning it into the community's settled view.

**Alopen** — SHOULD FIX.
- After the opening, the second paragraph tells the stele's claims as fact ("the emperor studied the teaching and ordered it spread"). This is the church's own monument, written about 146 years later, and is the only witness held. The narrator carries it forward.
- The order jumps from 781 to 845 to 1625 and only then to 635.
- The edict of 845 was aimed chiefly at Buddhism. The brief should also say plainly that the church of the stele then fades from the record. As it stands, it ends on 712 with no ending at all.

**Council of Chalcedon (451)** — SHOULD FIX.
- The council of 449 is told as "restored him and deposed Flavian". The violence there, which Chalcedon itself heard as charges and which led to Leo's "robbers", is left out, while the violence in Alexandria after 451 is told. This is uneven.
- Palestine: monks "made Theodosius bishop instead of Juvenal". Evagrius II.5 (Walford, lines 3405–3450) has Juvenal brought back with imperial orders, everyone Theodosius had ordained ejected, and "many sad occurrences" on both sides. Tell that as well.
- "The Church of the East received it in part" is Chabot's qualified view. The card holds that well, but the brief states it flatly.

**Third Council of Toledo (589)** — MUST FIX.
- "Sunna refused to turn Catholic … and he was shipped out of Spain" implies he was exiled for his faith alone. The only source (Lives of the Fathers of Mérida, `cic/texts/paulus-emeritanus-braulio-sisebut-pl80-lat_migne1863.txt` 12040–12071) tells the expulsion as the king's sentence after a discovered conspiracy against Masona. His fellow conspirators were stripped of property and exiled in chains. Sunna was offered another see if he converted, and he refused. Tell the plot, credited to the hostile source. Otherwise the piece distorts the record in a direction that neither side's framing supports.
- SHOULD FIX: "Paul of Mérida" is Migne's traditional attribution. The work is now generally treated as anonymous.

**John Wyclif** — MUST FIX.
- Blocking omission. The record's force against Wyclif's teaching ends, in the brief, with bans, banishment and an order to dig up bones. The brief stages Hus before Constance and stops at the council's mockery. It never says the same council burned him in July 1415, or that Wyclif's followers in England were tried and burned, which Thorpe's examination before Arundel presupposes. One clause on Hus's death is needed at minimum.
- SHOULD FIX: "he was banished from Oxford" follows Shirley. Wyclif had in fact withdrawn to Lutterworth by 1381, and the 1382 action fell chiefly on his followers. "Wanted its clergy back in poverty" also leaves out his call for lay lords to take church property, and the 1381 rising is not mentioned (the card's uncertain list holds both).

**Thomas à Kempis** — SHOULD FIX.
- "scholars still disagree" reports the 1886–1905 debate in the card's sources as current. Most modern scholarship accepts Thomas as author, or at least as compiler. The Gerson and Gersen claims are largely abandoned, as the card's own Ingram and Pohl already point out. Put the dispute in the past tense, or say "once disputed, now generally given to Thomas".

**Narsai** — SHOULD FIX.
- "His enemies called him … a follower of Theodore and Nestorius" frames the label only as slander. Narsai himself honoured Theodore, and by Connolly's report also Nestorius, as Doctors of his church. Saying so gives the Church of the East its own voice rather than its enemies' framing.
- The quarrel with Barsauma (in the card) could take one clause.

**Pachomius** — SHOULD FIX.
- "conscripts for Constantine's war" is the Lives' wording. The date (about 312, "the persecution was over") fits a war of Maximinus or Licinius better, so attribute the name to the Lives.
- "Palladius, seventy years later" is ambiguous, because the angel scene is about 95 years before 419–420. Make it "seventy years after his death".
- Otherwise exemplary: "The monks of Egypt already had a way of life" corrects the "inventor of monasticism" myth.

**Arius** — SHOULD FIX.
- Nicaea is told only by its curses and its exile. The listener never hears what it affirmed, that the Son is "of one substance" with the Father, which is the term the next sixty years were fought over. A student would leave unable to say what "Nicene" means.
- Otherwise the strongest piece in the batch on witnesses: Athanasius's absence at the death, the Antioch bishops refusing the name, and the survival of Arius's words only in hostile copies.

**Baudonivia** — SHOULD FIX.
- "naming no one" is accurate to her, but the listener never learns that Gregory of Tours names the opponent: Bishop Maroveus of Poitiers. Without that, the conflict between the convent and its bishop is lost.
- The violent revolt of the nuns at Holy Cross in 589–590, a decade before she wrote, goes unmentioned. One sentence noting her silence on it would stop students imagining an untroubled house.
