# Batch 3 — AI-sceptic listening review

Reviewer stance: a listener who distrusts generated prose and is tuned to hear it. Twelve `<slug>.brief.txt` files, read in the order given in `Pieces.txt`, as if heard back to back. No other evaluation file, card, outline, review or earlier version was read.

## Verdict

At the word level, the batch is mostly clean. There is almost none of the usual assistant voice: no "it's worth noting", no "a testament to", no tidy closing moral, no stacked hedges, no disclaimers. Uncertainty is stated in a few plain words ("the year is disputed", "scholars still disagree"). Most of the voice comes from the sources, and that is the right instinct.

The machine shows at the structure level. Heard in a row, the twelve share one house shape and one narrator: a careful source critic. Nearly every piece opens cold on a scene or quote, steps back in paragraph two to "He was a ___ of ___", names a witness and flags its bias, sets two accounts side by side, signposts an afterlife paragraph with a short pivot sentence, and closes on a quote or a death. Each step can be defended on its own. Together, twelve times over, they make a template. The ear starts predicting the next move by piece five.

The second tell is compression. Chalcedon, Toledo and Alopen read like source cards turned into sentences. Facts arrive in packed series, with no breath and no scene, and chronology jumps without warning. That is not assistant cadence. It is the other sign of generation: content laid out by checklist rather than told.

Pieces that sound told: **Justin Martyr, Moses the Black, John Wyclif, Arius, Baudonivia**, and Vardan mostly.
Pieces where I hear the machine: **Council of Chalcedon, Alopen, Third Council of Toledo**, and in places Pachomius and Narsai.

**Above 3 of 12:** the source flag (12), cold open then step back (11), two accounts side by side (8), quote in the first paragraph (6), the afterlife pivot sentence (6), the absence formula (5), quote ending (5), death ending (5), uncertainty clause (5), the out-of-order return (4), narrator's not-X-but-Y (4).

The first three are near-universal. They are the template. Each is honest historiography, and the project's own rules call for naming sources and uncertainty. The fault is not that they appear. It is that they appear in the same slot, in the same shape, every time, so the listener hears the method rather than the person.

## Word level: what I did not hear, and what I did

Not heard: assistant openers or closers, "delve", "tapestry", "testament", "remarkable", tidy lessons, "in many ways", "it is important to note", self-reference, disclaimers as a crutch.

Heard, in small doses:
- Formula topic sentences in the afterlife slot (listed above). "His X outlived him" is the closest the batch comes to stock phrase.
- Narrator glosses that interpret where the source has already done the work: "She meant not to repeat the poet, but to tell what he had passed over"; "The king heard loyalty"; "The monks of Egypt already had a way of life" (a correction answering a claim no one made — an audit note in the narration).
- Source-audit asides that belong in a footnote, not the ear: "the Coptic Life has none"; "as the sources count"; "by the dating of her nineteenth-century editor"; "which a modern editor reads as failing health".
- Card-to-prose compression: charges, canons and receptions joined by commas in one breath (Chalcedon, Toledo); a fact tacked on with no link ("In Palestine, monks back from the council made Theodosius bishop…").
- Eleven sentences over 28 words, chiefly Justin (3), Wyclif (2) and Pachomius (2), the longest 38 (Vardan) — long for the ear, past the house limit of about 25.

## Mechanical checks for a batch of 500

Each check runs on the text alone and reports both a per-piece flag and a batch-level rate. The batch rate matters most: a move that is fine once becomes a template above a threshold share.

1. **Paragraph-2 step-back detector.** Flag when paragraph 1 has no "was born / was a / lived" clause and paragraph 2's first sentence matches `^(He|She|[A-Z]\w+) (was|lived|taught|had been)\b` or `^The (quarrel|dispute|council) was`. Batch alert if over 40% of pieces.
2. **Opening-type classifier.** Tag the first sentence: quote, dated scene ("In \d", "One night", "In the …century"), present-tense scene, anecdote ("They used to say"), plain identity. Alert when any one tag is over 35% of the batch, or when the same tag runs in 3 adjacent pieces in publication order.
3. **Ending-type classifier.** Tag the last sentence: quote, death, number or age, epithet, reception. Same alerts as check 2.
4. **Afterlife-pivot lexicon.** Regex the first sentence of each paragraph for `outliv|outlast|went (on|west|further)|travelled (much )?further|carried (him|her|it) on|did not live to see|lived on|survived (him|her)`. Flag each hit; alert if over 15% of the batch.
5. **Return-hinge detector.** `^(But first|Back (at|in)|Before that|Arius did not live|\w+ did not live to see)` at a paragraph start, after a paragraph that holds a date later than the death date on the card. Alert if over 10%.
6. **Absence-formula lexicon.** `No (\w+ )?(reply|witness|friend|record|account|source)\b.*(recorded|survives?|left)|only (from|through) (her|his|their)|every word .* that survives`. Per-piece cap 1; batch alert if over 20%.
7. **Source-flag density.** Count attribution verbs (`says|writes|wrote|tells|adds|counts|gives|complains|remembers`) per 100 words. Flag pieces above 2.0 per 100 (Narsai and Justin sit near it), and list pieces where a witness gloss ("a hostile witness", "the winners' record", "who was not there") appears more than once.
8. **Side-by-side variant counter.** Flag a piece with more than 2 sentences matching `(\w+) (says|has|puts) .*; (the )?\w+ (has|says|puts)` or `X says …, Y says …` within 2 sentences, or the phrases `has none|as the sources count|the sources differ`. Audit asides belong on the card.
9. **Narrator "not X but Y".** Match `\bnot [^.;"]{1,40}(, but | but |rather than)` and `rather than` outside quotation marks. Per-piece cap 1; batch alert if over 20%.
10. **Interpretive-gloss detector.** Narrator sentences outside quotes that start `She meant|He meant|The king heard|What \w+ rose for|The \w+ already had`. These are the narrator speaking for the subject. Hand-review every hit.
11. **List-run detector.** Flag any sentence with 3 or more coordinated `-ing` verbs or noun phrases separated by commas plus "and other", or more than 4 commas outside a quote. Flag a piece whose share of such sentences is over 15% (Chalcedon, Toledo).
12. **Orphan-fact detector.** Flag a paragraph of 1 sentence that shares no proper noun with the paragraph before or after it and holds no quote (Chalcedon's "In Palestine…", Alopen's last paragraph).
13. **Chronology jump.** Pull the years in order of appearance; flag a piece where the sequence moves back by more than 50 years more than once without a hinge word (Alopen's 781 → 845 → 1625 → 635).
14. **Quote-load ratio.** Quoted words over total words; flag above 30% (Chalcedon) as a minutes digest, and below 5% (Wyclif, Alopen) for a reviewer to check that the subject's voice is heard at all.
15. **Sentence-length ceiling for the ear.** Flag any sentence over 28 words, and any piece whose standard deviation of sentence length is under 6 (too even a rhythm is its own tell; check across the batch for convergence on one mean).
16. **Cross-piece n-gram reuse.** Across the batch, list every 4-gram outside quotations that appears in 5 or more pieces (e.g. "his own church", "the school's own account", "a writer of his own"), and every repeated paragraph-opening 3-gram. Anything above 1% of pieces gets a human ear.
17. **Template fingerprint.** Encode each piece as its paragraph-role sequence (scene / identity / witness flag / episode / variant / afterlife / return / death / quote-close) using the tags above; cluster the batch. If one sequence or its close neighbours covers more than 30% of pieces, the batch has a template, whatever the word-level scores say.
