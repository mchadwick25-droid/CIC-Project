In 450, an Armenian general stood before the Persian king and promised him an action whose fame would resound forever. The king heard loyalty. Lazar of Parpi, who wrote the story at the order of the general's nephew Vahan, saw another meaning: "But God took from them the promise that Vardan, general of the Armenians, had given: to make his name famous. For God gave him the title of martyr, which would last forever among all nations."

Vardan Mamikonian was a prince of Armenia, then ruled by Persia, and his grandfather Sahak, patriarch of the Armenian church, had taught him the Scriptures. That year King Yazdegerd the Second ordered every people to worship the sun and feed the holy fire of the Magi. Summoned to court with other princes, Vardan offered the king his life and goods, and refused only to change his faith. As Lazar gives his words: "But I received this religion from God as a child. To abandon it, and change it out of fear of men, is not possible!"

The king threatened chains, elephants, and exile for their wives and children. Both witnesses say the princes then pretended to accept his religion, but they tell it differently. Elishe, a churchman who says he saw these events, writes that a secret Christian at court showed some of them a way out. Inwardly they vowed to stay firm. In Lazar, the princes argued that refusing would doom countless families to forced worship. Vardan refused in anger, then wept and gave way when they swore on the Gospel to follow him.

When they came home, their own children ran from them in fear. Magi came to close the churches, and when Persian troops tried to break open a church door, the people stoned them. The princes rose, stoned a prince who would not join, burned fire-temples and killed Magi.

In the spring of 451 the armies met on the plain of Avarayr, with Armenians on both sides. As Elishe gives his words, Vardan told his men: "For a time we deceived that unjust prince. We took him in like a weak child, as if we had outwardly carried out his wicked orders. But the Lord is the hidden witness of our thoughts. He knows we held firm and never parted from him." Vardan was killed near the elephants, his army scattered, and the historians count 1,036 Armenian dead.

The widows raised the sons of the fallen, among them Vahan, son of Vardan's brother Hmayeak. About thirty years later he led a new rising, and his companions said they trusted in the death of their ancestors. His first demand of Persia was that no Armenian be made a Magus, and that Christians worship freely. In Lazar's telling, King Vagharsh granted every demand, forever and under seal, and named Vahan general of Armenia, in about 484, though the year is disputed.
