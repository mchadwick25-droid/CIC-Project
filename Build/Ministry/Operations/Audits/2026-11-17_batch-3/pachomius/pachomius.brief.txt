It is night in a prison by the Nile, early in the fourth century. Young men taken for Constantine's war are locked inside, and strangers bring them food. One recruit, about twenty, born to pagan parents in Upper Egypt, asks who they are. His own communities' Coptic Life gives the answer: "They are Christian people. They treat us this way out of love for the God of heaven." That night, Pachomius vowed to serve people all his life.

Once released, he was baptized and nursed a village through an epidemic. Then he learned from an old hermit, Palamon, whose rule had come down from older monks: half the night with the Scriptures, and fasting until evening.

At a deserted village called Tabennisi, by the river, the Lives say a voice told him to stay and build, because many would come. The Coptic Life says an angel called him three times and told him: "Pachomius, Pachomius, the will of God is that you serve the human race and unite them to Him."

Three men came first, and he did all their work, calling them new plants. Then he gave them rules: the same clothes, the same food, a decent bed. He would not have priests among his monks, and when Athanasius of Alexandria visited and a bishop asked for Pachomius to be ordained, he hid. His sister Mary came; he would not see her, but had a house built for her. Other women joined her, and he sent them the brothers' rules in a book.

At Esneh, the Greek Life says, monks and bishops questioned him over claims that he could see hidden thoughts. Then a man with a sword burst in to kill him, and the Life says the Lord saved him.

After his death his fellowship split, and Theodore, one of its leaders, wrote letters urging the breakaways back. Palladius, who visited Egypt about seventy years later, counted about seven thousand monks there under the rule.

More than fifty years after Pachomius died, Latin-speaking monks in his houses and at Metanoia, near Alexandria, could not read the rules in Egyptian or Greek. A priest sent Jerome books that had come from Alexandria. Jerome dictated a Latin version for them, for his own brothers and for Eustochium's nuns, so they could follow the monks of Tabennisi.

Early in the ninth century, in the Latin West, Benedict of Aniane gathered the old monastic rules into one book. Under each chapter of the Rule of Saint Benedict, he set passages from Pachomius and others, to show that it stood on theirs.

Pachomius himself had died as plague swept his monasteries, in the year 346 or 348.

Sick with fever, the Greek Life says, he sent back a better blanket: "Take this blanket away from here at once. It is not right that I should be better off in anything than the other brothers."
