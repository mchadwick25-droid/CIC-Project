One night on the upper Nile, strangers brought food to young men locked in a prison, conscripts for Constantine's war. Pachomius, the twenty-year-old son of pagan parents, asked who they were. His own communities' Coptic Life gives the answer: "They are Christian people. They treat us this way out of love for the God of heaven." That night, both his oldest Lives say, he vowed to serve people all his life.

The persecution was over, early in the fourth century. Released, he was baptized and nursed villagers through an epidemic. Then a hermit, Palamon, tested him with the rule handed down to him: night prayer, hard work and daily fasting. The monks of Egypt already had a way of life.

At a deserted village called Tabennisi, a voice told him to build, for many would come. The Coptic Life says an angel called him three times: "Pachomius, Pachomius, the will of God is that you serve the human race and unite them to Him." Palladius, seventy years later, adds a brass tablet bearing the rule; the Coptic Life has none.

At the start he did all the work for his first three brothers and nursed them himself. Then he gave them rules: the same clothes and food for all, and a steward over each house. He would have no priests among his monks, and when a bishop asked Athanasius to ordain him, he hid.

He did not see his sister Mary, but built her a house and sent the women who joined her the brothers' rules. The Coptic Life puts their house in the village, Palladius across the river.

At Esneh, as monks and bishops questioned him in church about reading hidden thoughts, a man with a sword burst in to kill him; the Greek Life says the Lord saved him.

He died in 346 or 348, after plague had killed a hundred or more across the monasteries. On his sickbed he sent back a better blanket: "Take this blanket away from here at once. It is not right that I should be better off in anything than the other brothers."

Petronius, his chosen heir, died within days, and Horsiesius and Theodore led on. The fellowship split, and Theodore's letters urged the breakaways back. Palladius, visiting Egypt, counts about seven thousand monks there under the rule, and Cassian drew on "the monks of Tabenna". More than fifty years after Pachomius died, Latin-speaking monks in those houses could not read the rule.

A priest sent Jerome the books from Alexandria, and he dictated a Latin version for them, and for his own brothers and the nuns of Eustochium.

Through that Latin the rule went west, where the abbot Benedict of Aniane set it beneath each chapter of Benedict's Rule, as one of the rules that Rule stood on.
