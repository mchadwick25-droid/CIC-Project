In the Greek Life of Pachomius, by a monk of the next generation, the great hermit Antony speaks to the monks after their father's death: "When I first began to live as a monk, there was no community anywhere in which anyone cared or feared for the salvation of others. Once the persecution was over, each of the early monks trained himself in the monk's life on his own. Later, with God's help, your father did this great good."

Before him, in Upper Egypt, each monk lived by his own way, a hermit in a cell, sometimes with a pupil. Pachomius drew such monks into one shared life under one written rule. His brothers lived in houses inside a walled monastery, each house under a head. They wore the same clothes, ate at one table and worked at their trades together. Nine monasteries along the Nile came to form one fellowship.

Born to pagan parents early in the fourth century, he was taken at about twenty for Constantine's war. Locked up with other recruits, he asked who the strangers feeding them were. The Coptic Life, from his own communities, gives the answer: "They are Christian people. They treat us this way out of love for the God of heaven." That night he vowed to serve people all his life. Once free and baptized, he became a hermit's pupil, until at a deserted village called Tabennisi he began to build.

He would have no priests among his monks, and when a bishop wanted him ordained, he hid. At Esneh, the Greek Life says, monks and bishops questioned him over claims that he read hidden thoughts. As he finished, a man driven by an evil spirit burst in with a sword to kill him, but the Life says the Lord saved him with the help of those present.

In 346 or 348, as a plague swept the monasteries, he fell ill and asked for a lighter blanket. When he saw it was finer than the brothers' own, he sent it back: "Take this blanket away from here at once. It is not right that I should be better off in anything than the other brothers." After his death the fellowship split, though Theodore's letters urged the breakaways back.

Palladius, writing about seventy years later, counted some seven thousand monks under the rule. Latin-speaking monks in the houses could not read its Egyptian or Greek, so priests sent Jerome the books and pressed him. About 404 he dictated a Latin version for them, and for his own brothers and Eustochium's nuns. Through that Latin the rule reached the West. In the early ninth century the abbot Benedict of Aniane set it under the chapters of Benedict's Rule, as one of the old rules it agreed with.
