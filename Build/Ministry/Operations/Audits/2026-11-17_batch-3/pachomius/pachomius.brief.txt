Two old Lives tell the story of Pachomius, one in Coptic from his own communities, one in Greek by a monk of the next generation. In the Greek Life, Antony, the great hermit, speaks to Pachomius's monks after their father's death: "When I first began to live as a monk, there was no community anywhere in which anyone cared or feared for the salvation of others. Once the persecution was over, each of the early monks trained himself in the monk's life on his own. Later, with God's help, your father did this great good."

In Upper Egypt in the early fourth century, each monk lived by his own way, a hermit in a cell, sometimes with a pupil. Pachomius drew them into one shared life under one written rule. His brothers lived in houses inside a walled monastery, each house under a head. They wore the same clothes, ate at one table and worked at their trades together, and rank went by who came first, not by age. Nine monasteries along the Nile came to form one fellowship.

He was born to pagan parents and taken at about twenty for Constantine's war. Locked up with other recruits, he asked who the strangers bringing them food were. The Coptic Life gives the answer: "They are Christian people. They treat us this way out of love for the God of heaven." That night he vowed to serve people all his life. Once free, he was baptized and learned the solitary way from the hermit Palamon. Later, at a deserted village called Tabennisi, the Lives say, a voice told him to build, because many would come.

He would have no priests among his monks, and when a bishop wanted him ordained, he hid. At Esneh, the Greek Life says, bishops and monks questioned him in church, and a man with a sword burst in.

In 346 or 348 a plague swept the monasteries, and as he lay ill he asked for a lighter blanket. When he saw it was finer than the brothers' own, he sent it back: "Take this blanket away from here at once. It is not right that I should be better off in anything than the other brothers." After his death the fellowship split, though Theodore's letters urged the breakaways back.

Palladius, visiting Egypt about seventy years later, counted some seven thousand monks under the rule. Latin-speaking monks in the houses could not read its Egyptian or Greek, so priests sent Jerome the books and pressed him. About 404 he dictated a Latin version, for them, and for his own brothers and Eustochium's nuns. Through that Latin the rule reached the West, where in the early ninth century the abbot Benedict of Aniane set it under the chapters of Benedict's Rule, as one of the old rules it agreed with.
