# Pachomius: outline

Every reference is to `Source_Sheet.md` (item codes) and through it to file and line in `cic/texts/`. Each beat names the Life it rests on. "Bohairic Life" is the Coptic Life from his own communities (in Amélineau's French). "Greek Life" is the Greek Life (in Cardon's Latin). "Arabic Life" is a later, fuller Arabic Life (in Amélineau's French).

- **Length:** about 2½ to 3 minutes (about 400 to 450 words). Reason: a major figure (census `major`), whose two oldest Lives disagree on things a listener will meet (the angel's tablet, the women's house). The connection also needs room: it runs from a Coptic rule through Greek and Latin to a ninth-century abbot. Do not fill past what the beats hold.

- **Setting:**
  - Time: early 300s, after "the persecution" ended (the Lives' word; Bohairic 5093, Greek 80977) and Constantine ruled. Dates of birth and death are uncertain (S4): say "in the early fourth century"; he died in the middle of the century, in 346 or 348.
  - Place: Upper Egypt, the Thebaid, along the Nile. Born in the district of Esneh (S1). Tabennisi was "a deserted village by the river" (S2). His later head house was Phbôou, which the Greek calls Pabau (S3). Spoken words: "Upper Egypt", "the upper Nile", "a deserted village called Tabennisi". His houses stood in and by villages along the Nile, not in open desert.
  - Tradition: census `desert-monasticism`. Spoken name: "the monks of Egypt". He belongs to Egypt's Coptic-speaking church, which still keeps his feast (I4).

- **Thread:** He drew monks out of solitude into a shared life under one written rule. Men and women in linked houses lived under the same rule and ate and dressed alike, and that rule was copied into Greek, Latin and Ge'ez long after him.

- **Shape:** Founder or leader.

- **Openings:**
  1. Night in a prison on the Nile. Young conscripts are locked up, and strangers bring them food. He asks who they are (E1; quote 1). Both Lives tell the night; the words of quote 1 are the Bohairic Life's, and the Lives place it in different towns.
  2. His deathbed, in the Greek Life. After forty days of plague fever, he sends back a better blanket (E13; quote 3).

- **Story beats:**
  1. **Conscript.** Both Lives: born to pagan parents, he was taken at about twenty for Constantine's war, and Christians fed the locked-up recruits. The words "for love of the God of heaven" are the Bohairic Life's (5145-5146). The Greek Life says only that Christians, moved by pity, brought food, and that he asked about them and was told (80985-80992). Both Lives: that night he vowed to serve people all his life (E1, E2). Widely Accepted. The town is Esneh in the Bohairic Life and Thebes in the Greek, so do not name it, or name both.
  2. **Baptism and apprenticeship.** Bohairic Life: released, he settled in a deserted village, Schénésît (5195-5196), was baptized, and nursed villagers through an epidemic (5283-5291). The Greek Life also has his baptism, at Chenoboscia, the same place (81023-81027). Bohairic Life: he then went to the hermit Palamon. Palamon tested him with "the rule of the monastic life as those before us taught it to us": half the night awake with the Scriptures, much manual work, and fasting every day until evening in summer (5424-5434). Widely Accepted. This shows the desert life was older than him.
  3. **Tabennisi.** Both Lives: at a deserted village a mile away, a voice told him to stay and build, for many would come. Both Lives: an angel told him God's will was that he serve the human race (E5, E6; quote 2). Keep both in the Lives' voice. Widely Accepted as the Lives' account.
  4. **The first community.** Bohairic Life only (6252-6336, 6436-6482): three men came. At first he did all the work and nursed them himself, calling them "new plants". Then he gave them rules: the same clothes, the same food, a decent bed. As numbers grew, he set a steward and a helper over each house, and the brothers serving at table were changed every three weeks. Widely Accepted.
  5. **Monks, not clergy; and his sister.** Bohairic Life: he would not have priests among his monks, "because of envy and vainglory", and kept them under the bishops (E9). Both Lives: when Athanasius came up the Nile and a bishop asked for him to be ordained, he hid. The Bohairic says he hid "among the great crowd" (6673-6674); the Greek, "among the brothers", until the bishop had passed (82514-82520). Say "he hid", or give both (E11). Bohairic Life: his sister Mary came to see him. He would not see her, but sent word through the doorkeeper and had a dwelling built for her. Other women joined her. He set an old monk, Peter, over them and sent them the brothers' rules written in a book (E10). The Bohairic Life puts her house in the village; Palladius and the Ethiopic Rules put the women across the river. Widely Accepted that there was a women's house; Contested where it stood.
  6. **Conflict.** Greek Life: by his own count the federation held nine monasteries. At Latopolis (Esneh), monks and bishops met in the church to question him over claims that he could see hidden thoughts. As he finished his answer, a man with a sword burst in to kill him; the Life says the Lord saved him with the help of those present (E12). The Arabic Life tells a related clash at Esneh differently: bishops and monks, out of envy, met in the church there to drive his brothers out of the monasteries in their dioceses (32859-32861). Widely Accepted that there was a clash at Esneh; the details are each Life's own.
  7. **Death.** Greek Life (86510-86672): after Easter a plague struck, and across all the monasteries a hundred or more died. He fell ill, refused the better blanket, named Petronius to follow him, and died on 14 Pachon. Theodore and three brothers secretly moved his body to another place. The Arabic Life agrees on 14 Pachon and on Petronius (35674, 35825-35877), but says no one has known where he was buried (35733-35734). Petronius died within days; Horsiesius followed (both). Widely Accepted.

- **What came of them** (about a third of the story; heaviest first):
  1. **The rule travelled.** Palladius, about seventy years later, counts 7,000 monks living under it (I1). About 404 Jerome put the rules into Latin, from a Greek translation of the Egyptian, working from books sent to him from Alexandria (I1; Documented by Jerome). Cassian, who had been among the Egyptian monks, drew separately on "the monks of Tabenna". In the early ninth century, Benedict of Aniane (died 821) gathered the old rules into a *Concordia of Rules*. Under each chapter of Benedict's Rule he set passages from the rule of Pachomius and others, to show that Benedict's Rule stood on theirs (Ardo 30903-30911; the Concordia, e.g. 66976). The Concordia's quotations are Documented, since the compilation is his own act; its purpose is his disciple Ardo's account. The Ethiopian church kept a Ge'ez version, much of it Palladius's account of the rule.
  2. **The fellowship outlived him, but not unbroken.** Petronius, Horsiesius and Theodore led it. It split after his death, and Theodore's letters urged the breakaways back into unity (Gennadius, I2). Athanasius wrote to its leaders, and the gatherings at Phbôou at Easter and in August went on (I2, E9).
  3. **Defended:** equality inside the common life, to his last days (the blanket); monks without clerical rank, under the bishops (I3).
  4. **Remembered:** the Coptic church keeps his day, 14 Bashons; a seventh-century Coptic Life calls him "the father of the coenobite life" (I4).

- **Quotes** (2 or 3 at most):
  1. Rendered from Amélineau's French of the Bohairic Life, 5145-5146 (the Coptic OCR is unreadable): **"They are Christian people. They treat us this way out of love for the God of heaven."** Job: the opening, and the reason he became a Christian. Bohairic Life only; the Greek Life has no speech here.
  2. Rendered from Amélineau's French, Bohairic 6247-6248. The Life says the angel spoke "three times", and the manuscript writes the name twice; Amélineau added a third (his note e, 6260). The Greek Life's Latin (81973-81974) gives the same charge, without the name. **"Pachomius, Pachomius, the will of God is that you serve the human race and unite them to Him."** Job: the angel's charge. It ties back to his prison vow and stays in the Life's voice ("the Life says an angel called him three times and told him").
  3. Rendered from Cardon's Latin of the Greek Life, 86630-86633 (the Greek is not held legibly): **"Take this blanket away from here at once. It is not right that I should be better off in anything than the other brothers."** Job: what he defended, in his own reported words at the end. Greek Life.

- **Connection to carry into the story:** The rule outlived the man through translation. Jerome's preface (I1, 4336-4392) says that many Latin-speaking monks lived in the communities of the Thebaid and at the monastery of Metanoia. (Metanoia was the renamed Canopus; the editor's note places it by Alexandria.) They could not read the Egyptian or Greek in which the rules of Pachomius, Theodore and Horsiesius were written. A priest, Silvanus, sent Jerome the books, which had come from Alexandria. Another priest, Leontinus, came with brothers to press him. Jerome dictated a Latin version, made from the Greek translation of the Egyptian. Jerome also says he made the Latin for his own brothers and for the nuns under Paula's daughter Eustochium, so that they could follow the example of the Tabennesiot monks. These are the first Latin communities the preface names as receiving the rule; the preface names no town for them. The listener needs to understand that Jerome worked from books sent to him, more than fifty years after Pachomius died, to serve monks and nuns who wanted to live his way. The Latin then carried the rule west. Benedict of Aniane's *Concordia* quotes it chapter by chapter under Benedict's Rule; its editor identifies the Pachomian text there as Jerome's Latin (I1). Tell it through those acts: monks asking, Jerome dictating for his brothers and Eustochium's nuns, an abbot setting it under Benedict's Rule. Do not add a "this matters because" sentence. Cassian is a separate channel; do not tie him to Jerome's Latin.

- **Links:**
  - Athanasius of Alexandria: met him (visited Tabennisi; Pachomius hid from ordination); later wrote to his successors.
  - Antony: never met. In the Greek Life, Antony says he often wanted to see him and hoped to meet him in heaven.
  - Palladius: wrote of him and visited one of his houses.
  - Sozomen: wrote of him, following Palladius.
  - Jerome: translated his rule.
  - John Cassian: drew on the rule of "the monks of Tabenna".
  - Gennadius: wrote his notice ("founder of the Egyptian monasteries").

- **Who tells it:**
  - The Bohairic Life: the fellowship's own Coptic Life, anonymous, here in Amélineau's French of 1889. Its date is not fixed in the held texts, and its text breaks off before his death.
  - The Greek Life: anonymous, by a monk the Bollandists call nearly contemporary, here in a 17th-century Latin translation. Bracketed passages in it were added from another Latin version.
  - The Arabic Life: later and fuller; only it explains why the body was hidden.
  - Palladius (about 420): a visitor writing about 70 years later. Sozomen and the Ethiopic Rules repeat him.
  - Jerome (about 404): a translator, not a witness to the life.
  - Where it matters, the listener needs to know this. The angel's charge is in both Lives, and Jerome and Gennadius also speak of an angel behind the rule. The tablet first appears in Palladius. The Greek Life has it only in bracketed passages, which its editors judged were taken from Palladius. The Coptic Life comes from the communities he founded; the Greek Life is by a monk of the next generation.

- **Do not say:**
  - "An angel gave him the rule on a brass tablet" as narrator's fact → "Palladius, writing about seventy years later, says an angel gave him the rule on a brass tablet; his own community's Coptic Life has the angel, but no tablet."
  - "The Greek Life says the angel gave him a tablet" → "only in bracketed passages its editors say were not in their Greek manuscript".
  - "Pachomius, Pachomius, Pachomius" as the Life's words → "the Life says the angel called him three times".
  - "He founded the first monastery" or "he invented monastic life" → "he gathered monks into a shared life under one rule"; Palamon was already teaching "the rule ... as those before us taught it to us". "Founder of the Egyptian monasteries" is Gennadius's phrase and stays in his voice.
  - "Tabennisi, an island in the Nile" → "a deserted village by the river".
  - "Athanasius ordained him a priest" (a modern editor's slip) → "he hid so that he would not be ordained".
  - "He hid in the crowd" or "he hid among the brothers" as the one account → "he hid" (the two Lives differ).
  - "Across the river" for his sister's house, said as fact → "Palladius puts the women's house across the river; the Coptic Life puts it in the village".
  - "He is buried at ..." or "his grave is lost" as fact → "the Greek Life says Theodore moved his body to another place; the Arabic Life says no one has known where".
  - Any single number of monks as fact → "Palladius counts seven thousand in his own day"; keep "about" and the witness.
  - "More than a hundred died of the plague" → "across all the monasteries, a hundred or more".
  - "Born in 292" or "died in 348" as fact → "in the early fourth century ... he died in 346 or 348".
  - Naming the enemy in Constantine's war, or the year he was conscripted → the Lives name neither.
  - "He refused to see his sister because ..." → the record gives no motive; say only that he sent word and did not see her.
  - "Pachomius wrote the Rule himself" → "the rules of Pachomius, Theodore and Horsiesius", as Jerome names them; none of the held text is certainly in his own hand.
  - "Theodore succeeded him" → "he named Petronius; Petronius died within days, and Horsiesius followed". The Ethiopian calendar's "Theodore" is a late variant.
  - "He forbade contact with Arians and Origenists" → leave it out; the deathbed line is bracketed in the Latin and may not be in the Greek.
  - "Benedict of Aniane thought Pachomius's rule better or harder than Benedict's" → "he set Pachomius's rule under Benedict's, as one of the rules it agreed with". If his youth is told: "as a young monk, Ardo says, he thought Benedict's Rule was for beginners and reached for Basil and Pachomius". That was a phase he left behind.
  - "Cassian read Jerome's Latin" → Cassian says only that he drew on the rule of "the monks of Tabenna".
  - "Jerome never met Pachomius" as a stated fact → "Jerome worked from books sent to him, more than fifty years after Pachomius died".
  - "The Great Persecution" → "the persecution" (the Lives' word).

- **Leave out:**
  - The wonder stories (the crocodile, demons in many shapes, the vision of hell, the brother who walked on coals): a pile of wonders, and none carries the thread.
  - Macarius's disguised visit and the nun who feigned madness (Palladius): side stories about other people.
  - The calumny and two suicides in the women's house (Palladius 33): this belongs to Palladius's own day, not to Pachomius's acts. It is left out for that reason, not to soften the record.
  - The Arabic Life's reason for hiding the body (to stop a church being built over it): late, single witness.
  - Abu Salih's claim that his body lay in a chest at Butij: late, and against the Lives.
  - The Instructions attributed to him (Sahidic): the attribution is untested in the held texts.
  - "Koinonia" as his own word: only the census alias supports it, and no held line is cited.
