# Pachomius: outline

Every reference is to `Source_Sheet.md` (item codes) and through it to file and line in `cic/texts/`. Each beat names the Life it rests on. "Bohairic Life" is the Coptic Life from his own communities (in Amélineau's French). "Greek Life" is the Greek Life (in Cardon's Latin). "Arabic Life" is a later, fuller Arabic Life (in Amélineau's French).

- **The heart of their life:** Monks in Egypt before him lived each on his own, by his own way: a hermit in a cell, sometimes with a pupil (H1; E4). Pachomius drew them into one shared life under one written rule. His brothers lived together in houses inside a walled monastery, each house under a head; Jerome, describing the houses some fifty years later, says about forty brothers lived in each. They wore the same clothes, ate the same food at one table, and worked at their trades together. Rank went by who came first, not by age (H5, E8, E9). His sister's house of women lived under the same rules (E10). The houses were linked into one fellowship of nine monasteries under one head, and twice a year the brothers met at Phbôou: at Easter to keep the feast together, and in August to give their accounts (H3, H7, E9). Hermits came to live beside him, and the abbot of an older community asked to come under his rule (H4). The Greek Life has Antony himself say that when he began, no such community existed anywhere (H2). In time the rule went into Greek, and about 404 Jerome put it into Latin from the Greek; from that Latin it reached the West, where an abbot of the ninth century set it under the chapters of Benedict's Rule (I1).
  - What the sources hold, plainly: that he founded the shared, rule-bound life of Egypt's monks, as a fellowship of houses, is Widely Accepted (both Lives, Jerome, Palladius, Sozomen, Gennadius), and is the editors' Dominant Modern Reconstruction (Ladeuze, Schodde; H8). "Founder" and "father of the coenobite life" are later witnesses' words (Gennadius; the Life of Pisentius) and stay in their voice. "The first monastery anywhere" and "he invented monastic life" are not held: Palamon taught an older rule, and the Greek Life says one man, Aotas, had tried before and failed (E4, H2). "Koinonia" as his own word is not held (H9).

- **Length:** about 2½ to 3 minutes (about 380 to 450 words at Neo's 150 a minute). Reason: a major figure (census `major`), and the heart needs room to be shown, not named: what the houses were like, and how the rule went from Coptic through Greek and Latin to a ninth-century abbot. The previous outline's room for how the Lives differ (the tablet, where the women's house stood) and the sword at Esneh is cut to a clause each, or to the card. Do not fill past what the beats hold.

- **Setting:**
  - Time: early 300s, after "the persecution" ended (the Lives' word; Bohairic 5093, Greek 80977; Antony's words in H2) and Constantine ruled. Dates of birth and death are uncertain (S4): say "in the early fourth century"; he died in the middle of the century, in 346 or 348.
  - Place: Upper Egypt, the Thebaid, along the Nile. Born in the district of Esneh (S1). Tabennisi was "a deserted village by the river" (S2). His later head house was Phbôou (Pabau or Bau in Greek and Latin sources) (S3). Spoken words: "Upper Egypt", "the upper Nile", "a deserted village called Tabennisi". His houses stood in and by villages along the Nile, not in open desert.
  - Tradition: census `desert-monasticism`. Spoken name: "the monks of Egypt". He belongs to Egypt's Coptic-speaking church, which still keeps his feast (I4).

- **Shape:** Founder or leader.

- **Openings:**
  1. (Recommended.) Antony's words, as the Greek Life reports them after Pachomius died: when Antony first became a monk, there was no community anywhere where monks cared for each other; each lived the monk's life on his own; then "your father" did this great good (H2; quote 1). It names the change at once. Heard once, the writer must say who Antony was in a phrase (the great hermit, whose fame, the Life says, reached the emperors: Greek 86884-86886; Pachomius calls him the model of the solitary life, 87648) and that he is speaking to Pachomius's monks.
  2. Night in a prison on the Nile. Young conscripts are locked up, and strangers bring them food. He asks who they are (E1; quote 2). Plain and drawing, but the heart must follow within about 100 words: tell the vow, then go straight to what he built, and come back to the hermit years after.

- **Story beats** (beats 1 and 2 carry the heart and fall in the first minute; the rest deepens it):
  1. **What was new.** Greek Life (H2): Antony, the great hermit, told Pachomius's mourning monks that when he began there was no such community; each monk lived it on his own, and their father had done this great good. The Life's own Pachomius, questioned at Latopolis, set the few monks living apart, "two or five or ten at most", against "we", a great multitude filling nine monasteries (H3). Widely Accepted as the Greek Life's account. Say what a "monastery" was here: a walled place with houses, a church, and brothers living and working together (Bohairic 8175-8178, H4: the wall, a small church, the houses).
  2. **How the shared life ran.** Bohairic Life (E8, E9) and Jerome's preface (H5): the same clothes and food for all, and a decent bed. Each house had a head and a second (Jerome, describing the houses some fifty years later, says about forty brothers lived in each), and brothers of one trade lived in one house. They ate together; rank went by who had entered first, not by age. The brothers on duty changed every three weeks. One head stood over all the monasteries, Twice a year the brothers met at Phbôou: at Easter to keep the feast together, and in August to give their accounts. He would have no priests among his monks, "because of envy and vainglory", and kept them under the bishops (E9). His sister Mary's women's house was sent the brothers' rules in a book (E10). Widely Accepted. Jerome's figure of fifty thousand at Easter is his own and far above Palladius's; leave it out or keep it in Jerome's voice.
  3. **Where he came from.** Both Lives: born to pagan parents, taken at about twenty for Constantine's war. Christians fed the locked-up recruits; the words "for love of the God of heaven" are the Bohairic Life's (E1). That night he vowed to serve people all his life (E2). Released, he was baptized and nursed villagers through an epidemic (E3). Widely Accepted. The town is Esneh in the Bohairic Life and Thebes in the Greek, so do not name it, or name both.
  4. **The older way: a hermit's apprentice.** Both Lives: he went to live with the hermit Palamon, to share his solitary life (H1). Bohairic Life: Palamon taught "the rule of the monastic life as those before us taught it to us": half the night awake with the Scriptures, much manual work, and fasting every day until evening in summer (E4). Widely Accepted. This is the older life he would change: a strict rule kept alone, or by a pupil beside one teacher.
  5. **Tabennisi and the first brothers.** Both Lives: at a deserted village by the river, a voice told him to stay and build, for many would come, and an angel told him God's will was that he serve the human race (E5, E6). Keep both in the Lives' voice; one sentence is enough. Bohairic Life: three men came. At first he did all the work and nursed them himself, calling them "new plants". Then he gave them rules (E8). Five hermit brothers came to live beside him, and the old abbot of a community at Schénésît asked that his monastery be put "under the rule of the common life" given to Pachomius (H4). Widely Accepted (Bohairic only in these details).
  6. **Not clergy, and not unopposed.** Both Lives: when Athanasius came up the Nile and a bishop asked for him to be ordained, he hid (E11). Greek Life (86393-86400, 86463-86466): at Latopolis (Esneh), monks and bishops met in church to question him over claims that he could see hidden thoughts. As he finished his answer, a man with a sword burst in to kill him; the Life says the Lord saved him, with the help of those present (E12). Tell all three parts: what the questioning was about, that the man came to kill him, and that the Life says he was saved. The Life says only that the man was driven by an evil spirit ("ἃ [a] malo genio actus", 86464); it does not say anyone sent him, so do not set the bishops and the sword side by side as cause and act. One sentence: the record's conflict stays in, the detail goes to the card. Widely Accepted that there was a clash at Esneh; the details are each Life's own.
  7. **Death.** Greek Life (E13): after Easter a plague struck, and across all the monasteries a hundred or more died. He fell ill, refused the better blanket (quote 3), named Petronius to follow him, and died on 14 Pachon. Theodore and three brothers secretly moved his body. Petronius died within days; Horsiesius followed. Widely Accepted.

- **What came of them** (about a third of the story; heaviest first):
  1. **The rule travelled.** Palladius, writing about seventy years later, counts about seven thousand monks under the rule (I1). (He was in Egypt about 388 to 400, by Clarke's notes, `palladius_lausiac-history_clarke1918.txt` 713, 877, 1177, editor; he wrote the Lausiac History about 419-420.) About 404 Jerome put the rules into Latin, from a Greek translation of the Egyptian, working from books sent to him from Alexandria (I1; Documented by Jerome). Cassian, who had been among the Egyptian monks, drew separately on "the monks of Tabenna". In the early ninth century Benedict of Aniane (died 821) gathered the old rules into a *Concordia of Rules*: under each chapter of Benedict's Rule he set passages from the rule of Pachomius and others, to show that Benedict's Rule stood on theirs (I1). The Concordia's quotations are Documented, since the compilation is his own act; its purpose is his disciple Ardo's account. The Ethiopian church kept a Ge'ez version, much of it Palladius's account of the rule.
  2. **The fellowship outlived him, but not unbroken.** Petronius, Horsiesius and Theodore led it. It split after his death, and Theodore's letters urged the breakaways back into unity (Gennadius, I2). Athanasius wrote to its leaders, and the gatherings at Phbôou at Easter and in August went on (I2, E9).
  3. **Defended:** equality inside the common life, to his last days (the blanket); monks without clerical rank, under the bishops (I3).
  4. **Remembered:** the Coptic church keeps his day, 14 Bashons; a seventh-century Coptic Life calls him "the father of the coenobite life" (I4, H8).

- **Quotes** (3):
  1. Rendered from Cardon's Latin of the Greek Life, 86887-86893 (the Greek is not held legibly): **"When I first began to live as a monk, there was no community anywhere in which anyone cared or feared for the salvation of others. Once the persecution was over, each of the early monks trained himself in the monk's life on his own. Later, with God's help, your father did this great good."** Job: the heart, in a witness who stood for the older way. Antony's words as the Greek Life reports them; "your father" is Pachomius, and the writer should make that plain. New in this outline; it replaces the angel's charge as a quote (the charge stays as a paraphrase in beat 5).
  2. Rendered from Amélineau's French of the Bohairic Life, 5145-5146 (the Coptic OCR is unreadable): **"They are Christian people. They treat us this way out of love for the God of heaven."** Job: the reason he became a Christian. Bohairic Life only; the Greek Life has no speech here.
  3. Rendered from Cardon's Latin of the Greek Life, 86630-86633 (the Greek is not held legibly): **"Take this blanket away from here at once. It is not right that I should be better off in anything than the other brothers."** Job: the equal shared life, held to his own deathbed. Greek Life.

- **Connection to carry into the story:** The shared life outlived the man through his rule, and the rule through translation. Jerome's preface (I1, 4336-4392) says that many Latin-speaking monks lived in the communities of the Thebaid and at the monastery of Metanoia, near Alexandria by the editor's note. They could not read the Egyptian or Greek in which the rules of Pachomius, Theodore and Horsiesius were written. A priest, Silvanus, sent Jerome the books, which had come from Alexandria, and another priest, Leontinus, came with brothers to press him. Jerome dictated a Latin version from the Greek translation of the Egyptian. He made it also for his own brothers and for the nuns under Paula's daughter Eustochium, so that they could follow the example of the Tabennesiot monks. The listener needs to understand two things. First, what the rule carried: not a hermit's discipline, but a way for many people to live together, with houses, heads and equal shares. Second, that it passed into Latin as books, more than fifty years after Pachomius died. Latin-speaking monks living in his own houses in Egypt could not read the Egyptian or Greek, and priests pressed Jerome to translate the rules. He made the Latin for them, and also for his own brothers and for the nuns under Eustochium. Through that Latin the rule later reached the monasteries of the West. The Latin then carried the rule on: Benedict of Aniane's *Concordia* quotes it chapter by chapter under Benedict's Rule; its editor identifies the Pachomian text there as Jerome's Latin (I1). Tell it through those acts: monks asking, Jerome dictating, an abbot setting it under Benedict's Rule. Do not add a "this matters because" sentence. Cassian is a separate channel; do not tie him to Jerome's Latin.

- **Links:**
  - Antony: never met. In the Greek Life, after Pachomius's death, Antony tells his monks there was no such community when he began, says he often wanted to see Pachomius, and hopes to meet him in heaven.
  - Athanasius of Alexandria: met him (visited Tabennisi; Pachomius hid from ordination); later wrote to his successors.
  - Palladius: wrote of him and visited one of his houses.
  - Sozomen: wrote of him, following Palladius.
  - Jerome: translated his rule.
  - John Cassian: drew on the rule of "the monks of Tabenna".
  - Gennadius: wrote his notice ("founder of the Egyptian monasteries").

- **Who tells it:**
  - The Bohairic Life: the fellowship's own Coptic Life, anonymous, here in Amélineau's French of 1889. Its date is not fixed in the held texts, and its text breaks off before his death.
  - The Greek Life: anonymous, by a monk the Bollandists call nearly contemporary, here in a 17th-century Latin translation. Antony's words (quote 1) are its report.
  - The Arabic Life: later and fuller.
  - Palladius (about 420): in Egypt about 388 to 400, writing about 70 years after Pachomius's death. Sozomen and the Ethiopic Rules repeat him.
  - Jerome (about 404): a translator, describing the houses from the books he was sent; not a witness to the life.
  - How-we-know gets one or two sentences, placed where the listener first hears a Life quoted: two old Lives, one in Coptic from his own communities, one in Greek by a monk of the next generation. The doubt is not the story here.

- **Do not say:**
  - "He founded the first monastery", "he invented monastic life" or "he was the first to found a community" → "he drew monks who lived alone into one shared life under one rule". Palamon was already teaching "the rule ... as those before us taught it to us", and the Greek Life says Aotas tried before him. "Founder of the Egyptian monasteries" is Gennadius's phrase and "the father of the coenobite life" the Life of Pisentius's; each stays in its witness's voice.
  - "The monks of Egypt already had a way of life" with nothing after it → say what he changed: from each monk on his own to many under one rule in shared houses. (added from Substance_Read.md, 2026-11-18)
  - "Every monk before him lived utterly alone" → "each lived the monk's life on his own way"; a pupil could live with an old teacher, as he did with Palamon, and some hermits had cells near each other (H1).
  - "Antony said Pachomius was greater than he" → Antony, in the Greek Life, says only that there was no such community when he began, and that their father did this great good.
  - "The koinonia, his own word for it" → "one shared life", "a fellowship of houses" (H9).
  - "Fifty thousand monks" as fact → if used, "Jerome heard that nearly fifty thousand gathered at Easter"; Palladius counts seven thousand.
  - "Two women's houses" as a Life's count → it is Ladeuze's; the Bohairic Life tells of his sister's house.
  - "An angel gave him the rule on a brass tablet" as narrator's fact → "Palladius, writing about seventy years later, says an angel gave him the rule on a brass tablet; his own community's Coptic Life has the angel, but no tablet."
  - "The Greek Life says the angel gave him a tablet" → "only in bracketed passages its editors say were not in their Greek manuscript".
  - "Pachomius, Pachomius, Pachomius" as the Life's words → "the Life says the angel called him three times".
  - "Tabennisi, an island in the Nile" → "a deserted village by the river". (Schodde's introduction repeats the island error; do not take it from there.)
  - "Athanasius ordained him a priest" (a modern editor's slip) → "he hid so that he would not be ordained".
  - "He hid in the crowd" or "he hid among the brothers" as the one account → "he hid" (the two Lives differ).
  - "Across the river" for his sister's house, said as fact → "Palladius puts the women's house across the river; the Coptic Life puts it in the village".
  - "He is buried at ..." or "his grave is lost" as fact → "the Greek Life says Theodore moved his body to another place; the Arabic Life says no one has known where".
  - Any single number of monks as fact → "Palladius counts seven thousand in his own day"; keep "about" and the witness.
  - "More than a hundred died of the plague" → "across all the monasteries, a hundred or more".
  - "Born in 292" or "died in 348" as fact → "in the early fourth century ... he died in 346 or 348".
  - Naming the enemy in Constantine's war, or the year he was conscripted → the Lives name neither.
  - "He refused to see his sister because ..." → the record gives no motive; say only that he sent word and did not see her.
  - "Pachomius wrote the Rule himself" → "the rules of Pachomius, Theodore and Horsiesius", as Jerome names them; none of the held text is certainly in his own hand.
  - "Theodore succeeded him" → "he named Petronius; Petronius died within days, and Horsiesius followed". The Ethiopian calendar's "Theodore" is a late variant.
  - "He forbade contact with Arians and Origenists" → leave it out; the deathbed line is bracketed in the Latin and may not be in the Greek.
  - "Benedict of Aniane thought Pachomius's rule better or harder than Benedict's" → "he set Pachomius's rule under Benedict's, as one of the rules it agreed with". If his youth is told: "as a young monk, Ardo says, he thought Benedict's Rule was for beginners and reached for Basil and Pachomius". That was a phase he left behind.
  - "Benedict of Nursia used Pachomius's rule" → not held; the tie is through Benedict of Aniane's Concordia, four centuries later.
  - "Cassian read Jerome's Latin" → Cassian says only that he drew on the rule of "the monks of Tabenna".
  - "Jerome never met Pachomius" as a stated fact → "Jerome worked from books sent to him, more than fifty years after Pachomius died".
  - "The Great Persecution" → "the persecution" (the Lives' word).
  - "Palladius's seven thousand placed after Jerome's Latin, or as 'living under it' where 'it' could be the Latin" → "Palladius, writing about seventy years later, counts about seven thousand monks under the rule", told before Jerome's Latin. (added from Review.md, 2026-11-18; dating corrected from Review_2.md, 2026-11-18)
  - "The fellowship carried on unbroken after him (by leaving out the split)" → "it split after his death, and Theodore's letters urged the breakaways back". (added from Review.md, 2026-11-18)
  - "The nuns asked Jerome for the rule", or "Jerome's Latin took the rule to the West in 404" → "the Latin-speaking monks in Egypt asked, through Silvanus and Leontinus; Jerome also made it for his own brothers and Eustochium's nuns." (added from Outline_Check_2.md, 2026-11-18)
  - "Forty to a house in Pachomius's day" as fact → "about forty, as Jerome describes the houses". (added from Outline_Check_2.md, 2026-11-18)
  - "The brothers gave him a better blanket and he refused it" → "he asked for a lighter one; when he saw it was finer than the brothers' own, he sent it back". (added from Outline_Check_2.md, 2026-11-18)
  - "Palladius visited Egypt about seventy years later" → "Palladius, writing about seventy years later" (his Egypt years were about 388 to 400; the Lausiac History is of 419-420). (added from Review_2.md, 2026-11-18)
  - "In the early fourth century, each monk lived by his own way" (which reads as if every monk of the time lived alone, his own houses included) → "Before him, each monk lived by his own way". (added from Review_2.md, 2026-11-18)
  - "The bishops tried to kill him" → the Greek Life says a man with a sword burst in; it does not say who sent him.

- **Leave out:**
  - How the Lives differ on the tablet and on where the women's house stood, beyond a clause: the card carries it. The doubt is not the story here.
  - The wonder stories (the crocodile, demons in many shapes, the vision of hell, the brother who walked on coals): a pile of wonders, and none carries the heart.
  - Aotas, who tried a community before him and failed (H2): a name the listener cannot place; it stands behind the Do not say on "first".
  - Macarius's disguised visit and the nun who feigned madness (Palladius): side stories about other people.
  - The calumny and two suicides in the women's house (Palladius 33): this belongs to Palladius's own day, not to Pachomius's acts. It is left out for that reason, not to soften the record.
  - The Arabic Life's reason for hiding the body (to stop a church being built over it): late, single witness.
  - Abu Salih's claim that his body lay in a chest at Butij: late, and against the Lives.
  - The Instructions attributed to him (Sahidic): the attribution is untested in the held texts.
  - Ladeuze's view that the novelty itself met resistance (H8): an editor's reading; the record's own conflict (Esneh) is told instead.
