## 1. The test, from one hearing

- **Who:** an Egyptian monk of the early fourth century, a former conscript and convert from a pagan family, who had first learned the hermit's life.
- **What happened:** where monks had each lived on their own, he drew them into one shared life under one written rule: houses inside a walled monastery, the same clothes, one table, work in common, rank by who came first. Nine monasteries became one fellowship. He hid from ordination, held to equality on his deathbed, and after his death the fellowship split.
- **Why the connection matters:** his rule outlived him. Latin-speaking monks in his houses could not read it, so Jerome put it into Latin, and through that Latin it reached the West, where Benedict of Aniane set it beside Benedict's Rule.

**The heart (from the census entry and what he is known for):** Pachomius turned the monk's life from a solitary discipline into a common one: many people living together in houses under one written rule, with equal shares and one fellowship of monasteries. Gennadius's "founder of the Egyptian monasteries", the census's "bound monks to a common rule in shared houses". Through translation, that rule became a pattern for monks far beyond Egypt.

**Does the first minute carry it?** Yes. The first 150 words run from the two Lives, through Antony's witness ("no community anywhere … your father did this great good"), to "Pachomius drew them into one shared life under one written rule", the houses inside a walled monastery, the head of each house, the same clothes and one table. The heart is stated plainly, shown concretely, and given its weight before the life story starts. The rest deepens it: the blanket shows the equality held to the end, and the closing paragraph carries the rule's spread. Not a children's book, and it does not talk down.

**Where I got lost:** at Esneh (see BLOCKING 2). A man with a sword bursts in, and the story moves straight to the plague. The listener does not know why he came, what the questioning was about, or what happened.

## Findings

**BLOCKING 1. "Palladius, visiting Egypt about seventy years later".** The held texts put Palladius's years in Egypt at about 388 to 400 (Clarke's notes: 388, line 713; Cellia "in 390 or 391", 877; Evagrius's death "in 399 or 400", 1177). That is some forty to fifty years after Pachomius died. "About seventy years" is when he *wrote* the Lausiac History (419-420, Source_Sheet row for Palladius). Change to: "Palladius, writing about seventy years later, counted some seven thousand monks under the rule." Keep the sentence before Jerome, as the outline's Do not say requires.
- **Do not say** (add to the outline; it also corrects beat 1 of "What came of them", which says "visiting Egypt about seventy years later"): "Palladius visited Egypt about seventy years later" → "Palladius, writing about seventy years later" (his Egypt years were about 388 to 400; the Lausiac History is of 419-420).

**BLOCKING 2. Esneh, left unfinished.** "At Esneh, the Greek Life says, bishops and monks questioned him in church, and a man with a sword burst in." Heard once, this is a fragment. The listener does not learn what the questioning was about, what the man came to do, or that he failed. The "and" also sets the bishops and the sword side by side, which leaves room for "the bishops tried to kill him", a line already on the Do not say list. The outline's beat 6 holds all of this (86393-86400, 86463-86466). Change to one sentence such as: "At Esneh, the Greek Life says, bishops and monks questioned him in church over claims that he could read hidden thoughts, and a man with a sword burst in to kill him; the Lord saved him, the Life says, with the help of those present." No new Do not say line is needed; the existing one covers the bishops.

**MINOR 3. Long sentences.** The closing sentence is 36 words ("Through that Latin … it agreed with."), and the tool reports a longest of 41. Split the last one, for example: "Through that Latin the rule reached the West. In the early ninth century the abbot Benedict of Aniane set it under the chapters of Benedict's Rule, as one of the old rules it agreed with." This also eases the two Benedicts.

**MINOR 4. Length.** At 472 words the story is about 3.1 minutes, against the outline's 380 to 450. The two fixes above add a few words, so trim elsewhere, for example by tightening the first sentence's description of the two Lives or the run of house details in paragraph 2. Do not cut the heart or the connection.

**MINOR 5. "In Upper Egypt in the early fourth century, each monk lived by his own way."** Pachomius's own houses also stand in the early fourth century, so the sentence reads as if every monk of the time lived alone. "Before him, in Upper Egypt, each monk lived by his own way …" keeps it to the older life.
