# Pachomius: outline

Every reference is to `Source_Sheet.md` (item codes) and through it to file and line in `cic/texts/`.

- **Length:** about 2½ to 3 minutes (about 400 to 450 words). Reason: a major figure (census `major`), whose two oldest Lives disagree on things a listener will meet (the angel's tablet, the women's house). The connection also needs room: it runs from a Coptic rule through Greek and Latin to a ninth-century abbot. Do not fill past what the beats hold.

- **Setting:**
  - Time: early 300s, after the Great Persecution ended and Constantine ruled (Bohairic 5093-5094; Greek 80977-80979). Dates of birth and death are uncertain (S4): say "in the early fourth century"; he died in the middle of the century, in 346 or 348.
  - Place: Upper Egypt, the Thebaid, along the Nile. Born in the district of Esneh (S1). Tabennisi was "a deserted village by the river" (S2). His later head house was Phbôou, which the Greek calls Pabau (S3). Spoken words: "Upper Egypt", "the upper Nile", "a deserted village called Tabennisi".
  - Tradition: census `desert-monasticism`, spoken name "the Desert Fathers and Mothers" or "the monks of the Egyptian desert". He belongs to Egypt's Coptic-speaking church, which still keeps his feast (I4).

- **Thread:** He drew monks out of solitude into a shared life under one written rule. Men and women in linked houses ate, dressed and worked alike, and that rule was copied into Greek, Latin and Ge'ez long after him.

- **Shape:** Founder or leader.

- **Openings:**
  1. Night in a prison on the Nile. Young conscripts are locked up, and strangers bring them bread. He asks who they are (E1; quote 1). Both Lives tell it, though they place it in different towns.
  2. His deathbed. After forty days of plague fever, he sends back a better blanket (E13; quote 3).

- **Story beats:**
  1. **Conscript.** Born to pagan parents, he was taken at about twenty for Constantine's war. Christians fed the locked-up recruits "for love of the God of heaven". That night he vowed to serve people all his life (E1, E2). Widely Accepted. The town is Esneh in the Bohairic Life and Thebes in the Greek, so the writer should not name it, or should name both.
  2. **Baptism and apprenticeship.** Released, he settled in a near-empty village, Schénésît (Chenoboskion), was baptized, and nursed villagers through an epidemic (E3). Then he went to the hermit Palamon. Palamon tested him with a rule "as those before us taught us": half the night in prayer, fasting, manual work (E4). Widely Accepted. This shows that the desert life was older than him.
  3. **Tabennisi.** At a deserted village a mile away, a voice told him to stay and build, for many would come. An angel told him God's will was that he serve the human race (E5, E6; quote 2). Keep both in the Lives' voice. Widely Accepted as the Lives' account.
  4. **The first community.** Three men came. At first he did all the work and nursed them himself, calling them "new plants". Then he gave them rules: the same clothes, the same food, a decent bed (E8). As numbers grew, he set over each house a head and helpers, and the work turned over every three weeks (E9). Widely Accepted.
  5. **Monks, not clergy; and his sister.** He would not have priests among his monks, "because of envy and vainglory", and kept them under the bishops. When Athanasius came up the Nile and a bishop asked for him to be ordained, he hid in the crowd (E9, E11). His sister Mary came to see him. He would not see her, but sent word through the doorkeeper and had a dwelling built for her. Other women joined her, and he sent them the brothers' rules written in a book (E10). The Bohairic Life puts her house in the village; Palladius and the Ethiopic Rules put the women across the river. Widely Accepted that there was a women's house; Contested where it stood.
  6. **Conflict.** By his own count, the federation held nine monasteries (E12). A koinonia is a "fellowship": the census gives this as his own name for the linked houses (S5). At Latopolis (Esneh), monks and bishops met in the church to question him over claims that he could see hidden thoughts. As he finished his answer, a man with a sword burst in to kill him; the Life says the Lord saved him with the help of those present (E12). From the Greek Life only. Widely Accepted as that Life's account.
  7. **Death.** After Easter a plague killed more than a hundred brothers. He fell ill, refused the better blanket, named Petronius to follow him, and died on 14 Pachon. Theodore and three brothers secretly moved his body (E13). Widely Accepted. Petronius died within days; Horsiesius followed.

- **What came of them** (about a third of the story; heaviest first):
  1. **The rule travelled.** Palladius, about seventy years later, counts 7,000 monks living under it (I1). About 404, Latin-speaking monks in the communities of the Thebaid and at the monastery of Metanoia could read neither Egyptian nor Greek. Urged by priests who sent him the books, Jerome put the rules into Latin from a Greek translation of the Egyptian (I1; Documented by Jerome). Cassian drew on "the monks of Tabenna" for the West. In the early ninth century, Ardo wrote that his abbot, Benedict of Aniane (died 821), counted Benedict's Rule as one for beginners and reached for Basil and Pachomius (I1, Ardo). The Ethiopian church kept a version in Ge'ez.
  2. **The fellowship outlived him.** Petronius, Horsiesius and Theodore led it. Athanasius wrote to them, and the gatherings at Phbôou at Easter and in August went on (I2, E9).
  3. **Defended:** equality inside the common life, to his last days (the blanket); monks without clerical rank, under the bishops (I3).
  4. **Remembered:** the Coptic church keeps his day, 14 Bashons; a seventh-century Coptic Life calls him "the father of the coenobite life" (I4).

- **Quotes** (2 or 3 at most):
  1. Rendered from Amélineau's French of the Bohairic Life, 5145-5146 (the Coptic OCR is unreadable): **"They are Christian people. They treat us this way out of love for the God of heaven."** Job: the opening, and the reason he became a Christian.
  2. Rendered from Amélineau's French, Bohairic 6247-6248; the Greek Life's Latin (81973-81974) says the same: **"Pachomius, Pachomius, Pachomius, the will of God is that you serve the human race and unite them to him."** Job: the angel's charge. It ties back to his prison vow and stays in the Life's voice ("the Life says an angel told him").
  3. Rendered from Cardon's Latin of the Greek Life, 86630-86633 (the Greek is not held legibly): **"Take this blanket away from here at once. It is not right that I should be better off in anything than the other brothers."** Job: what he defended, in his own reported words at the end.

- **Connection to carry into the story:** The rule outlived the man through translation. Jerome's preface (I1, 4336-4354) says that many Latin-speaking monks lived in the communities of the Thebaid and at the monastery of Metanoia. (Metanoia was the renamed Canopus; the editor's note places it by Alexandria.) They could not read the Egyptian or Greek in which the rules of Pachomius, Theodore and Horsiesius were written. A priest, Silvanus, sent Jerome the books, and another, Leontinus, came with brothers to press him. Jerome dictated a Latin version, made from the Greek translation of the Egyptian. The listener needs to understand that Jerome never met Pachomius. Jerome was serving Latin monks who were already living by his rule in Egypt. The listener also needs to know that the Latin was the copy that carried the rule west: Cassian used the Tabennesiot rule, and four centuries later Benedict of Aniane set it beside Basil as a harder rule than Benedict's (Ardo, 28948-28951). Tell it through those acts: monks asking, Jerome dictating, an abbot reaching for it. Do not add a "this matters because" sentence.

- **Links:**
  - Athanasius of Alexandria: met him (visited Tabennisi; Pachomius hid from ordination); later wrote to his successors.
  - Antony: never met. In the Greek Life, Antony says he often wanted to see him and hoped to meet him in heaven.
  - Palladius: wrote of him and visited one of his houses.
  - Sozomen: wrote of him, following Palladius.
  - Jerome: translated his rule.
  - John Cassian: drew on his rule.
  - Gennadius: wrote his notice ("founder of the Egyptian monasteries").

- **Who tells it:**
  - The Bohairic Life: the fellowship's own Coptic Life, anonymous, here in Amélineau's French of 1889. Its date is not fixed in the held texts, and its text breaks off before his death.
  - The Greek Life: anonymous, by a monk the Bollandists call nearly contemporary, here in a 17th-century Latin translation. Bracketed passages in it were added from another Latin version.
  - The Arabic Life: later and fuller; only it explains why the body was hidden.
  - Palladius (about 420): a visitor writing about 70 years later. He is the source of the brass tablet. Sozomen and the Ethiopic Rules repeat him.
  - Jerome (about 404): a translator, not a witness to the life.
  - Where it matters, the listener needs to know that the angel comes from the Lives and the tablet from Palladius. The Coptic Life comes from the communities he founded; the Greek Life is by a monk of the next generation.

- **Do not say:**
  - "An angel gave him the rule on a brass tablet" as narrator's fact → "Palladius, writing about seventy years later, says an angel gave him the rule on a brass tablet; his own community's Coptic Life has the angel, but no tablet."
  - "He founded the first monastery" or "he invented monastic life" → "he gathered monks into a shared life under one rule"; Palamon was already teaching "the rule ... as those before us taught us". "Founder of the Egyptian monasteries" is Gennadius's phrase and stays in his voice.
  - "Tabennisi, an island in the Nile" → "a deserted village by the river".
  - "Athanasius ordained him a priest" (a modern editor's slip) → "he hid so that he would not be ordained".
  - "Across the river" for his sister's house, said as fact → "Palladius puts the women's house across the river; the Coptic Life puts it in the village".
  - Any single number of monks as fact → "Palladius counts seven thousand in his own day"; keep "about" and the witness.
  - "Born in 292" or "died in 348" as fact → "in the early fourth century ... he died in 346 or 348".
  - Naming the enemy in Constantine's war, or the year he was conscripted → the Lives name neither.
  - "He refused to see his sister because ..." → the record gives no motive; say only that he sent word and did not see her.
  - "Pachomius wrote the Rule himself" → "the rules of Pachomius, Theodore and Horsiesius", as Jerome names them; none of the held text is certainly in his own hand.
  - "Theodore succeeded him" → "he named Petronius; Petronius died within days, and Horsiesius followed". The Ethiopian calendar's "Theodore" is a late variant.
  - "He forbade contact with Arians and Origenists" → leave it out; the deathbed line is bracketed in the Latin and may not be in the Greek.

- **Leave out:**
  - The wonder stories (the crocodile, demons in many shapes, the vision of hell, the brother who walked on coals): a pile of wonders, and none carries the thread.
  - Macarius's disguised visit and the nun who feigned madness (Palladius): side stories about other people.
  - The calumny and two suicides in the women's house (Palladius 33): this belongs to Palladius's own day, not to Pachomius's acts. It is left out for that reason, not to soften the record.
  - The Arabic Life's reason for hiding the body (to stop a church being built over it): late, single witness. If the hidden grave is told, say only that his disciple moved it in secret.
  - Abu Salih's claim that his body lay in a chest at Butij: late, and against the Lives.
  - The Instructions attributed to him (Sahidic): the attribution is untested in the held texts.
