In the 400s, a teacher named Narsai fled the city of Edessa by night. Barhadbeshabba, a writer of his own church, tells it in praise about a century later. Narsai found some Persians at the city church, he says, and asked them to carry his books to Nisibis, for the books were all his treasure. Edessa lay in the Roman Empire; Nisibis, just inside Persia, where Christians under the shahs formed the Church of the East.

Narsai was Persian-born, and had studied at Edessa, whose school took students from Persia. There he and his fellow student Barsauma read the Bible commentaries of Theodore of Mopsuestia, newly put from Greek into Syriac. Their church called Theodore the Interpreter. Later Narsai led that school for some twenty years, and the school's own accounts say he explained Scripture each day in commentaries set to chant.

His enemies called him a heretic, a follower of Theodore and Nestorius, and said he sided with Persia and wanted to betray the city. Barhadbeshabba writes that he refused to agree with them even outwardly, and that, warned he would be burned, he fled that night. The year is uncertain: 457, or after 471.

He meant to go deeper into Persia, but Barsauma, now bishop of Nisibis, welcomed him with honour, asked him to found a school there and promised help. Students came, the school's own account says, from Persia, from Syria, and most of those at Edessa. In 489 the Persian school at Edessa was rooted out. Even his enemy Simeon, who called him the Leper, wrote that Narsai founded the school at Nisibis. In 496, under his headship, it took written rules.

He died in the early 500s, after forty, forty-five or fifty years at Nisibis, as the sources count, and the school outlasted him. It named Barsauma and Narsai as its founders, his kinsman Abraham later led it, and it swore to keep the canons of Narsai. Barhadbeshabba, speaking to the school about ninety years later, put it this way: "Schools multiplied in the empire of the Persians. Edessa grew dark; Nisibis grew bright. The empire of the Romans filled with error, and that of the Persians with the knowledge of the fear of God." A catalogue from the 1200s credits Narsai with three hundred and sixty poems.

His friends called him the Harp of the Holy Spirit.

One of his verse homilies says of God: "In baptism he recast our image, as in a furnace. In place of our clay, he has made us spiritual gold."
