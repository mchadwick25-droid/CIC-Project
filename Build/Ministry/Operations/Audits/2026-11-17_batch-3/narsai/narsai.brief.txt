In the 400s, on the night he left Edessa, the teacher Narsai asked some Persians to carry his books to Nisibis. They were, a writer of his own church tells a century later, all the treasure he had.

Edessa lay in the Roman Empire, while Nisibis lay just across the border in Persia, in what is now southeastern Turkey. There the city's bishop, Barsauma, asked Narsai to found a school and promised him help, and the school counts both men as its founders. Students came from Persia, from Syria and from Edessa. They learned the Bible the way of Theodore of Mopsuestia, a Greek scholar whose commentaries had been put into Syriac. Narsai's enemies had condemned that teaching, but at Nisibis, in the Church of the East, the church under the Persian kings, it went on for more than a century.

Narsai taught in verse too. The school's own account says he wrote more than three hundred sermons in metre, of which forty-seven were printed in 1905. Of God's work in baptism, he wrote: "In baptism he recast our image, as in a furnace. In place of our clay, he has made us spiritual gold."

Born in Persian territory, he had studied at Edessa's school for Persian students and led it for twenty years. Then his enemies called him a heretic and said he sided with Persia. His biographer, Barhadbeshabba, says that when Narsai was warned he would be burned, he fled that night, in 457 or after 471.

In 496, while he was its head, the school at Nisibis took written rules. Yet in Persia he was suspected from the other side, for his biographer says students later denounced him to the king as an enemy of Persia.

He died in the early 500s, after some forty years or more at Nisibis. His kinsman Abraham, who had shared his cell, later led the school, and a century on it was still renewing the rules it kept in his name. About 550, a Latin writer in Constantinople met a Persian trained at Nisibis, where, he wrote, God's law was taught as a set course, step by step.

Barhadbeshabba, speaking to the school about ninety years after Narsai's death, put it this way: "Schools multiplied in the empire of the Persians. Edessa grew dark; Nisibis grew bright. The empire of the Romans filled with error, and that of the Persians with the knowledge of the fear of God."
