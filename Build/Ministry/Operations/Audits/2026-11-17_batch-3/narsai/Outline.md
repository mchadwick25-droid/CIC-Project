# Outline: Narsai of Nisibis

File and line references point to `Source_Sheet.md` items and, through them, to `cic/texts/`. Short names: History = Barhadbeshabba's *History of the holy Fathers* (Nau, PO 9); Cause = *Cause of the Foundation of the Schools* (Scher, PO 4); Homilies = Connolly 1909; Simeon = Simeon of Beth Arsham's letter (Assemani, BO I).

- **Length:** about 2½ minutes (about 375 words). A major figure, and the story needs room to make the frontier move clear: a school leaving Roman Edessa for Persian Nisibis. Not the full 3, because almost every scene in his life rests on one later writer of praise, the History.

- **Setting:**
  - Century: the 400s, into the early 500s. Head of the Edessa school twenty years, from about 437 or after 452 (Nau, History 36651-36655, 36700-36705), leaving in 457 or after 471; death in the early 500s (S6, D1-D2). Spoken: "in the 400s"; "in the early 500s".
  - Places: Edessa, a city of the Roman Empire with a school for Persian students (S1, E2); Nisibis, just inside the Persian Empire, "between the two empires" (S3, Cause 20690-20693). Both are in today's southeastern Turkey (Edessa is Şanlıurfa; Nisibis is Nusaybin). He was born in Persian territory, at Ma'alta (S4).
  - Tradition: the Church of the East, the church of Christians under the Persian shahs (census). Spoken: "the Church of the East". It taught Scripture the way of Theodore of Mopsuestia, whom it called "the Interpreter" (E2, Cause 20400-20401).

- **Thread:** He carried the teaching of Scripture across the Roman-Persian frontier and, with Bishop Barsauma, set up at Nisibis the school that trained the Church of the East's teachers.

- **Shape:** Teacher or thinker.

- **Openings:**
  1. The night he fled Edessa, he found Persians at the city church and asked them to carry his books to Nisibis, "for that was all his treasure" (History 36830-36839, E5). A scene from the History; tell it as the History's.
  2. What his accusers said among themselves, as the History reports it: how has a Persian won over the Romans? (Quote 3; History 36693-36695, E4.)

- **Story beats:**
  1. **A Persian student in a Roman city.** Born at Ma'alta in Persian territory, he went to the school at Edessa, where students from Persia studied. He studied there alongside Barsauma, later bishop of Nisibis. There they read Theodore's Bible commentaries, newly put into Syriac (E2; Cause 20482-20551). Widely Accepted (Cause; History 36538-36539; Bar Hebraeus 4264-4267); the Cause has them go to Edessa together (20482-20491), the History has them meet there. Explain: Theodore wrote in Greek; the head teacher had wished his commentaries were in Syriac, the school's language, until they were translated (Cause 20493-20494, 20547-20548).
  2. **Head of the school for twenty years.** When the head teacher died, the school asked for Narsai. He agreed only if others taught reading and spelling, so that he could explain Scripture. Every day he gave commentaries set to chant (E3; Cause 20553-20560). Twenty years: Widely Accepted (Cause, History, Mari, Bar Hebraeus). His condition and the daily chanted commentary: the Cause's and the History's telling; Inferential-Thin as details. Explain: the "interpreter" was the master who explained Scripture.
  3. **Accused, and out by night.** His enemies said he was a heretic, following Theodore and Nestorius (History 36684-36686). They also said he sided with Persia and wanted to betray the city. They asked him to agree outwardly; he refused. Warned that he would be burned, he fled by night (E4-E5; History 36643-36839). That he left under a charge of heresy: Widely Accepted. The scenes and speeches: Inferential-Thin, the History's. The year: Contested (457, or after 471).
  4. **Kept at Nisibis.** He meant to go deeper into Persia to teach. Barsauma, now bishop of Nisibis, brought him into the city with honour. He asked him to found a school there and promised help. He argued that the move was providence, not accident (E6-E7; Cause 20611-20619). The request: Widely Accepted. The speech: the Cause's.
  5. **The school grows.** The Cause says students came from Persia and Syria, and most of those at the Edessa school (Cause 20739-20743; the school's own later account, Inferential-Thin). The History says only that "brothers" gathered from every side (History 37080-37081). Even his enemy Simeon wrote that Narsai founded the school at Nisibis (Simeon, BO I 75018-75020). Founding: Documented. In 489 the Persian school at Edessa was rooted out (S1; Chron. Edessa 189); Simeon says it was by the work of Bishop Cyrus and the order of the emperor Zeno (Bar Hebraeus editors 3743-3747, after Assemani). Documented. In 496 the school took written rules, under Narsai as its head (E9). Documented, from Guidi's reading.
  6. **Suspected on both sides; teaching in verse.** He fell out with Barsauma, then they made peace (E10; the cause is disputed, D6). The History says he answered a rival preacher, Jacob of Serugh, whose verse was drawing people away, with *mimre* of his own, sermons in metre, one for every day of the year (History 37258-37271). Mari, much later, says Jacob imitated Narsai (Mari 14272). Who answered whom: Contested (D5). Later, students denounced him to the Persian king as an enemy of Persia (E13; History 37307-37335). That he wrote many verse homilies: Documented. The king scene: Inferential-Thin.
  7. **Death.** He died in the early 500s, after forty, forty-five or fifty years at Nisibis, as the sources variously count (E15, D2). Modern editors put it about 502-504, counting from a departure in 457 (Connolly; Nau). Nau notes that a departure after 471 would put his death no earlier than 512 (History 36700-36705). Contested.

- **What came of them** (heaviest first; about a third of the story):
  1. **The school lasted, and his line of teachers ran it.** The school's own speech names Barsauma and Narsai as its founders. It says institutions that came from it now exist in several places (I1; Cause 17457-17465). Its heads after him include his kinsman Abraham, under whom, the History says, more than a thousand students gathered (I4; History 37657-37659). Documented (the school and its heads).
  2. **Its way of teaching the Bible was noticed abroad.** By about 550, a Latin writer in Constantinople, Junillus, described a Persian, Paul, trained "in the school of the Syrians in the city of Nisibis". There, he wrote, God's law is taught by public teachers "in order and by rule", as grammar and rhetoric are taught among us (I2; Junillus 22735-22738). Documented for the school. Junillus does not name Narsai.
  3. **His rules were kept and renewed.** Under Khosrau I the school swore to keep "the canons of Narsai". In 602 the school searched out the old rules and, with later additions, brought them back into force (I3; Guidi's summary, 9948-9995). Documented, Guidi's words.
  4. **His verse homilies were kept.** Abdisho's catalogue of the 1200s credits him with "three hundred and sixty poems" (I5; Badger 17649-17656). Forty-seven were printed in 1905, and four are in the Library in English (Homilies 253-262). Documented.
  5. **What he defended:** Theodore's way of reading Scripture. His accusers named it as the charge, and Mari says he "never" departed from it (I6). Widely Accepted. **Defended.**
  6. **Two names.** His enemies called him "the Leper"; his friends, "the Harp of the Holy Spirit" (I7). "Leper": Documented (Simeon). "Harp": the 1200s catalogue of Abdisho (Badger's English, "the Harp of the Soul", Badger 17649) and editors ("the Harp of the Holy Spirit"); Widely Accepted as a name.

- **Quotes** (2 or 3 at most):
  1. **The school's verdict.** Rendered from Scher's French (Cause 20743-20746, I1; the Syriac OCR is unreadable): "Schools multiplied in the empire of the Persians. Edessa grew dark; Nisibis grew bright. The empire of the Romans filled with error, and that of the Persians with the knowledge of the fear of God." Job: the school's own boast, made about ninety years later in the Cause, a speech to the school credited to Barhadbeshabba. Bring it in with its speaker named ("Barhadbeshabba, speaking to the school about ninety years later, put it this way"). Never use it as the narrator's summary of what happened.
  2. **His own words, on baptism.** Rendered from Connolly's English (Homilies 6857-6858, W1, Hom. XXII; the Syriac is not held): "In baptism he recast our image, as in a furnace. In place of our clay, he has made us spiritual gold." ("He" is God.) Job: lets the listener hear the poet-teacher's voice: the kind of image he set to chant for his students.
  3. **Optional, as an opening.** Rendered from Nau's French (History 36693-36695, E4; the Syriac OCR is unreadable): "How has a Persian, a man who is a stranger even to the order of logic, won over the Romans?" Job: shows the envy the History blames, and that he was an outsider in Edessa. "Romans" means people of the Roman Empire, Edessa's own people, not people from Rome.

- **Connection to carry into the story:** Narsai to the School of Nisibis, and through it to the Church of the East's later teachers. The Cause (20739-20746) and the History (37075-37081) say students came to him from Persia, from Syria and from Edessa itself. Simeon, his enemy, says Narsai the Leper set up the school at Nisibis (BO I 75018-75020). The listener needs three things: Edessa was in the Roman Empire and Nisibis in Persia; the teaching his Edessa enemies condemned went on across the border, where Barsauma, as the Cause has him say, hoped students would come "de toutes parts" (Cause 20690-20694); and it went on long after him, under heads from his own household, with rules kept in his name (I1, I3, I4). Carry it through acts: the flight, Barsauma's offer, students arriving, rules written, a kinsman taking his chair. Barsauma's motive for the offer may be given only as the speeches give it (Cause 20689-20735): to do good everywhere, and to resist error.

- **Links:**
  - Barsauma of Nisibis: studied with him at Edessa; brought him into Nisibis and founded the school with him; quarrelled with him and made peace.
  - Abraham of Beth Rabban: his kinsman; he renamed him and shared his cell; later head of the school.
  - Barhadbeshabba 'Arbaia: wrote his life.
  - Theodore of Mopsuestia: quoted and taught his books.
  - Jacob of Serugh: rival preacher in verse.
  - Simeon of Beth Arsham: wrote against his party, and listed him among its men by the nickname "the Leper" (BO I 74556-74557).
  - Henana of Adiabene: a later head of the school, whose followers, an editor's note says, cut hymns credited to Narsai.

- **Who tells it:**
  - Barhadbeshabba 'Arbaia (History, end of the 500s): a writer of the same church and school, writing in praise, about a century later. Nearly every scene of the life is his alone; the listener should hear his name for the flight, the books, the speeches at Edessa and Nisibis, the school's verdict (Quote 1), and any wonder.
  - The Cause (end of the 500s, perhaps the same Barhadbeshabba, by then a bishop): a speech to the school about its own founding. It is the school's own pride, not an outside judgement.
  - Simeon of Beth Arsham: a younger contemporary and opponent. Name him when the listener hears "the Leper" or his witness to the founding.
  - Narsai's own words come through R. H. Connolly's English of 1909.
  - Mari ibn Sulayman (1100s) and Bar Hebraeus (1200s, of the rival Syriac Orthodox church) wrote six or seven centuries later.
  - Junillus (c. 550) is an outsider writing in Latin.

- **Do not say:**
  - "He was expelled in 457 with the Persian students." Say: he left Edessa under accusation; the year is uncertain, 457 or after 471 (D1).
  - "Narsai founded the school alone" or "Barsauma founded it and appointed him." Say: Barsauma asked him to found it and promised help, and the school counts both as its founders (D3).
  - Any single year for his death, or "502-504" beside "after 471". Say: in the early 500s (D1-D2).
  - "Edessa grew dark; Nisibis grew bright", or "the Roman Empire filled with error", in the narrator's voice. Say it only as the Cause's words, with Barhadbeshabba named.
  - "He wrote his homilies to answer Jacob of Serugh" as fact. Say the History tells it so, and Mari says the reverse.
  - "Fifty years at Nisibis." Say: forty, forty-five or fifty, as the sources count (D2).
  - "The Edessa school closed, so its teachers joined him." The sources give no such cause. Say both facts side by side: the Cause says most of the Edessa students came to him, and in 489 the Persian school at Edessa was closed (Chron. Edessa).
  - "He wrote the rules of 496." Say: the school took written rules in 496 while he was its head, drawing on his work (Guidi's summary, E9).
  - "Nestorian" in the narrator's voice. Say "the Church of the East" and "followers of Theodore"; "Nestorian" only as the opponents' label.
  - "He was burned / nearly burned." Say: the History says he was warned he would be burned, and fled that night.
  - "He healed a boy." If used at all: "the History tells that…" (E14).
  - "360 homilies survive." Say: Abdisho's 1200s catalogue credits him with 360 poems; forty-seven were printed in 1905.
  - "Barsauma's wife's jealousy caused the quarrel" as fact. If told, credit it to the History, and say Bar Hebraeus tells it another way (D6).
  - Adding a description of his face, voice, family or feelings beyond the sources.

- **Leave out:**
  - The healing of the boy, the plague, the angels at his chair (E14; Mari): wonder stories from late or praising witnesses, and the shape warns against them.
  - His childhood flight from the Magi to a mountain, and the back-and-forth to his uncle's monastery (S5, E1): one late witness, and a side road.
  - Mari's flight "to an island" (D4) and Theodulus's blessing (I7): late, and the Theodulus meeting strains the dates.
  - The Chronicle of Arbela's account: its authenticity is contested.
  - Homily XVII's authorship debate (D8), and its quotes: authorship not settled.
  - Bar Hebraeus's "concubine": it belongs to a hostile story about married bishops and needs more room than the story has. If the quarrel is told, it must be told with both versions (Do not say).
  - Kept, not left out: the charges against him at Edessa and at the Persian court, and the threat of fire. These are the record's conflict and are told plainly.
