# Outline: Narsai of Nisibis

References point to `Source_Sheet.md` items and, through them, to `cic/texts/`. Short names: History = Barhadbeshabba's *History of the holy Fathers* (Nau, PO 9); Cause = *Cause of the Foundation of the Schools* (Scher, PO 4); Homilies = Connolly 1909; Simeon = Simeon of Beth Arsham's letter (Assemani, BO I); Statutes = Guidi 1890, introduction.

- **The heart of their life:** Narsai was a teacher of the Bible and a poet. His great work was the school at Nisibis, which he founded with Bishop Barsauma after he was driven from Edessa.
  - **What he built.** Barsauma, bishop of Nisibis, asked him to found a school there and promised help. Friendly and hostile witnesses agree that Narsai set it up. The school counts both men as its founders (E6, E8, I1; Cause 20611-20617, 17457-17460; Simeon BO I 75018-75020). Documented.
  - **How it lived.** Nisibis lay just inside the Persian Empire, on the frontier (S3). Students came from Persia and from Syria, and most of those at Edessa, by the Cause's account (E8; Cause 20739-20743; the school's own later voice). He led it for the rest of his life, some forty or more years (D2). In 496, while he was its head, the school took written rules; later it swore to keep "the canons of Narsai" (E9, I3; Guidi, editor). Documented.
  - **What it held.** It taught Scripture the way of Theodore of Mopsuestia, whom this church called "the Interpreter" (E2, I6). His accusers at Edessa named that as the charge against him. Mari says he "never" departed from it. Widely Accepted. **Defended.**
  - **What it changed.** It went on after him, under his kinsmen Abraham and John of Beth Rabban (I4). A century later it was still renewing its rules (H6). By about 550 a Latin writer in Constantinople knew it as the place where the Bible was taught "in order and by rule" (I2). The school's own speech of about 600 says other schools grew from it across Persia (I1, H4). Documented that it lasted a century and was known abroad. That it was the Church of the East's chief school, the start of its learning: the school's own boast and a modern editor's view (Connolly, H4), Dominant Modern Reconstruction. The sources hold students who went on to teach (Paul, I2; Bar 'Edta, H5) but no general statement that it "trained the church's teachers"; say what it taught and who came.
  - **His teaching in verse.** He taught in poetry as well as prose. The Cause, a century on, says he wrote "more than three hundred" verse homilies, sermons in metre (H1). Forty-seven survive in print, in couplets of twelve-syllable lines (H3, I5). They explain Scripture and the church's rites, baptism among them (W1). Documented. The Cause also says that for twenty years at Edessa he explained Scripture daily "with chant" (E3, H2): the Cause's alone, Inferential-Thin as a daily practice.
  - **The census check.** The census says "poet and teacher of Scripture", twenty years at Edessa, fled "when accused of heresy and of siding with Persia", founded with Barsauma "the school of the Church of the East". All held (E3, E4, E6-E8, I1). "Fled" is the History's telling; the year is Contested (D1).

- **Length:** about 2½ minutes, about 375 words (Neo, 150 a minute). He is a major figure, and the story must carry two things: a school moving from the Roman Empire into Persia, and the poet-teacher whose teaching it carried. Not the full 3, because nearly every scene of his life rests on one later writer of praise.

- **Setting:**
  - Century: the 400s, into the early 500s (S6, D1-D2). Spoken: "in the 400s"; "in the early 500s".
  - Places: Edessa, a city of the Roman Empire with a school for Persian students (S1, E2); Nisibis, just inside the Persian Empire, "between the two empires" (S3). Both in today's southeastern Turkey (Edessa is Şanlıurfa; Nisibis is Nusaybin). He was born at Ma'alta, in Persian territory (S4).
  - Tradition: the Church of the East, the church of Christians under the Persian shahs (census). Spoken: "the Church of the East".

- **Shape:** Teacher or thinker. The founding is the heart, but it is a school, and its impact is in its students and readers.

- **Openings:**
  1. **Recommended.** The night he left Edessa, he asked some Persians at the city church to carry his books to Nisibis, "for that was all his treasure" (E5; History 36835-36839). Then straight to the heart: at Nisibis, across the border, he and Bishop Barsauma founded a school. Plain heard once: a man, a night, his books, a border city. Credit it in one short clause ("a writer of his own church tells, a century later"), not more.
  2. **Alternative.** Open on the school itself: just inside Persia, at Nisibis, a teacher driven out of the Roman Empire started a school that lasted over a century (E8, H6). Clearer still, but loses the image; the books can follow in the flight beat.

- **Story beats** (the heart lands by about word 120; the rest deepens it):
  1. **The books, and the school (opening into the heart, words 1-90).** The night flight and the books, briefly (E5; Inferential-Thin, the History's). At Nisibis, just inside Persia, he and Bishop Barsauma founded a school. Students came from Persia, from Syria and from Edessa itself (E8; Documented for the founding). It taught the Bible the way of Theodore of Mopsuestia, which his enemies had condemned (E2, E4, I6). It went on after him for more than a century, and became known as far as Constantinople (I2, H6). Explain: Theodore was a Greek Bible scholar whose commentaries had been put into Syriac, the school's language.
  2. **A poet who taught (words 90-150).** He taught in verse as well as prose. He wrote, the school's own account says, more than three hundred sermons in verse (H1). Forty-seven survive, on Scripture and the church's rites (I5, H3). Quote 2 can go here, or close the story. Documented.
  3. **A Persian at Edessa.** Born in Persian territory, he studied at Edessa's school for Persian students, alongside Barsauma (E2). There they read Theodore's commentaries in Syriac. When the head teacher died, the school asked for him. He agreed if others taught reading and spelling, so he could explain Scripture. He led it twenty years, and the Cause says he gave daily commentaries "with chant" (E3, H2). Twenty years: Widely Accepted. The condition and the chant: the Cause's, Inferential-Thin. Explain: the "interpreter" was the master who explained Scripture.
  4. **Accused.** His enemies called him a heretic, a follower of Theodore and Nestorius. They also said he sided with Persia and meant to betray the city. Asked to agree outwardly, he refused; warned he would be burned, he fled that night (E4-E5). Left under a heresy charge: Widely Accepted. Scenes and speeches: the History's, Inferential-Thin. Year: Contested, 457 or after 471 (D1).
  5. **Kept at Nisibis.** He meant to go deeper into Persia to teach. Barsauma brought him into the city with honour, asked him to stay and found a school, and promised help. The Cause has Barsauma call the move providence, not accident (E6-E7). The request: Widely Accepted. The speech: the Cause's. In 489 the Persian school at Edessa was closed (S1, Documented). In 496 the school took written rules under him (E9).
  6. **Suspected on both sides.** He quarrelled with Barsauma, and they made peace (E10; cause Contested, D6). The History says he answered a rival preacher, Jacob of Serugh, with verse homilies of his own; Mari says the reverse (E12, D5). Later, the History says, students denounced him to the Persian king as an enemy of Persia (E13, Inferential-Thin). Suspected of Persia at Edessa, of Rome in Persia.
  7. **Death.** He died in the early 500s, after forty, forty-five or fifty years at Nisibis, as the sources count (E15, D2). Contested.

- **What came of them** (heaviest first; about a third of the story, much of it now told in beat 1 and deepened here):
  1. **The school lasted, and his household ran it.** The school names Barsauma and Narsai as its founders and says institutions from it stood "in several places" (I1). Its later heads included his kinsman Abraham, whom he had renamed and who shared his cell; the History says more than a thousand students gathered under Abraham (I4). The order of the first heads is Contested (D7): do not say who came next. Documented (the school and its heads); the thousand is the History's.
  2. **Its way of teaching the Bible was known abroad.** About 550, Junillus in Constantinople met a Persian, Paul, trained "in the school of the Syrians in the city of Nisibis", where God's law is taught by public teachers "in order and by rule", as grammar and rhetoric are taught among us (I2). Documented for the school. Junillus does not name Narsai.
  3. **His rules were kept and renewed.** The school swore to keep "the canons of Narsai"; in 602 it sought out the old rules and brought them back into force (I3, H6). Documented, Guidi's words.
  4. **His verse homilies were kept.** "More than three hundred" in the Cause; 360 in Abdisho's catalogue of the 1200s; forty-seven printed in 1905; four in the Library in English (H1, I5). Documented.
  5. **What he defended:** Theodore's way of reading Scripture (I6). **Defended.**
  6. **Two names.** His enemies called him "the Leper"; his friends, "the Harp of the Holy Spirit" (I7). "Leper": Documented (Simeon). "Harp": Widely Accepted as a name.

- **Quotes** (2 at most):
  1. **The school's verdict.** Rendered from Scher's French (Cause 20743-20746, I1; the Syriac OCR is unreadable): "Schools multiplied in the empire of the Persians. Edessa grew dark; Nisibis grew bright. The empire of the Romans filled with error, and that of the Persians with the knowledge of the fear of God." Job: the school's own boast about what Narsai began, made about ninety years later. Bring it in with its speaker named ("Barhadbeshabba, speaking to the school about ninety years later, put it this way"). Never the narrator's summary. Best in "What came of them".
  2. **His own words, on baptism.** Rendered from Connolly's English (Homilies 6857-6858, W1, Hom. XXII; the Syriac is not held): "In baptism he recast our image, as in a furnace. In place of our clay, he has made us spiritual gold." ("He" is God.) Job: lets the listener hear the poet-teacher, the kind of image he gave his students in verse.
  - Dropped: the accusers' question ("How has a Persian ... won over the Romans?"). It served as an opening; the new opening leads into the school, and the accusation is told in paraphrase.

- **Connection to carry into the story:** Narsai to the school at Nisibis, and through it to the Church of the East's later teachers. Simeon, his enemy, says Narsai set up the school at Nisibis (BO I 75018-75020). The Cause and the History say students came from Persia, from Syria and from Edessa (E8). The listener needs three things: Edessa was in the Roman Empire and Nisibis in Persia; the teaching his Edessa enemies condemned went on across the border; and it went on long after him, under heads from his own household, with rules kept in his name, and was known as far as Constantinople (I1-I4, H6). Carry it through acts: Barsauma's offer, students arriving, rules written, a kinsman taking his chair, a Persian student met abroad. Barsauma's motive only as the speeches give it (Cause 20689-20735): to do good everywhere, and to resist error.

- **Links:**
  - Barsauma of Nisibis: studied with him at Edessa; brought him into Nisibis and founded the school with him; quarrelled with him and made peace.
  - Abraham of Beth Rabban: his kinsman; he renamed him and shared his cell; later head of the school.
  - Barhadbeshabba 'Arbaia: wrote his life.
  - Theodore of Mopsuestia: read and taught his books.
  - Jacob of Serugh: rival preacher in verse.
  - Simeon of Beth Arsham: wrote against his party and called him "the Leper".
  - Henana of Adiabene: a later head of the school, whose followers, an editor's note says, cut hymns credited to Narsai.

- **Who tells it:**
  - Barhadbeshabba 'Arbaia (History, end of the 500s): same church and school, writing in praise, about a century later. Nearly every scene of the life is his. In the story: one short clause at the opening, and his name with Quote 1. The doubt is not the story.
  - The Cause (end of the 500s, perhaps the same writer): a speech to the school about its own founding; the school's pride, not an outside judgement.
  - Simeon of Beth Arsham: a younger contemporary and opponent. Name him with "the Leper", or as the enemy who still credits Narsai with the school.
  - Junillus (c. 550): an outsider writing in Latin. Name him with the "in order and by rule" line.
  - Narsai's own words come through R. H. Connolly's English of 1909.
  - Mari (1100s) and Bar Hebraeus (1200s, of the rival Syriac Orthodox church) wrote six or seven centuries later.

- **Do not say:**
  - "He was expelled in 457 with the Persian students." Say: he left Edessa under accusation; the year is uncertain, 457 or after 471 (D1).
  - "Narsai founded the school alone" or "Barsauma founded it and appointed him." Say: Barsauma asked him to found it and promised help, and the school counts both as its founders (D3).
  - "He trained the Church of the East's teachers / bishops / clergy" as a sweeping fact, or "every teacher of the church came from Nisibis". Say what is held: students came from Persia, Syria and Edessa; the school lasted over a century; Junillus met one of its students; the school said other schools grew from it.
  - "The school's library began with the books he sent." The sources do not say what became of the books.
  - "The school was founded in 457" or any founding year. Say: after he left Edessa (D1).
  - "Every day he sang his commentaries" as the History's, or as certain. Say: the school's own account says he gave daily commentaries with chant.
  - Any single year for his death, or "502-504" beside "after 471". Say: in the early 500s (D1-D2).
  - "Edessa grew dark; Nisibis grew bright", or "the Roman Empire filled with error", in the narrator's voice. Only as the Cause's words, with Barhadbeshabba named.
  - "He wrote his homilies to answer Jacob of Serugh" as fact. Say the History tells it so, and Mari says the reverse.
  - "Fifty years at Nisibis." Say: forty, forty-five or fifty, as the sources count (D2).
  - "The Edessa school closed, so its teachers joined him." Say both facts side by side, with no cause between them (E8, S1).
  - "He wrote the rules of 496." Say: the school took written rules in 496 while he was its head (E9).
  - "Nestorian" in the narrator's voice. Say "the Church of the East" and "followers of Theodore"; "Nestorian" only as the opponents' label.
  - "He was burned / nearly burned." Say: the History says he was warned he would be burned, and fled that night.
  - "360 homilies survive." Say: the Cause says more than three hundred, Abdisho's 1200s catalogue 360; forty-seven were printed in 1905.
  - "Barsauma's wife's jealousy caused the quarrel" as fact. If told, credit it to the History, and say Bar Hebraeus tells it another way (D6).
  - Adding a description of his face, voice, family or feelings beyond the sources.
  - Leaving out the charge at the Persian court, or the threat of fire. Say: the History tells that students later denounced him to the Persian king as an enemy of Persia. (Review.md, 2026-11-18.)

- **Leave out:**
  - The healing of the boy, the plague, the angels at his chair (E14; Mari): wonder stories from praising or late witnesses.
  - His childhood flight from the Magi and the back-and-forth to his uncle's monastery (S5, E1): one witness, a side road.
  - The camel stable (E8): the History's alone, and colour the heart does not need.
  - Mari's flight "to an island" (D4) and Theodulus's blessing (I7): late, and the dates strain.
  - The Chronicle of Arbela: authenticity contested.
  - Homily XVII and its authorship debate (D8).
  - Bar Hebraeus's "concubine": a hostile story needing more room than this has; if the quarrel's cause is told, both versions (Do not say).
  - Kept, not left out: the charges at Edessa and at the Persian court, and the threat of fire. These are the record's conflict and are told plainly.
