In the 400s, a teacher named Narsai fled the city of Edessa by night. Barhadbeshabba, a writer of his own church, tells it in praise about a century later. Narsai found some Persians at the city church, he says, and asked them to carry his books to Nisibis, for the books were all his treasure. Edessa lay in the Roman Empire. Nisibis lay just inside Persia, where Christians under the shahs formed the Church of the East.

Narsai was Persian-born, and had studied at Edessa's school for Persian students. There he and his fellow student Barsauma read Theodore of Mopsuestia's Bible commentaries, newly put from Greek into Syriac. Later he led that school for some twenty years, and its own accounts say he explained Scripture each day in commentaries set to chant.

His enemies called him a heretic, a follower of Theodore and Nestorius, and said he sided with Persia and wanted to betray the city. Barhadbeshabba writes that he refused to agree even outwardly and, warned he would be burned, fled that night. The year is uncertain: 457, or after 471.

He meant to go deeper into Persia, but Barsauma, now bishop of Nisibis, welcomed him with honour, asked him to found a school and promised help. Students came, the school's own account says, from Persia, from Syria, and most of those at Edessa. In 489, says his enemy Simeon, who called Narsai the Leper, Bishop Cyrus and the emperor Zeno rooted out the school at Edessa. Seven years later, with Narsai as its head, the school at Nisibis took written rules.

In Persia, too, Barhadbeshabba tells, students later denounced him to the king as an enemy of Persia. He died in the early 500s, after forty, forty-five or fifty years at Nisibis, as the sources count. His school counted Barsauma and Narsai as its founders. It went on training the Church of the East's teachers under his kinsman Abraham, and swore to keep the canons of Narsai. About ninety years later, Barhadbeshabba told the school: "Schools multiplied in the empire of the Persians. Edessa grew dark; Nisibis grew bright. The empire of the Romans filled with error, and that of the Persians with the knowledge of the fear of God."

His friends called him the Harp of the Holy Spirit.

One of his verse homilies says of God: "In baptism he recast our image, as in a furnace. In place of our clay, he has made us spiritual gold."
