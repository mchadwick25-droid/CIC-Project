# Outline: Arius

Sources by row number refer to `Source_Sheet.md` in this folder; file and lines are given there and repeated for the quotes.

- **Length:** about 2.5 to 3 minutes (about 400 words). Reason: a major figure, and this is the batch's hostile-source piece. The listener has to be told, at each turn, whose voice is speaking: Arius's own, his opponents', or his side's. That placing takes room the plain story does not.

- **Setting:**
  - Time: from about 318 or 319 (Robertson's dating; S4) to his death in 336 (Robertson; S8). Spoken: "the early three-hundreds", "a few years after the persecutions ended".
  - Place: Alexandria in Egypt, where he ran one of the city's churches, called Baucalis (S2, S3); later Palestine, after Alexander expelled him (epiphanius 45522-45524), where he wrote to Eusebius of Nicomedia before he had yet come to Nicomedia (epiphanius 45647-45650), Nicaea in 325 (E13), Constantinople at his death (E17). Spoken: "Alexandria", "the new capital, Constantinople".
  - Tradition: told within the Homoian churches, the census's "Homoian ('Arian') Christianity" (informal name "The Homoian Churches"). He comes before them: they took shape after his death, and their bishops refused to be called his followers (I2-I4). Spoken: "the churches their opponents called Arian".
  - Office: a presbyter, a parish priest under the bishop of Alexandria, who also taught the Scriptures (S1, S4, S5). Spoken: "a priest of Alexandria".

- **Thread:** He forced the church to say in its creed whether the Son of God had a beginning, and the creed's closing curses took up his circle's own phrases to reject them.

- **Shape:** Teacher or thinker.

- **Openings:**
  1. His own complaint to a friend, in his voice (it is his polemic, not the narrator's finding; see Do not say): "We are persecuted because we said: the Son has a beginning, but God has no beginning" (Q1). It lets him speak first, in a story his opponents mostly told.
  2. The record's own problem: the emperor ordered his books burned (E15), and nearly every word of his that survives was copied by men who wanted him condemned (I6). The record does not say the burning order is why the rest is lost; tell the two facts side by side. A listener can be asked how you hear a man whose enemies kept his letters.

- **Story beats:**
  1. **The priest and the question.** In Alexandria each presbyter ran his own church and preached to his own people (S3). Arius was one, and taught the Scriptures (S4, S5). Around 318 he clashed with Bishop Alexander over the Son of God. Socrates, writing about a century later, says Arius heard Alexander's teaching as Sabellian: that is, as making Father and Son one and the same. He answered that a son who is begotten must have a beginning (E1). Constantine's letter of 324 tells it differently: Alexander asked his priests about a passage of Scripture, and Arius answered rashly (E2). Confidence: Contested (how it began); Documented (that he was a presbyter there).
  2. **His own words.** He wrote to Eusebius, bishop of Nicomedia, his "fellow-Lucianist" (both had learned under the teacher Lucian of Antioch; S6). He says his bishop drove him out of the city "as atheists". He states his belief (Theodoret's English, npnf203 6041-6044; for the writer's paraphrase, not further quotes): the Son subsisted "before time, and before ages, as perfect God, only begotten and unchangeable", and "before He was begotten ... He was not". "We are persecuted because we said: the Son has a beginning, but God has no beginning" (E7). What was at stake for him, in the letter he and his fellow presbyters and deacons sent to Alexander (npnf204 57094-57096): God alone is without beginning; to make the Son eternal alongside him would bring in two unbegotten first principles, and to make the Son a part of the Father would make the bodiless God compound and divisible, like a body (paraphrase, not quotes; E8; epiphanius 45899-45912; npnf204 57166-57196). Confidence: Documented.
  3. **Deposed, and singing.** Alexander's council removed him with other priests, deacons and two bishops from Libya, and Alexander's letter called them "enemies of Christ" (E6). Arius set his teaching in a verse book, the Thalia (the name, the editor notes, belonged to songs for feasts; npnf204 57023). It opens in his own voice (Q2). Athanasius, who copied the lines, mocked the tune as "effeminate" (E9, E11). Philostorgius, a later historian on Arius's side, says he wrote songs for sailors, millers and travellers (E12). Confidence: Documented (the Thalia's opening); Contested (the songs, which survive only in Photius's hostile summary).
  4. **Nicaea, 325.** The emperor Constantine called a council of bishops at Nicaea (epiphanius 46220-46224). It condemned his opinions, the emperor sent him into exile, and he was barred from Alexandria (E13). He was not condemned alone: the council's letter says the bishops Theonas of Marmarica and Secundus of Ptolemais suffered the same condemnation (npnf202 3196-3199). The creed closed with curses on anyone who said "there was a time when the Son of God was not", "before he was begotten he was not" or "he was made of things that were not", or that he is "a creature" or "subject to change" (E14). Eusebius of Caesarea, who signed, wrote home that these curses banned phrases such as "of things which do not exist" and "there was a time when he was not" (E20). Constantine then ordered any book by Arius burned, with death for anyone who hid one (E15). Confidence: Documented (the decisions and the order; not how fully the order was carried out).
  5. **Recalled.** Later the emperor received him again. He and the deacon Euzoius handed in a short creed in Scripture's words, without the disputed phrases, and asked to be "reunited to our mother, the Church" (E16). Socrates calls this pretence; that judgment is Socrates's. Confidence: Documented (the document).
  6. **Constantinople, 336.** Arius swore to the emperor that his faith was right, and his allies, led by Eusebius of Nicomedia, wanted him received into communion in the city's church. Bishop Alexander of Constantinople ("aged" is the editor's word, npnf204 4869) refused, saying "the inventor of the heresy ought not to be admitted". Eusebius's party threatened to bring him in anyway: "to-morrow, though it be contrary to your desire, Arius shall have communion with us" (npnf204 70079-70085; these are Athanasius's words for both sides, for the writer's paraphrase, not further quotes). The next day was a Sunday (epiphanius 46099; npnf202 5520-5522). The bishop lay on the floor of the church and prayed that God would take either Arius or himself (npnf204 70087-70098, 4870-4871). Before that Sunday came, Arius withdrew to relieve himself and died (E17). Athanasius tells the death in the words the Bible uses for the death of Judas, who betrayed Jesus: "falling headlong he burst asunder in the midst" (npnf204 70104-70106; also 34525-34527 and 39556). He calls it a sign that the Arian heresy is "hateful in the sight of God" (70060-70063). He had it from the presbyter Macarius, and says he hesitated to write it lest he seem to gloat (70051-70053). Tell it as his account, in his words, credited to him. Sozomen gives three views: a sudden failure of the heart, God's judgment, or, among his own followers, sorcery (E19). Confidence: Documented (sudden death the day before his reception); the cause is Contested and stays in each witness's voice.

- **What came of them** (heaviest first; about a third of the story):
  1. **The creed's curses take up his circle's phrases.** Two of the curses repeat his letter's phrases, "before he was begotten he was not" and "of things that were not" (E14 against E7; I1). A third, "there was a time when he was not", is the slogan his opponents and the council's letter give him, close to the Thalia's "once he was not" as Athanasius quotes it (E10, E13). The curses on "a creature" and "subject to change" condemn what his opponents charged him with; his own letters call the Son "unchangeable" and "a perfect creature of God, but not as one of the creatures" (E7, E8). Socrates says the council cursed "the very expressions of his tenets" (npnf202 3261-3263), and Eusebius of Caesarea names two of them (E20). Documented.
  2. **His name, refused by those it was pinned on.** "Arian" became his opponents' word for everyone who would not accept Nicaea's teaching. The bishops it named would not take it. At Antioch in 341 they wrote, "How could bishops follow a priest?", and added that they had examined his faith and "admitted him rather than followed him" (Q3; I2; npnf204 57421-57422). Athanasius answered that they wrote so because they were "in general and lasting odium for their heresy" (npnf204 57413-57414). Both claims stay in their owners' voices. Auxentius, bishop of Milan, wrote in 364 that he had never known Arius, never seen him, and did not know his teaching (I3). At Aquileia in 381, when Ambrose had Arius's letter read out, the bishops Palladius and Secundianus said they did not know Arius or what he had said (I4). The council cursed them and deposed them both (I4). Give these as paraphrase, not further quotes. What they said they defended was their own faith, which they said came "from the beginning" (I2). Documented.
  3. **His voice survives only in his opponents' books.** His letters and verse come to us because Theodoret, Epiphanius and Athanasius copied them. Each says why: Theodoret, so "the names of those who were implicated" would be known; Epiphanius, to show that nothing had been said against him falsely; Athanasius quotes the Thalia to mock it (I6, E9). No held text says the burning order is why nothing else survives. Documented.
  4. **Even the one historian on his side found fault.** Philostorgius, born nearly thirty years after Arius died (philostorgius 36), praised him, in Photius's words, "to the skies", yet faulted him for teaching that God cannot be known, even by the Son (I5; E10). Contested (it reaches us through Photius).
  5. **The quarrel outlived him** (I8). Documented.
  - **What he defended** (marked): that God alone is without beginning, and that the bodiless Father is not made compound or divisible by having a Son (E8; beat 2). He held to it under deposition and exile; his last creed to Constantine kept to Scripture's words and left out the disputed phrases (E16).

- **Quotes, 2 or 3 at most:**
  1. **Q1.** Rendered from the Greek of Arius's letter to Eusebius, in Epiphanius, Panarion 69.6 (`epiphanius_panarion-haer-48-69-corpus-haereseologicum-t2-pars-posterior-grc-lat_oehler1860.txt`, lines 45736-45738), checked against Theodoret's English (`npnf203_theodoret-jerome-gennadius-rufinus.xml`, lines 6045-6046).
     Greek: "Διωκόμεθα δὲ ὅτι εἴπομεν, Ἀρχὴν ἔχει ὁ υἱὸς, ὁ δὲ θεὸς ἄναρχός ἐστι."
     Rendering: **"We are persecuted because we said: the Son has a beginning, but God has no beginning."**
     Job: Arius speaks for himself, in his own complaint, before any opponent describes him. It states his whole idea in one line.
  2. **Q2.** Rendered from the opening of the Thalia as Athanasius quotes it, Against the Arians 1.5 (`npnf204_athanasius-select-works-letters.xml`, lines 41472-41479; the rendering rests on this), compared with the Greek of `athanasius_opera-omnia-pg26-grc-lat_migne1857.txt`, lines 1189-1194 and 1221-1225 (OCR; the file marks it a second witness; not checked against the page image).
     Greek: "Τούτων κατ' ἴχνος ἦλθον ἐγὼ βαίνων ὁμοδόξως ὁ περικλυτὸς, ὁ πολλὰ παθὼν διὰ τὴν Θεοῦ δόξαν, ὑπό τε Θεοῦ μαθὼν σοφίαν καὶ γνῶσιν ἐγὼ ἔγνων."
     Rendering: **"I have walked in their footsteps, sharing their belief. I am the famous one, who has suffered much for the glory of God. Taught by God, I have come to know wisdom and knowledge."**
     Set-up for the writer: "their" means the teachers named just before, in paraphrase "God's chosen, wise and taught by God" (npnf204 41461-41470); give that before the quote. Job: his voice as a teacher, proud and wounded, and the listener hears that it reaches us only because Athanasius copied it to mock it.
  3. **Q3.** Rendered from the Greek of the eastern bishops' letter from Antioch, 341, in Socrates, Church History 2.10 (`socrates_historia-ecclesiastica-tomus1-grc-lat_hussey1853.txt`, lines 16656-16657), checked against Athanasius, De Synodis 22 (`npnf204_athanasius-select-works-letters.xml`, lines 57418-57419).
     Greek: "Ἡμεῖς οὔτε ἀκόλουθοι Ἀρείου γεγόναμεν. πῶς γὰρ ἐπίσκοποι ὄντες ἀκολουθήσομεν πρεσβυτέρῳ;"
     Rendering: **"We have not been followers of Arius. For how could we, who are bishops, follow a priest?"**
     Job: the turn in the legacy. The side his name was pinned on speaks for itself and refuses it.

- **Connection to carry into the story:** The council of Nicaea took two phrases Arius and his circle used, "before he was begotten he was not" and "he is from things that were not" (his letter, E7), and wrote them into the end of its creed as teachings the church curses (E14). The council's own letter says it condemned "all the blasphemous expressions he has uttered" (E13). The listener needs to understand that the creed's last lines answer him directly: two of his circle's own phrases are inside the creed, as what it denies. The other curses, on "a creature" and "subject to change", condemn what his opponents said he taught. The second thing the listener needs: "Arian" was his opponents' name for them. The eastern bishops at Antioch in 341, whom Athanasius counted as Arians, said they had examined his faith and received him, but did not follow him as bishops follow a priest (Q3, I2). Later Homoian bishops said they did not know him: Auxentius of Milan, that he had never known Arius, never seen him, nor known his teaching (I3); Palladius and Secundianus at Aquileia, that they did not know who Arius was or what he had said (I4). Athanasius answered that they said so because the name was hated. Both claims stay in their owners' voices; and at Aquileia the council deposed the two bishops who would not condemn him. Carry both through what people did: the council writing its curses, the bishops writing their refusal.

- **Links:**
  - Alexander of Alexandria: his bishop, who deposed him (E6); Arius wrote to him (E8).
  - Eusebius of Nicomedia: wrote to him (E7); fellow pupil of Lucian; pressed for his return (E17).
  - Lucian of Antioch: learned from him, by the title "fellow-Lucianist" (S6).
  - Constantine: wrote to him (E2); ordered his books burned (E15); recalled him (E16, E17).
  - Council of Nicaea: condemned him (E13, E14).
  - Athanasius of Alexandria: quoted his book (E9); wrote of his death (E17).
  - Epiphanius, Theodoret: kept his letters (E7, E8).
  - Socrates, Sozomen: told his life (E1, E18, E19).
  - Philostorgius: wrote his history from his side (E3, E12, I5).
  - Auxentius of Milan, Palladius and Secundianus: said they did not know him (I3, I4).

- **Who tells it:**
  - Arius himself: two letters, the Thalia's opening and the creed he gave Constantine, written between about 318 and his death. Every copy is held by an opponent.
  - Alexander of Alexandria: his bishop and accuser, in his own time.
  - Constantine: the emperor, in his own time; first a peacemaker who called the question trifling, then the man who ordered his books burned.
  - Athanasius: his fiercest opponent, writing in the 350s; quotes the Thalia to mock it; was not in Constantinople at the death and says so.
  - Epiphanius: a hunter of heresies, writing about 375; keeps his letters and gives a hostile portrait.
  - Socrates (his history stops in 439; npnf202 1265), Sozomen and Theodoret: church historians about a century on, all on the Nicene side.
  - Philostorgius: the one historian on Arius's side; his history ran to 425 (Walford's notice, philostorgius 36). His book is lost; we have only a summary by Photius, a patriarch of the 800s, who calls him a liar. Even praise of Arius reaches us through an opponent.
  - The listener must know, at the death and at the Thalia, that the teller wanted him condemned.

- **Do not say:**
  - "Arius founded the Arian church" or "the Homoians followed Arius" → "his opponents named the movement after him; the Antioch bishops of 341 said they did not follow him; later Homoian bishops said they did not know him."
  - "The Homoians had nothing to do with Arius" → "the Antioch bishops said they received him but did not follow him"; Athanasius said they disowned the name because it was hated.
  - "Every curse in the creed was a phrase of Arius's" or "Arius taught that the Son could change" → "his own letters call the Son unchangeable; Athanasius and the council's letter say he taught the Son could change."
  - "Arius was persecuted" or "driven out as an atheist" in the narrator's voice → "he wrote that his bishop was persecuting him and had driven him out 'as atheists'"; Alexander's letter calls Arius's party "enemies of Christ". His letter also calls his opponents "heretics" and "unlearned men".
  - "Eusebius of Nicomedia and Theognis were exiled with him after Nicaea" → leave out; Socrates says so, but the editor says other witnesses put their exile later and judges Socrates wrong (npnf202 1484-1487).
  - "318 bishops" or any count of bishops at Nicaea → no count; Epiphanius's "CCCXVIII" is a traditional number, not a roll.
  - "Alexander prayed that God would take him or Arius first" → "prayed that God would take either Arius or himself".
  - "Arius taught that Jesus was just a man" or "an ordinary creature" → his own words: "a perfect creature of God, but not as one of the creatures", existing "before time, and before ages" (E7, E8).
  - "God struck him down" or "he died by God's judgment" in the narrator's voice → "Athanasius saw God's judgment in it"; or give Sozomen's three views.
  - "He burst open in the street" or other gore from Socrates (E18) → Athanasius's account, which uses the Judas words; give it as his, and add no gore beyond it.
  - "Athanasius saw him die" → "Athanasius was not there; he had the account from the presbyter Macarius."
  - "He was jealous that Alexander became bishop" → no motive. Theodoret says jealousy, a century later; Philostorgius says the opposite; the editor calls such tales invention (E4).
  - "He first said 'there was a time when the Son was not' to Alexander's face" as fact → "Socrates, a century later, tells it that way"; or use his own letter's words.
  - "Sailors and millers sang the Thalia" → Philostorgius speaks of "songs" for sailors, millers and travellers, not of the Thalia, and only through Photius's summary.
  - "All his books were burned" → "the emperor ordered them burned"; the record gives the order, not the result.
  - "Seven hundred virgins followed him" as fact, or his "tall, gloomy, snake-like" look as plain description → only as Epiphanius's hostile account, fifty years later; better left out.
  - "Arius was born in Libya in 256" or any birth year → no year is held; Libya is only "they say" (Epiphanius).
  - "In 336" stated as certain → "in 336, by the usual dating" is fine; the year is the editor's.
  - "Arius argued at the council" or invented speeches at Nicaea → the record says his opinions were examined before the emperor and condemned; give no scene.
  - "Presbyter" without help → "a priest" or "a parish priest", ranking below the bishop.
  - "Arius recanted" → he and Euzoius gave a creed in Scripture's words; Socrates calls it pretence; that is Socrates's judgment.
  - "His allies pressed for (or asked for) his reception" with no refusal or threat → "the bishop refused to admit him; Eusebius's party threatened to bring him to communion the next day against the bishop's wishes (Athanasius's account)." (added from Review.md, 2026-11-18)

- **Leave out:**
  - Epiphanius's portrait and the 700 virgins: hostile caricature; it would take the story's room to place it fairly.
  - Socrates's description of the death in detail, and the later tale of the shunned seat (Sozomen): late, lurid, adds nothing that is secure.
  - Epiphanius's order of events, which puts the death before Nicaea: in conflict with every other witness.
  - Baucalis as a nickname (Philostorgius): a side puzzle.
  - The Melitian schism and Arius's earlier career under Peter: tales the editor judges invented; side story.
  - Fine points of the Thalia's metre and the Sotades comparison beyond one word.
  - Never leave out the force: the deposition, the exile, the burning order with its death penalty, and Athanasius's open contempt. These stay, told plainly and credited.
