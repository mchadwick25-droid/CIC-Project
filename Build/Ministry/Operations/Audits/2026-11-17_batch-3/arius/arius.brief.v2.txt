"We are persecuted because we said: the Son has a beginning, but God has no beginning." Arius wrote that to a friend in the early three-hundreds. Nearly every word of his that survives was copied by men who wanted him condemned.

He was a priest of Alexandria in Egypt, with a church of his own under the city's bishop, Alexander. Around 318 the two clashed over the Son of God. Socrates, writing a century later, says Arius heard his bishop making Father and Son one and the same, and replied that a begotten son must have a beginning.

His friend was Eusebius, bishop of Nicomedia. Arius told him that his bishop had driven him out "as atheists". The Son, he wrote, existed before time, as perfect God, unchanging, yet before he was begotten, he was not. For Arius, God alone has no beginning, and a Son eternal beside him would mean two first principles without beginning.

Alexander's council removed him, calling his party "enemies of Christ", and Arius set his teaching in verse. His Thalia praises earlier teachers, wise and taught by God, then turns to himself: "I have walked in their footsteps, sharing their belief. I am the famous one, who has suffered much for the glory of God. Taught by God, I have come to know wisdom and knowledge." We have those lines only because his fiercest opponent, Athanasius, copied them to mock them.

In 325 the emperor Constantine called the bishops to Nicaea, where the council condemned Arius's opinions and the emperor exiled him. Its creed ended by cursing anyone who said "before he was begotten he was not", or that the Son came "of things that were not". Those were his circle's own phrases, written into the creed as what the church denies. Constantine then ordered his books burned, with death for anyone who hid one.

His opponents called everyone who rejected Nicaea "Arian", but the bishops it named refused the name. At Antioch in 341 they wrote: "We have not been followers of Arius. For how could we, who are bishops, follow a priest?" Athanasius answered that they disowned the name because it was hated. Forty years later, at Aquileia, two bishops said they did not know who Arius was, and the council deposed them both.

Arius did not live to see any of that. The emperor took him back, and his allies in the new capital, Constantinople, pressed to restore him to communion.

The bishop there lay on the church floor and prayed that God would take either Arius or himself. Athanasius, who was not there, told what followed in the Bible's words for the death of Judas, and saw God's judgment in it. On the day before he was to be received, Arius withdrew to relieve himself and died.
