# Outline: Arius

Sources by row number refer to `Source_Sheet.md` in this folder; file and lines are given there and repeated for the quotes.

- **Length:** about 2.5 to 3 minutes (about 400 words). Reason: a major figure, and this is the batch's hostile-source piece. The listener has to be told, at each turn, whose voice is speaking: Arius's own, his opponents', or his side's. That placing takes room the plain story does not.

- **Setting:**
  - Time: from about 318 or 319 (Robertson's dating; S4) to his death in 336 (Robertson; S8). Spoken: "the early three-hundreds", "a few years after the persecutions ended".
  - Place: Alexandria in Egypt, where he ran one of the city's churches, called Baucalis (S2, S3); later Palestine and Nicomedia in exile (epiphanius 45641-45650), Nicaea in 325 (E13), Constantinople at his death (E17). Spoken: "Alexandria", "the new capital, Constantinople".
  - Tradition: told within the Homoian churches, the census's "Homoian ('Arian') Christianity" (informal name "The Homoian Churches"). He comes before them: they took shape after his death, and their bishops refused to be called his followers (I2-I4). Spoken: "the churches their opponents called Arian".
  - Office: a presbyter, a parish priest under the bishop of Alexandria, who also taught the Scriptures (S1, S4, S5). Spoken: "a priest of Alexandria".

- **Thread:** He forced the church to say in its creed whether the Son of God had a beginning, and the creed answered him in his own words.

- **Shape:** Teacher or thinker.

- **Openings:**
  1. His own complaint to a friend: "We are persecuted because we said: the Son has a beginning, but God has no beginning" (Q1). It lets him speak first, in a story his opponents mostly told.
  2. The record's own problem: the emperor ordered his books burned (E15), and nearly every word of his that survives was copied by men who wanted him condemned (I6). A listener can be asked how you hear a man whose enemies kept his letters.

- **Story beats:**
  1. **The priest and the question.** In Alexandria each presbyter ran his own church and preached to his own people (S3). Arius was one, and taught the Scriptures (S4, S5). Around 318 he clashed with Bishop Alexander over the Son of God. Socrates, writing about a century later, says Arius heard Alexander's teaching as Sabellian: that is, as making Father and Son one and the same. He answered that a son who is begotten must have a beginning (E1). Constantine's letter of 324 tells it differently: Alexander asked his priests about a passage of Scripture, and Arius answered rashly (E2). Confidence: Contested (how it began); Documented (that he was a presbyter there).
  2. **His own words.** He wrote to Eusebius, bishop of Nicomedia, his "fellow-Lucianist" (both had learned under the teacher Lucian of Antioch; S6). He says his bishop drove him out of the city "as atheists". He states his belief (Theodoret's English, npnf203 6041-6044; for the writer's paraphrase, not further quotes): the Son subsisted "before time, and before ages, as perfect God, only begotten and unchangeable", and "before He was begotten ... He was not". "We are persecuted because we said: the Son has a beginning, but God has no beginning" (E7). What was at stake for him, in his own letter to Alexander: God alone is without beginning; to make the Son eternal alongside him would bring in two unbegotten first principles, and to make the Son a part of the Father would make the bodiless God compound and divisible, like a body (paraphrase, not quotes; E8; epiphanius 45899-45912; npnf204 57166-57196). Confidence: Documented.
  3. **Deposed, and singing.** Alexander's council removed him with other priests, deacons and two bishops from Libya, and Alexander's letter called them "enemies of Christ" (E6). Arius set his teaching in a verse book, the Thalia (the name, the editor notes, belonged to songs for feasts; npnf204 57023). It opens in his own voice (Q2). Athanasius, who copied the lines, mocked the tune as "effeminate" (E9, E11). Philostorgius, a later historian on Arius's side, says he wrote songs for sailors, millers and travellers (E12). Confidence: Documented (the Thalia's opening); Contested (the songs, which survive only in Photius's hostile summary).
  4. **Nicaea, 325.** The emperor Constantine called a council of bishops at Nicaea (epiphanius 46220-46224). It condemned his opinions, the emperor sent him into exile, and he was barred from Alexandria (E13). The creed closed with curses on anyone who said "there was a time when the Son of God was not", "before he was begotten he was not" or "he was made of things that were not" (E14). Constantine then ordered any book by Arius burned, with death for anyone who hid one (E15). Confidence: Documented (the decisions and the order; not how fully the order was carried out).
  5. **Recalled.** Later the emperor received him again. He and the deacon Euzoius handed in a short creed in Scripture's words, without the disputed phrases, and asked to be "reunited to our mother, the Church" (E16). Socrates calls this pretence; that judgment is Socrates's. Confidence: Documented (the document).
  6. **Constantinople, 336.** Arius swore to the emperor that his faith was right, and his allies, led by Eusebius of Nicomedia, set the next day, a Sunday, for him to receive communion in the city's church. Bishop Alexander of Constantinople ("aged" is the editor's word, npnf204 4869) lay on the floor of the church and prayed that God would take either him or Arius first. Before that Sunday came, Arius withdrew to relieve himself and died there (E17; Sunday from Epiphanius, epiphanius 46099, and Socrates, npnf202 5520-5522). Athanasius tells it as God's judgment, from the report of the presbyter Macarius, and says he hesitated to write it lest he seem to gloat. Sozomen gives three views: a sudden failure of the heart, God's judgment, or, among his own followers, sorcery (E19). Confidence: Documented (sudden death the day before his reception); the cause is Contested and stays in each witness's voice.

- **What came of them** (heaviest first; about a third of the story):
  1. **The creed answers him in his words.** The curses that close the creed of Nicaea repeat his phrases, "before he was begotten he was not", "of things that were not", and set them down as what the church rejects (E14 against E7; I1). The creed's last lines keep his teaching inside it, as the thing denied. Documented.
  2. **His name, refused by his own side.** "Arian" became his opponents' word for everyone who would not accept Nicaea's teaching. The bishops it named would not take it. At Antioch in 341 they wrote, "How could bishops follow a priest?" (Q3; I2). Auxentius, bishop of Milan, wrote in 364 that he had never known Arius, never seen him, and did not know his teaching (I3). At Aquileia in 381, when Ambrose had Arius's letter read out, the bishops Palladius and Secundianus said they did not know Arius or what he had said (I4). Give these as paraphrase, not further quotes. What they defended was not Arius but their own faith, which they said came "from the beginning" (I2). Documented.
  3. **His voice survives only in his opponents' books.** After the burning order, his letters and verse come to us because Theodoret, Epiphanius and Athanasius copied them, so their readers could see his error for themselves (I6). Documented.
  4. **Even his friend found fault.** Philostorgius praised him "to the skies", yet faulted him for teaching that God cannot be known, even by the Son (I5; E10). Contested (it reaches us through Photius).
  5. **The quarrel outlived him** (I8). Documented.

- **Quotes, 2 or 3 at most:**
  1. **Q1.** Rendered from the Greek of Arius's letter to Eusebius, in Epiphanius, Panarion 69.6 (`epiphanius_panarion-haer-48-69-corpus-haereseologicum-t2-pars-posterior-grc-lat_oehler1860.txt`, lines 45736-45738), checked against Theodoret's English (`npnf203_theodoret-jerome-gennadius-rufinus.xml`, lines 6045-6046).
     Greek: "Διωκόμεθα δὲ ὅτι εἴπομεν, Ἀρχὴν ἔχει ὁ υἱὸς, ὁ δὲ θεὸς ἄναρχός ἐστι."
     Rendering: **"We are persecuted because we said: the Son has a beginning, but God has no beginning."**
     Job: Arius speaks for himself, in his own complaint, before any opponent describes him. It states his whole idea in one line.
  2. **Q2.** Rendered from the opening of the Thalia as Athanasius quotes it, Against the Arians 1.5 (`npnf204_athanasius-select-works-letters.xml`, lines 41472-41479), compared with the Greek of `athanasius_opera-omnia-pg26-grc-lat_migne1857.txt`, lines 1222-1225 (OCR; the file marks it a second witness).
     Greek: "κατ' ἴχνος ἦλθον ἐγὼ βαίνων ὁμοδόξως ὁ περικλυτὸς, ὁ πολλὰ παθὼν διὰ τὴν Θεοῦ δόξαν, ὑπό τε Θεοῦ μαθὼν σοφίαν καὶ γνῶσιν ἐγὼ ἔγνων."
     Rendering: **"I have walked in their footsteps, sharing their belief. I am the famous one, who has suffered much for the glory of God. Taught by God, I have come to know wisdom and knowledge."**
     Job: his voice as a teacher, proud and wounded, and the listener hears that it reaches us only because Athanasius copied it to mock it.
  3. **Q3.** Rendered from the Greek of the eastern bishops' letter from Antioch, 341, in Socrates, Church History 2.10 (`socrates_historia-ecclesiastica-tomus1-grc-lat_hussey1853.txt`, lines 16656-16657), checked against Athanasius, De Synodis 22 (`npnf204_athanasius-select-works-letters.xml`, lines 57418-57419).
     Greek: "Ἡμεῖς οὔτε ἀκόλουθοι Ἀρείου γεγόναμεν. πῶς γὰρ ἐπίσκοποι ὄντες ἀκολουθήσομεν πρεσβυτέρῳ;"
     Rendering: **"We have not been followers of Arius. For how could we, who are bishops, follow a priest?"**
     Job: the turn in the legacy. The side his name was pinned on speaks for itself and refuses it.

- **Connection to carry into the story:** The council of Nicaea took the phrases Arius and his circle used, "before he was begotten he was not" and "he is from things that were not" (his letter, E7), and wrote them into the end of its creed as teachings the church curses (E14). The council's own letter says it condemned "all the blasphemous expressions he has uttered" (E13). The listener needs to understand that the creed's last lines are a direct answer to him: his words are inside the creed, as what it denies. The second thing the listener needs: "Arian" was his opponents' name. The eastern bishops at Antioch in 341, whom Athanasius counted as Arians, and later the Homoian bishops Auxentius, Palladius and Secundianus, said plainly they did not follow him (Q3, I3, I4). So the movement told within this tradition did not inherit his teaching from him as its founder. It was named after him by the other side. Carry both through what people did: the council writing its curses, the bishops writing their refusal.

- **Links:**
  - Alexander of Alexandria: his bishop, who deposed him (E6); Arius wrote to him (E8).
  - Eusebius of Nicomedia: wrote to him (E7); fellow pupil of Lucian; pressed for his return (E17).
  - Lucian of Antioch: learned from him, by the title "fellow-Lucianist" (S6).
  - Constantine: wrote to him (E2); ordered his books burned (E15); recalled him (E16, E17).
  - Council of Nicaea: condemned him (E13, E14).
  - Athanasius of Alexandria: quoted his book (E9); wrote of his death (E17).
  - Epiphanius, Theodoret: kept his letters (E7, E8).
  - Socrates, Sozomen: told his life (E1, E18, E19).
  - Philostorgius: wrote his history from his side (E3, E12, I5).
  - Auxentius of Milan, Palladius and Secundianus: refused his name (I3, I4).

- **Who tells it:**
  - Arius himself: two letters, the Thalia's opening and the creed he gave Constantine, written between about 318 and his death. Every copy is held by an opponent.
  - Alexander of Alexandria: his bishop and accuser, in his own time.
  - Constantine: the emperor, in his own time; first a peacemaker who called the question trifling, then the man who ordered his books burned.
  - Athanasius: his fiercest opponent, writing in the 350s; quotes the Thalia to mock it; was not in Constantinople at the death and says so.
  - Epiphanius: a hunter of heresies, writing about 375; keeps his letters and gives a hostile portrait.
  - Socrates (his history stops in 439; npnf202 1265), Sozomen and Theodoret: church historians about a century on, all on the Nicene side.
  - Philostorgius: the one historian on Arius's side; his history ran to 425 (Walford's notice, philostorgius 36). His book is lost; we have only a summary by Photius, a patriarch of the 800s, who calls him a liar. Even praise of Arius reaches us through an opponent.
  - The listener must know, at the death and at the Thalia, that the teller wanted him condemned.

- **Do not say:**
  - "Arius founded the Arian church" or "the Homoians followed Arius" → "his opponents named the movement after him; its bishops said they did not follow him."
  - "Arius taught that Jesus was just a man" or "an ordinary creature" → his own words: "a perfect creature of God, but not as one of the creatures", existing "before time, and before ages" (E7, E8).
  - "God struck him down" or "he died by God's judgment" in the narrator's voice → "Athanasius saw God's judgment in it"; or give Sozomen's three views.
  - "He burst open in the street" or other gore from Socrates (E18) → Athanasius's plainer account, from Macarius, and only as his report.
  - "Athanasius saw him die" → "Athanasius was not there; he had the account from the presbyter Macarius."
  - "He was jealous that Alexander became bishop" → no motive. Theodoret says jealousy, a century later; Philostorgius says the opposite; the editor calls such tales invention (E4).
  - "He first said 'there was a time when the Son was not' to Alexander's face" as fact → "Socrates, a century later, tells it that way"; or use his own letter's words.
  - "Sailors and millers sang the Thalia" → Philostorgius speaks of "songs" for sailors, millers and travellers, not of the Thalia, and only through Photius's summary.
  - "All his books were burned" → "the emperor ordered them burned"; the record gives the order, not the result.
  - "Seven hundred virgins followed him" as fact, or his "tall, gloomy, snake-like" look as plain description → only as Epiphanius's hostile account, fifty years later; better left out.
  - "Arius was born in Libya in 256" or any birth year → no year is held; Libya is only "they say" (Epiphanius).
  - "In 336" stated as certain → "in 336, by the usual dating" is fine; the year is the editor's.
  - "Arius argued at the council" or invented speeches at Nicaea → the record says his opinions were examined before the emperor and condemned; give no scene.
  - "Presbyter" without help → "a priest" or "a parish priest", ranking below the bishop.
  - "Arius recanted" → he and Euzoius gave a creed in Scripture's words; Socrates calls it pretence; that is Socrates's judgment.

- **Leave out:**
  - Epiphanius's portrait and the 700 virgins: hostile caricature; it would take the story's room to place it fairly.
  - Socrates's description of the death in detail, and the later tale of the shunned seat (Sozomen): late, lurid, adds nothing that is secure.
  - Epiphanius's order of events, which puts the death before Nicaea: in conflict with every other witness.
  - Baucalis as a nickname (Philostorgius): a side puzzle.
  - The Melitian schism and Arius's earlier career under Peter: tales the editor judges invented; side story.
  - Fine points of the Thalia's metre and the Sotades comparison beyond one word.
  - Never leave out the force: the deposition, the exile, the burning order with its death penalty, and Athanasius's open contempt. These stay, told plainly and credited.
