# Outline: Arius

Sources by row number refer to `Source_Sheet.md` in this folder; file and lines are given there and repeated for the quotes.

- **The heart of their life:**
  - **What he taught.** Arius, a parish priest of Alexandria, taught that God alone is without beginning. The Son was begotten by the Father "before time, and before ages", as "perfect God", yet "before he was begotten ... he was not" (E7). In his own words, the Son "is not eternal or co-eternal" with the Father (N1). His reason: to make the Son eternal beside the Father would bring in two beings without beginning, and to make him a part of the Father would make the bodiless God divisible, like a body (E8, N1). His bishop, Alexander, preached "God always, the Son always" (E7, as Arius reports it). Documented.
  - **Why it divided the church.** The question was whether the Son is God in the same full sense as the Father, or a lesser being the Father made before all else. Each side held that the other dishonoured God: Alexander's council called Arius's party "enemies of Christ" and deposed them (E6); Arius wrote that he was persecuted for the truth (E7). By 324 the emperor Constantine wrote that "the holy people, rent into diverse parties, no longer preserved the unity of the one body" (N2). Documented.
  - **Nicaea's answer.** In 325 the council said in its creed that the Son is "begotten, not made", "of one substance with the Father", and "very God of very God". It cursed phrases his circle used, and the emperor exiled him (N3, E13, E14). Documented.
  - **What became of his teaching.** It did not end with him (I8). The bishops his opponents called Arian refused the name (I2). Their creed of 360 dropped Nicaea's word "substance" as unscriptural and said the Son is "like the Father" as the Scriptures teach (N4). For a time this was the faith the emperors backed (N5, N6, N7). Ulfila took it to the Goths, and the Goths passed it to "all people of their speech" (N6, N7). Its core, the Father "alone unbegotten" and the Son "subject and obedient in all things" to him, stands in Ulfila's own creed (N8). In 589, at Toledo, the Gothic bishops of Spain gave up what they called "the faith and communion that came from Arius" (N11). Documented, each in its witness's words.
  - **What the sources will not hold.** That the later churches "held with him that the Son was not eternal" (the census wording) is not in any held Homoian text in those words (N12): Inferential-Thin as phrased. What they do hold, Documented: the Father alone unbegotten and everlasting (N8, N10), the Son made by him and subject to him (N8, N9), and "like" him (N4). Whether this was Arius's teaching is Contested: its own bishops said they did not follow him (I2); its opponents, and at last the Gothic bishops of 589, called it his (N5-N7, N11).
  - **To be told in the first minute** (about 150 words): his teaching in his own words, the split it made, Nicaea's answer, and one sentence that his teaching lived on, in later forms and under his opponents' name, as the faith of emperors for a time and of the Gothic peoples for two centuries. The rest of the story deepens each of these in time order.

- **Length:** about 3 minutes, about 430 words, no more than 450. Reason: a major figure whose story runs past his death. It carries two arcs, the man and the churches named for him, and the listener must be told at the turns whose voice is speaking, since nearly all of it comes from his opponents. Room is found by cutting the death to about 45 words and leaving out Aquileia, the Thalia quote and Philostorgius (see Leave out).

- **Setting:**
  - Time: from about 318 or 319 (Robertson's dating; S4) to his death in 336 (Robertson; S8); the story then follows his teaching to 341 (I2), 359-360 (N4, N5), the 370s (N6, N7) and 589 (N11). Spoken: "the early three-hundreds", "a few years after the persecutions ended".
  - Place: Alexandria in Egypt, where he ran one of the city's churches (S2, S3); Nicaea in 325 (E13); Constantinople at his death (E17); then the lands of the Goths beyond the Danube (N6, N7) and Spain (N11). Spoken: "Alexandria", "the new capital, Constantinople".
  - Tradition: told within the Homoian churches, the census's "Homoian ('Arian') Christianity" (informal name "The Homoian Churches"). He comes before them: they took shape after his death, and their bishops refused to be called his followers (I2). Spoken: "the churches their opponents called Arian". "Homoian" need not be said aloud; if it is, give its meaning, from the Greek for "like" (N4).
  - Office: a presbyter, a parish priest under the bishop of Alexandria, who also taught the Scriptures (S1, S4, S5). Spoken: "a priest of Alexandria".

- **Shape:** Teacher or thinker. The impact is in his later readers, here whole churches who would not take his name.

- **Openings:**
  1. His own complaint to a friend (Q1): "We are persecuted because we said: the Son has a beginning, but God has no beginning." Heard once, it states the whole question, and it lets him speak first in a story his opponents mostly told. It leads straight into what he taught and why it split the church. It is his polemic, not the narrator's finding (see Do not say).
  2. (Second choice.) Constantine's picture of the quarrel in 324: the people "rent into diverse parties" over a question he thought "trifling" (N2, E2). It opens on the division and asks what could matter so much.

- **Story beats:**
  1. **The priest and his teaching** (heart). Arius was a priest of Alexandria who ran his own church and taught the Scriptures (S3, S4, S5). Around 318 he clashed with his bishop, Alexander, over the Son of God (S4). He wrote to Eusebius, bishop of Nicomedia, and to Alexander himself: God alone has no beginning; the Son was made by God before time, as perfect God, but is not eternal beside him (E7, E8, N1). Why: two beings without beginning would make two first principles, and a Son who is part of the Father would make God divisible (E8, N1). How it began is told three ways and is not needed (E1-E3). Confidence: Documented (his letters); Contested (how the clash began).
  2. **The church divides** (heart). Alexander's council deposed him with other priests, deacons and two bishops from Libya, calling them "enemies of Christ" (E6). Arius said his bishop had driven them out "as atheists" (E7). He set his teaching in verse, the Thalia (E9, E11). Here, in a sentence: we have his letters and verse only because his opponents copied them, Athanasius to mock the Thalia (I6, E9). The quarrel spread: Constantine wrote in 324 that the people were "rent into diverse parties" (N2). Confidence: Documented.
  3. **Nicaea, 325** (heart). The emperor called the bishops to Nicaea (epiphanius 46220-46224). Its creed said the Son is "begotten, not made", "of one substance with the Father" (N3). It closed by cursing anyone who said "before he was begotten he was not" or that he came "of things that were not": phrases from Arius's own letter (E14, E7, I1). The council condemned his opinions; the emperor exiled him and ordered his books burned, with death for anyone who hid one (E13, E15). Within the first minute, add the one forward sentence that his teaching outlived him (see the heart). Confidence: Documented (the decisions and the order; not how fully the order was carried out).
  4. **Recalled; Constantinople, 336** (about 45 words). The emperor took him back after he and Euzoius gave a creed in Scripture's words, without the disputed phrases (E16). In Constantinople the bishop refused to admit him to communion and prayed that God would take either Arius or himself; Arius died suddenly the day before he was to be received (E17). Athanasius, who was not there, saw God's judgment in it (E17). Confidence: Documented (sudden death the day before his reception); the cause is Contested and stays in Athanasius's voice.
  5. **The name refused, 341.** His opponents called everyone who rejected Nicaea "Arian". At Antioch the eastern bishops wrote that they were not his followers (Q3): they had examined his faith and received him, not followed him (I2). Athanasius answered that they disowned the name because it was hated (I2). Confidence: Documented (both claims, each in its owner's voice).
  6. **The later form, 359-360.** At Ariminum and then Constantinople, councils backed by the emperor set out a new creed: the word "substance" was dropped because Scripture never uses it, and the Son was called "like the Father" as the Scriptures teach (N4). Jerome, an opponent, wrote that the whole world "groaned, and was astonished to find itself Arian" (N5). Ulfila, bishop of the Goths, accepted that creed (N4). Under the emperor Valens, preachers of this faith went to the Goths; Ulfila made them letters and translated the Scriptures into their language (N6, N7). His own creed, kept by his pupil, calls the Father "alone unbegotten" and the Son "our Lord and God", yet "subject and obedient in all things" to the Father (N8; paraphrase). The Goths passed it on to "all people of their speech" (N7). Confidence: Documented (the creeds and Ulfila's words); Widely Accepted (Jordanes's account of the spread).
  7. **Toledo, 589.** More than two hundred and fifty years after his death, the Gothic bishops of Spain entered the catholic church and cursed anyone who still held "the faith and communion that came from Arius", which, they said, they had held until then (N11). The story may end here: the people who had refused his name gave up their faith under it. Confidence: Documented.

- **What came of them** (heaviest first; about a third of the story):
  1. **The creed of Nicaea, written against his teaching.** Its central clauses, "begotten, not made", "of one substance with the Father", answer him, and its closing curses take up phrases from his own letter (N3, E14, E7, I1). Eusebius of Caesarea, who signed, wrote home that the curses banned phrases such as "of things which do not exist" (E20). Documented.
  2. **The churches later called Arian.** For two and a half centuries churches held, in their own words, that the Father alone is unbegotten and the Son is less than him and subject to him: the bishops of 341 (I2), the creed of 360 (N4), Ulfila and the Goths (N6-N9), and the Gothic church of Spain until 589 (N11). They would not be called his followers (I2), and taught "like" rather than "of one substance". The story must give this full room: without it the refusal of the name sounds like a quarrel over a label. Documented in each witness's words; Contested whether it was his teaching (see the heart).
  3. **His voice survives only in his opponents' books.** Theodoret, Epiphanius and Athanasius copied his letters and verse, each to condemn him (I6, E9). No held text says the burning order is why nothing else survives. Documented.
  - **What he defended** (marked): that God alone is without beginning, and that the bodiless Father is not made two or divisible by having a Son (E8, N1). He held to it under deposition and exile; his last creed to Constantine kept to Scripture's words (E16).

  2. **Q3.** Rendered from the Greek of the eastern bishops' letter from Antioch, 341, in Socrates, Church History 2.10 (`socrates_historia-ecclesiastica-tomus1-grc-lat_hussey1853.txt`, lines 16658-16659), checked against Athanasius, De Synodis 22 (`npnf204_athanasius-select-works-letters.xml`, lines 57418-57419).

  - Q2 (the Thalia's opening, rendered earlier) is dropped from this story: its set-up costs words the heart now needs, and Q1 already gives his voice. The Thalia stays as a fact in beat 2.

- **Connection to carry into the story:** What passed from Arius was his teaching that the Father alone is without beginning and the Son, made by him, is less than him (E7, N1). It passed to churches that would not take his name. The bishops at Antioch in 341 received him but said they did not follow him (I2). The creed of 360 replaced Nicaea's "of one substance" with "like the Father according to the Scriptures" (N4). Ulfila, who accepted that creed, carried this faith to the Goths, and his own creed keeps Arius's core: the Father "alone unbegotten", the Son subject to him in all things (N8). In 589 the Gothic bishops of Spain gave up "the faith and communion that came from Arius, held by us until now" (N11). The listener needs three things: these were real churches, and for a time the emperors' church; they taught in their own words, not his; and the name "Arian" was their opponents' until, at Toledo, their own bishops used it of the faith they left. Carry it through what people did: bishops writing their refusal, a council writing a new creed, a missionary translating, a council cursing the faith it had held. No "this matters because" sentence.

- **Who tells it:**
  - Arius himself: two letters, the Thalia's opening and the creed he gave Constantine. Every copy is held by an opponent.
  - Alexander of Alexandria and Constantine: in his own time, his accuser and the emperor.
  - Athanasius: his fiercest opponent, writing in the 350s; quotes the Thalia to mock it; was not in Constantinople at the death and says so.
  - Socrates, Sozomen, Theodoret: church historians about a century on, all on the Nicene side.
  - Jerome (c. 379) and Jordanes (c. 551): opponents of the later churches. "Arian" is their word.
  - Auxentius, Ulfila's pupil: the movement's own voice, in a letter kept in an opponent's manuscript (OCR).
  - The acts of Toledo, 589: the Gothic bishops in their own council.
  - How-we-know gets one sentence, at the Thalia (beat 2), that his words survive only in his opponents' books; and a clause at the death, that Athanasius was not there. The doubt is not itself the story.

- **Do not say:**
  - "Arius founded the Arian church" or "the Homoians followed Arius" → "his opponents named them after him; the Antioch bishops of 341 said they did not follow him."
  - "The Homoians had nothing to do with Arius" → "the Antioch bishops said they received him but did not follow him"; Athanasius said they disowned the name because it was hated; the Gothic bishops at Toledo called their faith the one "that came from Arius".
  - "The Goths were Arians" or "Ulfila taught Arius's doctrine" in the narrator's voice → "the faith their opponents called Arian"; Ulfila's creed in his own terms (Father alone unbegotten; the Son our Lord and God, subject to the Father).
  - "The Homoians taught that the Son was not eternal" → not in their held words; "that the Father alone is unbegotten, and the Son is subject to him in all things" (N8, N12).
  - "Arius taught the Son was created in time" or "was just a man" or "an ordinary creature" → his own words: begotten "before time, and before ages", "perfect God", "a perfect creature of God, but not as one of the creatures", "not eternal" beside the Father (E7, E8, N1).
  - "Arius denied that Christ was God" → he called the Son "perfect God"; the dispute was whether the Son is God in the same full sense as the Father.
  - "The whole world became Arian" or "the empire was Arian" in the narrator's voice → Jerome's words, credited to him, an opponent.
  - "Valens converted the Goths" → "in Valens's time, Socrates and Jordanes say, the Goths took this faith, with preachers he sent"; both are Nicene writers.
  - "Toledo ended Arianism" → "the Gothic bishops of Spain gave it up at Toledo in 589."
  - "Every curse in the creed was a phrase of Arius's" or "Arius taught that the Son could change" → "two curses repeat his letter's phrases; his own letters call the Son unchangeable; Athanasius and the council's letter say he taught the Son could change."
  - "Arius was persecuted" or "driven out as an atheist" in the narrator's voice → "he wrote that he was persecuted, and driven out 'as atheists'"; Alexander's letter calls Arius's party "enemies of Christ".
  - "Eusebius of Nicomedia and Theognis were exiled with him after Nicaea" → leave out (npnf202 1484-1487).
  - "318 bishops" or any count of bishops at Nicaea → no count.
  - "Alexander prayed that God would take him or Arius first" → "prayed that God would take either Arius or himself".
  - "God struck him down" or "he died by God's judgment" in the narrator's voice → "Athanasius saw God's judgment in it."
  - "He burst open in the street" or other gore → none; the death is told in one sentence.
  - "Athanasius saw him die" → "Athanasius was not there."
  - "He was jealous that Alexander became bishop" → no motive (E4).
  - "He first said 'there was a time when the Son was not' to Alexander's face" as fact → use his own letter's words.
  - "Sailors and millers sang the Thalia" → leave out.
  - "All his books were burned" → "the emperor ordered them burned."
  - "Arius was born in Libya in 256" or any birth year → no year is held.
  - "In 336" stated as certain → "in 336, by the usual dating" is fine.
  - "Arius argued at the council" or invented speeches at Nicaea → no scene.
  - "Presbyter" without help → "a priest", ranking below the bishop.
  - "Arius recanted" → he and Euzoius gave a creed in Scripture's words.
  - "His allies pressed for his reception" with no refusal → "the bishop refused to admit him" (Review.md, 2026-11-18).
  - "The council made up homoousios to defeat Arius" or any motive for its wording → give the creed's words, not a motive.

- **Leave out:**
  - Aquileia (381) and Auxentius of Milan (I3, I4): the Antioch letter carries the refusal of the name; the later churches need the room.
  - The Thalia's opening as a quote (Q2) and the sailors' songs (E12): see Quotes.
  - Philostorgius's fault-finding (I5): a side point that needs its own placing.
  - The Judas words and Sozomen's three views of the death (E17, E19): the death is cut to one beat; Athanasius's judgment stays, credited.
  - Maximinus's defence of Arius's wording (N10): it supports the outline's reading of the later churches but needs more placing than the story has room for.
  - The Vandals, Burgundians and Lombards by name: no held line is used for them here; Jordanes's "all people of their speech" carries the spread.
  - Epiphanius's portrait and the 700 virgins; Socrates's gory death; Epiphanius's order of events; Baucalis as a nickname; the Melitian schism; the Thalia's metre.
  - Never leave out the force: the deposition, the exile, the burning order with its death penalty, Athanasius's open contempt, and Toledo's curse. These stay, told plainly and credited.
