# Outline: the Council of Chalcedon (451)

Every reference points to `Source_Sheet.md` (item number) and through it to the file and line in `cic/texts/`. Items H1–H8 were added for this revision.

**The heart of their life.** In 451 the bishops at Chalcedon decided what the church would say Christ is. He is one and the same Son, fully God and fully human, "known in two natures", without confusion, change, division or separation, in one person (B15, H1). They meant to shut out two errors at once: splitting Christ into two Sons, and mixing God and man into one (H2). Leo of Rome's Tome gave them the reason for "two": each nature does what belongs to it, in communion with the other (B5). Documented. That decision divided the churches, and the division lasted. The Greek and Latin churches made it their fourth general council and a test of who belonged (E1, H3, H4; Widely Accepted for the long view, Documented for 553 and Gregory). The churches of Egypt and Syria refused it from the start, and Armenia by synod in the sixth century (E2, E3, E4, E5; Documented; the 506 date Contested). They confessed Christ "one nature from two", condemned Eutyches as well, and honoured the bishop the council deposed, Dioscorus of Alexandria, as a confessor (E2, E4, E5, B20). In 1912 a former Armenian patriarch still described them as one communion: the Armenian, Syrian, Coptic and Ethiopian churches, apart from the Greek and Latin (H6; Documented as his account).
*Not held by the sources, so not said:* that the two formulas meant the same (Evagrius thought so, E4; the refusers did not), or that the split is simply "Monophysite against Orthodox". What is held is two families of churches, each keeping its own memory of 451.

**Length:** about 560 words, about 4 minutes 20 seconds in Daniel's voice at about 130 words a minute. The project lead ruled 560 words for this piece; keep near it and do not pass it. The reason: a major council whose result is a lasting fork on the tree, and two families of churches have to be told fairly, each in its own witnesses. **Word budget:** opening and heart, about 130 (the first minute); what called it, about 60; the Tome, about 45; Dioscorus and his deposition, about 85; the turn to "in two natures", about 50; other business, about 30; what came of it, about 160. **Priorities if long:** cut the Church of the East first, then Juvenal, then the Session III answers to one clause. Never cut the lasting division, the Tome's reason, the deposition, or the violence in Alexandria.

**Setting**
- Year: 451, autumn. Sessions from 8 October to 1 November by Hefele's dating (A3); the acts date only the deposition, 13 October (B11). Spoken: "in the autumn of 451".
- Place: Chalcedon, a city of Bithynia, in the church of the martyr Euphemia, on the Bosporus, facing Constantinople (A1, A2). First called to Nicaea; moved so the emperor could attend from the capital (A4, A5). Spoken: "Chalcedon, just across the water from Constantinople".
- Who called it: the emperor Marcian, with the empress Pulcheria (A1, A4, B18). Leo of Rome sent legates and his letter, the Tome; he did not come (A4). Almost everyone present was a Greek or Eastern bishop (A10). More than five hundred bishops, by their own letter to Leo (D1). Armenia was absent: its bishops were in prison or exile after the battle of Avarayr that May (A9).
- Traditions (census spoken names): Church and Empire (gathered it); the Church of Alexandria (its bishop condemned); the Syriac Orthodox Church and Armenia of Avarayr (refused it); the Church of the East (received it in part). All three that refused are living traditions on the tree.

**Shape:** Council. The spine is the decision about Christ and the division it made. Two or three voices carry it: Leo (by his letter and legates), Dioscorus, and the refusing churches' own chronicle.

**Openings** (use the first)
- Evagrius, a historian on the council's own side, more than a century later: the whole quarrel turned on "the change of a single letter" (E4). In Greek, *ek* ("from") against *en* ("in"): "from two natures" against "in two natures" (B14, B16). This makes plain sense heard once, and it leads straight to the heart: what the council decided, and why one letter split the churches.
- The refusing churches' own chronicle: the council tore Christ's seamless robe into ten thousand pieces (E4). Plain heard once; it states the division first and the decision second.
- Not as an opening: Dioscorus's "I accept 'from two'". It needs words the listener has not yet been given (Substance_Read, 2026-11-18). Use it in beat 4, once "from two" and "in two" are explained.

**Story beats**
1. **The heart, told first (within the first 130 words).** The one letter (E4). In the autumn of 451, at Chalcedon across the water from Constantinople, more than five hundred bishops called by the emperor Marcian defined Christ as one person "in two natures": fully God and fully human, neither mixed nor split (B15, H1, H2, D1). "From two natures", the phrase they refused to make the rule, meant one nature formed out of the two (B8, B14). The Greek and Latin churches came to keep this as their fourth council (E1). The churches of Egypt and Syria, and later Armenia, refused it, and they still stand apart as a family of churches (E2, E5, H6). *A "nature" here means what Christ is, as God and as man; a "person", who he is.* Documented (decision); Widely Accepted (lasting division, credited to Ormanian if a date is wanted).
2. **What called it.** In 448 a synod at Constantinople under its bishop Flavian condemned Eutyches, a monastery head, who said Christ was "of two natures before the union" but of one after it (B1). In 449 a council at Ephesus under Dioscorus of Alexandria restored Eutyches and deposed Flavian, who cried "I protest" (B2, B3). Leo called that meeting one "not of judges but of robbers" (B4). After Theodosius II died, Marcian and Pulcheria called a new council (A1, A4). Documented.
3. **Leo's reason for "two".** Leo had answered Flavian with a letter, the Tome. It taught that each nature "does the acts which belong to it, in communion with the other": the Word works miracles, the flesh suffers injuries (B5). When it was read, bishops cried "Peter has spoken thus through Leo" and "Leo and Cyril taught the same thing" (B10). Not all at once: bishops from Illyria and Palestine doubted that very passage and asked for time (B5, Latin). This is the council side's reason, and it must be heard before the refusers' reasons. Documented.
4. **Dioscorus on trial and deposed (Sessions I and III; compress).** Leo's legates demanded that Dioscorus not sit as a judge (B6). As the minutes of 449 were read, he stopped the reading: he accepted "from two", not "two" (B8; quote 2). In Session III, held without the emperor's officials, he was summoned three times; the minutes record that he said his guards held him back and asked for the officials and senate to be present (B11b). Leo's legates deposed him, in Leo's name and with Peter, for receiving Eutyches, keeping Leo's letter from being read in 449, excommunicating Leo, the written charges against him, and refusing the summonses (B11, B11a). The acts date it 13 October (B11). He was exiled to Gangra (E2). The refusing churches' chronicle remembers him saying he would sooner see his hand cut off than sign (B20; credit it). Documented (sentence and answers as minuted); the hand saying Inferential-Thin for the words.
5. **The turn to "in two natures" (Session V; compress).** A draft said Christ was "of two natures", and most bishops wanted it signed as it stood (B14a). Leo's legates said they would leave and hold a council in the West (B14). The imperial judges asked: "the most holy Leo or Dioscorus?" The bishops cried, "We believe as Leo," and "two natures" was added (B14). The Definition was written, and it banned any other creed, on pain of deposition for clergy and anathema for monks and lay people (B15). Marcian came in person to the sixth session and was hailed as "teacher of the faith" (B18). Documented.
6. **Other business (one or two sentences).** Monks were put under their bishops (C1). Jerusalem gained the three provinces of Palestine (B21, acts). Canon 28 ranked Constantinople, "New Rome", next after old Rome; Leo's legates protested and Leo, by Peter's authority, declared the bishops' assent void (C2, C3, C4). Documented.

**What came of them** (about a third of the story; ranked heaviest first; this is where the story ends)
1. **A lasting split into two families of churches.** *The refusing side:* the Church of Alexandria, the Syriac Orthodox Church and the Armenian Church kept apart from the council. The Coptic Church's own History, compiled in the tenth century, says "the creeds were separated, and the sees were torn asunder", and that Dioscorus's faith "persists in the see of the evangelist Saint Mark to this day" (H7). Armenia "remained firm in her original resolve" (H5). In 1912 Ormanian wrote that the Syrian, Coptic and Ethiopian churches keep their own leaders "without abandoning their communion with the Armenian Church" (H6). *The accepting side:* the Fifth Council in 553 said that those who do not receive the four councils, Chalcedon among them, are "aliens from the Catholic Church" (H3). Gregory of Rome, in his first year as bishop (590–591), wrote that he received four councils as he received the four Gospels, Chalcedon among them, condemning "Eutyches and Dioscorus" (H4; paraphrase, not a fourth quote). *Defended:* on one side "one nature from two", held as the faith of Athanasius and Cyril (B20; Zachariah 2366–2367); on the other the Definition and the Tome, "a common pillar against misbelievers" (E1). Documented for each witness; Widely Accepted for the division lasting.
2. **Egypt refused at once, with violence on both sides.** When Proterius took Dioscorus's place, Priscus, a historian who arrived in Alexandria then, says a crowd drove soldiers into the old temple of Serapis and burned them alive, and that the 2,000 troops the emperor sent abused "the wives and daughters of the Alexandrians" (E2). The Syriac chronicle says believers were killed at the altar (E2). Clergy, monks and people broke communion and kept Dioscorus's name (E2). The Coptic History says he suffered "for the orthodox faith" and died at Gangra with "the crown of martyrdom"; the Coptic Synaxarium still keeps his feast on 7 Tout (E2). Documented as each witness's account.
3. **The refusers' reasons, in their own words.** They did not follow Eutyches: Dioscorus wrote from exile that Christ's flesh was not "foreign from our body" (E4), and Armenia's synod condemned Eutyches by name, by Ormanian's account (E5). Severus of Antioch, some sixty years on, said the council cured Eutyches's error with "an opposite infection", the teaching of Nestorius (E4). For them, "two natures" divided Christ. Their chronicle says the council tore Christ's robe into ten thousand pieces (E4; quote 3); Michael the Syrian repeated it some seven hundred years later (H8). Documented.
4. **It did not hold at once even in the empire.** Later emperors set it aside: Basiliscus ordered the Tome and "all things said and done at Chalcedon" anathematised, and Zeno's Henotikon of 482 condemned any other opinion "whether at Chalcedon or in any synod" (E1a). Only later did the Greek and Latin churches settle on it (E1, H3). Documented.
5. **Palestine.** Monks back from the council made a monk, Theodosius, bishop of Jerusalem in Juvenal's place (E3). Documented. (May drop.)
6. **Rome and Constantinople** disagreed from the start over canon 28 (C3, C4, E6). Documented. (Told in beat 6.)
7. **The Church of the East** kept the council's canons and Definition in its own collection; its editor says it received the council in so far as it condemned Eutyches (E7). Contested. (Cut first if long.)

**Quotes, 2 or 3 at most**
1. The Definition (B15). Rendered from the Greek as Evagrius copied it (Bidez–Parmentier 5243–5246), checked against Mansi's Latin (10892–10902).
   > "One and the same Jesus Christ, Son, Lord, Only-begotten, is known in two natures: without confusion, without change, without division, without separation."
   Job: the heart itself, in the words the Greek and Latin churches kept; the four "withouts" are what the narrator cannot say better. Use it in the first minute.
2. Dioscorus, Session I (B8). Rendered from the Greek in Percival (npnf214 19256–19259) and Mansi 6 65950–65956 (Latin, with margin 65905; Greek column garbled, 65973–65975).
   > "I accept 'from two'. 'Two' I do not accept. I am forced to be shameless: my soul is at stake."
   Job: the other side of the heart in the mouth of the man on trial. Use it only after "from two" and "in two" are explained.
3. The Syriac chronicle known as Zachariah's (E4). Rendered from Hamilton and Brooks's English (the Syriac is not held), checked against Michael the Syrian's French (2857–2861).
   > "That council tore into ten thousand pieces the whole robe of Christ, woven in one piece from the top."
   Job: the refusing churches' verdict in their own image; it lets the division be felt, not only stated.

**Connection to carry into the story:** The council is on the tree because the branches fork here. One decision gave the Greek and Latin churches their words for Christ and, in the same act, set the churches of Egypt, Syria and Armenia on their own road (B15, E1, E2, E5, H3–H6). The listener must understand four things the writer has to state, not leave to inference. First, the division lasted: each family kept its own memory, the Coptic Church its feast of Dioscorus, the Syriac Church its chronicle, Armenia its synods, and in 1912 these churches were still one communion apart from the Greek and Latin (E2, H5–H8). Second, the refusers also condemned Eutyches (E4, E5): this was not a fight between Chalcedon and Eutyches's followers. Third, both sides claimed Cyril of Alexandria (the acclamation "Leo and Cyril taught the same thing", B10; the chronicle's "his faith was like that of Athanasius, and Cyril", Zachariah 2366–2367), and the words at stake were "from two natures" against "in two natures". Do not call "one nature from two" Cyril's own formula in the narrator's voice; no Cyril text is cited for it. Fourth, the same sentence that deposed Dioscorus made him, for Egypt and Syria, a confessor; each side's reason for the sentence is credited to that side (B11, B12, E2). Carry this through what each church did and kept. Let the story end on the fork, the two families of churches, not on Dioscorus's death.

**Links**
- Leo of Rome: wrote to Flavian (the Tome, read and acclaimed at the council); sent the legates; wrote to Pulcheria against canon 28 (B5, B10, A4, C4).
- Dioscorus of Alexandria: on trial at the council; deposed and exiled; kept as confessor and martyr by Egypt and Syria (B8, B11, E2); kept his feast: the Coptic Synaxarium, 7 Tout (E2).
- Eutyches: not present; his teaching condemned in the Definition (B1, H2).
- Flavian of Constantinople: dead before the council; declared orthodox (B7).
- Theodoret of Cyrrhus: received after he anathematized Nestorius (B19).
- Juvenal of Jerusalem: voted for Eutyches in 449, with the majority in 451; gained Palestine; driven out by monks (B2, B21, E3).
- Severus of Antioch: wrote against it (E4).
- Timothy Aelurus: wrote a refutation of it, kept only in Armenian; exiled to Gangra like Dioscorus (E4, F).
- Gregory the Great: received it as one of four councils, like the four Gospels (H4).
- Marcian and Pulcheria: called it; Marcian attended (A1, B18).

**Who tells it** (in the story, a sentence or two at most; the doubt is not the story)
- *The acts* (Percival's English, Mansi's Latin and Greek): the minutes, sentence, Definition and canons, kept by the side that won. One clause where the deposition is told: "the minutes, kept by the winning side".
- *The Syriac chronicle known as Zachariah's* (finished 569; from Zachariah's Greek history, written by 518; the refusing side). Name it as the refusing churches' own chronicle when quoting it or the hand saying.
- *Evagrius* (Greek, over a century later, on the council's side): for the opening, and for Priscus on Alexandria.
- *Leo's letters* and *Gregory's letter* (their own words): the Latin side.
- *The History of the Patriarchs* (10th century, the Coptic Church's own memory) and *the Coptic Synaxarium* (medieval calendar): Egypt's memory.
- *Severus of Antioch* (his own letter, about 60 years later) and *Michael the Syrian* (12th century, Syriac Orthodox patriarch): Syria's memory.
- *Malachia Ormanian* (1912, former Armenian patriarch of Constantinople): Armenia, and the family of churches in his day. Ter-Minassiantz (1904) reads the 506 synod as condemning Chalcedon only silently; the story says "in the sixth century" or credits Ormanian.
- *Chabot and Wigram* (modern editors): the Church of the East.

**Leave out**
- Procedure beyond what beats 4 and 5 hold: the Session IV vote count (B13), Anatolius's "not for the faith" exchange in full (keep it for the card unless the deposition needs it), the judges' remark that the draft used Dioscorus's phrase, the illness answer, the emperor's threat (Percival's summary), the 350 signatures. These took about 200 words in the last version and kept the heart late.
- The death of Nestorius on the road, his tongue eaten by worms, and Marcian's sympathy for him (Zachariah III.1): legend from a hostile witness; Evagrius disputes the recall (A8 context).
- Theodoret bribing Leo with "the gift which blinds the eyes" (Zachariah III.1): a hostile allegation with no other support.
- Aetius beating Amphilochius, Eustace of Berytus's shorthand note "under compulsion" (Zachariah III.1): single hostile witness.
- The Coptic Synaxarium's story of the emperor and empress having Dioscorus's beard pulled and teeth knocked out (E2): legend.
- Theopistus's History of Dioscorus (the slap, Dioscorus's letter calling Leo a tree to be cut down), and Pulcheria striking Dioscorus (a later tradition Nau mentions): legend.
- The visions of the Plerophories: legend, two generations later.
- Chalcedon made a metropolis in name (C5); the other canons beyond 4 and 28; the Euthymius and Leontius evidence for "in two natures"; the Fifth Council's Three Chapters dispute (for the card).
- The full roll of bishops and session-by-session dates beyond 8 and 13 October.
- Timothy Aelurus's own arguments: held only in Armenian OCR, not rendered; name the work, do not characterize its content.
- Not left out: the deposition, the burning at the Serapeum and the killings at the altar, the legates' threat, the ban on any other creed. These are told as plainly as the Definition.
