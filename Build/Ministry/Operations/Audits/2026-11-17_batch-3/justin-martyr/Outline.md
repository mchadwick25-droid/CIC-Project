# Outline: Justin Martyr

File abbreviations and full line references are in `Source_Sheet.md` (same folder). R1 = First Apology (Greek), R2 = Second Apology (Greek), AR = Dialogue with Trypho (Greek), GEB = Acts of Justin (Greek, Gebhardt), ANF1 = English of Justin, the Acts and Irenaeus, TAT/ANF2 = Tatian, EUS = Eusebius with McGiffert's notes.

- **Length:** stretch toward 3 minutes (about 400–450 words). Reason: a major figure (census `major: true`) with an unusually full record: his own account of his search and turn, his petition to the emperor, a question-and-answer account of his trial by a later, unknown hand, and later witnesses who quoted him. The martyr shape needs room for the life and what he left, not only the end. Rough budget, so the end does not swallow the story: about 130 words for the search and turn; about 110 for Trypho, the petition and the Word (two or three sentences each for Trypho and for Crescens); about 70 for the trial and death; about 140 for what came of him. Use Opening 2 or Quote 3 at the trial, not both.

- **Setting:**
  - Time: the middle of the second century; the First Apology about 150–155, from his own "a hundred and fifty years" since Christ's birth (R1 1227–1228); death about 165 (McGiffert's reckoning from Rusticus's prefecture, EUS 23775–23780). Spoken: "the middle of the second century", "about the year 165".
  - Place: his family were "of Flavia Neapolis in Syria Palaestina" (R1 72–75; ANF1 13965–13969). That he was born there, and that the town stood in Samaria, near old Shechem, is McGiffert's statement as editor (EUS 22420–22422); taught at Rome, "for the second time" living there (GEB 1347–1354; ANF1 29309–29312). Spoken: "a Roman town in Samaria", "Rome".
  - Tradition: told within the census tradition "The Second-Century Greek Apologists" (short name "The Apologists"). Spoken: "the Greek-speaking Christian writers who defended the faith to the Roman world". An "apology" here is a formal defence, the word's old courtroom sense, not saying sorry.
  - The danger: being a Christian was itself the charge. A teacher, Ptolemaeus, was asked "this one thing only, whether he was a Christian", and executed; a bystander, Lucius, who protested, was led away too (R2 136–158).

- **Thread:** A philosopher who found his philosophy in Christ, argued to the emperor that Christians were condemned for a name alone, and left books that later teachers used to defend the one God who made the world.

- **Shape:** Martyr.

- **Openings:**
  1. A stranger stops him at dawn in a colonnade and says "Philosopher, greetings!", because of the cloak he wears. The stranger is Trypho, a Jew who has fled a war (AR 72–74, 87–89).
  2. The prefect asks, "Where do you meet?" Justin answers that the God of the Christians is not shut in by any place, then gives his address: he lodged upstairs at a bath-house in Rome. The copies differ over the names, and "one Martinus" may be a copyist's addition (Otto). Sources: GEB 1339–1354 (Greek: "above one Martinus, of the bath of Timothinus"; the manuscripts read "Timiotinus", GEB 1363); ANF1 29302–29312 ("above one Martinus, at the Timiotinian Bath"); Otto's note, `justin-martyr_opera-addubitata-grc-lat_otto1879.txt` 25472–25489, judging "τινὸς Μαρτίνου" a gloss and reading "I lodge in the upper storey of the bath of Timothinus".
  3. An old man by the sea speaks with him, sends him to the prophets, walks off, and is never seen again (AR 175–180, 428–429).

- **Story beats:**
  1. **The search.** From Flavia Neapolis, he went looking for the truth about God through the schools of philosophy. A Stoic taught him nothing about God; a Peripatetic asked for his fee first; a Pythagorean told him to learn music, astronomy and geometry first; with a Platonist he thought, "in my stupidity", that he would soon see God. Why: he wanted knowledge of God, which he calls the goal of Plato's philosophy. Source: AR 139–169. Documented (his own account).
  2. **The turn.** Walking alone near the sea, he met an old man who told him the prophets, older than all the philosophers, had seen and spoken the truth. "A fire was lit in my soul." He kept the philosopher's cloak afterwards (Eusebius: "in the guise of a philosopher he preached the divine word"). He also writes that seeing slandered Christians unafraid of death convinced him they could not be living in vice. Sources: AR 175–180, 399–435; R2 368–370; EUS 22442, 22455–22456. Documented (own account); the cloak also in Eusebius, later.
  3. **The argument with Trypho.** He debated a Jewish refugee, Trypho, that the prophets spoke of Jesus. The argument has a hard edge: Justin tells Trypho that the Jews' present sufferings, their land laid waste and Jerusalem closed to them, come to them "justly", and that synagogues curse believers in Christ. Yet the two parted as friends, each praying for the other. Sources: AR 87–89, 788–806, 6589–6607. Documented (Justin's account). Place: Eusebius says Ephesus (EUS 24311); Justin does not name the city.
  4. **The teacher and the petition.** At Rome he taught anyone who came to his lodging. He addressed a petition to the emperor Antoninus Pius, his adopted sons and the Senate, on behalf of people "of every race" who were unjustly hated. He asked them to judge Christians by what they did, not by the name (R1 124–126, First Apology 4). He wrote that Christ is the Word in whom every race has shared, so that Socrates and Heraclitus, who lived by the Word, are Christians, he says flatly, though they were thought godless. The Greek *logos* means both "word" and "reason"; Justin uses both senses at once. Sources: GEB 1347–1354; R1 68–75, 78–96, 124–126, 1230–1235; R2 136–158. Documented. No reply from the emperor is recorded.
  5. **The enemy he expected.** He questioned Crescens, a Cynic philosopher, and, he says, proved that Crescens knew nothing (R2 283–285; "in public discussions" is Eusebius's later phrase, EUS 23797). He wrote that he expected to be plotted against and killed, perhaps by Crescens, "that lover of bravado". Why: Crescens was denouncing Christians as godless in public. Source: R2 273–293; ANF1 16849–16851. Documented that he expected it; whether Crescens caused his death is Contested (see Do not say).
  6. **The trial.** The account of his trial has it as follows. Arrested with six others, he stood before Rusticus, prefect of Rome, and was told to obey the gods. He said he had tried to learn every teaching and had settled on the Christians'. Of the companions' answers, use only Euelpistus's: an imperial slave, he said he had gladly heard Justin but had the faith from his parents (this serves What came of them 3). Asked whether he really thought he would go up to heaven, Justin said he did not suppose it; he knew. Asked to sacrifice, he said no one in his right mind turns from true worship to godlessness. The demand was to sacrifice to the gods, as the emperor commanded (GEB 1433–1434; ANF1 29396–29397). Source: GEB 1300–1432; ANF1 29266–29387. Rusticus, the refusal and the death: Widely Accepted. The wording of the exchanges belongs to the record, a record set out as question and answer by an unknown later hand (see Who tells it); attribute it to the account.
  7. **The death.** Rusticus sentenced all who refused to sacrifice to be scourged and beheaded "according to the laws". Some believers secretly took the bodies and buried them in a fitting place. Source: GEB 1433–1441; ANF1 29396–29406. Widely Accepted. Year about 165: Dominant Modern Reconstruction.

- **What came of them** (heaviest first; about a third of the story):
  1. **His books were kept and used against rival teachers.** Within a generation Irenaeus quoted his book against Marcion: "I would not have believed the Lord Himself, if He had announced any other than He who is our framer, maker, and nourisher" (ANF1 44907–44914). Tertullian put "Justin, philosopher and martyr" first in a list of those who had written against the founders of heresies (ANF3 43162–43163). Eusebius copied long passages and listed his works (EUS 24209–24212); Photius was still reading him in the ninth century (PHO 11699–11718). *Defended:* the one God who made the world, against Marcion's teaching of a greater, other God (R1 729–731; Irenaeus as above). His own book against all the heresies is lost (R1 743–745).
  2. **His death was kept as a record and became his name.** The trial record was copied and read; Tertullian's "philosopher and martyr" is the name the church kept. (McGiffert, editor: the tradition "is crystallized in his name", EUS 23764–23766.)
  3. **His hearers carried him on.** Irenaeus and Hippolytus, both later, say Tatian was his hearer or disciple (ANF1 33664; ANF5 13005). Tatian himself calls him "the most admirable Justin" and repeats his teaching that demons act like kidnappers holding people for ransom (TAT 7956–7960; ANF2 6526–6533). Euelpistus said at the trial that he had gladly heard Justin's words (GEB 1386–1387).
  4. **His idea of the Word shared by every race** (R1 1230–1235; R2 332–337, 405–408). Documented as his teaching; this outline does not trace later witnesses carrying it.
  5. Optional, if room: **his description of a Sunday gathering**: readings from "the memoirs of the apostles" or the prophets, a talk by the one presiding, prayers, bread and wine with water, the people's "Amen", portions taken by deacons to the absent, and a collection for orphans, widows, prisoners and strangers (R1 1759–1778; ANF1 16502–16528). Describe the gathering only; no held source ranks it as earliest or fullest.

- **Quotes, 2 or 3 at most:**
  1. **Dialogue 8.1–2**, rendered from the Greek (AR 429–435; ANF1 17853–17857 as cross-check):
     "At once a fire was lit in my soul. A love took hold of me for the prophets, and for those men who are friends of Christ. As I turned his words over in my mind, I found this to be the only philosophy that is sure and useful. That is how, and why, I am a philosopher."
     Job: the turn, in his own voice, and the paradox that he became a Christian without ceasing to call himself a philosopher.
  2. **First Apology 46**, rendered from the Greek (R1 1230–1233; ANF1 15685–15692 as cross-check):
     "We have been taught that Christ is the firstborn of God. We have shown already that he is the Word, in whom every race of people has had a share. And those who lived with the Word are Christians, even if they were counted godless."
     Job: his central idea, in the petition he sent to the emperor. The narrator must first make clear that "Word" (*logos*) also means "reason", and may then name Socrates and Heraclitus as his examples, which follow in the same sentence (R1 1233–1234).
  3. **Acts of Justin V.3**, rendered from the Greek (GEB 1410–1411, 1420–1421; OCR, checked against ANF1 29371–29374):
     Rusticus: "So you suppose that you will go up into the heavens, to receive some reward?" Justin: "I do not suppose it. I know it, and I am fully sure of it."
     Job: the stand itself, at the trial, in the record's own words.

- **Connection to carry into the story:** Justin to **Tatian**, his hearer. Irenaeus, writing about fifteen years after Justin's death, says Tatian "was a hearer of Justin's, and as long as he continued with him he expressed no such views; but after his martyrdom he separated from the Church" and taught his own system, condemning marriage (ANF1 33663–33667). Tatian, in his own Address to the Greeks, calls him "the most admirable Justin" and says Crescens tried to bring death on "Justin, and indeed on me" because Justin exposed the philosophers as greedy frauds (TAT 7956, 8105–8111; ANF2 6526, 6550–6556). What the listener must understand: Justin ran something like a philosopher's school in his lodging (GEB 1347–1353), and Irenaeus and Hippolytus say Tatian was one of his hearers. What passed: Justin's teaching, of which Tatian repeats one point by name, that the so-called gods are like robbers who seize people and then release them for ransom; "the most admirable Justin", Tatian writes, "rightly denounced them as robbers" (TAT 7956–7960; ANF2 6526–6533). The quarrel with Crescens passed too: Tatian says it endangered them both. Tatian kept his teacher's memory with open admiration. That he broke with the Church after Justin's death is Irenaeus's judgment, a hostile one ("excited and puffed up", ANF1 33666), and must be credited to Irenaeus (Hippolytus, also later, says only that Tatian did not hold his master's opinions, ANF5 13005–13006). Carry it through what each did, not a summary sentence.

- **Links:**
  - Tatian: heard him teach (so Irenaeus and Hippolytus); praised him and repeated his teaching in writing (TAT 7956–7960).
  - Irenaeus of Lyon: quoted his book (ANF1 44907–44914, 53355); the first to mention his martyrdom, in McGiffert's judgment as editor (EUS 23765).
  - Marcion of Pontus: wrote against him while Marcion was still teaching (R1 729–731).
  - Tertullian: named him "philosopher and martyr" (ANF3 43162).
  - Hippolytus of Rome: called Tatian his disciple (ANF5 13005).
  - Eusebius of Caesarea: wrote his life and quoted his books (EUS 23744–23873, 24205–24315).
  - Epiphanius of Salamis: recorded his martyrdom, with a mistaken emperor (EPI 15927–15935).
  - Jerome: wrote his life and listed his works (JER 39966–39997).
  - Antoninus Pius and his sons: wrote to (R1 68–71).
  - Trypho: argued with (AR 87–89).
  - Crescens: public opponent (R2 273–293).
  - Rusticus: judged him (GEB 1309 ff.).

- **Who tells it:**
  - **Justin himself**, in his own time: the Dialogue, the two Apologies. Almost everything about his life before the trial is his own account; the conversion story is told as a scene to win a reader, and the listener should hear it as his telling.
  - **The trial record (Acts of Justin)**: later; author and date unknown. Dods (translator): "nothing is known as to the date or authorship", yet it is "generally reckoned among the most trustworthy of the Martyria" (ANF1 29216–29218). McGiffert (editor): "belonging to a later age (probably the third century)", but "comparatively truthful" (EUS 23767–23771). It is set out like a court record, though written down later by an unknown hand, and the held text comes through one medieval collection: Gebhardt reprints Otto (GEB 209), and Otto traces his text to one Grottaferrata manuscript of the tenth-century saints' lives of Symeon Metaphrastes, a collection he describes as partly gathered unchanged and partly composed or interpolated (Otto 1836–1852, 1868–1875); Otto also judges part of the title "of a later age" (Otto 1945–1950).
  - **Tatian**, in his own time; his hearer according to Irenaeus and Hippolytus; also Crescens's personal enemy, so his portrait of Crescens is a partisan's.
  - **Irenaeus**, about fifteen years after; hostile to Tatian.
  - **Eusebius**, about 150 years after; quotes Justin and Tatian, and draws from Tatian more than Tatian says (see Do not say).
  - **Epiphanius**, about 210 years after; unreliable on date and age.

- **Do not say:**
  - "Crescens had him killed" / "Crescens betrayed him." Say: Justin expected Crescens or others to plot his death; Tatian says Crescens tried to bring about the death of both Justin and Tatian; Eusebius and Jerome, much later, say it was Crescens's plot. The trial record does not name Crescens.
  - "The court transcript" / "his exact words at the trial." Say: "the account of his trial has him say …". No beat may be placed in a particular version of the Acts, or called the oldest or shortest form.
  - "The baths of Martinus" / "Martinus's house." Say: "rooms above a bath-house".
  - "Tatian says he studied under Justin." Tatian does not say so. Say: "Irenaeus and Hippolytus say Tatian was his hearer".
  - Crescens "stirred up the persecution": that is the ANF editor's preface (ANF1 29209–29211), a modern view; the Acts do not name Crescens.
  - "Rusticus, Marcus Aurelius's teacher." Not in any held source. Say only "Rusticus, the prefect of Rome".
  - "Under the emperor Marcus Aurelius" as fact. Say "about the year 165"; if the reign is named, say it is the likelier of two traditions (McGiffert). Never "under Hadrian".
  - "Poisoned" / "hemlock." The record says scourged and beheaded; hemlock is a later addition Dods calls "absurd".
  - "He met Trypho at Ephesus" in Justin's voice. Eusebius places it at Ephesus; Justin names only a colonnade.
  - "The old man was a hermit / an angel / a Christian teacher named …" Justin gives no name or calling; he says only an old man, gentle and dignified, whom he never saw again.
  - "The emperor read it / was moved / stopped the persecution." No reply is recorded.
  - "Six companions" is right; do not say "five" (one heading lists five names) or "seven" unless counting Justin.
  - "Euelpistus was converted by Justin." He said he gladly heard Justin but had the faith from his parents.
  - "Justin was a Samaritan by birth" as settled. Say he came from a Roman town in Samaria and called the Samaritans "my people"; his family background is disputed.
  - "Justin and Trypho debated as friends" without the hard edge. If the debate is told, say that Justin told Trypho the Jews' sufferings were deserved, as well as that they parted as friends.
  - "The first Christian philosopher" / "the first apologist." No held source says so; Jerome, in a letter, names Quadratus and Aristides as earlier defenders, whose "example was afterwards followed by Justin" (`npnf206_jerome-principal-works.xml` 16580–16596).
  - "Atheists" for *atheoi*: say "godless", or "called godless because they would not worship the gods". The modern word misleads.
  - "Safe" for ἀσφαλῆ in any paraphrase of Q1: say "sure".
  - A "so" or "because" linking his conversion to fear or to the old man alone: he gives two reasons in two places, the prophets (Dialogue) and the courage of Christians facing death (Second Apology).

- **Leave out:**
  - Epiphanius's date under Hadrian and his age of thirty: late and, says Holl, impossible (EPI 15961–15962).
  - The quarrel over whether Eusebius altered Tatian's sentence (EUS 23880–23910): a scholars' dispute; it belongs on the card.
  - Whether there was one apology or two (EUS 22600–22601): card material.
  - Hadrian's letter appended to the First Apology (R1 1794–1803): a side document of disputed standing.
  - Justin's account of fallen angels and demons (R2 179–197): a side road at this length, apart from the robbers-and-ransom point Tatian repeats (Connection).
  - The works transmitted under his name that are not his (Hortatory Address, Discourse to the Greeks, On the Sole Government of God, Letter to Diognetus).
  - The wife's divorce in Second Apology 2, beyond what is needed to explain Ptolemaeus's case.
  - Any claim about versions (recensions) of the Acts: the census's "three recensions" rests on Musurillo, not held; the held text is a single one (Otto, via Gebhardt).
