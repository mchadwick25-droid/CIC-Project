# Outline: Justin Martyr

File abbreviations and full line references are in `Source_Sheet.md` (same folder). R1 = First Apology (Greek), R2 = Second Apology (Greek), AR = Dialogue with Trypho (Greek), GEB = Acts of Justin (Greek, Gebhardt, Otto's text), KK = Acts of Justin (Greek, Franchi's text, in Knopf–Krüger), DEL = Delehaye 1921, ANF1 = English of Justin, the Acts and Irenaeus, TAT/ANF2 = Tatian, EUS = Eusebius with McGiffert's notes, ADD = `addai_doctrine-of-addai.txt`. Sheet items S, E, I, D and H are cited by their tags.

- **The heart of their life:** Justin was a Greek-speaking philosopher who became a Christian and never stopped calling himself a philosopher: he had found, he said, "the only philosophy that is sure and useful" (AR 433–435). At Rome he taught anyone who came to his lodging (GEB 1347–1354; Widely Accepted). About 150–155 (the date is the First Apology's own, R1 1227–1228) he wrote a formal defence of the Christians to the emperor Antoninus Pius, his sons and the Senate (R1 68–75; Documented). It made two claims. First, a name decides nothing: judge Christians by what they do, not by the name "Christian", which was itself the charge (R1 124–126; R2 136–158; H1, S5). Second, Christ is the Word, the *logos* (also "reason"), in whom every race has a share, so that those who lived by it, Socrates among them, were Christians though called godless (R1 1230–1235; E7). So as not to seem to hide anything, the same defence sets out what Christians did when they met on Sunday: readings, a talk, prayers, bread and wine shared, gifts for the poor (R1 1577–1579, 1759–1785; H2, H3, E8). He also wrote up a long argument with a Jew, Trypho, that the prophets spoke of Jesus (AR; E5). About 165 he refused to sacrifice to the gods before the prefect of Rome and was beheaded (GEB 1423–1441; Widely Accepted; the year a Dominant Modern Reconstruction). His books were kept and used, above all against Marcion, for the one God who made the world (I1), and the church kept his name as Tertullian gave it: "Justin, philosopher and martyr" (ANF3 43162). Each part checked: "philosopher" (own words, Documented); "taught at Rome" (the trial record, author unknown, Widely Accepted); "defended Christians in writing to the emperors" (Documented; only the First Apology is addressed to the emperor and his sons; the Second opens "O Romans" (R2 97; H6), and whether it is a separate work is Contested, D6, so say "to the emperor", singular, or "the emperor and his sons"); "Trypho" (Documented as his own account); "Sunday" (Documented); "death at Rome" (Widely Accepted).

- **Length:** stretch toward 3 minutes: about 420–450 words, at Neo's 150 a minute. Reason: a major figure (census `major: true`) whose record is unusually full and whose heart has several parts that each need a sentence or two: the defence and its two claims, the Sunday gathering, the turn to Christ, Trypho, the trial, and a hearer who carried him into another tradition. Rough budget: about 150 words for the opening and the heart (beat 1); about 45 for Sunday (beat 2); about 70 for the turn (beat 3); about 30 for Trypho (beat 4); about 25 for Crescens (beat 5); about 55 for the trial and death (beat 6); about 110 for what came of him, Tatian included. Crescens is told once only.

- **Setting:**
  - Time: the middle of the second century; the First Apology about 150–155, from his own "a hundred and fifty years" since Christ's birth (R1 1227–1228; S3); death about 165 (McGiffert's reckoning from Rusticus's prefecture, EUS 23775–23780). Spoken: "the middle of the second century", "about the year 165".
  - Place: his family were "of Flavia Neapolis in Syria Palaestina" (R1 72–75; S1). That he was born there, and that the town stood in Samaria near old Shechem, is McGiffert's statement as editor (EUS 22420–22422). He taught at Rome, living there "for the second time" (GEB 1347–1354; S7). Spoken: "a Roman town in Samaria", "Rome".
  - Tradition: told within "The Second-Century Greek Apologists" (short name "The Apologists"). Spoken: "the Greek-speaking Christian writers who defended the faith to the Roman world". An "apology" here is a formal defence, the word's old courtroom sense, not saying sorry.
  - The danger: being a Christian was itself the charge. A teacher, Ptolemaeus, was asked "this one thing only, whether he was a Christian", and was ordered led away to punishment; Lucius, a Christian who protested, was led away too, and a third man after him (R2 136–158; S5). Death is implied, not stated: do not say "executed".

- **Shape:** Martyr, told from the work outward: the defence first, the death as its seal.

- **Openings:**
  1. (Recommended.) The words he set at the head of his petition: he, Justin, son of Priscus, from Flavia Neapolis, writes to the emperor "on behalf of people of every race who are unjustly hated and abused", "myself being one of them" (R1 72–75; ANF1 13965–13969). Plain heard once; it names him, his place, his addressee and his cause, and leads straight into the defence. Paraphrase it in the narrator's voice; it is not one of the three quotes.
  2. A stranger stops him at dawn in a colonnade and says "Philosopher, greetings!", because of the cloak he wears (AR 72–81). Leads into "a philosopher who became a Christian". If used, the stranger is not yet named; Trypho is named at beat 4.

- **Story beats** (beat 1 carries the heart and ends by about 150 words; the rest deepens it):
  1. **The defence, and what it cost.** A philosopher, wearing the philosopher's cloak (AR 77–81; EUS 22442; S8), taught at Rome in rooms above a bath-house, and anyone who wished could come (GEB 1347–1354). He wrote to Antoninus Pius, his sons and the Senate on behalf of the Christians. Why: Christians were punished for the name alone, as with Ptolemaeus and Lucius (R2 136–158). His first claim: a name is neither good nor bad apart from the deeds behind it; judge what we do (R1 124–126; H1). His second: Christ is the Word in whom every race shares (Quote 2). Add one clause that the same defence set out openly what Christians did when they met on Sunday (the detail stays in beat 2), so that every part of the heart is named inside the first ~150 words. Close the beat with one forward sentence: some ten or fifteen years later (about 165), he died for this faith at Rome, beheaded (GEB 1433–1441). Documented, except the death (Widely Accepted). The word to explain: *logos*, meaning both "word" and "reason", before Quote 2.
  2. **What Christians did on Sunday.** So that he would not seem to deal unfairly by leaving it out, he told the emperor how Christians were made new in baptism and what they did when they met (R1 1577–1579; H2). On "the day called Sunday", those in town and country gathered; the "memoirs of the apostles" or the prophets were read "as long as time allows"; the one presiding spoke, urging them to imitate these good things; all stood and prayed; bread, wine and water were brought; the presider gave thanks; the people said "Amen"; deacons carried portions to the absent; those who could gave, and the presider cared for orphans, widows, the sick, prisoners and strangers. Why Sunday: the first day, when God made the world, and the day Christ rose from the dead (R1 1759–1785; ANF1 16502–16534; E8, H3). Documented. "Amen" needs no gloss. The "memoirs of the apostles" are his word for the Gospels' kind of book; say "the apostles' memoirs" or "the memoirs of the apostles", and do not say "the four Gospels" or "the New Testament".
  3. **How the philosopher found Christ.** He had gone through the schools (the Dialogue does not say where; the Platonist came "to our city", unnamed, AR 164): a Stoic taught him nothing about God; a Peripatetic wanted his fee first; a Pythagorean sent him away because he had not first studied music, astronomy and geometry (AR 149–158); with a Platonist he hoped, "in my stupidity", to see God soon (AR 139–169; E1). Walking alone near the sea, he met an old man who told him the prophets, older than all the philosophers, had seen and spoken the truth, then left, and Justin never saw him again (AR 175–180, 399–435; E2). Quote 1. He gives a second reason elsewhere: he had seen slandered Christians unafraid of death, and knew they could not be living in vice (R2 368–370; E4). Documented as his own account, told as a scene to win a reader.
  4. **The argument with Trypho.** He wrote up his debate with Trypho, a Jew who had fled the recent war, that the prophets spoke of Jesus (AR 87–89; E5). The argument has a hard edge: Justin tells Trypho that the Jews' present sufferings, their land laid waste and Jerusalem closed to them, come to them "justly" (AR 788–791). Yet the two parted as friends, each praying for the other (AR 6589–6607). Documented (Justin's account). Tell the hard edge and the friendship together, in two sentences at most.
  5. **The enemy he expected.** Crescens, a Cynic philosopher, was denouncing Christians as godless in public. Justin questioned him and, he says, proved that he knew nothing, and wrote that he expected to be plotted against and killed, perhaps by Crescens, "that lover of bravado" (R2 273–293; E10). Documented that he expected it; whether Crescens caused his death is Contested (D1; see Do not say). Told once only.
  6. **The trial and the death.** In the account of his trial, he stood with six others before Rusticus, prefect of Rome, and was told to obey the gods. He said he had tried to learn every teaching and had settled on the Christians'. Of the companions, Euelpistus, an imperial slave, said he had gladly heard Justin but had the faith from his parents. Then Quote 3. Asked to sacrifice, Justin said no one in his right mind turns from true worship to godlessness. Rusticus sentenced all who refused to sacrifice, as the emperor commanded, to be scourged and beheaded "according to the laws"; some believers secretly took the bodies and buried them in a fitting place (GEB 1300–1441; ANF1 29266–29406; E11, E12). Rusticus, the refusal and the death: Widely Accepted. The wording belongs to the record, a question-and-answer account by an author we do not know; credit it to "the account of his trial". The year about 165: Dominant Modern Reconstruction.

- **What came of them** (heaviest first; about a third of the story, told after beat 6):
  1. **His books were kept and used against rival teachers.** Within a generation Irenaeus quoted his book against Marcion (ANF1 44916–44917). Paraphrase only, not a quote: Irenaeus quotes Justin saying he would not have believed the Lord himself had he proclaimed any God other than the one who made us. Tertullian names him, "Justin, philosopher and martyr", among those who wrote against the heresy-founders in their own day (ANF3 43161–43163). Eusebius copied long passages and listed his works (EUS 24209–24212); Photius was still reading him in the ninth century (PHO 11699–11718). *Defended:* the one God who made the world, against Marcion's teaching of another, greater God (R1 729–731; I1). His own book against all the heresies is lost (R1 743–745).
  2. **A hearer carried him into another tradition: Tatian** (the Connection, below).
  3. **His death became his name.** The trial record was copied and read; Tertullian's "philosopher and martyr" is the name the church kept (I2). One clause; it may close the story.
  4. **His idea of the Word shared by every race** (E7). Documented as his teaching; this outline does not trace later witnesses carrying it (I4). Already told in beat 1; do not retell.
  5. **His Sunday account** (E8). Already told in beat 2. The only later use found is a modern editor's (I5); do not claim later reception for it.

- **Quotes, 2 or 3 at most:**
  1. **Dialogue 8.1–2**, rendered from the Greek (AR 429–435; ANF1 17853–17857 as cross-check):
     "At once a fire was lit in my soul. A love took hold of me for the prophets, and for those men who are friends of Christ. As I turned his words over in my mind, I found this to be the only philosophy that is sure and useful. That is how, and why, I am a philosopher."
     Job: the turn, in his own voice, and why he kept the name "philosopher". Beat 3.
  2. **First Apology 46**, rendered from the Greek (R1 1230–1233; ANF1 15685–15692 as cross-check):
     "We have been taught that Christ is the firstborn of God. We have shown already that he is the Word, in whom every race of people has had a share. And those who lived with the Word are Christians, even if they were counted godless."
     Job: his central idea, in the defence he sent to the emperor. Beat 1. Before it, the narrator makes clear that "Word" (*logos*) also means "reason"; after it, the narrator may name Socrates and Heraclitus, his examples in the same sentence (R1 1233–1234).
  3. **Acts of Justin V.3**, rendered from the Greek of Franchi's text (KK 1754–1757, OCR; H7), the better text, from manuscripts of the 9th–10th centuries; checked against Otto's text (GEB 1410–1411, 1420–1421) and ANF1 29371–29374. Franchi adds "good" (χρηστάς) and "for certain" (ἀκριβῶς), which Otto's text lacks:
     Rusticus: "So you suppose that you will go up into the heavens, to receive some good rewards?" Justin: "I do not suppose it. I know it for certain, and I am fully sure of it."
     Job: the stand itself, at the trial, in the record's words. Beat 6.

- **Connection to carry into the story:** Justin to **Tatian**, his hearer, who became a teacher in his own right and made the one-book Gospel that the Syriac-speaking church at Edessa read aloud. What the listener must understand, in this order:
  1. Who Tatian was: by his own account born "in the land of the Assyrians" and schooled first in Greek learning (ANF2 7460–7464; H4). Irenaeus and Hippolytus, both later, say he was Justin's hearer or disciple (ANF1 33664; ANF5 13005–13006; I3). Spoken: "a man from Assyria who heard Justin teach at Rome" (the place of the hearing is not given by any source; say "heard Justin teach", and leave the city out if in doubt).
  2. What passed: Tatian kept Justin's memory with open admiration. In his Address to the Greeks he calls him "the most admirable Justin" and repeats his teaching that the so-called gods are like robbers who seize people and then free them for ransom (TAT 7956–7960; ANF2 6526–6533). He says Crescens tried to bring death on "Justin, and indeed on me" (TAT 8105–8111; ANF2 6550–6556). The listener should hear that Tatian carried on Justin's attack on the gods and his quarrel with the philosophers.
  3. Who Tatian became: Eusebius, about 150 years later, says Tatian made "a certain combination and collection of the Gospels", which he called the *Diatessaron* (Greek for "through four"), the four Gospels woven into one book (EUS 26424–26426, 26433; Widely Accepted that it was his). A later Syriac account of the church at Edessa, the Doctrine of Addai, set in the apostle Addai's day and legendary for that day (Inferential-Thin), has the people there coming to hear "the reading of the Old and New Testament, of the Diatessaron" (ADD 65; a later text, its date the editor's). Phillips's "seems to have been publicly read at Edessa up to the fourth century" is an editor's note (ADD 221). Spoken, keeping its witness: "one book woven from the four Gospels; a later Syriac account of the church at Edessa says the people came to hear it read". Give no date for that reading, and name no place beyond Edessa.
  4. The break, from a hostile witness: Irenaeus says that after Justin's martyrdom Tatian "separated from the Church", set up as a teacher of his own, and taught that marriage was corruption (ANF1 33663–33672; H4). This is Irenaeus's judgment ("excited and puffed up") and must be credited to him. Hippolytus says only that Tatian "did not entertain similar opinions with his master" (ANF5 13005–13006).
  Carry it through what each did. The story may end on Tatian's book, read aloud at Edessa, or on "philosopher and martyr"; it must not end on the break with the Church.

- **Links:**
  - Tatian: heard him teach (so Irenaeus and Hippolytus); praised him and repeated his teaching in writing (TAT 7956–7960).
  - Irenaeus of Lyon: quoted his book (ANF1 44907–44914, 53355); the first to mention his martyrdom, in McGiffert's judgment as editor (EUS 23765).
  - Marcion of Pontus: wrote against him while Marcion was still teaching (R1 729–731).
  - Tertullian: named him "philosopher and martyr" (ANF3 43162).
  - Hippolytus of Rome: called Tatian his disciple (ANF5 13005).
  - Eusebius of Caesarea: wrote his life and quoted his books (EUS 23744–23873, 24205–24315).
  - Epiphanius of Salamis: recorded his martyrdom, with a mistaken emperor (EPI 15927–15935).
  - Jerome: wrote his life and listed his works (JER 39966–39997).
  - Antoninus Pius and his sons: wrote to (R1 68–71).
  - Trypho: argued with (AR 87–89).
  - Crescens: public opponent (R2 273–293).
  - Rusticus: judged him (GEB 1309 ff.).

- **Who tells it:** How-we-know gets one sentence in the story, at the trial ("the account of his trial, by an author we do not know, has him say …"). The doubt is not the story.
  - **Justin himself**, in his own time: the Dialogue, the two Apologies. Almost everything about his life before the trial is his own account; the conversion is told as a scene to win a reader, and the listener should hear it as his telling ("he writes", "he says").
  - **The trial record (Acts of Justin)**: author unknown. Its opening and closing sentences are a later frame; Delehaye (1921) judges the hearing itself drawn from a witness's record, perhaps the court's own, and calls it the oldest such account after Polycarp's (DEL 4950–4955, 4996–5013; H8). McGiffert dates the text as we have it probably to the third century, "comparatively truthful" (EUS 23767–23771); Dods: nothing known of date or author, yet "generally reckoned among the most trustworthy of the Martyria" (ANF1 29216–29218). Two texts are held: Otto's, reprinted by Gebhardt, from one late manuscript; and Franchi's, in Knopf–Krüger, from manuscripts of the 9th–10th centuries (KK 1793–1812; DEL 5043–5048; D7, H7). Neither clears up the address in Rome (DEL 5054–5057). Core facts (Rusticus, refusal, beheading): Widely Accepted. Card material beyond the one sentence.
  - **Tatian**, in his own time; his hearer according to Irenaeus and Hippolytus; also Crescens's personal enemy, so his portrait of Crescens is a partisan's.
  - **Irenaeus**, about fifteen years after; hostile to Tatian.
  - **Eusebius**, about 150 years after; quotes Justin and Tatian; the first to name the Diatessaron, which he had not seen (McGiffert, EUS 26433–26435).
  - **The Doctrine of Addai**: a later Syriac account, legendary for the apostle's day; the narrator names it as "a later Syriac account of the church at Edessa" whenever the Edessa reading is told.
  - **Epiphanius**, about 210 years after; unreliable on date and age.

- **Do not say:**
  - "Crescens had him killed" / "Crescens betrayed him." Say: Justin expected Crescens or others to plot his death; Tatian says Crescens tried to bring about the death of both; Eusebius and Jerome, much later, say it was Crescens's plot. The trial record does not name Crescens.
  - "The court transcript" / "his exact words at the trial." Say: "the account of his trial has him say …". No beat may be placed in a particular version of the Acts, or called the oldest or shortest form.
  - "A legend written long after" / "written down much later" for the trial account. The held scholarship does not support it (DEL 4950–4972, 5009–5013; McGiffert dates the text as we have it, not the hearing). Say "the account of his trial, by an author we do not know".
  - "The baths of Martinus" / "Martinus's house." Say: "rooms above a bath-house".
  - "Tatian says he studied under Justin." Tatian does not say so. Say: "Irenaeus and Hippolytus say Tatian was his hearer".
  - Tertullian made him "the first" or "the foremost" writer against heresies. "First" is only list order (ANF3 43161–43163). Say "Tertullian names him, philosopher and martyr, among those who wrote against the heresy-founders in their own day."
  - "Justin's school" / "he founded a school at Rome". The record has him lodging above a bath-house and sharing "the words of truth" with anyone who came (GEB 1347–1354). Say "taught anyone who came to him".
  - "The Diatessaron came from Justin's teaching" / "Justin used a Gospel harmony". Not in the held sources. The story may end on Tatian's book only if it does not imply that Justin passed it on.
  - "Executed" for Ptolemaeus. Justin says led away and punished (R2 148–158).
  - "From Samaria he set out" for the search through the schools. The Dialogue names no place.
  - "Tatian heard him at Rome" as the sources' statement. No held source names the city of the hearing.
  - "Tatian made the Diatessaron under Justin" / "Justin's school produced it" / "Tatian wrote it in Syriac" / "the first Gospel harmony". None is in the held sources. Say: Eusebius says Tatian wove the four Gospels into one book.
  - "The Diatessaron was the Syriac churches' Bible / their only Gospel." The Doctrine of Addai says only that it was read at Edessa, with the Old and New Testament. Say: "read aloud at Edessa".
  - "Tatian founded the Encratites" as plain fact. Say: Irenaeus, hostile to him, says Tatian brought in a teaching against marriage after Justin's death.
  - Crescens "stirred up the persecution": the ANF editor's preface (ANF1 29209–29211), a modern view; the Acts do not name Crescens.
  - "Rusticus, Marcus Aurelius's teacher." Not in any held source. Say only "Rusticus, the prefect of Rome".
  - "Under the emperor Marcus Aurelius" as fact. Say "about the year 165"; if the reign is named, say it is the likelier of two traditions (McGiffert). Never "under Hadrian".
  - "He wrote to the emperors" in a way that implies both Apologies went to the emperor. The First Apology is to Antoninus Pius, his sons and the Senate. Say "to the emperor" or "to the emperor and his sons".
  - "Poisoned" / "hemlock." The record says scourged and beheaded; hemlock is a later addition Dods calls "absurd".
  - "He met Trypho at Ephesus" in Justin's voice. Eusebius places it at Ephesus; Justin names only a colonnade.
  - "The old man was a hermit / an angel / a Christian teacher named …" Justin gives no name or calling.
  - "The emperor read it / was moved / stopped the persecution." No reply is recorded.
  - "Six companions" is right; not "five" or "seven" unless counting Justin.
  - "Euelpistus was converted by Justin." He gladly heard Justin but had the faith from his parents.
  - "Justin was a Samaritan by birth" as settled. Say he came from a Roman town in Samaria and called the Samaritans "my people"; his family background is disputed (D5).
  - "Justin and Trypho debated as friends" without the hard edge. Tell both.
  - "The first Christian philosopher" / "the first apologist." No held source says so; Jerome names Quadratus and Aristides as earlier defenders (`npnf206_jerome-principal-works.xml` 16580–16596).
  - "The earliest / fullest description of Christian worship" / "the first Mass" / "the Eucharist as the church celebrates it today." No held source ranks or links it so. Describe the gathering only.
  - "The four Gospels were read" / "the New Testament was read" for the Sunday readings. Say "the memoirs of the apostles" or "the prophets".
  - "To show the emperor Christians had nothing to hide" as a modern gloss beyond his words. Say his own reason: so that he would not seem to deal unfairly by leaving it out.
  - "Atheists" for *atheoi*: say "godless", or "called godless because they would not worship the gods".
  - "Safe" for ἀσφαλῆ in any paraphrase of Quote 1: say "sure".
  - A "so" or "because" linking his conversion to fear or to the old man alone: he gives two reasons in two places, the prophets (Dialogue) and the courage of Christians facing death (Second Apology).

- **Leave out:**
  - Epiphanius's date under Hadrian and his age of thirty: late and, says Holl, impossible (EPI 15961–15962).
  - The quarrel over whether Eusebius altered Tatian's sentence (EUS 23880–23910): a scholars' dispute; card material.
  - Whether there was one apology or two (D6): card material.
  - Hadrian's letter appended to the First Apology (R1 1794–1803): a side document of disputed standing.
  - Justin's account of fallen angels and demons (R2 179–197), apart from the robbers-and-ransom point Tatian repeats.
  - The baptism rite of First Apology 61, beyond the clause that introduces the Sunday account: a side road at this length.
  - Theodoret's replacing of the Diatessaron with the four Gospels, and Ephrem's commentary on it: editors' reports, and Tatian's own story, not Justin's (H4).
  - Tatian's denial of Adam's salvation and his Aeons: Irenaeus's charges, a side road here.
  - Eusebius's "a genuine lover of the true philosophy" (H5): card material.
  - The works transmitted under his name that are not his (D8).
  - The wife's divorce in Second Apology 2, beyond what is needed to explain Ptolemaeus's case.
  - Any claim about versions (recensions) of the Acts: the census's "three recensions" rests on Musurillo, not held.
