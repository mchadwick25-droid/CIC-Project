# Review 2: Justin Martyr

Brief reviewed: `justin-martyr.brief.txt` (fresh draft from the revised outline). Read against `Outline.md`, `Source_Sheet.md`, `Outline_Check_2.md`, and the kit (`Standard.md`, `Shapes.md`, `Process.md`).

## The test, from one hearing

- **Who:** Justin, a Greek-speaking philosopher from a Roman town in Samaria who became a Christian, taught at Rome, and was beheaded there about 165.
- **What happened:** He wrote to the emperor to defend Christians, who were punished for the name alone. He argued that they should be judged by their deeds and that Christ is the Word every race has shared in, Socrates included. He described their Sunday gathering, told how he came to the faith, argued with Trypho, and at his trial told the prefect Rusticus he knew for certain that he would be rewarded.
- **Why the connection matters:** His books were used against Marcion. His hearer Tatian carried on his attack on the gods and wove the four Gospels into one book, which a later Syriac account says was read aloud at Edessa. The church kept Tertullian's name for him, "philosopher and martyr".

All three lines can be filled from one hearing.

## The heart of his life (from the census entry and what he is known for)

Justin was a philosopher who found in Christ and the prophets "the only philosophy that is sure and useful", and never stopped calling himself a philosopher. At Rome he taught anyone who came to him, and he wrote to the emperor Antoninus Pius in defence of Christians, who were condemned for the name alone. His defence asks that they be judged by what they do, claims Christ as the Word (*logos*, also "reason") in whom every race has a share, and sets out openly what Christians did on Sunday. About 165 he refused to sacrifice and was beheaded at Rome. That death is half of the name the church kept: philosopher and martyr. (Census knownFor: "A philosopher who became a Christian teacher at Rome, defended Christians to the emperor, and was beheaded for refusing to sacrifice.")

### Does the first minute carry it?

No, not all of it. Word 150 falls at the end of "He counted Socrates among them." By then the listener has the philosopher, the Christian, the teaching at Rome, the petition to the emperor, the name-and-deeds claim and the Word, with Quote 2. That part of the heart is given real weight. But the Sunday account runs from word 151 to about 195, and the death, the "martyr" in his name and the last part of what the census says he is known for, comes only at word 197 to 208 (about 1:20). The outline asked for both inside the first ~150 words: one clause for Sunday and one forward sentence for the death to close beat 1 (Outline, beat 1; Outline_Check_2, M1). See B1.

Where I got lost: nowhere seriously. The step back from his death to "His road to that faith" is signposted and works. Nothing reads as a children's book or talks down.

## Verdict: REVISE

## Findings

**B1. BLOCKING. The death, part of the heart and of his name, comes after the first minute.** It lands at word ~197–208 (about 1:20), after the Sunday account; the census names the beheading as one of the three things he is known for, and the outline's beat 1 asked for one forward sentence on the death, and one clause naming the Sunday account, inside the first ~150 words. Change: close the first movement on his death. Right after "He counted Socrates among them," say in one sentence that about the year 165 he was beheaded at Rome for this faith. Then name the Sunday account in a clause ("He also told the emperor, openly, what Christians did on Sunday: ...") and let its detail follow. To keep word 150 near that sentence, take the cuts below from inside the first minute first (the 33-word sentence in M4 gives a few words; "son of Priscus" another three). The trial paragraph then deepens a death the listener already knows. The outline already asks for this, so no new Do not say line is needed.

**M1. MINOR. Attribution blur on Tatian.** "Irenaeus and Hippolytus also say that one of his hearers was Tatian, a man from Assyria, who praised him and carried on his attack ..." puts Tatian's own praise and his Assyrian birth inside what Irenaeus and Hippolytus say. The praise and the robbers teaching are Tatian's own book (ANF2 6526–6533). Change: "Irenaeus and Hippolytus say that one of his hearers was Tatian, a man from Assyria. In his own book, Tatian praised him and carried on his attack on the gods and the philosophers."

**M2. MINOR. Trypho is not marked as Justin's own account.** The outline's "Who tells it" asks that his life before the trial be heard as his telling. The conversion has "he says"; the Trypho beat does not. Change: "In a long book, he set out his argument with a Jew, Trypho, ..." or "He wrote up his argument with ..."

**M3. MINOR. "his books against Marcion".** Irenaeus cites Justin's "book against Marcion", singular (ANF1 44907). Heard once, the plural can also sound like several anti-Marcion books. Change to "his book against Marcion".

**M4. MINOR. One sentence runs 33 words**, past the ~30 limit: "A philosopher from a Roman town in Samaria, he taught anyone who came to his rooms in Rome, and he was a Christian at a time when the name itself was the charge." Change: split after "Rome", or drop "and he was a Christian at a time when" for "a Christian when ...".

## Length: 511 words, about 3:25 in Neo's voice

The outline's budget is about 420–450 words, and the Standard's ceiling for a major figure is three minutes. To reach about 450 without losing the heart (the defence and its two claims, the Word, the death, the turn, Trypho's hard edge with the friendship, the trial exchange, Tatian and Edessa, the name), cut in this order:

1. **The Crescens sentence** (about 22 words): "He wrote, too, that he expected to be killed, perhaps by Crescens, a Cynic philosopher who denounced Christians in public." It stands alone here: nothing later picks it up, and the trial account does not name him. Card material (it is already in the card's uncertain list).
2. **Compress the Sunday detail to one sentence** (about 12 words saved), keeping the memoirs of the apostles or the prophets, the bread and wine, and the gifts for orphans, widows, prisoners and strangers: "They heard the memoirs of the apostles or the prophets, prayed, shared bread and wine, and gave for orphans, widows, prisoners and strangers."
3. **Inside the first minute, small trims** to make room for the death sentence (B1): "son of Priscus" (3 words), and the M4 split, which shortens the 33-word sentence.
4. **If still long, the second reason for his turn** (about 14 words): "Elsewhere he gives a second reason: he had seen slandered Christians unafraid of death." It deepens the turn but is not the heart. Without it, no "so" or "because" may tie the turn to the old man alone; the brief as written has none.

Items 1–3 bring it to about 470, and item 4 to about 455. Do not cut the Trypho hard edge, the Irenaeus break (it is the hostile witness the outline requires), the Edessa reading, or the trial exchange.
