In the middle of the second century, Justin, son of Priscus, wrote to the emperor on behalf of people of every race who were unjustly hated, himself among them.

A philosopher from a Roman town in Samaria, he taught anyone who came to his rooms in Rome, and he was a Christian at a time when the name itself was the charge. A name, he argued, is neither good nor bad, so judge Christians by what they do. The Greek word logos means both "word" and "reason", and for Justin, Christ was that Word.

"We have been taught that Christ is the firstborn of God. We have shown already that he is the Word, in whom every race of people has had a share. And those who lived with the Word are Christians, even if they were counted godless."

He counted Socrates among them. He also told the emperor what Christians did on the day called Sunday. They gathered to hear the memoirs of the apostles or the prophets, to pray and to share bread and wine, and their gifts went to orphans, widows, prisoners and strangers. About the year 165, he was beheaded at Rome for this faith.

His road to that faith had run through the schools of the philosophers. Then, walking alone by the sea, he met an old man who told him that the prophets, older than any philosopher, had seen and spoken the truth. At once, he says, a fire was lit in his soul. He had found "the only philosophy that is sure and useful", and that is why he still called himself a philosopher. Elsewhere he gives a second reason: he had seen slandered Christians unafraid of death.

He argued with a Jew, Trypho, that the prophets had spoken of Jesus, and told him that the sufferings of his people came to them justly. Yet the two parted as friends. He wrote, too, that he expected to be killed, perhaps by Crescens, a Cynic philosopher who denounced Christians in public.

The account of his trial, by an author we do not know, has him stand with six companions before Rusticus, the prefect of Rome. Rusticus asked, "So you suppose that you will go up into the heavens, to receive some good rewards?" Justin answered, "I do not suppose it. I know it for certain, and I am fully sure of it." Rusticus sentenced all who refused to sacrifice to be scourged and beheaded.

Within a generation, Irenaeus was quoting his books against Marcion, defending the one God who made the world. Irenaeus and Hippolytus also say that one of his hearers was Tatian, a man from Assyria, who praised him and carried on his attack on the gods and the philosophers. Irenaeus, hostile to Tatian, says that after Justin's death he left the Church. But Eusebius says Tatian wove the four Gospels into one book, and a later Syriac account of the church at Edessa says the people came to hear it read.

The church kept the name Tertullian gave him: Justin, philosopher and martyr.
