In the middle of the second century, Justin wrote to the emperor on behalf of people of every race who were unjustly hated, himself among them.

A philosopher from a Roman town in Samaria, he taught anyone who came to him in Rome. He was a Christian when the name itself was the charge. A name, he argued, is neither good nor bad, so judge Christians by what they do. The Greek logos means both "word" and "reason", and Justin called Christ that Word.

"We have been taught that Christ is the firstborn of God. We have shown already that he is the Word, in whom every race of people has had a share. And those who lived with the Word are Christians, even if they were counted godless."

He counted Socrates among them, and he set out openly what Christians did on Sunday. About the year 165, he was beheaded at Rome for this faith.

On Sunday, he wrote, Christians heard the memoirs of the apostles or the prophets, prayed, shared bread and wine, and gave for orphans, widows, prisoners and strangers.

His road to that faith ran through the philosophers' schools. Then, walking alone by the sea, he met an old man who told him that the prophets, older than any philosopher, had seen and spoken the truth. At once, he says, a fire was lit in his soul. He had found "the only philosophy that is sure and useful", and so he still called himself a philosopher.

He also wrote up his argument with a Jew, Trypho, that the prophets had spoken of Jesus, and told him that the sufferings of his people came to them justly. Yet the two parted as friends.

The account of his trial, by an author we do not know, has him stand with six companions before Rusticus, the prefect of Rome. Rusticus asked, "So you suppose that you will go up into the heavens, to receive some good rewards?" Justin answered, "I do not suppose it. I know it for certain, and I am fully sure of it." All who refused to sacrifice were scourged and beheaded.

Within a generation, Irenaeus was quoting his book against Marcion, defending the one God who made the world. Irenaeus and Hippolytus say that one of his hearers was Tatian, a man from Assyria. In his own book, Tatian praised him and carried on his attack on the gods and the philosophers. Irenaeus, hostile to Tatian, says that after Justin's death he left the Church. But Eusebius says Tatian wove the four Gospels into one book, and a later Syriac account of the church at Edessa says the people came to hear it read.

The church kept the name Tertullian gave him: Justin, philosopher and martyr.
