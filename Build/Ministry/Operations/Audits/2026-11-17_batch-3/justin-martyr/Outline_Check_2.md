# Outline check, round 2: Justin Martyr

## The checker's own account of the heart of his life (written before opening Outline.md)

Justin was a philosopher who went looking for the truth about God through the schools, and found it, by his own account, in the Hebrew prophets and in Christ: "the only philosophy that is sure and useful" (Dialogue 8). He did not stop being a philosopher. Keeping the philosopher's cloak, he taught at Rome and turned philosophy into a public defence of Christians at a time when the name alone was the charge.

His major contribution is that defence, written to the emperor Antoninus Pius and his sons (First Apology), and its three load-bearing moves:
1. **Judge the deed, not the name** (First Apology 2, 4; Second Apology 2): Christians should be punished only if proved to do wrong, not for being called Christians.
2. **Christ is the Word (logos, also "reason") that every race has shared in** (First Apology 46; Second Apology 10, 13): whoever lived by the Word, Socrates among them, was in that sense a Christian. This is the bridge he built between Greek philosophy and the faith.
3. **Show openly what Christians do** (First Apology 61-67): baptism and the Sunday gathering (readings, the president's teaching, bread and wine with water, the Amen, gifts for orphans, widows, prisoners and strangers), set out so that he would not seem to hide anything.

Alongside it: the argument with Trypho from the prophets (Dialogue), with its hard edge against the Jews told plainly; his writing against Marcion and the heresies, which Irenaeus quoted in defence of the one God who made the world; and his teaching circle, from which Tatian came (Irenaeus, Hippolytus). He expected to be killed, and about 165, before the prefect Rusticus, he refused to sacrifice and was scourged and beheaded with six others (later trial record, unknown author). Tertullian's "philosopher and martyr" became his name.

What the sources hold, in weight order: the books were kept and used (Irenaeus, Tertullian, Eusebius, Jerome, Photius); the death was kept as a record and became his name; his hearers carried him on. The reception of the Word idea and of the Sunday account is not traced in held later witnesses (sheet I4, I5), so those are told as his teaching, not as a proven legacy.

The heart, told in the first minute: a philosopher who found Christ as the true philosophy and used philosophy, before the emperor, to ask that Christians be judged by their deeds, to claim the Word shared by every people, and to show in the open what Christians did on Sunday.

## Findings

**B1. BLOCKING. The sweep missed a held critical text of the Acts of Justin and a held major scholarly judgment of it; the outline's "Who tells it" and how-we-know line are wrong as a result.**
- `cic/texts/knopf_ausgewaehlte-maertyrerakten-lat-grc-deu_krueger1929.txt` 1613-1819 prints the Acts of Justin from P. Franchi de' Cavalieri's new text (Studi e Testi 8, 1902), with an apparatus against Burkitt's oldest manuscript (C, JTS 11, 1910) and Otto (O) (1793-1812). The OCR is poor but legible enough at key points. The Source Sheet (D7, E11) and the outline ("The held text is one, Otto's, reprinted by Gebhardt") say only one text is held. That is false.
- `cic/texts/delehaye_passions-martyrs-genres-litteraires-fra_1921.txt` 4949-5056 (Delehaye, *Les passions des martyrs*, 119-121): the passion of Justin "a toujours passé pour une relation strictement contemporaine"; the opening and closing sentences are a later frame that "se détachent sans effort"; the compiler "disposait d'une relation de l'audience écrite par un témoin", possibly the official record of the interrogation; Otto, Gebhardt, Rauschen and Knopf's earlier edition depend on one very recent manuscript, Vat. 655, while Franchi collated three of the 9th-10th centuries and Burkitt found a fourth of the 9th; and no witness clears up the address in Rome, because the manuscript behind the whole tradition was damaged there. The sweep does not list either file.
- Effect on the outline: (a) "Who tells it" gives only McGiffert's "probably the third century" and the "tenth-century collection" story, so the one how-we-know sentence ("written down later by an unknown hand") carries one side of a judgment the held scholarship divides on; (b) Quote 3 is rendered from the late single-manuscript text when a better text is held. In the Franchi text, as far as the OCR shows (1782-1784), Rusticus says ἀμοιβάς τινας χρηστάς ("some good rewards"; apparatus 1792: C reads differently, O lacks the word) and Justin says ἀλλ' ἀκριβῶς ἐπίσταμαι ("I know it exactly").
- Correction: (1) Add both files to the Library sweep under "Used". (2) Rewrite the trial-record entry in "Who tells it" (and Source Sheet D7): "Author unknown. Its opening and closing sentences are a later frame; Delehaye (1921) judges the hearing itself drawn from a witness's record, perhaps the court's own, and calls it the oldest such account after Polycarp's; McGiffert dates the text as we have it probably to the third century. Two texts are held: Otto's, reprinted by Gebhardt, from one late manuscript; and Franchi's, in Knopf-Krüger, from manuscripts of the 9th-10th centuries." Confidence on the core (Rusticus, refusal, beheading) stays Widely Accepted. (3) Change the spoken how-we-know line to "the account of his trial, by an author we do not know, has him say ...", dropping "later", or keep "later" only if Delehaye's view is set beside it on the card. (4) Check Quote 3 against Franchi's text and either render from it ("So you suppose that you will go up to heaven to receive some good rewards?" "I do not suppose it. I know it exactly, and I am fully sure of it.") or state that the outline follows the Otto text and why. (5) Add to Do not say: "a court transcript" stays banned; but also not "a legend written long after", which the held scholarship does not support.
