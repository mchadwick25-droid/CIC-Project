In the middle of the second century, a man from a Roman town in Samaria went looking for the truth about God. His name was Justin, and he tried the schools of philosophy one by one. A Stoic taught him nothing about God, and with a Platonist he hoped, foolishly as he later saw it, to see God soon.

Then, walking alone by the sea, he met an old man he never saw again, who told him that the prophets, older than all the philosophers, had spoken the truth. Justin writes: "At once a fire was lit in my soul. A love took hold of me for the prophets, and for those men who are friends of Christ. As I turned his words over in my mind, I found this to be the only philosophy that is sure and useful. That is how, and why, I am a philosopher." Elsewhere he adds a second reason: he had watched slandered Christians face death without fear.

Still wearing the philosopher's cloak, he argued with a Jew named Trypho that the prophets spoke of Jesus. He told Trypho that the Jews' sufferings had come to them justly, yet the two parted as friends.

In Rome, where being a Christian was itself the charge, he taught in rooms above a bath-house. He sent the emperor Antoninus Pius a formal defence, asking that Christians be judged by what they did, not by their name. Christ, he wrote, is the Word, in Greek logos, which also means reason. Every race has shared in him, so Socrates, who lived by the Word, was a Christian too. No reply is recorded. When a Cynic named Crescens denounced Christians in public, Justin wrote that he expected to be plotted against and killed, perhaps by him.

About the year 165 he stood with six others before Rusticus, the prefect of Rome. In the account of the trial, written later by an unknown hand, Rusticus asks, "So you suppose that you will go up into the heavens, to receive some reward?" Justin answers, "I do not suppose it. I know it, and I am fully sure of it." They refused to sacrifice to the gods, and were scourged and beheaded.

His books outlived him. Within a generation Irenaeus was quoting him in defence of the one God who made the world, against Marcion, who taught of another, greater God.

His hearers carried him on. Irenaeus and Hippolytus say Tatian heard him teach, and Tatian praises him as most admirable, repeating his teaching that the so-called gods are robbers who hold people for ransom. Crescens, Tatian says, tried to bring death on them both. Irenaeus, no friend of Tatian, adds that after Justin's death his hearer left the Church.

Tertullian gave him the name the church has kept: Justin, philosopher and martyr.
