# Outline: Justin Martyr

File abbreviations and full line references are in `Source_Sheet.md` (same folder). R1 = First Apology (Greek), R2 = Second Apology (Greek), AR = Dialogue with Trypho (Greek), GEB = Acts of Justin (Greek, Gebhardt), ANF1 = English of Justin, the Acts and Irenaeus, TAT/ANF2 = Tatian, EUS = Eusebius with McGiffert's notes.

- **Length:** stretch toward 3 minutes (about 400–450 words). Reason: a major figure (census `major: true`) with an unusually full record: his own account of his search and turn, his petition to the emperor, a near-court record of his trial, and later witnesses who quoted him. The martyr shape needs room for the life and what he left, not only the end.

- **Setting:**
  - Time: the middle of the second century; the First Apology about 150–155, from his own "a hundred and fifty years" since Christ's birth (R1 1227–1228); death about 165 (McGiffert's reckoning from Rusticus's prefecture, EUS 23775–23780). Spoken: "the middle of the second century", "about the year 165".
  - Place: born at Flavia Neapolis in Samaria (R1 72–75; ANF1 13965–13969); taught at Rome, "for the second time" living there (GEB 1347–1354; ANF1 29309–29312). Spoken: "a Roman town in Samaria", "Rome".
  - Tradition: told within the census tradition "The Second-Century Greek Apologists" (short name "The Apologists"). Spoken: "the Greek-speaking Christian writers who defended the faith to the Roman world". An "apology" here is a formal defence, the word's old courtroom sense, not saying sorry.
  - The danger: being a Christian was itself the charge. A teacher, Ptolemaeus, was asked "this one thing only, whether he was a Christian", and executed; a bystander, Lucius, who protested, was led away too (R2 136–158).

- **Thread:** A philosopher who found his philosophy in Christ, argued in public and to the emperor that Christians were condemned for a name alone, and taught that every people had shared in the Word.

- **Shape:** Martyr.

- **Openings:**
  1. A stranger stops him at dawn in a colonnade and says "Philosopher, greetings!", because of the cloak he wears. The stranger is Trypho, a Jew who has fled a war (AR 72–74, 87–89).
  2. The prefect asks, "Where do you meet?" Justin answers that the God of the Christians is not shut in by any place, then gives his address: a room above the baths of one Martinus (GEB 1339–1354; ANF1 29302–29312).
  3. An old man by the sea speaks with him, sends him to the prophets, walks off, and is never seen again (AR 175–180, 428–429).

- **Story beats:**
  1. **The search.** From Flavia Neapolis, he went looking for the truth about God through the schools of philosophy. A Stoic taught him nothing about God; a Peripatetic asked for his fee first; a Pythagorean told him to learn music, astronomy and geometry first; with a Platonist he thought, "in my stupidity", that he would soon see God. Why: he wanted knowledge of God, which he calls the goal of Plato's philosophy. Source: AR 139–169. Documented (his own account).
  2. **The turn.** Walking alone near the sea, he met an old man who told him the prophets, older than all the philosophers, had seen and spoken the truth. "A fire was lit in my soul." He kept the philosopher's cloak afterwards (Eusebius: "in the guise of a philosopher he preached the divine word"). He also writes that seeing slandered Christians unafraid of death convinced him they could not be living in vice. Sources: AR 175–180, 399–435; R2 368–370; EUS 22442, 22455–22456. Documented (own account); the cloak also in Eusebius, later.
  3. **The argument with Trypho.** He debated a Jewish refugee, Trypho, that the prophets spoke of Jesus. The argument has a hard edge: Justin tells Trypho that the Jews' present sufferings, their land laid waste and Jerusalem closed to them, come to them "justly", and that synagogues curse believers in Christ. Yet the two parted as friends, each praying for the other. Sources: AR 87–89, 788–806, 6589–6607. Documented (Justin's account). Place: Eusebius says Ephesus (EUS 24311); Justin does not name the city.
  4. **The teacher and the petition.** At Rome he taught anyone who came to his lodging. He addressed a petition to the emperor Antoninus Pius, his adopted sons and the Senate, on behalf of people "of every race" who were unjustly hated. He asked them to judge Christians by what they did, not by the name. He wrote that Christ is the Word in whom every race has shared, so that Socrates and Heraclitus, who lived by the Word, were in a sense Christians, though called godless. The Greek *logos* means both "word" and "reason"; Justin uses both senses at once. Sources: GEB 1347–1354; R1 68–75, 78–96, 1230–1235; R2 136–158. Documented. No reply from the emperor is recorded.
  5. **The enemy he expected.** He clashed in public with Crescens, a Cynic philosopher, and wrote that he expected to be plotted against and killed, perhaps by Crescens, "that lover of bravado". Why: Crescens was denouncing Christians as godless in public. Source: R2 273–293; ANF1 16849–16851. Documented that he expected it; whether Crescens caused his death is Contested (see Do not say).
  6. **The trial.** Arrested with six others, he stood before Rusticus, prefect of Rome, and was told to obey the gods. He said he had tried to learn every teaching and had settled on the Christians'. His companions answered one by one: Euelpistus, an imperial slave, said he had gladly heard Justin but had the faith from his parents; Paeon said the same of his parents. Asked whether he really thought he would go up to heaven, Justin said he did not suppose it; he knew. Asked to sacrifice, he said no one in his right mind turns from true worship to godlessness. To "sacrifice" was the test: an offering to the gods for the emperor's order. Source: GEB 1300–1432; ANF1 29266–29387. Widely Accepted (near-court record, date unknown).
  7. **The death.** Rusticus sentenced all who refused to sacrifice to be scourged and beheaded "according to the laws". Some believers secretly took the bodies and buried them in a fitting place. Source: GEB 1433–1441; ANF1 29396–29406. Widely Accepted. Year about 165: Dominant Modern Reconstruction.

- **What came of them** (heaviest first; about a third of the story):
  1. **His books were kept and used against rival teachers.** Within a generation Irenaeus quoted his book against Marcion: "I would not have believed the Lord Himself, if He had announced any other than He who is our framer, maker, and nourisher" (ANF1 44907–44914). Tertullian named "Justin, philosopher and martyr" first among those who had written against the founders of heresies (ANF3 43162–43163). Eusebius copied long passages and listed his works (EUS 24209–24212); Photius was still reading him in the ninth century (PHO 11699–11718). *Defended:* the one God who made the world, against Marcion's teaching of a greater, other God (R1 729–731; Irenaeus as above). His own book against all the heresies is lost (R1 743–745).
  2. **His death was kept as a record and became his name.** The trial record was copied and read; Tertullian's "philosopher and martyr" is the name the church kept. (McGiffert, editor: the tradition "is crystallized in his name", EUS 23764–23766.)
  3. **His hearers carried him on.** Tatian heard him at Rome and called him "the most admirable Justin" (TAT 7956; ANF2 6526). Euelpistus said at the trial that he had gladly heard Justin's words (GEB 1386–1387).
  4. **His idea of the Word shared by every race** (R1 1230–1235; R2 332–337, 405–408). Documented as his teaching; this outline does not trace later witnesses carrying it.
  5. Optional, if room: **the earliest step-by-step description of a Sunday gathering**: readings from "the memoirs of the apostles" or the prophets, a talk by the one presiding, prayers, bread and wine with water, the people's "Amen", portions taken by deacons to the absent, and a collection for orphans, widows, prisoners and strangers (R1 1759–1778; ANF1 16502–16528). "Earliest" is not claimed by any held source; say "one of the fullest early descriptions" only as the narrator's plain description, or simply describe it.

- **Quotes, 2 or 3 at most:**
  1. **Dialogue 8.1–2**, rendered from the Greek (AR 429–435; ANF1 17853–17857 as cross-check):
     "At once a fire was lit in my soul. A love took hold of me for the prophets, and for those men who are friends of Christ. As I turned his words over in my mind, I found this to be the only philosophy that is sure and useful. That is how, and why, I am a philosopher."
     Job: the turn, in his own voice, and the paradox that he became a Christian without ceasing to call himself a philosopher.
  2. **First Apology 46**, rendered from the Greek (R1 1230–1233; ANF1 15692–15693 as cross-check):
     "We have been taught that Christ is the firstborn of God. We have shown already that he is the Word, in whom every race of people has had a share. And those who lived with the Word are Christians, even if they were counted godless."
     Job: his central idea, in the petition he sent to the emperor. The narrator must first make clear that "Word" (*logos*) also means "reason", and may then name Socrates and Heraclitus as his examples, which follow in the same sentence (R1 1233–1234).
  3. **Acts of Justin V.3**, rendered from the Greek (GEB 1410–1411, 1420–1421; OCR, checked against ANF1 29371–29374):
     Rusticus: "So you suppose that you will go up into the heavens, to receive some reward?" Justin: "I do not suppose it. I know it, and I am fully sure of it."
     Job: the stand itself, at the trial, in the record's own words.

- **Connection to carry into the story:** Justin to **Tatian**, his hearer. Irenaeus, writing about fifteen years after Justin's death, says Tatian "was a hearer of Justin's, and as long as he continued with him he expressed no such views; but after his martyrdom he separated from the Church" and taught his own system, condemning marriage (ANF1 33663–33667). Tatian, in his own Address to the Greeks, calls him "the most admirable Justin" and says Crescens tried to bring death on "Justin, and indeed on me" because Justin exposed the philosophers as greedy frauds (TAT 7956, 8105–8111; ANF2 6526, 6550–6556). What the listener must understand: Justin ran something like a philosopher's school in his lodging (GEB 1347–1353), and Tatian was one of those who came to hear him; teacher and pupil faced the same enemy. Tatian kept his teacher's memory with open admiration, yet after Justin's death went his own way. That "his own way" is Irenaeus's judgment, a hostile one, and must be credited to Irenaeus (Hippolytus says the same: Tatian "did not entertain similar opinions with his master", ANF5 13005–13006). Carry it through what each did, not a summary sentence.

- **Links:**
  - Tatian: heard him teach; praised him in writing (TAT 7956).
  - Irenaeus of Lyon: quoted his book (ANF1 44907–44914, 53355); first to mention his martyrdom.
  - Marcion of Pontus: wrote against him while Marcion was still teaching (R1 729–731).
  - Tertullian: named him "philosopher and martyr" (ANF3 43162).
  - Hippolytus of Rome: called Tatian his disciple (ANF5 13005).
  - Eusebius of Caesarea: wrote his life and quoted his books (EUS 23744–23873, 24205–24315).
  - Epiphanius of Salamis: recorded his martyrdom, with a mistaken emperor (EPI 15927–15935).
  - Jerome: wrote his life and listed his works (JER 39966–39997).
  - Antoninus Pius and his sons: wrote to (R1 68–71).
  - Trypho: argued with (AR 87–89).
  - Crescens: public opponent (R2 273–293).
  - Rusticus: judged him (GEB 1309 ff.).

- **Who tells it:**
  - **Justin himself**, in his own time: the Dialogue, the two Apologies. Almost everything about his life before the trial is his own account; the conversion story is told as a scene to win a reader, and the listener should hear it as his telling.
  - **The trial record (Acts of Justin)**: anonymous, date unknown. Dods (translator): "generally reckoned among the most trustworthy of the Martyria" (ANF1 29216–29218). McGiffert (editor) thinks it later, "probably the third century", but "comparatively truthful" (EUS 23767–23771). It reads like a court transcript: question, answer, sentence.
  - **Tatian**, his hearer, in his own time; also Crescens's personal enemy, so his portrait of Crescens is a partisan's.
  - **Irenaeus**, about fifteen years after; hostile to Tatian.
  - **Eusebius**, about 150 years after; quotes Justin and Tatian, and draws from Tatian more than Tatian says (see Do not say).
  - **Epiphanius**, about 210 years after; unreliable on date and age.

- **Do not say:**
  - "Crescens had him killed" / "Crescens betrayed him." Say: Justin expected Crescens or others to plot his death; Tatian says Crescens tried to bring about the death of both Justin and Tatian; Eusebius and Jerome, much later, say it was Crescens's plot. The trial record does not name Crescens.
  - "Rusticus, Marcus Aurelius's teacher." Not in any held source. Say only "Rusticus, the prefect of Rome".
  - "Under the emperor Marcus Aurelius" as fact. Say "about the year 165"; if the reign is named, say it is the likelier of two traditions (McGiffert). Never "under Hadrian".
  - "Poisoned" / "hemlock." The record says scourged and beheaded; hemlock is a later addition Dods calls "absurd".
  - "He met Trypho at Ephesus" in Justin's voice. Eusebius places it at Ephesus; Justin names only a colonnade.
  - "The old man was a hermit / an angel / a Christian teacher named …" Justin gives no name or calling; he says only an old man, gentle and dignified, whom he never saw again.
  - "The emperor read it / was moved / stopped the persecution." No reply is recorded.
  - "Six companions" is right; do not say "five" (one heading lists five names) or "seven" unless counting Justin.
  - "Euelpistus was converted by Justin." He said he gladly heard Justin but had the faith from his parents.
  - "Justin was a Samaritan by birth" as settled. Say he came from a Roman town in Samaria and called the Samaritans "my people"; his family background is disputed.
  - "Justin and Trypho debated as friends" without the hard edge. If the debate is told, say that Justin told Trypho the Jews' sufferings were deserved, as well as that they parted as friends.
  - "The first Christian philosopher" / "the first apologist." No held source says so; Jerome, in a letter, names Quadratus and Aristides as earlier defenders, whose "example was afterwards followed by Justin" (`npnf206_jerome-principal-works.xml` 16580–16596).
  - "Atheists" for *atheoi*: say "godless", or "called godless because they would not worship the gods". The modern word misleads.
  - "Safe" for ἀσφαλῆ in any paraphrase of Q1: say "sure".
  - A "so" or "because" linking his conversion to fear or to the old man alone: he gives two reasons in two places, the prophets (Dialogue) and the courage of Christians facing death (Second Apology).

- **Leave out:**
  - Epiphanius's date under Hadrian and his age of thirty: late and, says Holl, impossible (EPI 15961–15962).
  - The quarrel over whether Eusebius altered Tatian's sentence (EUS 23880–23910): a scholars' dispute; it belongs on the card.
  - Whether there was one apology or two (EUS 22600–22601): card material.
  - Hadrian's letter appended to the First Apology (R1 1794–1803): a side document of disputed standing.
  - Justin's account of fallen angels and demons (R2 179–197): true to him but a side road at this length.
  - The works transmitted under his name that are not his (Hortatory Address, Discourse to the Greeks, On the Sole Government of God, Letter to Diognetus).
  - The wife's divorce in Second Apology 2, beyond what is needed to explain Ptolemaeus's case.
  - The three versions of the Acts: rests on a source not held (Musurillo).
