John Wyclif sits in the master's chair in the Augustinian schools at Oxford, arguing about the bread of the Mass, as the chancellor's ban on that teaching is read aloud. His enemies, the Carmelite friars who kept the record, say he was shaken, but answered that no one could overturn his view.

He taught in fourteenth-century England, where even a hostile Leicester chronicle called him the most eminent doctor of theology of his day. He made scripture the test of the Western church's wealth and power, and wanted its clergy back in the poverty of the apostles.

The church taught that at the priest's words the bread's substance changes, and only its appearance remains. Wyclif agreed that Christ's body truly and really is the sacramental bread, but held that it is still bread. In 1382 a council in London condemned his conclusions while an earthquake shook the city, and he was banished from Oxford.

A tract under his name says Christ and his apostles taught in the language the people knew best. An English Bible came out of his circle, though how much was his own work is unknown. The Leicester chronicle complains that he put the Gospel into English for lay people and women who could read, so that its pearl was trampled by pigs.

Summoned to Rome in 1384, he wrote that he would humbly have come, but God had compelled him otherwise, which a modern editor reads as failing health. No friend left any record of his life or death. That editor's account is that a stroke took his speech at Mass in his parish of Lutterworth that December, and he died two days later.

His teaching outlived him. In 1407 his follower William Thorpe told Archbishop Arundel that many who talked with Wyclif wrote his teaching down and lived by it, and that many men and women held it still. In Prague, Jan Hus built his own book on the Church from Wyclif's, paragraph after paragraph.

In May 1415 the Council of Constance declared Wyclif a heretic and ordered his bones dug up, and Pope Martin V later ordered them burned.

A month after that verdict, Hus stood before the same council and spoke of a man he knew only through his books. "I do not know where John Wyclif's soul is. I hope that he is saved, and I fear that he may be damned. Yet in hope I would wish my soul to be where John Wyclif's soul is." The council shook their heads and mocked him.
