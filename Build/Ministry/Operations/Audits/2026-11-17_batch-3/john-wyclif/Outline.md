# Outline: John Wyclif

All files are under `cic/texts/`. Line numbers are the file's own. Full evidence is in `Source_Sheet.md` (items S, E, I, Q, and B for the Bible strand).

- **The heart of their life:** He held that God's law belongs to everyone, in the tongue they know best, and that Scripture is the test of the church's wealth and power; and he denied that the bread of the Mass stops being bread.
  - *His own case for it:* an English tract printed under his name answers friars who call it heresy to write God's law in English and make it known to lay people. Christ and his apostles, it says, taught in the tongue the people knew best (wyclif_english-works-hitherto-unprinted_matthew1880.txt 35750-35755, 35791-35795; the wider argument 35762-35799). *Contested* (authorship of the English tracts rests on editors, E13).
  - *What it made:* his circle made the first whole Bible in English. Its 1850 editors call these the earliest English versions to take in any large part of Scripture (wycliffite-bible_forshall-madden1850.txt 69-74). *Widely Accepted.*
  - *Who held the pen:* his hostile contemporary Knighton says Wyclif himself turned the Gospel into English (Quote 2). The 1850 editors think the New Testament "might probably" be his own (wycliffite-bible 2319-2320), and the Old Testament begun by his helper Nicholas Hereford (2407-2420). Pollard (1903) finds "substantially no evidence" for his own hand, and thinks his Oxford disciples did it, "urged no doubt by his wish" (pollard_fifteenth-century-prose-and-verse_1903.txt 840-846, 967-975). *Contested.* One or two sentences in the story.
  - *What it changed:* it was copied by hand and never printed in its own age (B4, B9). About 1408-09 Archbishop Arundel forbade anyone to translate Scripture into English, or read a translation from Wyclif's time, without a bishop's leave (foxe_acts-and-monuments-v3 20044-20055). A century on, people were still examined for owning a single English gospel (foxe v4 17982-17988, 17458-17461). It stayed in handwritten copies until printed English Scripture came. A century and a half later William Tyndale took up the same work from the Greek, and printed it: the same task done again, not built on Wyclif's text, for Tyndale says he had no earlier English to follow (walter 673, 692-695, 25533-25536). *Documented* (the decree, the 1521 charges, Tyndale's statement); Tyndale's Greek: *Widely Accepted* (Walter, editor, 692-695; B11 concerns his later revision, not 1526).
  - *The rest of the heart:* he measured the clergy against the poverty of the apostles (fasciculi-zizaniorum_shirley1858.txt 1635-1637, Shirley's summary), and in his own words counselled the pope to leave worldly lordship to the secular arm, "for so Christ did ... through his apostles" (fasciculi 27415-27421, "Sic enim Christus [f]ecit ... per suos apostolos"). Separately, he denied that the bread of the Mass stops being bread (beat 3). His followers, the Lollards, carried all of it on after the church condemned it.
  - Census check: "an English Bible came out of his circle, though how much was his own work is unknown" holds. "Died free, in his own parish" is the census's own inference from his death at Lutterworth (E18); no source says it in those words.

- **Length:** about 2 minutes 45 seconds (about 410 words, at about 150 a minute for Neo). Why the extra time: he is a major figure (census `major: true`), and the Bible strand needs room to say what was made, who made it, and what owning it cost, before the English and Bohemian strands. Hus is cut to about 50 words.

- **Setting:**
  - *When:* the late fourteenth century, from 1361 to 1384 (fasciculi 884-886; death December 1384: wycliffite-bible 1031). Say "the fourteenth century". The Bible strand runs on to 1521 and 1526.
  - *Where:* England. Oxford, where he was master of Balliol College and a doctor of theology (fasciculi 884-886, 1043-1047). Lutterworth, a parish in Leicestershire in the English Midlands, his from 1374 to his death (wycliffite-bible 979-981; knighton_chronicon-v2-lat_lumby1895.txt 16932-16934).
  - *Tradition:* the Latin church of the West, under the pope. His followers became the Lollards (census spoken name "the Lollards"); the name was given in scorn by opponents (Vaughan: wyclif_tracts-and-treatises_vaughan1845.txt 4033-4034). Name them in the story.

- **Shape:** Teacher or thinker. One idea, God's law in the people's tongue, shown through his own words and his enemy's.

- **Openings** (both lead straight into the heart):
  1. *His own question (recommended).* Friars in fourteenth-century England said it was heresy to write God's law in English. Wyclif's tract answers: Christ and his apostles taught in the tongue the people knew best; why not now? (Quote 1). Plain heard once, and it is the heart.
  2. *His enemy's complaint.* A hostile Leicester chronicler of his own day (the passage dated only "probably before 1400", wycliffite-bible 870-871) complains that this Oxford master put the Gospel into English, so that lay people, and women who could read, knew it better than learned clergy: the Gospel's pearl trampled by pigs (Quote 2). Credit it as hostile.

- **Story beats** (the first two carry the heart inside the first minute; the rest deepens it):
  1. **God's law in English.** His argument (Quote 1). Then the English Bible of his circle, the first whole Bible in English (wycliffite-bible 69-74). Knighton's charge that he translated the Gospel himself (Quote 2). Who held the pen is Contested: the 1850 editors think the New Testament probably his; Pollard finds no real evidence and thinks his students did it at his wish (sources above). Explain "lay people": everyone who was not clergy. *Contested* (his own hand); *Widely Accepted* (made in his circle).
  2. **Who he was, and why Scripture.** The most eminent doctor of theology of his day, even his hostile Leicester neighbour's chronicle says (knighton 16932-16937). Master at Oxford, in the crown's service, rector of Lutterworth from 1374 (wycliffite-bible 979-984). He wanted the clergy as poor as the apostles (fasciculi 1635-1637, Shirley's summary), and later told the pope himself to leave worldly lordship to the secular power, as Christ did through his apostles (fasciculi 27415-27421, his own words). In 1377 he answered charges at St Paul's in London, backed by John of Gaunt, duke of Lancaster (fasciculi 1686-1692), and papal letters denounced nineteen conclusions from his teaching (wycliffite-bible 995-997). *Widely Accepted*; date of the letters Contested.
  3. **The bread of the Mass, 1381.** Explain the custom: the church taught that at the priest's words the bread and wine change in their substance, so that only their appearance remains (fasciculi 11450-11457). Wyclif taught that the bread's substance remains (11406-11408). The Oxford ban was read out while he sat in the master's chair arguing the opposite; his enemies' record says he was shaken but held no one could overturn his view (11579-11585). In his Confession he held that Christ's body truly and really is the sacramental bread, but would not say it is that bread "essentially, substantially, corporeally" (11669-11684). John of Gaunt ordered him to stop speaking on it (11602-11616). *Documented* (as the hostile Carmelite collection records it). Keep this beat short.
  4. **The earthquake council, 1382.** A council of bishops and friars in London condemned twenty-four conclusions from his writings; an earthquake shook the city as it met, and he called it "their council of the earthquake" (wyclif_trialogus-lat_lechler1869.txt 694-700, 20169-20170). He was banished from the university and his Oxford followers made to recant (fasciculi 2696-2706). Both copies of Hereford's Old Testament break off mid-sentence in the book of Baruch; the 1850 editors *conjecture* the 1382 summons stopped him (wycliffite-bible 2414-2418). *Documented* (condemnation; the break); the cause: *Contested*, the editors' guess.
  5. **Lutterworth.** He went on writing from his parish. Summoned to Rome in 1384, he wrote that he would humbly have come, but God had compelled him otherwise (fasciculi 27423-27433; Quote kept in reserve below). He died there on 31 December 1384, after a stroke (wycliffite-bible 1031; fasciculi 2748-2751). One or two sentences; no editor's reading of his letter. *Widely Accepted* (date); circumstances *Contested*.
  6. **The book that stayed dangerous.** His followers, the Lollards, put their own Twelve Conclusions before Parliament about 1395 (fasciculi 28380-28384). In 1407 his follower William Thorpe told Archbishop Arundel that many who talked with Wyclif wrote his teaching down and lived by it, and that "full many men and women" held it still (pollard 7204-7220). About 1408-09 Arundel forbade new translations, and the reading of any from Wyclif's time, without a bishop's leave, on pain of excommunication (foxe v3 20044-20055). The Bible was copied by hand, never printed, and scarce, so a family might own a single gospel or one or two epistles, not a Bible (walter 673-679, editor, borne out by the registers B6-B8). The story must say this before the 1521 cases. In 1521 the bishop of Lincoln's registers record John Grosar examined for a book of the Gospels in English that had passed hand to hand, and an old man at Iver who sent a boy out of the house "that he should not hear and tell" before reciting Paul and the Gospels to his family (foxe v4 17982-17988, 17458-17461). A century and a half after Wyclif, William Tyndale took up the same work from the Greek, and printed it, in 1526; he wrote that he had no one's English to follow (tyndale_doctrinal-treatises_walter1848.txt 673, 692-695, 25533-25536). Set it apart from the Lollard sentences (a paragraph break, or "Long after"), so it is not heard as their outcome. Tell it as the same task done again, the end-point of the handwritten strand, never as built on Wyclif's text or as a result of it. *Documented* (as Foxe prints the registers; Tyndale's statement); Tyndale's Greek: *Widely Accepted* (Walter, editor).
  7. **The verdict, and Bohemia.** On 4 May 1415 the Council of Constance declared him a heretic and ordered his bones dug up; Pope Martin V later ordered them burned (mansi tomus 27, 65527-65560; raynaldus 7971-8025). Jan Hus of Prague, who had built his own book on the Church from Wyclif's (hus_de-ecclesia-the-church_schaff1915.txt 1509-1516), told the same council he hoped his soul would be where Wyclif's is (Quote 3). *Documented* (orders; Hus's words).

- **What came of them** (heaviest first; about a third of the story):
  1. **The English Bible and the people who read it.** His circle's Bible was, its 1850 editors judge, widely circulated among clergy and lay people from the late fourteenth century until the printed Bibles of Henry VIII (wycliffite-bible 69-74). It was forbidden without a bishop's leave from about 1408-09 (foxe v3 20044-20055), and in 1521 men and women were still examined for owning or reading a single English gospel or epistle (foxe v4 17982-17988, 17458-17461, 18089-18090, 18331-18332). *Defended by him:* God's law in the people's own tongue (Quote 1).
  2. **The Lollards.** His followers, named Lollards by their opponents, carried his teaching as a movement: Twelve Conclusions before Parliament about 1395 (fasciculi 28380-28384); Thorpe before Arundel in 1407, who conceded Wyclif was "a great Clerk" (pollard 7204-7220, 7268-7272). Their story continues in the Lollardy entry.
  3. **Bohemia.** Hus took "paragraph after paragraph" from Wyclif's book on the Church (schaff 1509-1516) and defended his memory at Constance (Quote 3). Short.
  4. **The church's answer.** Constance condemned forty-five articles and 260 more from Oxford (mansi 65502-65520), and ordered the bones dug up; Martin V ordered them burned. *Defended by the church:* the change of the bread's substance (beat 3), and the bishops' control of Scripture in English (Arundel).

- **Quotes, 2 or 3 at most:**
  1. *His own words, from Middle English.* "Here the friars and their backers say it is heresy to write God's law in English, and make it known to lay people. ... So Christ and his apostles taught the people in the tongue the people knew best. Why should people not do the same now?" Rendered from the Middle English of De Officio Pastorali, ch. 15, wyclif_english-works-hitherto-unprinted_matthew1880.txt 35750-35755 ("þe freris wit þer fautours seyn þat it is heresye to write þus goddis lawe in english, & make it knowun to lewid men") and 35791-35795 ("& þus crist & his apostlis tauȝten þe puple in þat tunge þat was moost knowun to þe puple; why shulden not men do now so?"; normalized from the OCR, see Source_Sheet E13). "Lewid men" means lay people, not the clergy: not "lewd". "Men" in the question means people in general. The ellipsis skips the friars' forty signs of a heretic and the argument from Pentecost and Jerome (35756-35790). *Job:* the heart of his life in his own voice. *Contested* authorship: say "a tract that goes under his name" or "his English tract", as the writer prefers; the card carries the doubt.
  2. *His enemy on the English Gospel, from Latin.* "This Master John Wyclif translated the Gospel from Latin into English, not the angels' tongue. So through him it is made common. It lies more open to lay people, and to women who can read, than it usually lies to clergy of real learning and understanding. And so the Gospel's pearl is scattered and trampled by pigs." Rendered from knighton_chronicon-v2-lat_lumby1895.txt 16948-16969 ("Hic magister Johannes Wyclif Evangelium ... transtulit de Latino in Anglicam linguam non angelicam ... et a porcis conculcatur"); the clause at the ellipsis, on Christ giving the Gospel to the clergy to hand on to lay people, is left out. "Not the angels' tongue" is a pun in the Latin (*Anglicam*, English; *angelicam*, angelic). *Job:* what the English Gospel meant to its opponents: Scripture leaving the clergy's hands for ordinary readers, women included. Credit it as a hostile witness. Use one or two of its sentences; the pearl sentence is the strongest.
  3. *Jan Hus at Constance, from Latin.* "I do not know where John Wyclif's soul is. I hope that he is saved, and I fear that he may be damned. Yet in hope I would wish my soul to be where John Wyclif's soul is." Rendered from palacky_documenta-mag-joannis-hus-lat-ces_1869.txt 15246-15249 ("Nescio, ubi est anima ipsius Joan. W. ... ubi est anima Joannis W."), in the record of Hus's hearing of 7 June 1415 by his companion Peter of Mladoňovice. The council mocked him, shaking their heads (15250). *Job:* the Bohemian strand in one stroke. Owned at Constance; first said c. 1403, about twelve years earlier (palacky 15241-15245). The last sentence alone may serve.
  - *In reserve, not one of the three:* his letter to Urban VI, 1384: "If I have erred in these things, I am willing to be corrected humbly, even by death, if need be. If I could travel in person as I wished, I would humbly come before the pope. But God has compelled me otherwise. And he has taught me, as a rule, to obey God rather than men." (fasciculi 27423-27433, rendered from the Latin.) Use only in place of Quote 3, and then without the editor's reading of "God has compelled me".

- **Connection to carry into the story:** Scripture in English passed from Wyclif's circle to ordinary readers, and they paid for it. Arundel's decree names the books "lately set forth in the time of John Wickliff" (foxe v3 20051-20052), and in 1521 a book of the Gospels in English went from a priest to a man and his wife "which ... used to read upon the same", and on to another (foxe v4 17984-17988). The listener needs to understand three things. The translations were handwritten, so a family might own one gospel, not a Bible (walter 673-679). Owning or reading one without a bishop's leave was a crime the bishops tried as heresy. Tyndale's printed New Testament of 1526 did the same work again from the Greek; it was not built on Wyclif's text, as Tyndale himself says (walter 25528-25536). Let it come through what Grosar, Durdant and Tyndale did, not through a summary line.

- **Links:**
  - Nicholas Hereford: his follower; an early note gives him much of the Old Testament translation.
  - William Thorpe: heard him teach.
  - Thomas Arundel: examined his follower Thorpe; forbade English Scripture without a bishop's leave.
  - Jan Hus: read his books; quoted his book; defended his memory.
  - William Tyndale: named him, in his Prologue to Jonah, as a preacher of repentance whom England did not heed (walter 29439-29443). No tie of text.
  - John of Gaunt: his patron in 1377; silenced him on the sacrament in 1381.
  - Council of Constance and Martin V: condemned him; ordered his bones dug up and burned.
  - John Foxe: told his story; printed the bishops' registers.

- **Who tells it:**
  - *His own words:* the English tract (Matthew's 1880 edition; authorship rests on editors), and his Latin, kept for us by the **Fasciculi Zizaniorum**, a collection made by Carmelite friars, his enemies. The Oxford chair scene comes from them.
  - **The Leicester chronicle under Henry Knighton's name**: of his own day, hostile. Its Bible passage is dated only "probably before 1400" (wycliffite-bible 870-871); the annals run to 1395 (knighton 1270), so it may postdate his death. Say "of his own time" or "a chronicler of his own day", never "in his lifetime". It praises his learning and condemns his English Gospel.
  - **Three witnesses on his own hand**, each told in its own place, after Knighton's charge: (1) Knighton, hostile, says he translated the Gospel himself (Quote 2); (2) the Bible's 1850 editors think the New Testament was probably his own work (wycliffite-bible 2319-2320); (3) a later scholar, Pollard (1903), finds "substantially no evidence" and thinks his students made it at his wish (pollard 840-846, 967-975). No editor's names needed in the audio; they go on the card. For example: "The Bible's editors of 1850 thought the New Testament was probably his own work. A later scholar found no real proof, and thought his students made it at his wish."
  - **William Thorpe**, a follower, his own account of 1407. The one friendly voice.
  - **Bishop Longland's registers of 1521**, as John Foxe printed them about 1563. Foxe was a Protestant partisan; the charges are the bishops' own wording. Credit "the bishop's records" if a witness is named.
  - **Peter of Mladoňovice**, Hus's companion, eyewitness at Constance.
  - **William Tyndale**, his own words, 1526.
  - How-we-know gets a sentence or two, inside beat 1 only. No editor's reading in the death beat.

- **Do not say:**
  - "Scholars still disagree whether he translated any of it" as the whole account → tell all three witnesses: Knighton says he did; the 1850 editors think the New Testament probably his; Pollard finds no real proof and credits his students, at his wish.
  - Knighton "writing in Wyclif's lifetime" → the held texts date the passage only "probably before 1400" (wycliffite-bible 870-871). Say "of his own time" or "a chronicler of his own day".
  - Durdant "reciting the Gospels" → "reciting passages of Paul and the Gospels to his family" (foxe v4 17458-17461: to his children and their wives).
  - "Wyclif translated the Bible" → "his circle made the first whole Bible in English". Only his enemy Knighton says he translated the Gospel himself; scholars disagree how much, if any, was his own hand.
  - "the first English translation of Scripture" or "the first English Bible ever" → "the first whole Bible in English"; its 1850 editors say the earliest English versions to take in any large part of Scripture (wycliffite-bible 69-74).
  - "Tyndale used, revised or built on Wyclif's Bible" → Tyndale wrote that he "had no man to counterfeit", nor help from anyone's English before him (walter 25534-25536). Say he took up the same work, from the Greek.
  - "Tyndale carried on Wyclif's work", or any ending that makes Tyndale the outcome of Wyclif's Bible → Tyndale made his own version and says he followed no one's English (walter 25533-25536). "Took up the same work" is allowed only as the same task done again (project lead's ruling, 2026-11-18). The Jonah line is about Wyclif's preaching, not his Bible.
  - Hus's hope as a verdict on Wyclif's theology → at Constance he owned words he first said about twelve years earlier, when he knew only Wyclif's philosophy and his good name (palacky 15241-15245).
  - "Tyndale was inspired by Wyclif" or "Tyndale finished what Wyclif began" → no source says so. Tyndale named Wyclif as a preacher of repentance (walter 29439-29443); nothing more.
  - "a hundred and fifty years later" as exact → "about a century and a half later" (1384 to 1526; Walter's round figure).
  - "Arundel banned the English Bible" → he forbade translating, and reading a translation from Wyclif's time, without a bishop's leave. Keep "without a bishop's leave".
  - "People were burned for owning a gospel" → the 1521 registers record people examined and made to name others; do not add a punishment for a named person. Keep "examined" or "charged".
  - "Grosar" or "Durdant" given a trade, town detail, or feeling not in the record → only what Foxe's lines hold: Grosar received the book from a priest and lent it on; old Durdant sent a boy out "that he should not hear and tell".
  - "The 1521 books were Wyclif's translation" → the registers say "a book of the Gospels in English"; before 1526 no printed English gospel existed, so these were handwritten, but the registers do not name the version. Say "an English gospel, copied by hand".
  - "He denied that Christ is present in the sacrament" → he said Christ's body truly and really is the sacramental bread, but denied that the bread stops being bread.
  - "He refused to go to Rome" → he wrote that God had compelled him otherwise. Do not explain why.
  - "He was tried at the Black Friars council" → his conclusions were condemned there; Shirley says he was never summoned.
  - "Hereford was arrested mid-sentence" → both copies break off mid-sentence in Baruch; that the summons stopped him is the editors' guess.
  - "He died in despair" or "God struck him down" → only Walsingham, a hostile monk, says so. Leave it out.
  - "The Council of Constance burned his bones" → the council ordered them dug up (1415); Martin V ordered them burned (1427). Do not name the river, or give a year for the burning.
  - "Hus was his student" → Hus knew him through his books and his reputation (palacky 15243-15245).
  - "Hus defended Wyclif's teachings" → Hus defended his memory and hoped he was saved; he said he defended none of Wyclif's errors (palacky 15252-15254).
  - "Wyclif founded the Lollards" or "called them Lollards" → his followers were called Lollards, a name given in scorn by opponents.
  - "The pope condemned him as a heretic in 1377" → papal letters denounced nineteen conclusions. He was declared a heretic only in 1415, after his death.
  - "the king's uncle" for John of Gaunt → "the duke of Lancaster", or just John of Gaunt.
  - Do not add "Morning Star of the Reformation", a birth year, a birthplace, or his looks or temper.
  - Keep "forty-five articles and 260 more" if used; do not round.
  - Do not tell the stroke at Mass as settled; if the death needs a cause, say "after a stroke" and no more.

  - Walsingham's judgment story of the stroke, and the editor's reading of the letter to Urban.
  - The Prague tale of thunder breaking the door of St Paul's (palacky 12898-12902): a legend his accusers reported.
  - The birth year 1324 and the early Oxford colleges: disputed.
  - The Purvey revision of the Bible: a contested attribution, and a side story.
  - Richard Hun (foxe v4 14330-14332): a strong case, but his death needs more room than the story has.
