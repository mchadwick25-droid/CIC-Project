# Outline: John Wyclif

All files are under `cic/texts/`. Line numbers are the file's own. Full evidence is in `Source_Sheet.md` (items S, E, I, Q).

- **Length:** about 2 minutes 30 seconds (about 380 words). Why the extra time: he is a major figure (census `major: true`), and the record carries two strands of impact, England and Bohemia. Several points are also disputed, and the story has to say so plainly: the Bible, his death, the bones.

- **Setting:**
  - *When:* the late fourteenth century, from 1361 to 1384 (Balliol 1361: fasciculi-zizaniorum_shirley1858.txt 884-886; death December 1384: wycliffite-bible_forshall-madden1850.txt 1031). Say "the fourteenth century".
  - *Where:* England. Oxford, where he was master of Balliol College and a doctor of theology (fasciculi 884-886, 1043-1047). Lutterworth, a parish in Leicestershire in the English Midlands, his from 1374 to his death (wycliffite-bible 979-981; knighton_chronicon-v2-lat_lumby1895.txt 16932-16934). London, for the hearings of 1377 and 1382.
  - *Tradition:* the Latin church of the West, under the pope. His followers became the Lollards (census spoken name: "the Lollards"; a name given in scorn by opponents, Vaughan 1845: wyclif_tracts-and-treatises_vaughan1845.txt 4033-4034).

- **Thread:** He taught that scripture, put into the people's own language, is the test of the church's wealth, power and sacrament. His followers in England and in Bohemia carried that teaching on after the church had condemned it.

- **Shape:** Teacher or thinker.

- **Openings:**
  1. Oxford, 1381. The chancellor's ban on his teaching about the bread of the Mass is read out in the Augustinian schools. Wyclif is sitting in the master's chair, arguing the opposite. His enemies' collection of documents says he was shaken, but said no one could overturn his view (fasciculi 11579-11585; hostile Carmelite collection).
  2. Constance, 1415, thirty years after his death. Jan Hus tells the council he hopes his soul will be where Wyclif's is, and the council laughs at him (palacky_documenta-mag-joannis-hus-lat-ces_1869.txt 15243-15250).

- **Story beats:**
  1. **The Oxford doctor.** Master of Balliol by 1361, doctor of theology by 1374 (fasciculi 884-886, 1043-1047; Shirley's documents). His hostile Leicester neighbour's chronicle calls him the most eminent doctor of theology of his day, second to none in philosophy (knighton 16932-16937). He also served the crown: rector of Lutterworth by royal presentation in April 1374, envoy to Bruges the same year "to treat with the pope" (wycliffite-bible 979-984). *Documented.* (Lutterworth date: Contested.)
  2. **Church wealth and the first hearings, 1377.** He wanted the clergy to return to the poverty of the apostles, Shirley writes (fasciculi 1635-1637, the editor's summary). He answered a hearing at St Paul's in London in February 1377, backed by John of Gaunt, duke of Lancaster. The hearing broke up in confusion (fasciculi 1686-1692, Shirley's account; the primary chronicle is not held). Papal letters of Gregory XI then denounced nineteen conclusions from his teaching (wycliffite-bible 995-997). *Widely Accepted*; date of the letters Contested (30 May or 11 June).
  3. **The bread of the Mass, 1381.** Explain the custom: the church taught that at the priest's words the bread and wine are changed in their substance, so that only their appearance remains (the Oxford ban's own words, fasciculi 11450-11457). Wyclif taught that the bread's substance really remains (fasciculi 11406-11408). Oxford's chancellor condemned it. Wyclif appealed to the king, not to the pope. John of Gaunt ordered him to stop speaking on it (fasciculi 11602-11616). He answered with a written Confession: Christ's body truly and really is the sacramental bread, but he did not dare to say that it is "essentially, substantially, corporeally" that bread (fasciculi 11669-11684; "Non tamen audeo dicere", 11680). *Documented.*
  4. **The earthquake council, 1382.** In May 1382 a council of bishops and friars at the Black Friars in London condemned twenty-four conclusions from his writings. An earthquake shook the city as it met; Wyclif took it as God's verdict and called it "their council of the earthquake" (wyclif_trialogus-lat_lechler1869.txt 694-700, 20169-20170, 22562-22563). Shirley says he was never summoned, but was banished from the university, and his Oxford followers were made to recant (fasciculi 2696-2706). *Documented* (condemnation); whether he appeared: *Contested*.
  5. **God's law in English.** A tract printed under his name says the friars call it heresy to write God's law in English; Christ and his apostles, it answers, taught in the language best known to the people (wyclif_english-works-hitherto-unprinted_matthew1880.txt 35750-35755, 35791-35795). An English Bible came out of his circle. An early note gives much of the Old Testament to his follower Nicholas Hereford; how much was Wyclif's own work is unknown (wycliffite-bible 2407-2420; pollard_fifteenth-century-prose-and-verse_1903.txt 840-845). His enemy Knighton says he translated the Gospel himself (Quote 2). *Contested.*
  6. **Summoned to Rome, 1384.** From Lutterworth he wrote to Pope Urban VI and advised, "as counsel", that the pope should leave worldly power to secular rulers (fasciculi 27415-27416), and excused himself from coming (Quote 1). On 29 December, hearing Mass in his parish church, he was struck by a stroke and lost his speech; he died on 31 December 1384 (fasciculi 2748-2751, Shirley; wycliffite-bible 1031). *Widely Accepted* (date); circumstances *Contested*. The St Albans chronicler Walsingham, hostile, calls the stroke God's judgment (walsingham_historia-anglicana-v2-lat_riley1863.txt 11027-11040).
  7. **The verdict after death.** On 4 May 1415 the Council of Constance declared him a notorious heretic who died in heresy. It ordered his body and bones dug up, if they could be told apart from the bodies of the faithful (mansi tomus 27, 65527-65560). In December 1427 Pope Martin V ordered the bishop of Lincoln to dig them up, burn them in public, and dispose of the ashes so that no trace remained (raynaldus_annales-ecclesiastici-tomus28-1424-1453-lat_theiner1874.txt 7971-8025). John Foxe, the Protestant martyrologist, says the bones were burned and the ashes thrown into the river (foxe_acts-and-monuments-v3_cattley-townsend1837.txt 8030-8045). *Documented* (orders); the burning: *Widely Accepted*, held only in Foxe.

- **What came of them** (heaviest first; about a third of the story):
  1. **His followers carried his teaching in England.** In 1407 the priest William Thorpe told Archbishop Thomas Arundel (date: pollard 1013, 7315) that many people loved Wyclif's teaching so much that they wrote it down and ruled their lives by it. "Full many men and women" still held it, he said, and he had learned it "especially of Wyclif himself" (pollard 7204-7220, 7238-7239). Even Arundel granted that Wyclif was "a great Clerk", though his teaching was condemned (pollard 7268-7272). By about 1395 his followers, the Lollards, put their own Twelve Conclusions before Parliament (fasciculi 28380-28384). *Defended by them:* his teaching itself.
  2. **The English Bible of his circle.** Its 1850 editors write that between 1382 and 1526 its versions spread "a great amount of scriptural truth" (wycliffite-bible 76-77; the editors' judgment).
  3. **Bohemia.** Jan Hus of Prague took "paragraph after paragraph" from Wyclif's book on the Church into his own (hus_de-ecclesia-the-church_schaff1915.txt 1510-1516; Schaff after Loserth). Hus would not call Wyclif a heretic and hoped he was saved (palacky 9054-9056, 9414-9417). *Defended:* Wyclif's memory, at Constance, before the council that had condemned Wyclif a month earlier (Quote 3; condemnation 4 May 1415, mansi 132797-132800; Hus's hearing 7 June).
  4. **The church's answer.** Constance condemned forty-five articles and 260 more from Oxford (mansi 65502-65520), and ordered the bones dug up; Martin V ordered them burned (beat 7). *Defended by the church:* the change of the bread's substance (beat 3).

- **Quotes, 2 or 3 at most:**
  1. *His own words, from Latin.* "If I have erred in these things, I am willing to be corrected humbly, even by death, if need be. If I could travel in person as I wished, I would humbly come before the pope. But God has compelled me otherwise. And he has taught me, as a rule, to obey God rather than men." Rendered from the Latin of his letter to Urban VI, 1384, fasciculi-zizaniorum_shirley1858.txt 27423-27433 ("Si autem in istis erravero ... plus Deo quam hominibus obedire"). *Job:* his stand in his own voice, at the end of his life. "God has compelled me" is his own phrase: Shirley reads it as his failing health, from another source, Gascoigne (fasciculi 2743-2748; note 2761-2763); the story must not turn it into defiance.
  2. *His enemy on the English Gospel, from Latin.* "This Master John Wyclif translated the Gospel from Latin into English, not the angels' tongue. So through him it is made common. It lies more open to lay people, and to women who can read, than it usually lies to clergy of real learning and understanding. And so the Gospel's pearl is scattered and trampled by pigs." Rendered from knighton_chronicon-v2-lat_lumby1895.txt 16948-16969 ("Hic magister Johannes Wyclif Evangelium ... transtulit de Latino in Anglicam linguam non angelicam ... et a porcis conculcatur"); the clause at the ellipsis, on Christ giving the Gospel to the clergy to hand on to lay people, is left out of the quote. "Not the angels' tongue" is a pun in the Latin (*Anglicam*, English; *angelicam*, angelic). *Job:* what the English Gospel meant to its opponents: Scripture leaving the clergy's hands for ordinary readers, women included. Credit it as a hostile witness.
  3. *Jan Hus at Constance, from Latin.* "I do not know where John Wyclif's soul is. I hope that he is saved, and I fear that he may be damned. Yet in hope I would wish my soul to be where John Wyclif's soul is." Rendered from palacky_documenta-mag-joannis-hus-lat-ces_1869.txt 15246-15249 ("Nescio, ubi est anima ipsius Joan. W. ... ubi est anima Joannis W."), in the record of Hus's hearing of 7 June 1415 by his companion Peter of Mladoňovice. The record says Hus first spoke these words twelve years before, when only Wyclif's philosophical books were known in Bohemia and he knew only of Wyclif's good life. The council mocked him, shaking their heads (15250). *Job:* the connection to Bohemia, and the cost of defending him.

- **Connection to carry into the story:** Wyclif's teaching passed to people who wrote it down and lived by it. In England, Thorpe tells the archbishop in 1407 that many "loved so much his learning that they writ it, and busily enforced them to rule themselves thereafter" (pollard 7213-7215). In Bohemia, Hus built his own book on the Church from Wyclif's, paragraph by paragraph (schaff 1514-1516), and defended Wyclif's memory at Constance (Quote 3). The listener needs to understand three things. The followers were not his Oxford pupils alone: Thorpe speaks of "men and women", and Knighton of lay readers and women. Hus knew him only through his books and his reputation; the books travelled from England to Bohemia (palacky 15243-15245). The same council that heard Hus out also condemned Wyclif and ordered his bones dug up. Let this come through what Thorpe and Hus did, not through a summary line.

- **Links:**
  - Jan Hus: read his books; quoted his book; defended his memory.
  - William Thorpe: heard him teach.
  - Thomas Arundel: examined his follower Thorpe.
  - Nicholas Hereford: his follower; an early note gives him much of the Old Testament translation.
  - John of Gaunt: his patron in 1377; silenced him on the sacrament in 1381.
  - Council of Constance and Martin V: condemned him; ordered his bones dug up and burned.
  - John Foxe: told his story.

- **Who tells it:**
  - *His own Latin* (letter to Urban, Confession, Trialogus). Kept for us by the **Fasciculi Zizaniorum**, a collection of documents against him gathered by Carmelite friars, his enemies (fasciculi; Shirley's 1858 edition). The listener should hear that the scene in the Oxford schools comes from them.
  - **The Leicester chronicle under Henry Knighton's name**: written in his own time by a canon of a nearby abbey, hostile. It praises his learning and condemns his English Gospel.
  - **Thomas Walsingham**, a monk of St Albans, his contemporary, hostile. Only he tells the stroke as God's judgment.
  - **William Thorpe**, a follower, writing his own account of his 1407 examination. The one friendly voice. Shirley notes that no friendly hand left any record of Wyclif's own life and death (fasciculi 2753-2755). That is worth saying.
  - **Peter of Mladoňovice**, Hus's companion, eyewitness at Constance.
  - **John Foxe**, a Protestant writing about 180 years later, partisan. Credit the burning and the river to him.
  - The editors (Shirley 1858, Forshall and Madden 1850, Pollard 1903) supply dates and the Bible debate. Their judgments are theirs, not the record's.

- **Do not say:**
  - "Wyclif translated the Bible" → "an English Bible came out of his circle"; an early note gives much of the Old Testament to Nicholas Hereford; how much Wyclif did himself is not known. Only his enemy Knighton says he translated the Gospel.
  - "the first English Bible" → if needed, its 1850 editors call these the earliest English versions of any large part of Scripture (wycliffite-bible 68-70).
  - "He denied that Christ is present in the sacrament" → he said Christ's body truly and really is the sacramental bread, but denied that the bread stops being bread.
  - "He refused to go to Rome" → he wrote that God had compelled him otherwise; Shirley reads this as his failing health (fasciculi 2743-2748).
  - "He was tried at the Black Friars council" → his conclusions were condemned there; Shirley says he was never summoned.
  - "He died in despair" or "God struck him down" → only Walsingham, a hostile monk, says so, and "as those present said". Keep it in his voice or leave it out.
  - "The Council of Constance burned his bones" → the council ordered them dug up (1415); Pope Martin V ordered them burned (1427); Foxe says it was done and the ashes thrown into the river.
  - Do not name the river (Swift) or give 1428 for the burning: no held source names either.
  - Do not say "forty-one years later" or give any year for the burning. Foxe's "forty-one years" and his heading's "A.D. 1425" (foxe 7913-7922) clash with Martin V's letter of December 1427. Only the orders are dated (1415, 1427).
  - "Hus was his student" → Hus knew him through his books and his reputation (palacky 15243-15245).
  - "Hus defended Wyclif's teachings" → Hus defended Wyclif's memory and hoped he was saved. He said he had defended none of Wyclif's errors (palacky 15252-15254).
  - "the king's uncle" for John of Gaunt → do not give Gaunt's family tie; Shirley calls him only "the duke of Lancaster" (fasciculi 1686-1688). In February 1377 Edward III was still alive (fasciculi 1730-1731).
  - "Wyclif founded the Lollards" or "called them Lollards" → his followers were called Lollards, a name given in scorn by opponents (Vaughan).
  - "The pope condemned him as a heretic in 1377" → papal letters denounced nineteen conclusions from his teaching. He was declared a heretic only in 1415, after his death.
  - Do not add "Morning Star of the Reformation", a birth year, a birthplace, or any description of his looks or temper for colour.
  - Keep "forty-five articles and 260 more"; do not round to "hundreds of errors".
  - "Do not tell the stroke at Mass as settled → credit it to Shirley's account, and say that no friendly hand left any record of his life and death (fasciculi 2753-2755); Walsingham's judgment story stays in his voice or is left out." (added from Review.md, 2026-11-18)

- **Leave out:**
  - John Ball and the rising of 1381. Knighton calls Ball his forerunner (knighton 16943-16947), but it is a hostile charge, and Vaughan rejects it. It needs more room than the story has to tell fairly.
  - The Prague tale of thunder breaking the door of St Paul's on his judges (palacky 12898-12902). It is a legend his accusers attributed to Hus, and Hus called the article untrue in many points (palacky 12910).
  - The birth year 1324 and the early Oxford colleges (Queen's, Merton, Canterbury Hall). They are disputed (Shirley against Vaughan and Forshall and Madden).
  - The Purvey revision of the Bible: a contested attribution, and a side story.
  - The details of the politics of 1377 (Gaunt and the London crowd). Name Gaunt as his protector; the rest swallows the story.
  - Gospel Doctor, the title in one Trialogus manuscript (lechler 1571-1573). Optional colour only, with its source; drop it if time is short.
