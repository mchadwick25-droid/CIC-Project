## The test, from one hearing

- **Who:** John Wyclif, an Oxford theologian and parish priest of Lutterworth in fourteenth-century England, in the Latin church under the pope.
- **What happened:** He argued that God's law belongs to lay people in English, and judged the church's wealth and power by Scripture. His circle made the first whole Bible in English. He denied that the bread of the Mass stops being bread. Oxford banned his teaching, a London council condemned his conclusions in 1382, and he died at Lutterworth in 1384. Constance declared him a heretic in 1415 and ordered his bones dug up.
- **Why the connection matters:** English Scripture passed from his circle to ordinary readers, who were examined for owning a hand-copied gospel more than a century later. Tyndale later did the same work again from the Greek, in print. In Prague, Hus built on his book on the Church.

All three lines come clear from one hearing.

## The heart of his life

Written from the census entry (`john-wyclif`: teaser, knownFor, legacy) before checking the brief against it: Wyclif held that Scripture is the measure of the church, so the clergy should be poor as the apostles were and God's law should reach every Christian in their own tongue. From that came the English Bible of his circle, his greatest legacy. He also denied that the bread of the Mass stops being bread. His followers, the Lollards, carried his teaching on, and Hus took it to Bohemia.

**Does the first minute carry it?** Yes. The first 150 words end just after the pearl sentence (word 147). In that span the listener hears his own case for English Scripture, the plain statement of his conviction, Scripture as the test of wealth and power, and his circle's Bible. That is later than the census weight might suggest, but the outline places it there and the ruling makes the Bible the centre. Not blocking.

## Findings

### BLOCKING

**B1. The three witnesses on Wyclif's own hand are flattened into one.** The ruling asks for the three witnesses: Knighton says he did; the 1850 editors think the New Testament probably his; Pollard finds no proof. The brief tells Knighton fully, but gives the other two only as "scholars still disagree whether he translated any of it himself", and before Knighton. A listener hears doubt, then the enemy's charge, and never learns that the Bible's own 1850 editors lean toward his hand for the New Testament (wycliffite-bible_forshall-madden1850.txt 2319-2320) while Pollard finds "substantially no evidence" and credits his disciples, "urged no doubt by his wish" (pollard_fifteenth-century-prose-and-verse_1903.txt 840-846, 967-975). "Whether he translated any of it" also leans toward Pollard's side. Contested is right, but the shape of the contest is lost.
*Cause in the outline:* beat 1 asks for both scholars, but "Who tells it" prescribes the single sentence "Scholars still disagree whether he translated any of it himself." The writer followed the second line.
*Change:* after Knighton's charge, say the scholars' two readings, for example: "The Bible's editors of 1850 thought the New Testament was probably his own work. A later scholar found no real proof, and thought his students made it at his wish." No names are needed; Forshall, Madden and Pollard go on the card.
*Do not say line for the outline:* "'Scholars still disagree whether he translated any of it' as the whole account → tell all three witnesses: Knighton says he did; the 1850 editors think the New Testament probably his; Pollard finds no real proof and credits his students. Replace the single sentence under Who tells it."

**B2. "A hostile chronicle from Leicester, written in his lifetime" is unsupported.** The held texts call Knighton contemporary, but the only dating of the passage is Forshall and Madden's: "a passage written probably before 1400" (wycliffite-bible_forshall-madden1850.txt 870-871). The chronicle's annals run to 1395 (knighton_chronicon-v2-lat_lumby1895.txt 1270), so this section may well have been written after Wyclif's death in 1384. The outline's opening 2 ("writing in his own lifetime") left room for the error.
*Change:* "A hostile Leicester chronicle of his own time" or "A hostile chronicler of his own day, at Leicester".
*Do not say line for the outline:* "Knighton 'writing in Wyclif's lifetime' → the held texts date the passage only 'probably before 1400' (wycliffite-bible 870-871). Say 'of his own time' or 'a chronicler of his own day'." Also correct opening 2 of the outline.

### MINOR

1. **"That was the heart of his life."** A kit term spoken aloud, a signpost a documentary narrator would not use. Let the next sentence carry it: "For him, God's law belonged to everyone ..."
2. **The scarce, hand-copied book.** The ruling names it, and the outline's connection asks the listener to understand that the translations were handwritten, so a family might own one gospel, not a Bible (walter 673-684; B9). The brief gives only "a handwritten English gospel book" for Grosar. Add one clause before 1521, for example: "It was copied by hand, never printed, and scarce, so a family might own a single gospel."
3. **Durdant "reciting the Gospels".** The register says he recited "certain places ... out of the Epistles of St. Paul, and of the Gospels" (foxe v4 17458-17461), to his children and their wives. Say "reciting passages of Paul and the Gospels to his family".
4. **Longest sentence 33 words** (the Hus sentence with its quote), past the Standard's "about 30". Split after "from Wyclif's".
5. **Tyndale's place.** The sentence itself keeps the ruling's form. Set inside the paragraph that opens "His followers ... carried it on", it could be heard as the Lollards' outcome. A paragraph break before it, or "Long after" in place of the time phrase, would keep it as the same task done again.
