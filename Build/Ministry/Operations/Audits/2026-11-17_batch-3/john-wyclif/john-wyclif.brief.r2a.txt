In fourteenth-century England, friars said it was heresy to write God's law in English for lay people, everyone who was not clergy. An English tract that goes under John Wyclif's name answers them: "So Christ and his apostles taught the people in the tongue the people knew best. Why should people not do the same now?"

That was the heart of his life. God's law belonged to everyone, in their own tongue, and Scripture was the test of the church's wealth and power. His circle made the first whole Bible in English, though scholars still disagree whether he translated any of it himself. A hostile chronicle from Leicester, written in his lifetime, says he put the Gospel into English himself, for lay people and for women who could read. In its words, "And so the Gospel's pearl is scattered and trampled by pigs."

The same chronicle called him the most eminent theologian of his day. He taught at Oxford, and from 1374 he was priest of Lutterworth, in the English Midlands. He wanted the clergy as poor as the apostles, and told the pope himself to leave worldly lordship to the secular power.

In 1381 he went further. The church taught that at the priest's words the bread of the Mass changed in substance, leaving only its appearance. Wyclif held that Christ's body truly is the sacramental bread, yet the bread is still bread. Oxford's ban on his teaching was read out while he sat in the master's chair, arguing the opposite.

The next year a London council condemned twenty-four conclusions from his writings as an earthquake shook the city. Banished from Oxford, he went on writing at Lutterworth, and died there after a stroke at the end of 1384.

His followers, called Lollards in scorn, carried it on. About 1408 Archbishop Arundel forbade anyone to translate Scripture into English, or read a translation from Wyclif's time, without a bishop's leave. In 1521 the bishop's records show John Grosar examined for a handwritten English gospel book that he had received from a priest and lent on. Old Durdant of Iver sent a boy out of the house, so he could not hear and tell, before reciting the Gospels to his family. About a century and a half after Wyclif, William Tyndale took up the same work from the Greek and printed it, writing that he had no one's English to follow.

In 1415 the Council of Constance declared Wyclif a heretic and ordered his bones dug up. Jan Hus of Prague, who had built his own book on the Church from Wyclif's, told that council: "Yet in hope I would wish my soul to be where John Wyclif's soul is."
