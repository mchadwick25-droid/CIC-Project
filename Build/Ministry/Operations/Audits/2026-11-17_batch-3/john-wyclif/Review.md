# Review: John Wyclif

Brief reviewed: `john-wyclif.brief.txt` (401 words). No earlier draft was read.

## The test, from one hearing

- **Who:** John Wyclif, a leading Oxford teacher of fourteenth-century England. He judged the Western church's wealth and power by scripture, and he taught that the bread of the Mass stays bread.
- **What happened:** Oxford banned his teaching on the bread. A London council condemned his conclusions in 1382 and he was banished from Oxford. An English Bible came out of his circle. He died of a stroke at Lutterworth in 1384. In 1415 the Council of Constance declared him a heretic and ordered his bones dug up, and Martin V later ordered them burned.
- **Why the connection matters:** his teaching outlived him. Men and women in England wrote it down and lived by it (Thorpe, 1407). In Prague, Hus built his book on the Church from Wyclif's, and he defended Wyclif's memory before the council that had just condemned him.

Where I got lost: "he wrote that God had compelled him otherwise". The wish to come to Rome has been cut, so on one hearing "otherwise" has nothing to point back to. Otherwise nothing lost me. It never reads as a children's book or talks down.

**One-hearing test alone: I would pass it.** All three lines come through clearly from a single hearing.

- **Craft 4.** A strong opening scene, a clear spine and an ending on Hus's own words. The connection is carried by what Thorpe and Hus did. The middle moves fast, and the 1377 conflict and Gaunt drop out.
- **Rigor 3.** Every quote and nearly every claim traces to the outline and the texts. But his death is told as settled fact, when the outline requires the story to say plainly that it is disputed. Thorpe's line is also slightly merged.
- **Accessibility 4.** Plain words, and the custom of the bread is explained inside the story. Grade 7.5 (names out 6.9), reading ease 77.0, average 17.4 words a sentence, longest 30. On a listener read it is not flat. Joining words are low (1.0 per 100), but cause and contrast still come through ("Then", "though", "so that", "Yet").

## Findings

1. **BLOCKING. His death is told as settled.** The brief says: "That December, at Mass in his parish of Lutterworth, a stroke took his speech, and he died two days later." The outline marks the circumstances *Contested* (beat 6) and says the story "has to say so plainly: the Bible, his death, the bones". The Mass scene is Shirley's account (fasciculi 2748-2751). Walsingham, who was hostile, tells the stroke as God's judgment, and Shirley writes that "no friendly hand has left us any ... memorial of the life and death" (fasciculi 2753-2755). **Change:** credit the account and say the gap plainly, for example: "No friend left any record of his life or death. The account his editor gives is that at Mass in his parish of Lutterworth, that December, a stroke took his speech, and he died two days later." Keep the date.
   **Do not say line to add to the outline:** "Do not tell the stroke at Mass as settled → credit it to Shirley's account, and say that no friendly hand left any record of his life and death (fasciculi 2753-2755); Walsingham's judgment story stays in his voice or is left out."

2. **MINOR. Thorpe's words are merged.** The brief says "many men and women loved it so much that they wrote it down and ruled their lives by it." In the text, the ones who "loved so much his learning that they writ it" are the "great many [who] commoned oft with him". "Full many men and women" are those who still held it in 1407 (pollard 7210-7216). **Change:** for example, "many who talked with him loved his teaching so much that they wrote it down and ruled their lives by it, and full many men and women held it still."

3. **MINOR. Praise in the narrator's voice.** "He was one of the great teachers of fourteenth-century England" is a judgment, and the Standard asks for praise to be credited. **Change:** credit it. Knighton, his hostile neighbour, calls him the most eminent doctor of theology of his day (knighton 16932-16937), or Arundel calls him "a great Clerk" (pollard 7268-7270).

4. **MINOR. Some of the conflict is missing.** Gaunt, the 1377 hearing, the papal letters and the forced recantations of his Oxford followers all drop out. The outline's Leave out still asks to "Name Gaunt as his protector". These are not essential to the test, but one clause would serve both the record's force and the stakes. **Change:** for example, a clause that his Oxford followers were made to recant, or that the duke of Lancaster first shielded him and then ordered him silent on the bread.

5. **MINOR. "God had compelled him otherwise" leaves the listener hanging.** Without the wish to come, "otherwise" has nothing to point to. **Change:** for example, "he wrote that he would gladly have come, but God had compelled him otherwise."

6. **MINOR. Hus's words were first spoken years earlier.** At Constance, Hus was repeating words he said he had first spoken twelve years before (palacky 15243-15249). As told, they read as freshly said. This is not wrong, because he did say them in the council (15250). Optional: "words he had first spoken twelve years before."

7. **MINOR. "He held that Christ and his apostles taught..."** The source is a tract printed under his name, and its authorship is *Contested* (Source_Sheet E13). **Change:** "A tract under his name says..."
