The Goths' bishops had taught their people to pray, "Glory to the Father, through the Son, in the Holy Spirit." In May 589, at Toledo, the Visigothic kings' capital in Spain, those bishops cursed that prayer. They cursed anyone who would not say, "Glory and honour to the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit." With their king Reccared, his queen and their nobles, they were giving up their church's faith and signing the Catholic faith of their Roman subjects.

The Goths' church had its own bishops, sometimes in the same cities as the Catholic ones. Its opponents named it Arian, after Arius, a priest of Alexandria, and it taught that the Son was less than the Father, and the Spirit less than both. A Frankish bishop, Gregory of Tours, argued with the Gothic king's envoy and reports him: "The Son is always less than the Father, because he does the Father's will. And it is not shown that the Father does his." No Gothic writing survives; we hear that church only through its victors.

Reccared had become Catholic in his first year as king. At his call, sixty-two bishops gathered. They heard his book of faith read out: the Son equal to the Father and eternal with him, the Spirit proceeding "from the Father and the Son". The Gothic bishops, clergy and nobles condemned the faith "that came from Arius", and confessed: "No one doubts that we and those before us went astray in the Arian heresy."

Not every Goth turned. One Gothic bishop, Sunna, was already gone. Paul of Mérida, a hostile later witness, says he refused to turn Catholic and was put on a boat out of Spain. The old church's buildings went to the Catholic bishops, and its married clergy had to live apart from their wives. By the king's edict, a cleric who defied the council was excommunicated, a noble lost half his property, and a lesser man lost all and was exiled. Children of mixed Jewish and Christian marriages were to be baptized, and idolatry was to be punished short of death.

Every congregation was to say the creed before communion, to strengthen, the king said, "our people's new conversion". The chronicler John of Biclaro wrote that the heresy was "cut out by the roots".

The king's words "from the Father and the Son" outlived the Goths' church. Spain said them at Mass. A modern editor traces them through the West, where they became a cause, or a pretext, of "bitter reviling between East and West".
