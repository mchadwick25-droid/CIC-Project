On the eighth of May, in the year 589, a king took his place among sixty-two bishops in Toledo, the Visigothic kings' capital in Spain. They had fasted for three days. Then a notary stood and read the king's book of faith aloud.

The king was Reccared, and the faith in the book was not the one his people had kept. The Goths had their own church, apart from the Catholic church of their Roman subjects. Its books are lost, so we know it only through its enemies, who called it Arian. Reccared became Catholic in his first year as king, and John of Biclaro, a Catholic whom the old king had exiled for ten years, says he won the Gothic clergy "by reason rather than command".

Not every bishop gave way. Paul of Mérida, a hostile witness writing later, tells of Sunna, a Gothic bishop. Offered a see elsewhere if he did penance, Sunna answered: "I do not know what penance is. So let this be plain to you: I know nothing of penance, and I will never be a Catholic. But I will live by the rite I have lived by. Or I will most gladly die for the religion I have held from my youth until now." He was put on a small boat out of Spain, before the council, if John's Sunna is Paul's, as it seems.

The king's book confessed the Father, the Son and the Spirit as one substance, the Spirit proceeding from the Father and the Son. Then the Gothic bishops, clergy and nobles said they had already given up their old faith when they followed him. They pronounced an anathema, a solemn curse that cuts a person off from the church, on rebaptism and on the faith that came from Arius. And they judged their own predecessors: "No one doubts that we and those before us went astray in the Arian heresy." Eight Gothic bishops signed, then nobles, then "all the elders of the Goths". Every word of this comes from the winning side. No Gothic witness survives.

The council then passed twenty-three canons. Every congregation was to say the creed aloud before the Lord's Prayer, to strengthen, as the king put it, "our people's new conversion". The Gothic churches and their property went to the Catholic bishop of each place. Converted clergy were to live apart from their wives. Jews were barred from public office, and children of Jewish-Christian marriages were to be baptized. The king's edict backed it all, with exile and the loss of property for those who defied it.

In Tuy, Lugo, Porto and Tortosa, a bishop converted from the old church and a Catholic bishop signed side by side. There had been two churches, with two bishops, in the same city. And every congregation, Gothic and Roman alike, now said one creed aloud before communion.
