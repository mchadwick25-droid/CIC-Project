The Goths' bishops had taught their people to pray, "Glory to the Father, through the Son, in the Holy Spirit." In May of the year 589, at Toledo, the Visigothic kings' capital in Spain, those bishops cursed that prayer. Now they would say, "Glory and honour to the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit."

Those few words held the whole quarrel. The Goths' own church, which its opponents named after Arius, a priest of Alexandria, taught that the Son was less than the Father, and the Spirit less than both. Gregory of Tours, a Frankish bishop, reports the Gothic king's envoy arguing it, though only to refute him: "The Son is always less than the Father, because he does the Father's will. And it is not shown that the Father does his." No Gothic writing survives, so that church is heard only through those who defeated it.

It stood apart from the Catholic church of the Goths' Roman subjects, with its own bishops. Then King Leovigild died in 586, and within a year his son Reccared became Catholic. He called the bishops of his realm to Toledo, and before sixty-two of them a notary read the king's book of faith aloud. In it the Son was equal to the Father and eternal with him, while the Spirit proceeded "from the Father and the Son". Reccared and Queen Baddo signed it.

After that, the Gothic bishops, clergy and nobles cursed the faith "that came from Arius". The council asked them to judge those who had gone before them, and they did: "No one doubts that we and those before us went astray in the Arian heresy. We have now learned the faith of the Gospel and the apostles, within the Catholic church."

The old church's buildings and property passed to the Catholic bishop of each place. In four cities, Tuy, Lugo, Porto and Tortosa, a bishop from the old church and a Catholic bishop signed side by side. Every congregation was now to say the creed aloud before communion, the king said, to strengthen "our people's new conversion".

A Catholic chronicler, John of Biclaro, wrote that the heresy was "cut out by the roots". Yet the words "and the Son" lived on, and Spain's councils said the creed with them at Mass in 653 and 681. A modern editor says 589 is the first time they appear in the creed, and that from Spain they spread through the West, though Rome held out until 1014. In time, he writes, they became the cause, or the pretext, of "bitter reviling between East and West".
