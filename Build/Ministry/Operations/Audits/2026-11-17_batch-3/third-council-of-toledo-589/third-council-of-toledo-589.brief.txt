The Third Council of Toledo opened after a three-day fast, with King Reccared seated among sixty-two bishops in the Visigothic kings' capital in Spain. His book of faith, read aloud by a notary, confessed Father, Son and Spirit as one substance, the Spirit proceeding "from the Father and the Son". It was May of the year 589, and the Goths' own church was about to give itself up.

The Gothic kings and their people kept that church apart from the Catholic church of their Roman subjects. No Gothic witness survives, so we hear it only through the men who defeated it, who remember exiles and forced rebaptism. Reccared turned Catholic soon after his father, Leovigild, died in 586. John of Biclaro, a Catholic whom Leovigild had exiled, says he won the Gothic clergy "by reason rather than command". Paul of Mérida, a hostile witness, says the Gothic bishop Sunna refused to turn Catholic. "I will most gladly die for the religion I have held from my youth until now," Sunna answered, and he was shipped out of Spain.

At the council, the Gothic bishops, clergy and nobles laid an anathema, the church's solemn curse, on the faith "that came from Arius", and on rebaptism. They confessed: "No one doubts that we and those before us went astray in the Arian heresy. We have now learned the faith of the Gospel and the apostles, within the Catholic church." In four cities, a converted bishop and a Catholic bishop signed side by side, two bishops for one city.

The canons set out how one church would pass into the other. Gothic church buildings and their property went to the local Catholic bishop, and converted clergy had to live apart from their wives. By 590, bishops at Seville complained that some bishops were not enforcing Toledo's rule against clergy keeping women in their houses. Three years after the council, the bishops at Saragossa let Gothic priests of chaste life serve again after a new blessing. Gothic relics were tested by fire, and anyone who hid them was cut off from the church. Churches consecrated by converted bishops before their Catholic blessing were consecrated again. John of Biclaro wrote that the heresy, "after long killings of Catholics and slaughter of the innocent", was cut out by the roots.

Other canons reached beyond the Goths. Jews might not have Christian wives, concubines or slaves, and children of such marriages were to be baptized. Idolatry had spread "through almost all Spain and Gaul", and each bishop was to destroy it and punish its followers short of death. Reccared's edict backed every decree. A disobedient cleric would be excommunicated, a noble would lose half his property, and a lesser man would lose everything and be exiled.

And before the Lord's Prayer at every Eucharist, every congregation, Gothic and Roman alike, was now to say the creed aloud. The king gave his reason: to strengthen "our people's new conversion", so that no one could plead ignorance of the faith.
