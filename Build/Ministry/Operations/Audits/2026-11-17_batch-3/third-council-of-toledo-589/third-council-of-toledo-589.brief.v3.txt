On the eighth of May, in the year 589, a king took his place among sixty-two bishops in Toledo, the Visigothic kings' capital in Spain. He had called them to restore church order. After a three-day fast, a notary read the king's book of faith aloud.

The king was Reccared, and the faith in the book was not the one his people had kept. The Goths had their own church, apart from the Catholic church of their Roman subjects. Its books are lost, so we know it only through its enemies, who called it Arian. Reccared's father, Leovigild, died in 586, and within the year Reccared became Catholic. John of Biclaro, a Catholic whom Leovigild had exiled, says he won the Gothic clergy "by reason rather than command".

Not every bishop gave way. Paul of Mérida, a hostile witness writing later, tells of Sunna, a Gothic bishop. Offered a see elsewhere if he did penance, Sunna answered: "I will never be a Catholic. But I will live by the rite I have lived by. Or I will most gladly die for the religion I have held from my youth until now." He was put on a small boat out of Spain, two years before the council by John's dating, which seems to name the same man.

The king's book confessed the Spirit as proceeding from the Father and the Son. Then the Gothic bishops, clergy and nobles said they had already given up their old faith. They pronounced an anathema, a solemn curse that cuts a person off from the church, on rebaptism, which Catholic writers remembered them forcing, and on the faith that came from Arius. And they judged their own predecessors: "No one doubts that we and those before us went astray in the Arian heresy." Every word of this comes from the winning side, and no Gothic witness survives.

The council passed twenty-three canons. Every congregation was to say the creed aloud before the Lord's Prayer, to strengthen, as the king put it, "our people's new conversion". The Gothic churches and their property went to the Catholic bishops, and converted clergy were to live apart from their wives. Jews were barred from public office, and children of Jewish-Christian marriages were to be baptized. Idolatry had spread through almost all Spain and Gaul, so each bishop, with the local judge, was to hunt it out and punish its followers short of death. Masters who allowed it among their slaves were excommunicated. The king's edict backed it all: a noble who defied it lost half his property, a lesser man lost everything and was exiled.

Did it hold? John says the heresy was cut out by the roots, after long killings of Catholics. Three years on, bishops at Saragossa were having the old church's relics tested by fire, cutting off anyone who hid them, and consecrating again the churches its bishops had consecrated before their Catholic blessing. A council at Seville complained that some bishops were ignoring Toledo's decree on clergy living with women.

In Tuy, Lugo, Porto and Tortosa, a bishop converted from the old church and a Catholic bishop signed side by side. There had been two churches, with two bishops, in the same city.

Now they would say one creed aloud, Gothic and Roman together, before every communion.
