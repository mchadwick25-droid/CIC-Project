The Third Council of Toledo opened after a three-day fast, with King Reccared seated among sixty-two bishops in the Visigothic kings' capital in Spain. His book of faith confessed Father, Son and Spirit as one substance, the Spirit proceeding "from the Father and the Son". It was May of the year 589, and the Goths' own church was about to give itself up.

The Gothic kings and people kept that church apart from their Roman subjects' Catholic church. No Gothic witness survives, so we hear it only through the men who defeated it, who remember Leovigild exiling Catholic bishops and rebaptizing Catholics. Leovigild died in 586, and his son Reccared soon turned Catholic. John of Biclaro, a Catholic Leovigild had exiled, says he won the Gothic clergy "by reason rather than command".

Not every bishop followed. The Lives of the Fathers of Mérida, a hostile later account, says Sunna, the city's Gothic bishop, plotted to kill its Catholic bishop, Masona. The plot was found out, and the king had the other plotters stripped of property and exiled in chains. Sunna was urged to turn Catholic. "I will most gladly die for the religion I have held from my youth until now," he answered. He was shipped out of Spain on a small boat.

At the council, Gothic bishops, clergy and nobles laid an anathema, the church's solemn curse, on the faith "that came from Arius", and on rebaptism. They confessed: "No one doubts that we and those before us went astray in the Arian heresy. We have now learned the faith of the Gospel and the apostles, within the Catholic church." In four cities, two bishops signed for each: one converted, one Catholic.

Under the canons, Gothic church buildings and property went to the local Catholic bishop, and converted clergy had to live apart from their wives. By 590, bishops at Seville complained that some bishops were not enforcing Toledo's rule against clergy keeping women at home. In 592, bishops at Saragossa let Gothic priests of chaste life serve again after a new blessing. Gothic relics were tested by fire, and anyone hiding them was cut off from the church. Churches consecrated by converted bishops before their Catholic blessing were consecrated again. John wrote that the heresy, "after long killings of Catholics and slaughter of the innocent", was cut out by the roots.

Beyond the Goths, Jews might not have Christian wives, concubines or slaves, and children of such marriages were to be baptized. Idolatry had spread "through almost all Spain and Gaul", and each bishop was to destroy it and punish its followers short of death. Under Reccared's edict, a cleric who disobeyed was excommunicated, a noble lost half his property, and a lesser man lost everything and was exiled.

And before the Lord's Prayer at every Eucharist, every congregation, Gothic and Roman alike, was now to say the creed aloud. The king's reason was to strengthen "our people's new conversion", so that no one could plead ignorance of the faith.
