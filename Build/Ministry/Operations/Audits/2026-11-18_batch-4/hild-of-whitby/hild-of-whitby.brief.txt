Caedmon, a man of Hild's house and no longer young, would leave the feast whenever the harp came near, because he could not sing. One night, Bede says, someone stood by him in his sleep and told him to sing the beginning of created things. In the morning he was brought to the abbess, Hild. Learned men judged the gift a grace from God, and she made him a monk and had him taught sacred history, which he turned into English song.

Hild ruled a monastery of men and women at Whitby, in Northumbria, and ran it on peace and charity. After the example of the earliest church, all goods were shared, so that no one there was rich and no one poor. She made her people study the Scriptures, so that many were fit to be priests.

Of her house Bede wrote, "Later we saw five bishops come from that same monastery. All of them were men of rare merit and holiness." In those days a bishop led the church of a whole region, and the English church was still young. Ordinary people, he writes, and sometimes even kings and princes, sought her advice.

Almost all we know of her comes from Bede, a monk in the same kingdom, who wrote about fifty years after she died. She was the daughter of King Edwin's nephew, and took the faith with King Edwin at the preaching of Paulinus. After thirty-three years of ordinary life she chose the monastic life, and after Bishop Aidan called her home from the East Angles, she ruled first Hartlepool, then Whitby.

The newly converted English had their faith from Rome and from the Irish monks of Iona, whom Bede calls the Scots, and the two kept Easter on different dates. In 664 the kings met in her house to settle the date of Easter. Bishop Colman led the Scots' side, and Hild and her people held with it, while the priest Wilfrid argued for Rome. King Oswy ruled for Rome's side, for Peter, keeper of heaven's keys, and Bede says all present agreed. Colman, his teaching rejected, went home with those who would not accept it, but Bede does not say how Hild took the ruling.

A fever held Hild for six years, and she never stopped teaching her people. In the seventh year, in 680, she called the women together at cockcrow and urged them to keep the peace of the Gospel among themselves and with all others. All who knew her called her Mother.
