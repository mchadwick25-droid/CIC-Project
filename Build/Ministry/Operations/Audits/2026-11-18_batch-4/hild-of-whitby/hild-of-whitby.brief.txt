Caedmon, a man of Hild's house and no longer young, would leave the feast whenever the harp came near, because he could not sing. One night, Bede says, someone stood by him in his sleep and told him to sing the beginning of created things. In the morning he was brought to the abbess, learned men judged the gift a grace from God, and Hild made him a monk and had him taught sacred history, which he turned into English song.

Her house was at Whitby, in seventh-century Northumbria, a monastery of men and women that she ran on peace and charity. After the example of the earliest church, all goods were shared, so that no one there was rich and no one poor. She made her people study the Scriptures, so that many were fit to be priests, and Bede says he later saw five bishops come from that house. Ordinary people, and sometimes even kings and princes, sought her advice. In 664 the kings met in her house to settle the date of Easter, and she held with the Irish-trained side, which lost.

Almost all we know of her comes from Bede, a monk in the same kingdom, who wrote about fifty years after she died. She was the daughter of King Edwin's nephew, and took the faith with him at the preaching of Paulinus. After thirty-three years of ordinary life she turned to the monastic life. Bishop Aidan called her home from the East Angles, and she became abbess first at Hartlepool, then at Whitby.

The newly converted English there had their faith from Rome and from the Irish monks of Iona, whom Bede calls the Scots, and the two traditions kept Easter on different dates. At Whitby, Bishop Colman led the Scots' side, with Hild and her people behind him, while the priest Wilfrid argued for Rome. King Oswy ruled for Peter, keeper of heaven's keys, and Bede says all present agreed. Colman, his teaching rejected, went home with those who would not accept it. Bede does not say how Hild took the ruling.

In those days a bishop led the church of a whole region. One of her men, Oftfor, went on from her houses to Archbishop Theodore in Kent and then to Rome, and was later chosen a bishop. Caedmon's verses, Bede says, turned many minds toward heaven, and none who tried religious poems in English after him could match him.

A fever held Hild for six years, but she never stopped teaching her people, and in the seventh year, in 680, she called the women of the house together at cockcrow and urged them to keep the peace of the Gospel among themselves and with all others. All who knew her called her Mother. Of her house Bede wrote, "Later we saw five bishops come from that same monastery. All of them were men of rare merit and holiness."
