# The outline: Hild of Whitby

Sheet references (S, M, I, L, D, N) point to `Source_Sheet.md` in this folder, which gives each file and line. Rewritten for batch 4 from `Outline.r50.md`, with the heart set first and told in the first minute (see `Heart_Test.md`).

- **The heart of their life:** Hild ruled a monastery of men and women at Whitby, in Northumbria, after a first house at Hartlepool (M6, M8, M10; Documented as Bede's account; whether she founded Whitby or set an existing house in order is Contested within Bede, D1). She ran it on peace and charity, with all goods shared after the example of the earliest church, so that no one there was rich and no one poor; and she made her people read the Scriptures so closely that many became fit to be priests (M9, M11; Documented as Bede's account). Bede says he later saw five bishops come from that house (I1; Documented, Bede writes "we saw"), and that ordinary people, and sometimes even kings and princes, sought her advice (M12; Documented as Bede's praise; no king is named). A man of her house who could not sing was brought to her with a gift of song that Bede counts as heaven's; she made him a monk and had him taught sacred history, which he turned into English verse (M17–M19, I2; Documented as Bede's account; the gift is a wonder in Bede's voice). In 664 the kings met in her house to settle the date of Easter, and she held with the Irish-trained side, which lost (M13–M15; Documented; Bede gives no words of hers, N5). All who knew her called her Mother (I4; Documented).

- **Length:** about 2 minutes 45 seconds (about 410 words at 150 a minute). Why it stretches toward 3: she is a major figure of her church, and the story must carry four things that each need a scene or a line: the house she ran, Caedmon, the synod held in her house, and the bishops who came from it. Why it stops short of 3: almost everything rests on one witness, Bede, who gives her no inner life to fill. The first minute (about 150 words) tells the heart: Caedmon received and made a monk, the house and its Scripture, the five bishops, the kings and princes, and the synod in one clause.

- **Setting:** the seventh century (S1); she died in 680, and the synod was in 664. Northumbria, the kingdom of the English north of the River Humber (S2). Her two houses: Hartlepool and Whitby; say only those names, or "Streaneshalch, which Bede says means the Bay of the Lighthouse", now Whitby (S2). The tradition: the English there had taken the faith within living memory, from two missions, one from Rome and one from the Irish monks of Iona, whom Bede calls the Scots (S3a 1–3). Spoken word for the church: "the newly converted English of Northumbria" (S3a 1). Nothing about coast or cliffs is in the record (N6).

- **Shape:** Founder or leader.

- **Openings** (each leads straight into the house she ran):
  1. **Chosen.** A man at a feast who leaves the table whenever the harp comes near him, because he cannot sing. That night, Bede says, someone stood by him in his sleep and told him to sing the beginning of created things (M17). In the morning he is brought to the abbess, Hild; her learned men judge the gift to be God's grace, and she makes him a monk (M18). The scene is resolved inside the opening, in her act, and turns at once to the kind of house it was. About 50 words.
  2. Bede's count: "Later we saw five bishops come from that same monastery" (Quote 1), then the question what kind of house that was. If this opening is used, the closing goes to Mother (Quote 3) or the York litany, not to Quote 1 again.

- **Story beats** (beats 1 and 2 and the first clause of 5 fall in the first minute; the rest follow in time order):
  1. **Caedmon brought to her.** A man of her house, older and unable to sing, received a gift of song in a dream, in Bede's telling. The steward, his superior, brought him to her. Before many learned men she had him tell his dream and repeat his verses; they judged it a grace from God and set him a passage of Scripture, which he brought back next morning in verse. She told him to leave ordinary life for the monastic one, took him into the brotherhood, and had him taught the whole of sacred history, which he turned into English song: the creation, Genesis, Exodus, the life of Christ. M17–M19. Documented as Bede's account; the gift is a wonder in Bede's voice. Bede gives no date (N4).
  2. **The house she ran.** Whitby was a monastery of men and women (M10). Two years after 655 she gained land for ten households at Streaneshalch, and built, or set in order, a house there (M8, D1). She put it under a rule, as she had her first house, and taught justice, devotion, chastity and, above all, peace and charity. After the example of the earliest church, no one there was rich and no one poor; all things were shared (M9). She made her people read the Scriptures and practise works of justice, so that many were found fit to be priests (M11: Bede's "so that"). Bede says he later saw five bishops come from that house (I1: here a clause, in Bede's voice; the quote is kept for the close). Ordinary people in their needs, and sometimes even kings and princes, sought her advice (Quote 2; M12). "A rule" is a written pattern of daily life for a monastery. Documented as Bede's account (founding: Contested, D1).
  3. **Where she came from.** Of noble birth, the daughter of a nephew of King Edwin. She took the Christian faith with Edwin, at the preaching of Paulinus, the first bishop of the Northumbrians; Bede dates Edwin's baptism at York to Easter 627. After thirty-three years of ordinary life she chose the monastic life. She went to the East Angles, meaning to cross to a monastery in Gaul where her sister was a nun, and live there as an exile for the Lord's sake, leaving home for good; her sister's example drew her. After a year Bishop Aidan of Lindisfarne called her home. She lived a year with a few companions on a small holding by the River Wear. M1–M5. Documented (Aidan calling her home: from the Latin, D7).
  4. **Abbess at Hartlepool.** She was made abbess of Heruteu, which Bede calls "the island of the hart" and an editor gives as Hartlepool. She set it in order under a rule, as learned men taught her; Aidan and others often came to teach her. In 655 King Oswy gave his daughter Elfleda, not yet a year old, to God for life, and she was placed in Hild's monastery. M6, M7. Documented.
  5. **The synod in her house, 664.** First minute, one clause: the kings met in her house to settle the date of Easter, and she held with the Irish-trained side. Told in full here: the Irish-trained clergy and those who followed Rome kept Easter on different dates, and cut the clergy's hair differently (the tonsure, the shaved part of a cleric's head). Bede says it was told that at court Easter was sometimes kept twice in one year. The kings, father and son, met at her monastery to decide. Bishop Colman led the Scots' side, and Hild and her people held with it, as did Bishop Cedd; the priest Wilfrid argued for Rome. King Oswy ran the debate and ruled for Peter, keeper of heaven's keys; Bede says all present agreed. Colman, his teaching rejected, went home with those who would not accept the Roman Easter; Cedd changed sides. M13–M15. Documented. Bede gives no words of hers, and does not say how she took the ruling (N5, D4). **DEFENDED** (I6).
  6. **Illness and death.** A fever held her for six years, and she never stopped thanking God or teaching her people. In the seventh year, at cockcrow on 17 November 680, aged sixty-six, she took communion, called the women of the house together and urged them to keep the peace of the Gospel among themselves and with all others. M20, M21. Documented. (Optional, one sentence, in Bede's voice: that night a nun named Begu at Hackness, about thirteen miles away, saw her soul carried up in light by angels. M22.)

- **What came of them** (about a third of the story; items 1 and 2 are pulled forward into the first minute in short form, and told fully here):
  1. **A school of bishops.** Bede says he saw five bishops come from her monastery (Quote 1). One of them, Oftfor, studied the Scriptures in both her houses, went on to Archbishop Theodore in Kent, and then to Rome, and was later chosen a bishop. A sixth, Tatfrid, was chosen but died before he could be made bishop. Bede follows another, John, to Hexham and then York. I1. Documented.
  2. **Song in English.** Bede says Caedmon's verses often turned many minds from the world toward heaven, and that others after him tried religious poems in English, but none could match him. I2. Documented.
  3. **Her house went on.** The king's daughter she raised became a teacher and later a head of Whitby. King Oswy, his queen and King Edwin were buried there. I3. Documented.
  4. **Her name.** All who knew her called her Mother (Quote 3); the fame of her work and goodness reached many far away and turned them to better lives. Eight centuries later, York prayers still asked "Saint Hild" to pray for them. I4, I5. Documented.
  5. **DEFENDED:** at Whitby she held with the Easter of her Irish teachers, and the king ruled against it. I6. Documented.
  End on an event or the record's words: Bede's five bishops (Quote 1), if Opening 1 is used; otherwise Mother or the York litany.

- **Quotes, 2 or 3 at most** (all rendered from Bede's Latin in HEL, checked against Giles's English in HE; re-checked in both files 2026-11-18):
  1. **Five bishops.** Latin: "Denique V ex eodem monasterio postea episcopos uidimus, et hos omnes singularis meriti ac sanctitatis uiros" (HEL 24946–24948; Plummer's margin note "underher." left out). Giles: "In short, we afterwards saw five bishops taken out of that monastery, and all of them men of singular merit and sanctity" (HE 10973–10975). **Rendering:** "Later we saw five bishops come from that same monastery. All of them were men of rare merit and holiness." ("Denique", a link word meaning "in short", is left to the narration.) Job: the measure of what she built, in an eyewitness's words; the close. I1.
  2. **Kings and princes.** Latin: "Tantae autem erat ipsa prudentiae, ut non solum mediocres quique in necessitatibus suis, sed etiam reges ac principes nonnumquam ab ea consilium quaererent, et inuenirent" (HEL 24936–24939). Giles: "Her prudence was so great, that not only indifferent persons, but even kings and princes, as occasion offered, asked and received her advice" (HE 10964–10967). **Rendering:** "Her wisdom was so great that not only ordinary people in their needs, but even kings and princes, sometimes sought her advice and found it." Job: her standing, inside the first minute (beat 2). M12.
  3. **Mother.** Latin: "quam omnes, qui nouerant, ob msigne [read insigne] pietatis et gratiae matrem uocare consuerant" (HEL 24994–24995). Giles: "whom all that knew her called Mother, for her singular piety and grace" (HE 11014–11016). **Rendering:** "All who knew her used to call her Mother, for her outstanding devotion and grace." Job: how her own people remembered her. I4.

- **Connection to carry into the story:** what passed from Hild was a way of running a house, and the people it made. Bede ties the two together himself: she made her people read the Scriptures and do works of justice "so that" many were fit to be priests, and then he says he saw five bishops come from that house (M11, I1). The listener needs to understand that a bishop then led the whole church of a region, so a single monastery sending out five, in a church converted within living memory, was supplying the young English church with its leaders. Oftfor shows the path: Scripture in both her houses, then Theodore in Kent, then Rome, then a bishop's seat (I1). The second thing that passed is Caedmon's song: she is the one who took an unlettered man's gift, had him taught the Bible, and so let it be sung in English; Bede says many were turned to heaven by it and no one after could match him (M18, I2). The writer must make plain that she did not compose it and did not "discover" him; she received him, made him a monk and set him to learn. Carry both through what she did, not a "this mattered because" sentence.

- **Links:** Edwin, king (*kin; took the faith with him*, L1). Paulinus (*heard him preach*, L2). Aidan (*taught her*, L3). Oswy and Elfleda (*received his daughter; taught her*, L6). Colman (*sided with him*, L7). Wilfrid of Ripon (*opposed at the synod*, L8). Five bishops: Bosa, Oftfor, John, Wilfrid of York and one more (*came from her house*, L9). Cedd (*sided with her, then changed*, L10). Caedmon (*received him*, L11). Bede (*wrote her life*, L12). Theodore (*a pupil of hers went to him*, L13).

- **Who tells it:**
  - **Bede**, monk of Jarrow, *Ecclesiastical History*, finished in 731: about fifty years after her death, in the same kingdom, while men from her house were still bishops. Aloud, after the first minute, not before it: "Almost all we know of Hild comes from Bede, a monk in the same kingdom, who wrote about fifty years after she died." In the first minute, credit Bede only where his voice is needed: the gift of song, the five bishops he "saw", and the kings and princes.
  - **Stephen**, *Life of Wilfrid*, written after 710 for Wilfrid's monastery: places the synod "in the presence of the most devout mother Hild" (M16). Not needed aloud.
  - **The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle**, put together about two centuries later: records her death in 680 (M21). Not needed aloud.
  - **The York litany**, 15th–16th century: "Saint Hild, pray" (I5). Say "centuries later" if used.
