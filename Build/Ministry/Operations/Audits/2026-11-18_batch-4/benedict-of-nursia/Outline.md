# Outline: Benedict of Nursia

Sheet references (S, M, R, I, D, A) point to `Source_Sheet.md` in this folder; A1–A5 are under "Added for batch 4". File short names: GRG = Gregory's *Dialogues* (English of 1608), GRG-ed = Gardner's notes there, AS = Gregory's Latin in the *Acta Sanctorum*, RBL / RBE = the Rule in Latin / in Gasquet's English, MGH = the Frankish councils, PAUL = Paul the Deacon.

- **The heart of their life:** He founded houses of monks in Italy: twelve at Subiaco, then one on the mountain at Monte Cassino (M9, M12; Documented as Gregory's account). For them he wrote a Rule, a short book on how monks live together under an abbot (M18, R2; Documented, OWN). It calls the monastery "a school for the Lord's service" and hopes to lay down nothing harsh (Q1, R1). A monk promises, for life, to stay in one community, to turn his whole way of life to God, and to obey (R3, A5). He works with his hands at set times and reads holy books at others (R4). Every guest is received as Christ (R5). The community chooses its abbot, who must temper everything so that the strong have something to strive for and the weak do not run away (Q2, R6). Benedict called it "this little Rule written for beginners" (R8). All of the Rule's content: Documented (OWN). What it changed: about two centuries later Frankish rulers made it the rule for monasteries in their lands. Carloman's council of 742 bound the monks and nuns of the lands he ruled to it, and in 802 Charlemagne had it read at Aachen and ordered monasteries put right by it (I1a, I1c; Documented, LATER). The name "Benedictines" is later still (S6b).

- **Essence:** He founded houses of monks in Italy and wrote for them a short Rule for living together, "a school for the Lord's service". Frankish rulers later made it the law for their monasteries.

- **Also told:** Subiaco and Monte Cassino; the abbot the community chooses; the vow for life; Carloman in 742 and Charlemagne in 802 by name.

- **Length:** 2½ minutes, about 375 words. He is a major figure, and the heart needs room for two things: what the Rule held, and what later rulers did with it. The life itself rests on one later witness, so keep the life beats lean and do not stretch them. The first minute is about 150 words and carries the whole heart (beat 1).

- **Setting** (facts for the writer, not a sentence to paste):
  - Time: Gregory sets him "in the time of the Goths" (S3a, GRG 4417–4418). Modern editors put his birth "about 480" (S3b, GRG-ed 12628). Plain spoken word: "Italy, around the year 500, when Gothic kings ruled it."
  - Place: born in "the province of Nursia" (S2, GRG 3414), schooled in Rome, then Subiaco, almost forty miles from Rome (GRG 3488–3490; AS 77920–77921), then Monte Cassino. Gasquet calls Nursia "a city of Umbria" (RBE-ed 170–171); Gregory does not. Add no landscape.
  - Tradition: census "Early Benedictine / Italian Monasticism" (S6). Plain spoken words: "the monks of Italy"; "a household of monks under an abbot" (RBE 799–800). Never "the Benedictine Order" for his lifetime.

- **Shape:** Founder or leader. What he built, how it ran and who it served: the monasteries, and above all the Rule that outlasted them. The cave years and the wonders are background to that, kept lean and in Gregory's voice.

- **Openings:**
  1. **Chosen.** Gregory the Great filled a whole book with Benedict's wonders, then told his readers to look elsewhere: read his Rule, "for the holy man could in no way teach otherwise than he lived" (Q3; M18, A1; GRG 5563–5572, AS 80434–80440). It leads straight into the Rule, and it says plainly how we know him. Introduce Gregory in a half-clause as the pope who wrote his life about fifty years after his death; the fuller how-we-know comes in beat 3.
  2. Alternative, if the ending changes: in 802, abbots and monks gathered at Charlemagne's court at Aachen and read this Rule together (I1c; MGH 7794–7796). If used, the story must not also end on Aachen.

- **Story beats:**
  1. **The Rule, and what it built (the first minute).** Gregory's pointer (opening 1). In Italy, around 500, under Gothic kings, Benedict founded twelve houses of monks at Subiaco and later one at Monte Cassino (M9, M12). For monks living together under an abbot the community chooses, he wrote a rule (M18, R2, R6): "a school for the Lord's service", in which he hoped to lay down nothing harsh and nothing heavy (Q1; R1). A monk promises to stay for life in one community, to turn his whole way of life to God, and to obey (R3; RBL 8568–8569; A5). Because "idleness is an enemy of the soul", the Rule sets times for work with the hands and other hours for holy reading (R4; RBE 3113–3116). Then the reach, in one sentence: about two centuries later, Frankish rulers ordered the monasteries of their lands to live by it (I1a, I1c; told in full under What came of them). Rule: Documented (OWN). Founding: Documented as Gregory's account. Frankish orders: Documented (LATER).
  2. **The abbot and the guest.** The community chooses its abbot, "or a part of it, however small, acting with greater wisdom", for his life and wisdom, even if he is the last to have joined (R6; RBE 3900–3906). He must temper everything (Q2; RBL 9527–9528). Every guest is received as Christ (R5; RBL 7799–7800). He called it 'this little Rule written for beginners', and pointed those who wanted more to Basil's Rule and the Collations, Institutes and Lives of the Fathers (R8; RBE 4290–4300). Documented (OWN). This beat may begin just inside the first minute or just after it.
  3. **How we know, and the young man who left Rome.** How-we-know line here (see Who tells it). Born in Nursia to a good family, he was sent to Rome to study. He saw many students fall into loose living, so he gave up his books and his father's house and wealth to serve God alone (S2, M1; GRG 3414–3425). At Subiaco a monk, Romanus, gave him the monk's habit and kept his secret. For three years he lived in a narrow cave, known only to Romanus, who let his bread down on a long rope with a little bell tied on (M4; GRG 3488–3512; AS 77936–77938). In time many came to him, bringing food and taking away teaching (M6; GRG 3557–3567). Documented as Gregory's account.
  4. **The monks who tried to poison him.** DEFENDED: regular discipline. Monks of a nearby monastery whose abbot had died begged him to lead them. He warned them their ways and his would not agree, but gave in, and made them keep the regular life (M8; GRG 3648–3669). Some of them put poison in his wine (GRG 3679–3682; AS 78183–78185). Gregory says the glass shattered when he made the sign of the cross over it (WONDER, Gregory's voice). He told them calmly to find a father suited to their ways, and went back to the wilderness (GRG 3690–3706). Documented as Gregory's account. "Regular life" means life by a monastic rule; it was their house's discipline, not his written Rule (D3).
  5. **Twelve houses, and the temple of Apollo.** Many came to him. He built twelve monasteries at Subiaco, set a head over each with twelve monks, and kept a few with himself (M9; GRG 3845–3850; AS 78422–78425). When a jealous local priest set out to corrupt his young monks, he left the houses in others' care and moved on with a few monks, to protect the young ones (M11; GRG 4091–4098). On the high mountain at Cassino stood an ancient temple where country people still worshipped Apollo, with sacred woods around it. He smashed the idol, overturned the altar and burned the woods, built chapels to St Martin and St John in their place, and by constant preaching kept calling the people around to the faith (M12, A3; AS 78762–78767). Modern editors date the move about 528 or 529 (S3b, S3c). Documented as Gregory's account; date Dominant Modern Reconstruction.
  6. **The king of the Goths.** Gregory says Totila, king of the Goths, came to him; seeing Benedict from afar, the king threw himself to the ground. Benedict went to the king himself, raised him from the ground and rebuked him: you do much evil and have done much evil; now at last stop. From then on, Gregory says, the king was less cruel (M13b, A2; AS 79150–79164). Editors date the meeting probably to 542 (M13c). Documented as Gregory's account. The test with the disguised guardsman and the prophecy of Totila's reign are WONDERS: leave out.
  7. **His death.** Six days before he died, Gregory says, he had his grave opened; then a fever took him. On the sixth day he had his monks carry him into the chapel, took communion, and died standing, held up by his disciples, his hands raised to heaven. He was buried in the chapel of St John he had built where Apollo's altar stood (M19; GRG 5582–5591, 5612–5614; AS 80448–80457, 80469–80471). Documented as Gregory's account. No early source gives the year; Benedictine tradition says 543 (S3b, S3c): Contested.

- **What came of them** (about a third of the story; its first sentence is pulled forward into beat 1):
  1. **His Rule became law for Frankish monasteries.** In 742 the first council of Carloman, ruler of the Franks, held with Boniface, ordered that monks and nuns in the monasteries of the lands he ruled "order and live" and govern their life "by the rule of Saint Benedict" (I1a; MGH 2199, 2203–2204, 2324–2325). In 802 Charlemagne gathered the abbots and monks who were at Aachen, where they read the Rule together and learned men set it out before them (MGH "eam tradiderunt sapientes"); he then ordered that whatever had been done in the monasteries against it be put right by it (I1c; MGH 7794–7805). Both LATER (about 200 and 260 years). Documented.
  2. **The Rule outlived the house that made it.** In Gregory's own lifetime the Lombards broke into Monte Cassino by night, while the monks slept, and plundered everything, yet, he says, not one monk was taken (M20; GRG 4657–4661; AS 79401–79404). Paul the Deacon, writing in the eighth century, adds that the monks fled to Rome "carrying with them the manuscript of the Holy Rule" (I2; PAUL 7832–7835). Documented as each writer's account.
  3. **Gregory's book carried his story.** Pope Zacharias put Gregory's *Dialogues* into Greek, and an English bishop put them into Anglo-Saxon for Alfred the Great (I3; GRG-ed 1269–1276). Documented as Gardner's account (EDITOR). Card only unless there is room.
  - End on item 1, the Aachen order of 802, in the record's own terms, or on his death (beat 7) if item 1 has already landed.

- **Quotes** (three; all three are used):
  - **Q3 (the opening).** Source: Gregory's Latin, AS 80434–80440: "Nam scripsit monachorum Regulam, discretione praecipuam, sermone luculentam: cujus si quis velit subtilius mores vitamque cognoscere, potest in eadem institutione regulae omnes magisterii illius actus invenire: quia sanctus vir nullo modo potuit aliter docere quam vixit." Rendered by me from the Latin: **"For he wrote a rule for monks, outstanding in good judgment and clear in its language. Whoever wants to know his ways and his life more closely can find, in what that Rule lays down, all that he did as a teacher. For the holy man could in no way teach otherwise than he lived."** The writer may quote only the last sentence, or the first and last, and give the middle in plain sense. Job: the narrator of the wonders points us to the Rule as the truer picture of the man; it opens the heart.
  - **Q1.** Source: RBL 1240–1245, Latin: "Constituenda est ergo nobis Dominici schola servitii; in qua institutione nihil asperum, nihil grave nos constituturos speramus." Rendered by me from the Latin: **"So we must found a school for the Lord's service. In founding it, we hope to lay down nothing harsh and nothing heavy."** Job: his own voice; the gentleness of the Rule. Beat 1.
  - **Q2.** Source: RBL 9527–9528, Latin: "sic omnia temperet, ut et sit quod fortes cupiant, et infirmi non refugiant". Rendered by me from the Latin: **"Let him so temper everything that the strong have something to long for, and the weak do not run away."** The "him" is the abbot; the writer says so in the lead-in. Job: the Rule's balance between strong and weak. Beat 2.

- **Connection to carry into the story:** The Rule passed from Benedict's own houses into the law of the Frankish church. Carloman's council of 742 told the monks and nuns of his lands to live by "the rule of Saint Benedict" (MGH 2324–2325). At Aachen in 802 the abbots and monks read it together, and Charlemagne ordered whatever in the monasteries went against it put right by it (MGH 7794–7805). What the listener needs: Benedict wrote a rule for monks, but no source has him make it binding beyond his own houses, and there was no order in his lifetime. Frankish rulers two centuries later took up his book and made it the measure of the monasteries in their lands. Paul the Deacon's saved manuscript (PAUL 7832–7835) shows the book travelling apart from the house that made it. Tell this through those acts, not a "this matters because" sentence.

- **Links:**
  - Gregory the Great — wrote his life (from four of Benedict's disciples; never met him).
  - Constantinus, Valentinianus, Simplicius, Honoratus — his disciples; told Gregory his story.
  - Scholastica — his sister; met him once a year; buried in his grave.
  - Romanus — met; gave him the habit and fed him.
  - Maurus and Placidus — his young disciples.
  - Totila, king of the Goths — met him.
  - Basil of Caesarea — his Rule names Basil's Rule.
  - John Cassian — his Rule has the Collations read and commends the Institutes (the identification is the editors').
  - Carloman and Boniface — their council of 742 made his Rule law in Carloman's lands.
  - Charlemagne — had his Rule read and enforced at Aachen, 802.
  - Paul the Deacon — wrote verses on his miracles; told of the abbey's fall and the saved manuscript.
  - Pope Zacharias — translated Gregory's Life into Greek.

- **Who tells it:**
  - **Gregory the Great**, *Dialogues*, Book II, written in 593, about fifty years after Benedict died. By his editor's dating Gregory was born only a year or two before Benedict died, and he never met him; he names four disciples as his sources. He wrote to show God's power in a saint, so wonders fill the book. Line to say aloud, at the start of beat 3 (the opening has named him in a half-clause): **"His life was written down by Pope Gregory the Great, about fifty years after his death, from the memories of four of his monks."** This is a sentence or two, not the story.
  - **Benedict himself**, the Rule: his own words, in his own lifetime.
  - **The Frankish councils** of 742 and 802: about two and two and a half centuries later. The 742 text is Carloman's own decree; the 802 report is from the annals of Lorsch, a chronicle of the time, not the synod's own decree.
  - **Paul the Deacon**, a monk-historian of the eighth century, about two and a half centuries after Benedict.
  - **Modern editors** (Gardner 1911, Gasquet 1909) give the dates; say "as modern editors date it" if a year is used.

- **Do not say:**
  - Wrong: "He founded the Benedictine Order." Right: "He founded monasteries at Subiaco and Monte Cassino." The name and the order are later (S6b; "General of his order" at GRG 3439 is the 1608 translator's phrase).
  - Wrong: "He wrote the first monastic rule in the West" / "He invented Western monasticism." Right: his Rule points readers to older guides, Basil's Rule and the Collations, Institutes and Lives of the Fathers, and calls itself "a little Rule written for beginners" (R8).
  - Wrong: "His Rule has nothing harsh in it" / "a gentle rule" as plain fact. Right: "he hoped to lay down nothing harsh" (R1: "speramus", we hope). The promise binds for life: from that day a monk may not leave or shake off the Rule's yoke (A5). Tell the gentleness and the binding together.
  - Wrong: "Two hundred and fifty years after he wrote it" / any gap counted from the writing. Right: "about two centuries later" (742) and "in 802". No source dates the writing (D3).
  - Wrong: "In 742 the Franks made his Rule law" / "all Frankish monasteries". Right: "Carloman's council, for the lands he ruled" (MGH 2203–2204).
  - Wrong: "Charlemagne made every monastery in Europe follow the Rule." Right: "In 802 Charlemagne gathered the abbots and monks who were at Aachen, where they read the Rule together, and ordered that anything done in the monasteries against it be put right by it" (MGH 7794–7805). It was his realm, not Europe.
  - Wrong: "Charlemagne gathered (or summoned) the abbots and monks to Aachen to read the Rule." Right: "gathered the abbots and monks who were at Aachen, where they read it together" (MGH 7794–7796).
  - Wrong: "The Pope ordered the Rule for all monks." Right: it was Carloman's council (742) and Charlemagne's synod (802).
  - Wrong: "It became the only rule of the West." Right: leave out; that is Gasquet's 1909 judgment (I1d), card only.
  - Wrong: "The Benedictines take their name from him", said of his own time. Right: the name is later (S6b); say it, if at all, as a later name.
  - Wrong: "the monks at the nearby monastery rejected his Rule." Right: "he made them keep the regular life"; that was their house's discipline. Gregory does not say he had yet written his Rule (D3).
  - Wrong: "born in 480" / "died in 543" said as fact. Right: "born around 480, as modern editors date it"; for his death, "by Benedictine tradition, in 543", or give no year (S3, D2).
  - Wrong: "He founded Monte Cassino in 529." Right: "around 529" (editors; Gregory gives no year).
  - Wrong: "He wrote the Rule at Monte Cassino" / "in 530" / "in 540". Right: "he wrote a rule for his monks". Gregory gives no place or year (D3).
  - Wrong: "the monks poisoned him" / "all the monks". Right: "some of them put poison in his wine" (GRG 3679–3682; AS "aliqui"). He was not harmed.
  - Wrong: "the glass shattered" as plain fact. Right: "Gregory says the glass shattered when he made the sign of the cross over it." Keep every wonder in Gregory's voice.
  - Wrong: "at Vicovaro" as Gregory's word. Right: "a nearby monastery". The place name is an editor's identification (D5). Do not add "for three years" there (Gasquet, not Gregory).
  - Wrong: "twelve monasteries of twelve monks" with nothing more. Right: keep "and he kept a few with himself" if the number of monks is given (GRG 3847–3850).
  - Wrong: "He left Subiaco because he was afraid" / "to escape the priest". Right: to protect his young monks; Gregory says he "gave place to envy" (GRG 4091–4095).
  - Wrong: "He converted the pagans of the region" / "brought the people to the faith" / "He tore down a temple." Right: "he smashed the idol, overturned the altar, burned the sacred woods, and built chapels in their place; by constant preaching he kept calling the people around to the faith" (A3; AS 78762–78767). The 1608 "brought ... to embrace" says more than Gregory's Latin.
  - Wrong: "Totila was converted" / "repented" / "made peace with the Church". Right: "from then on, Gregory says, the king was less cruel" (A2; AS 79163–79164). The prophecy of nine years' reign is Gregory's wonder; leave it out.
  - Wrong: "Totila knelt for his blessing" / "lifted him with his own hands" / "Much wickedness do you daily commit." Right: "the king threw himself to the ground"; "Benedict went to the king himself and raised him"; "you do much evil, and have done much evil; now at last stop" (A2). "With his own hands" and "daily" are the 1608 translator's.
  - Wrong: "Ora et labora" / "Pray and work", as the Rule's motto. Right: these words are not in the Rule. Use "idleness is an enemy of the soul" (R4).
  - Wrong: "discretion" in its modern sense (secrecy, tact). Right: "good judgment", "a sense of measure".
  - Wrong: "conversion of manners" / "good manners". Right: "a turning of his whole way of life to God" (R3).
  - Wrong: "The abbot was appointed." Right: "The community chose its abbot", or a smaller part of it with sounder judgment (R6).
  - Wrong: "the monk's day was work and reading" / "his day was divided between work and reading". Right: "set times for work, and other set hours for holy reading" (RBE 3113–3116).
  - Wrong: "Scholastica, his twin sister" / "Abbess Scholastica". Right: "his sister Scholastica". Gregory gives neither "twin" nor "abbess" (M16).
  - Wrong: "His nurse" turned into "his mother". Right: leave the nurse out (sieve story is left out).
  - Wrong: "forty miles" / "over forty miles" from Rome. Right: "almost forty miles" (GRG 3489–3490; AS "quadraginta fere").
  - Wrong: "he knew the day he would die" as plain fact. Right: in Gregory's voice, or left out (GRG 5577–5578; WONDER).
  - Wrong: "The Lombards destroyed Monte Cassino in 589" as Gregory's statement. Right: "In Gregory's own lifetime the Lombards sacked it by night"; the year is an editor's, and contested (D6). Not "destroyed and the monks killed": Gregory says not one monk was taken.
  - Wrong: "the monks saved the Rule", in Gregory's voice. Right: the saved manuscript is Paul the Deacon's report, from the eighth century (I2).
  - Wrong: "Benedictine monks converted Europe" / "Augustine of Canterbury was a Benedictine." Right: no early held source says so; this is Gasquet's 1909 view (D8). Leave out.
  - Wrong: "Gregory knew him" / "his disciple Gregory". Right: Gregory never met him; he wrote from four disciples (M2).
  - Wrong: "He fled the sin of Rome." Right: "he saw many of the students fall into loose living", and left his books and his father's house (GRG 3416–3423).
  - Wrong: "lived in a cave for years alone." Right: "three years", known only to Romanus (GRG 3501–3503).
  - Do not add for colour: his feelings at the cave, the look of the mountain, weather, his appearance, Benedict's age at any event, or any words of his not quoted or paraphrased here.

- **Leave out:**
  - The sieve, the nettles, the blackbird, the devil breaking the bell, the crow and the poisoned loaf, Maurus running on water, the iron rising, the boy raised from the dead, the oil, the dragon, the nuns at Mass, Scholastica's storm, the soul as a dove, the vision of the whole world, the disguised guardsman, the prophecy of Totila's reign (M3, M7, M10, M11, M13a, M14, M16, M17): a pile of wonders is the trap. One is kept, the shattering glass, in Gregory's voice.
  - Paul the Deacon's ravens, angels and heavenly voice (D9): late legend.
  - The removal of his bones to France (I4, D7): contested even by Paul; a side story.
  - The council of Estinnes (I1b): the word "rule" there is an editor's supply.
  - The Rule of the Master (D4): no held source argues it.
  - Gasquet's claims about European civilization, missionaries and the "only monastic Rule of the West" (D8, I1d): a modern advocate's view; card only.
  - Dante (I3): card only.
  - Exact dates beyond "around 500" and "around 529" unless voiced as the editors'.
  - Not left out: the destruction at Cassino, the poisoning, Totila's cruelty, the Lombard sack and the Rule's lifelong bond. These are told as plainly as the Rule's gentleness.
