Pope Gregory the Great filled his life of Benedict with wonders, and then pointed his readers somewhere else, to the rule Benedict wrote for monks. "For the holy man could in no way teach otherwise than he lived."

Benedict lived in Italy around the year 500, when Gothic kings ruled it, and founded twelve houses of monks at Subiaco, then one at Monte Cassino. For monks living together under an abbot, he wrote a rule. "So we must found a school for the Lord's service. In founding it, we hope to lay down nothing harsh and nothing heavy." Yet the promise binds for life: a monk vows to stay in one community, to turn his whole way of life to God, and to obey. Because the Rule calls idleness an enemy of the soul, it sets times for work with the hands and other hours for holy reading. About two centuries later, Frankish rulers ordered the monasteries of their lands to live by it.

The community chooses its abbot, even if he was the last to join. Of the abbot, the Rule says: "Let him so temper everything that the strong have something to long for, and the weak do not run away." Every guest is received as Christ. Benedict called it a little rule for beginners.

His life was written down by Pope Gregory the Great, about fifty years after his death, from the memories of four of his monks. Gregory says that in Rome, Benedict saw many students fall into loose living. So he left his books and his father's house to serve God alone, and spent three years in a cave near Subiaco.

Later, monks of a nearby house begged him to lead them. He held them to its regular life, and some of them put poison in his wine. Gregory says the glass shattered when Benedict made the sign of the cross over it.

Gregory says that at Monte Cassino, where country people still worshipped Apollo, Benedict smashed the idol, overturned the altar, burned the sacred woods and built chapels. He tells too how Totila, king of the Goths, threw himself to the ground before Benedict, who raised him and told him to stop his evil. From then on, the king was less cruel.

Gregory records that in his own lifetime the Lombards sacked Monte Cassino. Paul the Deacon later wrote that the monks fled to Rome with the manuscript of the Rule. In 742, Carloman's council bound the monks and nuns of his lands to the rule of Saint Benedict. And in 802, Charlemagne gathered the abbots and monks at Aachen to read it together, then ordered that whatever in the monasteries went against it be put right by it.
