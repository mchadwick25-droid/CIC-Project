# Source sheet: Benedict of Nursia

Researcher's sheet for varied batch 2, 2026-11-17. The writer gets the outline, not this sheet; the checker uses both.

**Files and short names used below** (all in `cic/texts/` unless marked):
- **GRG** = `gregory-great_dialogues_gardner1911.txt`. Gregory the Great, *Dialogues*, Book II, "Of the Life and Miracles of St. Bennet", in the English of 1608 by "P.W.", re-edited in 1911 by Edmund G. Gardner. Book II runs from line 3402 to 5700. **GRG-ed** = Gardner's own Introduction and Notes in the same file (lines 1023–1320 and 12615–12817). Gardner is a modern editor, not a witness.
- **RBE** = `benedict_rule-of-saint-benedict_gasquet1925.txt`. The Rule, in English by Cardinal Gasquet (1925; his introduction is of 1909). **RBE-ed** = Gasquet's introduction (lines 78–617).
- **RBL** = `benedict_rule-of-st-benedict-lat-eng_hunter-blair1907.txt`. The Rule in Latin with a facing English translation, ed. Oswald Hunter Blair (1907). This is the copy for the Latin wording.
- **MGH** = `capitulatio-de-partibus-saxoniae_pertz1881.txt`. MGH *Capitularia* I (1881). It holds Carloman's capitulary of 742, the capitulary of Estinnes (Liftinas), and the annals of Lorsch for 802.
- **PAUL** = `paul-the-deacon_history-of-the-langobards_foulke1907.txt`. Paul the Deacon, *History of the Langobards*, tr. W. D. Foulke (1907). **PAUL-ed** = Foulke's notes.
- **CAS** = `npnf211_sulpitius-severus-vincent-lerins-cassian.xml`. Edgar Gibson's introduction to Cassian (NPNF II.11). Modern editor.
- **CEN** = `cic-website/data/world-census.json` (not in `cic/texts/`). Used only for the tradition's name.

**Conventions.** Line numbers are each file's own lines. Quotes are exact, OCR faults included; where the OCR breaks a word I give the reading in [square brackets]. In GRG, RBE and RBL the OCR doubles spaces; I have printed them as single spaces, closed up the space the OCR puts before a colon or semicolon, joined words split across lines, and left out running heads, page numbers, footnote markers and the marginal calendar dates printed in RBL and the marginal line numbers in MGH. Nothing else is changed. GRG's chapter headings are the 1608 translator's.

**Witness kinds used.**
- OWN = the Rule. Gasquet: "There appears never to have been any serious doubt cast upon the fact that the book as we have it now is substantially what came from the pen of the author" (RBE-ed 569–572).
- LATER (about 50 years) = Gregory. He wrote the *Dialogues* "in 593, three years after his elevation to the papacy" (GRG-ed 1025–1026). He "was born probably a year or two before the death of St. Benedict" (GRG-ed 1182–1183), so he never knew him. He names his informants: four of Benedict's disciples (GRG 3427–3441). He is the only early narrator of the life: Gardner says Book II "together with his Rule, is our only source for the story of his life" (GRG-ed 1176–1178). He writes to show God's work in a saint, and the book is full of wonders.
- LATER (about 200 to 260 years) = the Frankish councils of 742, 743 and 802 (MGH), and Paul the Deacon, who wrote in the eighth century (PAUL 2, "c. 720-799").
- LATE-LEGEND = Paul the Deacon's additions to the life, which he says Gregory "did not at all describe" (PAUL 3276–3288).
- EDITOR = Gardner (1911), Gasquet (1909/1925), Hunter Blair (1907), Foulke (1907), Gibson (NPNF). They are modern scholars, about 1,350 years later. Their dates and judgments go on the card, or are told as theirs.

**Translation note.** Gregory's Latin is **not held**. Only one sentence of it is held, the praise of the Rule, which Gardner quotes in a note (GRG-ed 12807–12810). Every other line of Gregory below is from the English of 1608. The Rule's Latin is held (RBL), and Rule renderings are made from it.

## 1. Setting

S1. **His name.** "he was called Benedicts [Benedictus] or Bennet" (GRG 3408). Gregory makes a pun on it: "blessed by grace, and blessed in name" (GRG 3407–3408). LATER. Documented. The 1608 English always calls him "Bennet"; a writer should say "Benedict".

S2. **Birthplace and schooling.** "He was born in the province of Nursia, of honourable parentage, and brought up at Rome in the study of humanity" (GRG 3414–3416). LATER (Gregory). Documented as Gregory's account. "the study of humanity" means the liberal studies, letters.

S3. **Century.** No early held source gives a year for his birth, his move to Cassino, or his death.
- (a) Gregory places his later life "in the time of the Goths, when Totilas, their king" (GRG 4417–4418). LATER. Documented as Gregory's placing.
- (b) Gardner: "St. Benedict was born at Nursia about 480" (GRG-ed 12628). He founded Monte Cassino "about 529. Here in 543, fourteen years later, he died" (GRG-ed 1134–1136); in the notes, "in 528 or 529" (GRG-ed 12691–12692). The 543 dates are "According to the tradition" (GRG-ed 12777–12779). EDITOR.
- (c) Gasquet: "born at Nursia, a city of Umbria, in the year 480"; he moved to Monte Cassino "about the year 528"; "He died, according to the tradition of the Benedictine Order, on March 21, 543" (RBE-ed 170–173, 199–203). EDITOR.
- (d) The catalogue line of both Rule files reads "Benedict of Nursia (c. 480-c. 547)" (RBE 3; RBL 3). This is the Library's own metadata, not a source.
- Confidence: born about 480 = Dominant Modern Reconstruction. Death in 543 = Contested (a tradition; the file metadata gives c. 547). Founding of Monte Cassino about 528 or 529 = Dominant Modern Reconstruction.
- Plain ways to say it that the sheet supports: "around the year 500"; "in the early sixth century"; "when Gothic kings ruled Italy". It does not support an exact year of birth or death said as fact.

S4. **The Goths and the danger.** Gregory tells of Totila, king of the Goths, and of "A certain Goth there was called Galla, an Arian heretic, who, in the time of King Totilas, did with such monstrous cruelty persecute religious men of the Catholic church, that what priest or monk soever came in his presence, he never departed alive" (GRG 5213–5217). LATER. Documented as Gregory's account. Gardner warns: "It was naturally impossible for St. Gregory to take an impartial view of the Goths, and modern historians depict Totila in far more favourable colours" (GRG-ed 12737–12740). EDITOR.

S5. **Famine.** "there was a great dearth in the same country of Campania: so that all kind of people tasted of the misery" (GRG 4767–4769); and "At such time as there was a great dearth in Campania, the man of God had given away all the wealth of the Abbey to poor people" (GRG 5098–5100). LATER. Documented as Gregory's account.

S6. **Tradition.** The census name is **Early Benedictine / Italian Monasticism**, informally "The Early Benedictines", dated "c. 500-604", region Italy (CEN 39174, 39176, 39177, 39180). This name is for the card.
- (a) Plain spoken forms a narrator may say, each tied to the record: (1) "the monks of Italy, in the time of the Gothic kings", supported by GRG 4417–4418 and the monasteries Gregory names. (2) "the monasteries of Subiaco and Monte Cassino" (GRG 3489, 3844–3848, 4167–4183). (3) "a household of monks under an abbot", in the Rule's own terms: the Cenobites, "those who live in a monastery under a Rule or an abbot" (RBE 799–800).
- (b) Do not say "the Benedictine Order" or "his order" for his own lifetime (see N3). Do not say "the early Benedictines begin with him" as if they were a body he set up; the name is later.

## 2. Recorded moments (Gregory, Book II, in order)

All of M1–M14 are LATER (Gregory, about 50 years on), Documented as Gregory's account. Any wonder is marked WONDER and must stay in Gregory's voice.

M1. **He left his studies.** "But for as much as he saw many by reason of such learning to fall to dissolute and lewd life, he drew back his foot ... lest, entering too far in acquaintance therewith, he likewise might have fallen into that dangerous and godless gulf: wherefore, giving over his book, and forsaking his father's house and wealth, with a resolute mind only to serve God, he sought for some place, where he might attain to the desire of his holy purpose" (GRG 3416–3425). Gregory's phrase for him: "instructed with learned ignorance, and furnished with unlearned wisdom" (GRG 3425–3426).

M2. **Gregory names his informants.** "All the notable things and acts of his life I could not learn; but those few, which I mind now to report, I had by the relation of four of his disciples: to wit, of Constantinus, a most rare and reverent man, who was next Abbot after him; of Valentinianus, who many years had the charge of the Lateran Abbey; of Simplicius, who was the third General of his order; and lastly of Honoratus, who is now Abbot of that monastery in which he first began his holy life" (GRG 3427–3441). Note: "General of his order" is the 1608 translator's phrase. Gardner says only that Constantinus and Simplicius "were his two immediate successors, the second and third Abbots of Monte Cassino" (GRG-ed 12630–12632).

M3. **At Enfide with his nurse: the sieve.** His nurse followed him to "a place called Enside" (Gardner: read Enfide, now Affile, GRG-ed 12635–12636). He prayed over a broken borrowed sieve and "found it so whole, that the place could not be seen where before it was broken" (GRG 3447–3461). WONDER. The townsmen hung it at the church door, where it stayed "even to these very troubles of the Lombards" (GRG 3465–3471). Side story; see Leave out.

M4. **The cave at Subiaco and Romanus's bread.** He "fled privily from his nurse, and went into a desert place called Sublacum, distant almost forty miles from Rome" (GRG 3488–3490). A monk, Romanus, "vested him with the habit of holy conversation" (gave him the monk's habit) and kept his secret (GRG 3494–3498). "lived there in a strait cave, where he continued three years unknown to all men, except to Romanus" (GRG 3501–3503). "Romanus, from the top thereof, upon a long rope, did let down the loaf, upon which also with a band he tied a little bell, that by the ringing thereof the man of God might know when he came with his bread" (GRG 3508–3512). The next line has the devil break the bell with a stone (GRG 3512–3515; WONDER, leave out).

M5. **He did not know it was Easter.** A priest, told in a vision to feed him (WONDER, GRG 3523–3533), found him in the cave and said, "Rise up, brother, and let us dine, because to-day is the feast of Easter." Benedict answered: "1 [I] know that it is Easter with me and a great feast, having found so much favour at God's hands as this day to enjoy your company" (for by reason of his long absence from men, he knew not that it was the great solemnity of Easter) (GRG 3540–3546).

M6. **Shepherds; his name spreads.** "certain shepherds found him in that same cave: and at the first, when they espied him through the bushes, and saw his apparel made of skins, they verily thought that it had been some beast: but after they were acquainted with the servant of God, many of them were by his means converted ... and many after this went to visit him, and for corporal meat which they brought him, they carried away spiritual food for their souls" (GRG 3557–3567).

M7. **The nettles.** Tempted by the memory of a woman, "seeing many thick briers and nettle-bushes to grow hard by, off he cast his apparel, and threw himself into the midst of them" (GRG 3583–3592). "as himself did afterward report unto his disciples, Ke [he] found all temptation of pleasure so subdued, that he never felt any such thing" (GRG 3600–3603). Gregory sets a blackbird sent by "the tempter" before it (GRG 3575–3580). Side story; see Leave out.

M8. **Vicovaro: the abbot they tried to poison.** DEFENDED: regular discipline.
- The monks of a nearby monastery whose abbot had died begged him to lead them; "long time he denied them, saying that their manners were divers from his, and therefore that they should never agree together" (GRG 3648–3655).
- "Having now taken upon him the charge of the Abbey, he took order that regular life should be observed, so that none of them could, as before they used, through unlawful acts decline from the path of holy conversation" (GRG 3665–3669).
- "some of them began to devise, how they might rid him out of the way: and therefore, taking counsel together, they agreed to poison his wine" (GRG 3679–3682).
- When he made the sign of the cross over the glass, "the glass, that was holden far off, brake in pieces, as though the sign of the cross had been a stone thrown against it" (GRG 3684–3687). WONDER.
- "with a mild countenance and quiet mind, he called the monks together, and spake thus unto them: 'Almighty God have mercy upon you, and forgive you: why have you used me in this manner? Did not I tell you beforehand, that our manner of living could never agree together? Go your ways, and seek ye out some other father suitable to your own conditions, for I intend not now to stay any longer amongst you.'" (GRG 3690–3698). He "returned back to the wilderness which so much he loved" (GRG 3698–3706).
- Gregory's judgment: where there are "none good at all, that may receive spiritual profit, often times all labour is lost" (GRG 3784–3794).
- Place: Gregory does not name it. Gardner: "said to have been at Varia (the modern Vicovaro)" (GRG-ed 12653–12654). Gasquet says he was there "for three years" (RBE-ed 345–347); Gregory gives no length. EDITOR.

M9. **Twelve monasteries at Subiaco.** "many were by him in the same place drawn to the service of almighty God, so that by Christ's assistance he built there twelve Abbeys; over which he appointed governors, and in each of them placed twelve monks, and a few he kept with himself" (GRG 3845–3850). "many noble and religious men of Rome came unto him, and committed their children to be brought up under him ... Then also Evitius delivered him Maurus, and Tertullius the Senator brought Placidus" (GRG 3851–3856). Gardner: "Tertullius the senator" is "simply Tertullus patricius" (GRG-ed 12669–12670).

M10. **Work at Subiaco.** He gave a Goth, "poor of spirit, that gave over the world", a bill-hook to clear briars "for the making of a garden" (GRG 3955–3960). The wonders that follow (the iron head rising, Maurus running on the water) are WONDER and are left out.

M11. **He left Subiaco because of a priest's envy.** "one Florentius, Priest of a church hard by ... began to envy the holy man's virtues" (GRG 4039–4042). After a poisoned loaf (WONDER: a crow carries it off, GRG 4054–4080) and an attempt to tempt his young monks (GRG 4084–4091), "fearing the danger which thereby might ensue to his younger monks ... he gave place to envy; and therefore, after he had for those abbeys and oratories which he had there built appointed governors ... himself, in the company of a few monks, removed to another place" (GRG 4091–4098). When Florentius died and a monk rejoiced, Benedict "was passing sorrowful, and lamented much ... and therefore he gave him penance" (GRG 4116–4120).

M12. **Cassino: the temple of Apollo.** "the town, which is called Cassino, standeth upon the side of an high mountain ... in this place there was an ancient chapel in which the foolish and simple country people, according to the custom of the old gentiles, worshipped the god Apollo. Round about it likewise upon all sides, there were woods for the service of the devils, in which, even to that very time, the mad multitude of infidels did offer most wicked sacrifice. The man of God coming thither, beat in pieces the idol, overthrew the altar, set fire on the woods, and in the temple of Apollo, he built the oratory of St. Martin, and where the altar of the same Apollo was, he made an oratory ot [of] St. John: and by his continual preaching, he brought the people dwelling in those parts to embrace the faith of Christ" (GRG 4167–4184). Gregory adds the devil crying "Cursed Bennet, and not blessed" (GRG 4184–4221; WONDER). Gregory gives no distance or year. Paul the Deacon says "almost fifty miles from Sublacus" (PAUL 3279; LATE-LEGEND, see L1).

M13. **Totila.**
- (a) The test. Totila sent his guardsman Riggo in the royal robes; Benedict said, "Put off, my good son, put off that apparel, for that which thou hast on, is none of thine" (GRG 4415–4451). WONDER (second sight).
- (b) The meeting. "Then Totilas himself in person went unto the man of God; and seeing him sitting afar off, he durst not come near, but fell down to the ground: whom the holy man ... with his own hands lifted him up from the earth ... he reprehended him for his wicked deeds ... saying: 'Much wickedness do you daily commit, and many great sins have you done: now at length give over your sinful life. Into the city of Rome shall you enter, and over the sea shall you pass: nine years shall you reign, and in the tenth shall you leave this mortal life.' The king, hearing these things, was wonderfully afraid, and desiring the holy man to commend him to God in his prayers, he departed: and from that time forward he was nothing so cruel as before he had been" (GRG 4464–4487). The prophecy is a WONDER.
- (c) Gardner dates the meeting "probably" to 542 (GRG-ed 12724–12725). EDITOR, Dominant Modern Reconstruction. Totila "in 552 was defeated by Narses and killed" (GRG-ed 12736–12737).

M14. **The famine and the poor.** "the man of God had given away all the wealth of the Abbey to poor people, so that in the cellar there was nothing left but a little oil in a glass" (GRG 5099–5101). When the cellarer refused to give that oil to a subdeacon, Benedict had the glass thrown out of the window "to the end that nothing might remain in the Abbey contrary to obedience" (GRG 5117–5120). The glass not breaking and the barrel filling are WONDERS (GRG 5121–5153).

M15. **Galla the Goth.** Galla drove a bound countryman to the abbey, where he found Benedict "sitting before the gate, reading upon a book" (GRG 5231–5232); the bonds fell away (WONDER, GRG 5247–5249). Benedict "rose not up from his reading, but calling for some of his monks, commanded them to have him in, and to give him some meat ... admonishing him not to use any more such rigour and cruel dealing" (GRG 5254–5259).

M16. **Scholastica.** "his sister called Scholastica, dedicated from her infancy to our Lord, used once a year to come and visit her brother" (GRG 5346–5348). She asked him to stay the night; he refused, "saying that he might not by any means tarry all night out of his Abbey" (GRG 5357–5361). She prayed and a storm kept him (WONDER, GRG 5362–5396). Gregory: "of right she did more which loved more" (GRG 5409–5410). Three days later he saw her soul rise "in the likeness of a dove" (WONDER, GRG 5427–5430), and had her buried "in that grave which he had provided for himself" (GRG 5434–5435). Gardner: by tradition she died "on February 10, 543" (GRG-ed 12777–12778). Gregory never calls them twins.

M17. **The vision of the whole world.** "the whole world, gathered as it were together under one beam of the sun, was presented before his eyes", and he saw "the soul of Germanus, Bishop of Capua, in a fiery globe to be carried up by Angels into heaven" (GRG 5483–5489). WONDER, "as himself did afterward report" (GRG 5483–5484). Gardner: Germanus "died in 540 or 541" (GRG-ed 12795–12796).

M18. **Gregory on the Rule.** "the man of God amongst so many miracles, for which he was so famous in the world, was also sufficiently learned in divinity: for he wrote a rule for his monks, both excellent for discretion and also eloquent for the style. Of whose life and conversation, if any be curious to know further, he may in the institution of that rule understand all his manner of life and discipline: for the holy man could not otherwise teach, than himself lived" (GRG 5563–5572). Gregory's Latin for the first part, quoted by Gardner: "Nam scripsit monachorum regu/am [regulam], discretione praecipuam, sermone luculentam" (GRG-ed 12809–12810). Gregory does not say where or when the Rule was written.

M19. **His death.** "The same year in which he departed this life, he told the day of his holy death to his monks" (GRG 5577–5578; WONDER, foreknowledge). "Six days before he left this world, he gave order to have his sepulchre opened, and forthwith falling into an ague ... upon the sixth day he commanded his monks to carry him into the oratory, where he did arm himself with receiving the body and blood of our Saviour Christ; and having his weak body holden up betwixt the hands of his disciples, he stood with his own lifted up to heaven, and as he was in that manner praying, he gave up the ghost" (GRG 5582–5591). Two monks saw a road of lamps to heaven (WONDER, GRG 5592–5608). "Buried he was in the oratory of St. John Baptist which himself built, when he overthrew the altar of Apollo" (GRG 5612–5614). No year is given.

M20. **The abbey's fall, as Benedict foretold.** Theoprobus found him weeping: "All this Abbey which 1 [I] have built, and all such things as 1 [I] have made ready for my brethren, are by the judgment of almighty God delivered to the gentiles, to be spoiled and overthrown: and scarce could I obtain of God to have their lives spared" (GRG 4643–4649). Gregory: "we ... know that very Abbey to be now suppressed by the Lombards. For not long since, in the night time, when the monks were asleep, they entered in, and spoiled all things, but yet not one man could they retain there" (GRG 4657–4661). The prophecy is a WONDER; the sack is Documented as Gregory's report of his own time. Gardner: "destroyed by the Lombards of the duchy of Beneventum in 589" (GRG-ed 12752–12753). Foulke: "This attack actually occurred A. D. 589 ... Some historians indeed place it as early as 582" (PAUL-ed 7822–7824). Year: Contested.

## 3. His own words: the Rule (OWN)

Gasquet's English (RBE) and the Latin (RBL). The Latin is quoted from RBL, OCR faults kept.

R1. **A school for the Lord's service.** Latin: "Constituenda est ergo nobis Dominici ... schola servitu [servitii]; in qua institutione nihil asperum, nihil grave nos constituturos speramus." (RBL 1240–1245; line 1242 between "Dominici" and "schola" holds only marginal dates and dots, no words). English: "We are therefore now about to institute a school for the service of God, in which we hope nothing harsh nor burdensome will be ordained." (RBE 768–770). OWN. Documented. It goes on: "do not for fear of this forthwith flee from the way of salvation, which is always narrow in the beginning" (RBE 773–775), and ends with "persevering in the monastery until death" (RBE 781–787).

R2. **For monks who live together.** "The first are the Cenobites: that is, those who live in a monastery under a Rule or an abbot" (RBE 799–800). Gasquet: "It is for this latter class only that his Rule is intended" (RBE-ed 458–459). OWN; EDITOR. Documented.

R3. **The promise.** Latin: "omnibus promittat de stabilitate sua, et CONVERSIONE MORUM SUORUM, et OBEDIBNTIA [OBEDIENTIA]" (RBL 8568–8569). English: "promise before God and His saints stability, amendment of manners and obedience" (RBE 3589–3596). OWN. Documented. Meaning for a newcomer: stability = staying for life in this one community; *conversio morum* = turning one's whole way of life over to God (Gasquet's "amendment of manners" uses "manners" in an old sense; do not say "manners"); obedience = to the abbot under the Rule. The novice writes the promise himself, or has it written "if he is uneducated", and lays it "upon the altar" (RBE 3601–3605). He may never afterwards "withdraw his neck from the yoke of the rule" (RBL 8556–8559; Gasquet quotes it, RBE-ed 390–392).

R4. **Work and reading.** Latin: "[O]TIOSITAS inimica est animae: et ideo certis temporibus occupari debent fratres in labore manuum: certis iterum horis in lectione divina." (RBL 7358–7364; the drop capital O is lost in the OCR). English: "Idleness is an enemy of the soul. Because this is so the brethren ought to be occupied at specified times in manual labour, and at other fixed hours in holy reading." (RBE 3113–3116). OWN. Documented.

R5. **Guests.** Latin: "[O]MNES supervenientes hospites tamquam Christus suscipiantur" (RBL 7799–7800). English: "Let all guests who come be received as Christ would be, because He will say, I was a stranger, and ye took Me in." (RBE 3309–3311). OWN. Documented. Note: the Latin says "received as Christ"; Gasquet's "as Christ would be" softens it slightly.

R6. **The abbot is chosen and must temper all things.** "that he be made abbot whom the whole community, in the fear of God, make choice of, or a part of it, however small, acting with greater wisdom" and "chosen because of his virtuous life and his wisdom, even if he be the last in the community" (RBE 3900–3906). Latin: "Haec ergo aliaque testimonia discretionis matris virtutum sumens, sic omnia temperet, ut et sit quod fortes cupiant, et infirmi non refugiant" (RBL 9526–9528). English: "Wherefore adopting these and like principles of discretion, the mother of virtues, let him so temper all things that the strong may have their scope and the weak be not scared." (RBE 3958–3961). Also: "Let him aim at being loved rather than feared" (RBE 3949). OWN. Documented.

R7. **Nothing before Christ.** "Nihil amori Christi praeponere" (RBL 2185), "To prefer nothing to the love of Christ" (RBE 1166); and in ch. 72, "Christo omnino nihil praeponant" (RBL 10298–10299), "set nothing whatever before Christ" (RBE 4261). OWN. Documented.

R8. **A little rule for beginners, pointing to older teachers.** Latin: "hanc minimam inchoationis Regulam descriptam, adjuvante Christo, perfice" (RBL 10416–10417). English: "accomplish first, by the help of Christ, this little Rule written for beginners" (RBE 4297–4300). He names what lies beyond it: "the Collations of the Fathers, their Institutes, their Lives, and the Rule of our Holy Father St. Basil" (RBE 4290–4291; Latin "Collationes Patrum, et Instituta et Vitae eorum; sed et Regula sancti Patris nostri Basilii", RBL 10409–10411). OWN. Documented.

R9. **Evening reading.** After supper "let all sit together whilst one of them read the Collations, or Lives of the Fathers, or some other book to edify the hearers" (RBE 2894–2901; Latin "legat unus Collationes, vel Vitas Patrum", RBL 6839–6840). OWN. Documented. Gibson takes these for Cassian's books: "S. Benedict himself, who enjoins that the Conferences should be read daily by the monks of his order" (CAS 15506–15507). EDITOR. That the "Collations" are Cassian's = Widely Accepted; the Rule itself names no author.

## 4. Impact as the record gives it (heaviest first)

I1. **The Rule became the norm for Frankish monasteries.** Heaviest: three councils across sixty years used it as law.
- (a) 742, Carloman's first council (with Boniface): "Et ut monachi et ancillae Dei monasteriales iuxta regulam sancti Benedicti ordinare et vivere, vitanii [vitam] propriam gubernare studeant" (MGH 2324–2325). "And that monks and handmaids of God in monasteries are to order and live, and govern their own life, by the rule of Saint Benedict." Date: "742. April. 21" (MGH 2199). Carloman is "dux Francorum" in Boniface's letter (MGH 2203). It bound "the part of the kingdom of the Franks which is in his power" (MGH 2203–2204). LATER (about 200 years). Documented.
- (b) 743 or a little after, at Estinnes (Liftinas): "Abbates et monachi receperunt sancti patris Benedicti [regulam] ad restaurandam normam regularis vitae" (MGH 2450–2451). "Abbots and monks received the [rule] of the holy father Benedict to restore the norm of the regular life." Note: the word "regulam" is the editor's bracket; his note says it was "add[ed] by a second hand" in one manuscript, and the others lack it ("b) rcgnlain sec. manu add. 4", MGH 2475). Date: "743. vel paullo post" (MGH 177). LATER. Documented; the word "rule" is the editor's supply.
- (c) 802, Aachen, from the annals of Lorsch: Charlemagne "congregavit universos abbates et monachos qui ibi aderant, et ipsi inter se conventum faciebant et legerunt regulam sancti patris Benedicti, et eam tradiderunt sapientes in conspectu abbatum et monachorum" (MGH 7794–7796); his order was "quicquid in monasteriis seu in monachis contra regula sancti Benedicti factum fuisset, hoc ipsud iuxta ipsam regulam sancti Benedicti emendare fecissent" (MGH 7802–7805). LATER (about 260 years). Documented.
- (d) Gasquet's summary: "by the end of the eighth century not merely had St. Benedict's Rule superseded all others, but in France the memory of any other code had so completely perished that it could be gravely doubted whether monks of any kind had existed before" (RBE-ed 426–432); and from the ninth to the twelfth century "it was practically the only monastic Rule of the West" (RBE-ed 580–581). EDITOR (a Benedictine cardinal, 1909). Widely Accepted.
- (e) The Rule says it does not shut out other customs: Gasquet notes Benedict "expressly refers his followers to the Rule of St. Basil and to other authorities for further guidance" (RBE-ed 404–416). EDITOR.

I2. **The Rule was copied and carried.**
- The monks who fled the Lombard sack "made their way to Rome carrying with them the manuscript of the Holy Rule (of the order) which the aforesaid father had composed" (PAUL 7832–7835). LATER (Paul, 8th century). Documented as Paul's account. "(of the order)" is the translator's addition.
- "the oldest MS. at present known is Hatton MS. 42, written in England in the seventh or eighth century" (RBE-ed 589–591). EDITOR.
- Gardner: the Rule "became the norm according to which generations of men and women throughout the western world devoted themselves to the highest spiritual life" (GRG-ed 1168–1171). EDITOR.

I3. **Gregory's Life carried his memory.**
- Gregory wrote it from four disciples (M2), about fifty years on, and pointed readers to the Rule for his true life (M18).
- Gardner: the *Dialogues* "were translated into Greek by one of St. Gregory's successors, Pope Zaccharias I. (741-752)", and into Anglo-Saxon "about 890, by Bishop Werferth of Worcester, at the instigation of Alfred the Great" (GRG-ed 1269–1276). "among the most popular reading of the Middle Ages" (GRG-ed 1276–1277). EDITOR.
- Paul the Deacon: "His biography, as is known, the blessed Pope Gregory composed in delightful language in his Dialogues. I also ... have braided together ... each of his miracles by means of corresponding distichs in elegiac meter", and a hymn (PAUL 3266–3274). LATER. Documented.
- Dante put Gregory's words about Cassino into Benedict's mouth (GRG-ed 12692–12704). EDITOR. Card only.

I4. **Monte Cassino, its fall and his relics.**
- Gregory: the abbey sacked by the Lombards and no monk lost (M20).
- Paul the Deacon repeats it, "in fulfilment of a prophecy of the venerable father Benedict" (PAUL 7805–7813), and lists the abbots after him: "Constantine ... after him Simplicius; after him Vitalis; finally Bonitus under whom this destruction occurred" (PAUL 7838–7841).
- Paul: later, when "a great solitude existed for a number of years past" at Cassino, Franks from Le Mans and Orléans "bore away the bones of the reverend father and also of the revered Scolastica his sister, and carried them to their own country where two monasteries were built" (PAUL 11199–11209). Paul disputes part of it: "it is certain that that venerable mouth ... and the eyes ... and the other members too have remained to us" (PAUL 11209–11213). LATER. Contested.

I5. **What he took from those before him.** The Rule names Basil and "the Collations of the Fathers, their Institutes, their Lives" (R8, R9). Gasquet: the Prologue's opening shows he "had before him the Latin translation of St. Basil's Admonitio" (RBE-ed 448–453). Gardner: "based, in part, upon the eastern rule of St. Basil" (GRG-ed 1162–1163). EDITOR. Widely Accepted that he drew on Basil and Cassian.

**What he defended, as marked in the record:** regular discipline at Vicovaro, at the risk of his life (M8, GRG 3665–3682); obedience in the house over his own ease (M14, GRG 5117–5120); the monks' own rule of not eating outside the monastery (GRG 4320–4324, card only).

## 5. Links to other people

- **Gregory the Great** — wrote his life (Dialogues II), from four of his disciples; praised his Rule (GRG 3427–3441, 5563–5572).
- **Constantinus, Valentinianus, Simplicius, Honoratus** — his disciples; told Gregory his story (GRG 3436–3441).
- **Scholastica** — his sister; met him once a year; buried in his grave (GRG 5346–5348, 5434–5435).
- **Romanus** — monk who gave him the habit and fed him in the cave (GRG 3494–3512).
- **Maurus and Placidus** — boys given to him at Subiaco; his disciples (GRG 3854–3859).
- **Totila, king of the Goths** — met him (GRG 4464–4487).
- **Sabinus, bishop of Canusium** — visited him (GRG 4491–4497; Gardner names him, GRG-ed 12744–12746).
- **Germanus, bishop of Capua** — Benedict saw his soul rise, by Gregory's account (GRG 5488–5504).
- **Servandus, deacon and abbot** — his friend in spiritual talk (GRG 5444–5458).
- **Marcius, a hermit** — Benedict sent him word: "If thou be God's servant, let the chain of Christ, and not any chain of iron, hold thee" (GRG 7144–7147, Dialogues III).
- **Basil of Caesarea** — the Rule names "the Rule of our Holy Father St. Basil" (RBE 4291).
- **John Cassian** — the Rule has his "Collations" and "Institutes" read and commended (RBE 2900, 4290; CAS 15506–15507 for the identification).
- **Carloman and Boniface** — their council of 742 told monks and nuns to live by his Rule (MGH 2201–2204, 2324–2325).
- **Charlemagne** — at Aachen in 802 had the Rule read and enforced (MGH 7790–7805).
- **Paul the Deacon** — wrote verses on each of his miracles; told of the sack and the bones (PAUL 3266–3274, 7805–7841, 11199–11213).
- **Pope Zacharias** — translated Gregory's Dialogues into Greek (GRG-ed 1269–1271).

## 6. Disagreements, gaps and late material

D1. **One witness to the life.** Every event of his life comes from Gregory, about 50 years later, from four disciples, in a book written to show wonders. Even Gregory marks one report as second-hand: "Honoratus, by whose relation I had this, saith not that he received it from his own mouth, but that he had it of other monks" (GRG 4505–4508).

D2. **Dates.** Birth about 480 (Gardner, Gasquet); Monte Cassino about 528 or 529 (Gardner, Gasquet); death 543 "according to the tradition" (Gardner, Gasquet), c. 547 in the Library's catalogue line. Gregory gives none of these.

D3. **Where and when the Rule was written.** Gregory is silent. Gasquet: at Subiaco "probably ... he conceived the lines of his celebrated monastic code, which he compiled finally when, about the year 528, he removed to ... Monte Cassino" (RBE-ed 197–201). EDITOR. Dominant Modern Reconstruction.

D4. **The Rule of the Master.** The Library holds its Latin (`regula-magistri-cresconius-pl88-lat_migne1862.txt`), but **no held scholarly source** argues the relation to Benedict's Rule; only the census and the file's own catalogue note say it. Not usable in the story.

D5. **Vicovaro's name, and three years there.** Editors' (M8). Gregory names neither.

D6. **The year of the Lombard sack.** Gardner 589; Foulke 589, "some ... as early as 582" (M20). Contested.

D7. **The bones.** Paul says Franks took them; Paul also says some remained (I4). Contested.

D8. **Gasquet's large claims.** That missionaries such as "Augustine in England, Boniface in Germany" were "trained ... under the Rule of St. Benedict" (RBE-ed 93–104), and that the Rule is "a chief factor in the work of European civilization" (RBE-ed 84–86), are a 1909 Benedictine cardinal's judgments. No early held source here says Augustine of Canterbury lived under Benedict's Rule. Card only, as Gasquet's view.

D9. **Paul's additions (LATE-LEGEND).** Ravens following him, two angels at each crossroad, a voice from heaven telling a hermit at Cassino to leave (PAUL 3276–3288). Paul says Gregory "did not at all describe" these. Not story material.

D10. **Not held.** Gregory's Latin text of Book II (except one sentence); any letter of Gregory naming Benedict (not searched further; the *Dialogues* suffice); any other sixth-century witness to Benedict. No modern study of the Rule's sources beyond Gasquet's and Gardner's remarks is held.
