Before a battle at Worms, the Caesar Julian was paying each of his soldiers a gift of money. A young soldier named Martin asked for his discharge instead, thinking it wrong to take the money if he meant to leave. As his friend Sulpicius Severus reports it, he said: "I am Christ's soldier. I am not allowed to fight."

This was Roman Gaul, in what is now France, in the fourth century. From the army Martin went to Hilary, bishop of Poitiers, and later set up a monastery nearby. Years after that, the people of Tours made him bishop, yet he lived on as a monk, in a wooden cell with eighty disciples who held everything in common. Cities wanted their priests from his monastery, and Sulpicius saw many of his monks made bishops. At an emperor's court, Martin would plead that a church's case was not for a worldly judge. Sulpicius wrote his life while he still lived, a book a modern historian calls a model for later lives of saints.

Almost all we know comes from that one admirer, who met him late in life. Born to pagans in what is now Hungary, Martin was fifteen when his own father informed on him, and he was chained and sworn into the army.

Years later, at Worms, Julian called his refusal cowardice. Martin offered to stand unarmed before the battle line, and was put back in prison. The next day the enemy surrendered before any battle, and Sulpicius credits Martin with that peace.

Arian opponents later had him beaten in public and driven from a city, and at his election some bishops scorned his poor looks and clothes. As bishop he tore down pagan temples and built churches where they had stood. Sometimes his preaching moved people to pull down their own, but once a crowd drove him off and he was hurt.

At Trier, bishops were prosecuting Priscillian and his followers for heresy before the emperor Maximus. Martin did not defend their teaching, but he begged that their blood not be shed. In Sulpicius's report he said: "It was a cruel wrong, never heard of before, for a judge of this world to try a case of the church." The trial waited while he was there, but after he left, Priscillian and his companions were put to death. On a later visit, as a former disciple tells it, Martin won the lives of men already condemned by sharing communion for one day with the bishops behind the deaths. He refused to sign his name, grieved over it, and never again went to a gathering of bishops.

At his funeral, it was said, almost two thousand monks gathered, and a great church later rose over his grave. Gregory, bishop of Tours almost two hundred years on, tells the faithful to keep his burial day, 11 November, and tells how King Clovis let his army take only grass and water from Martin's lands. By then the poet Fortunatus had put Sulpicius's book into verse, and Gregory added a book of Martin's miracles.
