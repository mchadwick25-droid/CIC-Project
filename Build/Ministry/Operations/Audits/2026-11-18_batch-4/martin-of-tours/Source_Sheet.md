# Source sheet: Martin of Tours

Researcher's sheet, varied batch 2, 2026-11-17. Evidence only; no story prose.

**Files and short names** (all in `cic/texts/` unless stated):
- **NP11** = `npnf211_sulpitius-severus-vincent-lerins-cassian.xml`. Sulpicius Severus in Alexander Roberts's English (NPNF 2nd series vol. 11): *Life of St. Martin* (Life), *Letters* I–III (Ep.), *Dialogues* I–III (Dial.), *Sacred History* = Chronicle (Chron.). Also Roberts's own introduction and notes, and Edgar Gibson's *Prolegomena* to Cassian.
- **HALM** = `sulpicius-severus_libri-qui-supersunt-chronica-vita-martini-lat_halm1866-csel1.txt`. The same works in Latin (Halm, CSEL 1, 1866), OCR.
- **GT** = `gregory-tours_history-of-the-franks-selections_brehaut1916.txt`. Gregory of Tours, *History of the Franks* (selections), tr. Ernest Brehaut, 1916, with Brehaut's introduction and notes, OCR.
- Held alternative, not cited here: `gregory-tours_history-of-the-franks-v2_dalton1927.txt`, Gregory of Tours, *History of the Franks*, vol. II (Books VI–X), tr. O. M. Dalton, 1927. Card only.
- **VF** = `venantius-fortunatus_opera-mgh-aa-4-lat_leo-krusch1881.txt`. Venantius Fortunatus, verse *Life of Martin* and its letter to Gregory (MGH, Leo 1881), Latin OCR.
- **CEN** = `cic-website/data/world-census.json` (setting and spoken tradition name only).

**Conventions.** Line numbers are the file's own. English is quoted exactly as in the file, with XML tags removed. Roberts prints his notes inline in the XML; where a note falls inside a quoted sentence it is cut and marked `[note omitted]`. Square brackets otherwise are the translator's. In the OCR files (HALM, GT, VF) I give the text as it stands and put a corrected reading in {braces} where the OCR is wrong; words split across lines are joined. Chapter titles in NP11 ("Martin restores a Catechumen to Life") are Roberts's headings, not Sulpicius's.

**Time gaps.** Rough estimates for the card only, resting on Brehaut's date of death, 397 (GT 595, 11933), and the file dates of each writer. Not story material.

**Witness kinds.**
- **CONTEMP** = Sulpicius Severus. He knew Martin, visited him and questioned him (Life 25, NP11 1830–1837), and wrote the Life while Martin was alive: Ep. I, written after the Life was "published", speaks of Martin as having "recently become subject to the power of fire" (NP11 1996–2003). For most events he was *not* present and relies on Martin and on "those who had lived with him" (NP11 1835–1837); for the death he was not present and sends readers to "those who were present" (Ep. III, NP11 2364–2366). His *Dialogues* II–III are spoken by "the Gaul", one of Martin's former disciples (NP11 2545–2548), in a dialogue Sulpicius wrote. The Chronicle runs "down to the year a.d. 400" (Roberts, NP11 441–443).
- **LATER** = Gregory of Tours, bishop of Tours, whose History ends with an epilogue "written in 594" (GT 352, 430): about 180–195 years after the death. Venantius Fortunatus, whose verse Life is addressed to Gregory (VF 24716–24720): about 175–190 years later.
- **MODERN** = Roberts (1894 edition), Gibson, Brehaut (1916). Used for framing and dates only.
- No witness here is LATE-LEGEND in the sense of an anonymous work of unknown date; but Gregory's stories of the body and the tomb are 180+ years later and are marked so.

**Not held.** Paulinus of Nola's letters to Sulpicius (only quoted by Roberts, NP11 455–466); Paulinus of Périgueux's verse Life (only named in VF 935); Gregory's own *Miracles of St Martin* in English (the Latin MGH Gregory file `gregory-tours_opera-mgh-srm-1-lat_arndt-krusch1885.txt` is held but was not searched for this sheet). No word written by Martin himself survives in these files. Every word of Martin's below is reported by Sulpicius.

## 1. Setting

S1. **Name.** Latin *Martinus* (HALM 6286, 6337 region). The census: "Martin of Tours" (CEN 94104). Documented.

S2. **Birth and upbringing.** "Martin, then, was born at Sabaria [note omitted] in Pannonia, but was brought up at Ticinum, [note omitted] which is situated in Italy. His parents were, according to the judgment of the world, of no mean rank, but were heathens." (Life 2, NP11 697–701). Roberts's notes: Sabaria = "Sarwar", Ticinum = "Pavia" (NP11 698–699). Brehaut's note on Gregory: Pannonia "In Hungary." (GT 1423). Gregory: "born at Sabaria, a city of Pannonia, of heathen parents, who still were not of the lowest station" (GT 1416–1418). CONTEMP; LATER. Documented.

S3. **Century.** No held text of Sulpicius gives a year. He names the rulers: Martin served "first under king Constantine, and then under Julian Cæsar" (NP11 703–704); he later dealt with "the Emperor Maximus" (NP11 1560). Gregory dates the death "In the second year of the reign of Arcadius and Honorius" and "in the consulship of Atticus and Cæsarius" (GT 1470, 1479–1480; OCR "Csesarius"). Brehaut: "St. Martin died in 397." (GT 11933); "he died on November 8, 397" (GT 595). MODERN. Widely Accepted. Spoken form the sheet supports: **"in the fourth century"**, **"late in the fourth century"** for his years as bishop. Gibson (MODERN): "Ligugé was founded shortly after 360, and Marmoutier rather later, after 371" (NP11 15446–15447). Dominant Modern Reconstruction. Do not give a birth year (see D1).

S4. **Place.** Tours, on the Loire, in Gaul. "the episcopate of the church at Tours" (NP11 1059–1060); his monastery "about two miles outside the city", shut in by "a precipitous rock of a lofty mountain" and "the river Loire" (NP11 1129–1132). Census: modern country France, place Tours (CEN 36820–36825; 94113–94115). Documented. Gaul was then under Roman emperors (Julian Caesar, Maximus, above). "Roman Gaul" is supported by those names.

S5. **Tradition.** Census name **Gallic Monastic-Ascetic Christianity** (CEN 36813), short name "Gallic Monasticism" (CEN 36814), world name "The Monk-Bishops of Gaul" (CEN 36856), dates "c. 360-450" (CEN 36815). The census's own description: "This tradition grew up around bishops who never stopped living as monks, and around wisdom first learned in the deserts of Egypt. Its first figure, Martin of Tours, was a Roman soldier." (CEN 36859). Plain spoken forms a narrator may say:
  1. **"the monks and monk-bishops of Gaul"**, from "The Monk-Bishops of Gaul" (CEN 36856).
  2. **"in Roman Gaul, in what is now France"** (S4).
  3. **"among the first monks of Gaul"**, supported only as Gibson's modern statement that when Cassian left Gaul "monasticism was a thing almost if not quite unknown there" and that "a few monasteries had been founded in the district of the Loire by S. Martin and S. Hilary of Poictiers" (NP11 15443–15446). Dominant Modern Reconstruction. Do **not** say "the first monk of the West" or "founder of Western monasticism": no held text says it, and Gregory's Tours speakers say "his first monastery was at Milan" (GT 1502–1503).
  Do not say "Gallic Monastic-Ascetic" aloud.

S6. **What being a catechumen meant.** At ten "he betook himself, against the wish of his parents, to the Church, and begged that he might become a catechumen." (NP11 710–712). Sulpicius glosses it: "he, by his good works, acted the part of a candidate for baptism" (NP11 750–751). So: a catechumen is one under instruction who is not yet baptised. CONTEMP. Documented.

S7. **What a donative was.** Julian "began to distribute a donative to the soldiers. As was the custom in such a case, they were called forward, one by one" (NP11 834–835). Latin *donatiuum* (HALM 6327; OCR "donatinum"). A gift of money paid out to soldiers. Documented.

S8. **The danger around Trier (for M10–M11).** Priscillian and his followers had been charged with heresy by the bishops Ydacius and Ithacius (Chron. II.50, NP11 11638–11640). The emperor Maximus held court at Trier (Treves) (NP11 11613–11615; 11653). Documented as Sulpicius's account.

## 2. Life events

M1. **Forced into the army.** "when an edict was issued by the ruling powers [note omitted] in the state, that the sons of veterans should be enrolled for military service, and he, on the information furnished by his father, (who looked with an evil eye on his blessed actions) having been seized and put in chains, when he was fifteen years old, was compelled to take the military oath" (Life 2, NP11 720–727). His father "was at first simply a soldier, but afterwards a military tribune" (NP11 701–702). Also: "This, however, was not done of his own free will" (NP11 704–705). CONTEMP. Documented as Sulpicius's account.

M2. **Soldier who lived like a monk.** He kept "only one servant", and "for the most part, he drew off his [servant's] boots and cleaned them with his own hand" (NP11 727–731). "he was regarded not so much as being a soldier as a monk" (NP11 739–740). He gave to "the needy", "clothing the naked, while he reserved nothing for himself from his military pay except what was necessary for his daily sustenance" (NP11 752–755). CONTEMP. Documented as Sulpicius's account (praise).

M3. **The cloak at Amiens.** "in the middle of winter, a winter which had shown itself more severe than ordinary, so that the extreme cold was proving fatal to many, he happened to meet at the gate of the city of Amiens [note omitted] a poor man destitute of clothing." (Life 3, NP11 767–773). "He had nothing except the cloak in which he was clad, for he had already parted with the rest of his garments for similar purposes. Taking, therefore, his sword with which he was girt, he divided his cloak into two equal parts, and gave one part to the poor man, while he again clothed himself with the remainder." (NP11 777–782). "some of the by-standers laughed, because he was now an unsightly object, and stood out as but partly dressed. Many, however, who were of sounder understanding, groaned deeply because they themselves had done nothing similar." (NP11 782–785). Latin: "chlamydem" (HALM 6273), "mediam diuidit" (HALM 6276). CONTEMP. Documented as Sulpicius's account. (Gregory and Fortunatus retell it, LATER.)

M4. **The dream of Christ in the half-cloak. WONDER, Sulpicius's voice.** "In the following night, when Martin had resigned himself to sleep, he had a vision of Christ arrayed in that part of his cloak with which he had clothed the poor man." (NP11 788–790). "he heard Jesus saying with a clear voice to the multitude of angels standing round—“Martin, who is still but a catechumen, clothed [note omitted] me with this robe.”" (NP11 792–795). Latin: "audit Iesum clara uoce dicentem : Martinus adhuc catechumenus hac me ueste contexit." (HALM 6285–6288; OCR "lesum", "sobac" {hac}, "coutexit" {contexit}). Sulpicius ties it to Matthew 25:40 (NP11 795–802). CONTEMP. Documented as Sulpicius's report of what Martin saw asleep.

M5. **Baptism, then two more years under arms.** "After this vision the sainted man was not puffed up with human glory, but, acknowledging the goodness of God in what had been done, and being now of the age of twenty years, he hastened to receive baptism. He did not, however, all at once, retire from military service, yielding to the entreaties of his tribune" (NP11 805–809). The tribune "promised that, after the period of his office had expired, he too would retire from the world" (NP11 812–814). Martin "continued, although but in name, to act the part of a soldier, for nearly two years after he had received baptism." (NP11 814–816). CONTEMP. Documented as Sulpicius's account.

M6. **"I am the soldier of Christ."** At Worms (city "of the Vangiones", note "the modern Worms", NP11 831–833), at the donative: "judging it a suitable opportunity for seeking his discharge—for he did not think it would be proper for him, if he were not to continue in the service, to receive a donative—he said to Cæsar, “Hitherto I have served you as a soldier: allow me now to become a soldier to God: let the man who is to serve thee receive thy donative: I am the soldier of Christ: it is not lawful for me to fight.”" (Life 4, NP11 836–842). Julian "stormed", "declaring that, from fear of the battle, which was to take place on the morrow, and not from any religious feeling, Martin withdrew from the service." (NP11 842–845). Martin: "“If this conduct of mine is ascribed to cowardice, and not to faith, I will take my stand unarmed before the line of battle tomorrow, and in the name of the Lord Jesus, protected by the sign of the cross, and not by shield or helmet, I will safely penetrate the ranks of the enemy.”" (NP11 847–851). "He is ordered, therefore, to be thrust back into prison" (NP11 851–852). "But, on the following day, the enemy sent ambassadors to treat about peace and surrendered both themselves and all their possessions." (NP11 853–855). Sulpicius's reading: "who can doubt that this victory was due to the saintly man?" (NP11 855–856). Latin: "hactenus, inquit ad Caesarem, militaui tibi: patere ut nunc militem Deo: donatiuum tuum pugnaturus accipiat, Christi ego miles sum: pugnare mihi non licet." (HALM 6334–6337; OCR "Shactenus", "militani", "nt", "donatlnum tnnm pugnatums", "snm"). Note: Latin *pugnaturus* is "the one who is going to fight"; Roberts's "the man who is to serve thee" is loose. CONTEMP. Documented as Sulpicius's account; the peace as Martin's doing is Sulpicius's interpretation.

M7. **To Hilary of Poitiers; made an exorcist.** "From that time quitting military service, Martin earnestly sought after the society of Hilarius, bishop of the city Pictava, [note omitted] whose faith in the things of God was then regarded as of high renown" (Life 5, NP11 872–879). "For some time Martin made his abode with him." (NP11 879). Hilary tried to make him a deacon; "when he constantly refused, crying out that he was unworthy", Hilary "appointed him to be an exorcist. Martin did not refuse this appointment, from the fear that he might seem to have looked down upon it as somewhat humble." (NP11 882–889). Roberts's note: the territory of the Pictavi "corresponded to the modern diocese of Poitiers" (NP11 874–877). CONTEMP. Documented.

M8. **Home to Pannonia; robbers; mother converted; exile.** Warned in a dream to visit his heathen parents (NP11 889–892); robbers in the Alps; one robber "believed" and was "afterwards seen leading a religious life", the source of the account (NP11 897–919). "he set free his mother from the errors of heathenism, though his father continued to cleave to its evils." (NP11 937–939). Against "the Arian heresy" in Illyria "he was publicly scourged, and at last was compelled to leave the city" (NP11 942–947). With Hilary "driven into exile" by "the violence of the heretics", "he established a monastery for himself at Milan", whence the Arian bishop Auxentius "violently expelled him from the city" (NP11 947–953). Then the island Gallinaria with a presbyter, living "for some time on the roots of plants" (NP11 954–960). CONTEMP. Documented as Sulpicius's account. Side matter; see Leave out.

M9. **The monastery near Poitiers.** When Hilary returned, "Martin followed in his footsteps; and having been most joyously welcomed by him, he established for himself a monastery not far from the town." (Life 7, NP11 977–980). Gibson names it Ligugé, "founded shortly after 360" (NP11 15446). Sulpicius does not name it. CONTEMP; MODERN. Documented (monastery); name Dominant Modern Reconstruction. Here Sulpicius tells the raising of a dead catechumen, a WONDER (NP11 980–1025), from that man's own account: "he was the first who offered himself to us both as a subject that had experienced the virtues [note omitted] of Martin, and as a witness to their existence." (NP11 1008–1013). Sulpicius: "From this time forward, the name of the sainted man became illustrious" (NP11 1023–1024).

M10. **Made bishop of Tours by a trick and a crowd.** "Martin was called upon to undertake the episcopate of the church at Tours; [note omitted] but when he could not easily be drawn forth from his monastery, a certain Ruricius, one of the citizens, pretending that his wife was ill, and casting himself down at his knees, prevailed on him to go forth. Multitudes of the citizens having previously been posted by the road on which he traveled, he is thus under a kind of guard escorted to the city." (Life 9, NP11 1058–1068). People came "not only from that town, but also from the neighboring cities ... to give their votes" (NP11 1068–1070). "A few persons, however, and among these some of the bishops, who had been summoned to appoint a chief priest, were impiously offering resistance, asserting forsooth that Martin's person was contemptible, that he was unworthy of the episcopate, that he was a man despicable in countenance, that his clothing was mean, and his hair disgusting." (NP11 1076–1081). Census date for him: "activeYear": 371 (CEN 94103), matching Gibson's "after 371" for Marmoutier. CONTEMP. Documented as Sulpicius's account; year Dominant Modern Reconstruction (not stated in Sulpicius).

M11. **A bishop who stayed a monk; the monastery outside Tours.** "he remained with the utmost constancy, the same as he had been before. There was the same humility in his heart, and the same homeliness in his garments. Full alike of dignity and courtesy, he kept up the position of a bishop properly, yet in such a way as not to lay aside the objects and virtues of a monk." (Life 10, NP11 1119–1125). He used "the cell connected with the church; but afterwards, when he felt it impossible to tolerate the disturbance caused by the numbers of those visiting it, he established a monastery for himself about two miles outside the city." (NP11 1126–1129). "Here, then, he possessed a cell constructed of wood. Many also of the brethren had, in the same manner, fashioned retreats for themselves, but most of them had formed these out of the rock of the overhanging mountain, hollowed into caves. There were altogether eighty disciples" (NP11 1134–1138). "No one there had anything which was called his own; all things were possessed in common." (NP11 1139–1140). "No art was practiced there, except that of transcribers, and even this was assigned to the brethren of younger years, while the elders spent their time in prayer." (NP11 1142–1144). "No one used wine, except when illness compelled them to do so. Most of them were clothed in garments of camels' hair." (NP11 1147–1148). Gibson calls it Marmoutier (NP11 15447). CONTEMP. Documented as Sulpicius's account. "transcribers" = copyists of books.

M12. **The false martyr's altar.** Martin doubted a shrine where "some martyrs had been buried": he "often asked ... that the name of the martyr, or the time when he suffered, should be made known to him", "because he had great scruples on these points, inasmuch as no steady tradition respecting them had come down from antiquity." (Life 11, NP11 1170–1178). He kept away, "by no means wishing to lessen the religious veneration with which it was regarded, because he was as yet uncertain, but, at the same time not lending his authority to the opinion of the multitude, lest a mere superstition should obtain a firmer footing" (NP11 1178–1182). Then a WONDER, Sulpicius's voice: a "shade" confessed he had been a robber; "Those who stood by heard ... the voice of the speaker, but they beheld no person." Martin "ordered the altar which had been there to be removed" (NP11 1186–1197). CONTEMP. Documented as Sulpicius's account.

M13. **Against the temples of the countryside.** "when in a certain village he had demolished a very ancient temple, and had set about cutting down a pine-tree, which stood close to the temple, the chief priest of that place, and a crowd of other heathens began to oppose him." (Life 13, NP11 1244–1248). "Martin carefully instructed them that there was nothing sacred in the trunk of a tree" (NP11 1250–1251). Outside the wonder, the record does not say Martin felled the tree; in the wonder the pagans cut it down themselves (NP11 1254–1267). Then the WONDER of the falling pine, Sulpicius's voice (NP11 1254–1288). Sulpicius's claim: "before the times of Martin, very few, nay, almost none, in those regions had received the name of Christ" (NP11 1292–1293); "wherever he destroyed heathen temples, there he used immediately to build either churches or monasteries." (NP11 1296–1298). At Leprosum "a multitude of the heathen resisted him to such a degree that he was driven back not without bodily injury." (Life 14, NP11 1319–1323). Not only force: "Very frequently, too, when the pagans were addressing him to the effect that he would not overthrow their temples, he so soothed and conciliated the minds of the heathen by his holy discourse that, the light of truth having been revealed to them, they themselves overthrew their own temples." (Life 15, NP11 1365–1369). Brehaut (MODERN): "He had devoted himself chiefly to the task of Christianizing the pagani or rural population of Gaul" (GT 122–124). CONTEMP. Documented as Sulpicius's account (acts); Sulpicius's claim of near-total conversion is his praise.

M14. **At Maximus's table.** At court, "in Martin alone, apostolic authority continued to assert itself" (Life 20, NP11 1564–1565). He refused the emperor's table, "saying that he could not take a place at the table of one who, out of two emperors, had deprived one of his kingdom, and the other of his life." (NP11 1567–1570). When he came at last, the cup was handed to him: "Martin, when he had drunk, handed the goblet to his own presbyter, as thinking no one worthier to drink next to himself" (NP11 1590–1592). CONTEMP. Documented as Sulpicius's account.

M15. **Trier: the Priscillian case (Chronicle).** Sulpicius on the accusers: "I would by no means blame their zeal in overthrowing heretics, if they had not contended for victory with greater keenness than was fitting. And my feeling indeed is, that the accusers were as distasteful to me as the accused." (Chron. II.50, NP11 11640–11643). Ithacius "proceeded even to such a pitch of folly as to charge all those men, however holy, who either took delight in reading, or made it their object to vie with each other in the practice of fasting, with being friends or disciples of Priscillian. The miserable wretch even ventured publicly to bring forward a disgraceful charge of heresy against Martin" (NP11 11646–11651). "For Martin, being then settled at Treves, did not cease to importune Ithacius, that he should give up his accusations, or to implore Maximus that he should not shed the blood of the unhappy persons in question. He maintained that it was quite sufficient punishment that, having been declared heretics by a sentence of the bishops, they should have been expelled from the churches; and that it was, besides, a foul and unheard-of indignity, that a secular ruler should be judge in an ecclesiastical cause." (NP11 11652–11660). Latin: "namque tum Martinus apud Treueros constitutus non desinebat increpare Ithacium, ut ab accusatione desisteret, Maximum orare, ut sanguine infelicium abstineret: satis superque sufficere, ut episcopali sententia haeretici iudicati ecclesiis pellerentur: saeuum esse et inauditum nefas, ut causam ecclesiae iudex saeculi iudicaret." (HALM 5901–5907; OCR "Don" {non}, "desinebatincrepare", "Ithflcium", "aecusatione", "Maiimum", "sai^ine" {sanguine}, "snfficere", "saeunm" {saeuum}, "nt cansam eccle-/aosiae" {ut causam ecclesiae}). Apparatus: "19 Beanm P, nonum b" (HALM 5924): the main manuscript P has a form of *saeuum* ("savage, cruel"); an early edition, b, reads *nouum* ("new"). Then: "denique quoad usque Martinus Treueris fuit, dilata cognitio est: et mox discessurus egregia auctoritate a Maximo elicuit sponsionem, nihil cruentum in reos constituendum." (HALM 5907–5910; OCR "nsque", "Tre-/neris", "etmoi discessnms ^r^a" {et mox discessurus egregia}, "Maiimo", "cmentnm" {cruentum}). Roberts's English of this sentence: "And, in fact, as long as Martin survived, the trial was put off; while, when he was about to leave this world, he, by his remarkable influence, obtained a promise from Maximus, that no cruel measure would be resolved on with respect to the guilty persons." (NP11 11660–11663). **Roberts mistranslates here (D3).** The Latin says "as long as Martin was at Trier" and "when he was about to depart", that is, leave Trier. Then Maximus, "led astray by Magnus and Rufus", gave the case to the prefect Evodius, and "the emperor decreed that Priscillian and his friends should be put to death." (NP11 11663–11674). CONTEMP (Sulpicius was not at Trier). Documented as Sulpicius's account.

M16. **Trier: the second visit (Dialogue III).** Spoken by "the Gaul" in Sulpicius's Dialogue. After Priscillian's death, Maximus planned "to send some tribunes armed with absolute power into the two Spains, to search out heretics, and, when found, to deprive them of their life or goods" (Dial. III.11, NP11 5090–5093). "a judgment was formed simply by appearances, so that one was deemed a heretic rather on his turning pale from fear, or wearing a particular garment, than by the faith which he professed." (NP11 5098–5101). "his chief request was, that tribunes, with the power of life and death, should not be sent into the Spains. For Martin felt a pious solicitude not only to save from danger the true Christians in these regions, who were to be persecuted in connection with that expedition, but to protect even heretics themselves." (NP11 5120–5125). He would not share communion with the bishops of Ithacius's party (NP11 5145–5147). When executioners were appointed "for those in whose behalf Martin had made supplication", "he rushed to the palace, though it was now night. He pledges himself that, if these people were spared, he would communicate; only let the tribunes ... be recalled. There is no delay: Maximus grants all his requests." (Dial. III.13, NP11 5176–5191). "Martin took part in the communion of that day, judging it better to yield for the moment, than to disregard the safety of those over whose heads a sword was hanging. Nevertheless, although the bishops strove to the uttermost to get him to confirm the fact of his communicating by signing his name, he could not be induced to do so." (NP11 5193–5198). On the road home "he was filled with mourning and lamentation that he had even for an hour been mixed up with the evil communion" (NP11 5199–5201). An angel then spoke to him: WONDER, the Gaul's voice (NP11 5207–5210). "He lived sixteen years after this, but never again did he attend a synod, and kept carefully aloof from all assemblies of bishops." (NP11 5217–5219; Latin "sedecim postea uixit annos", HALM 11929, OCR "uiiit"). "to communicate" = to share the church's communion with them, a public sign of fellowship. CONTEMP (report of a disciple, in Sulpicius's dialogue). Documented as their account; the "sixteen years" see D4.

M17. **Sulpicius visits him.** "I undertook what was to me a pleasant journey for the purpose of seeing him. At the same time, because already my mind was inflamed with the desire of writing his life, I obtained my information partly from himself, in so far as I could venture to question him, and partly from those who had lived with him, or well knew the facts of the case." (Life 25, NP11 1832–1837). "He went so far as in person to present me with water to wash my hands, and at eventide he himself washed my feet" (NP11 1842–1844). Martin's talk was "that the allurements of this world and secular burdens were to be abandoned in order that we might be free and unencumbered in following the Lord Jesus" (NP11 1848–1850), and he held up Paulinus, who sold "great possessions" (NP11 1852–1855). CONTEMP. Documented.

M18. **His manner.** "No one ever saw him enraged, or excited, or lamenting, or laughing; he was always one and the same" (Life 27, NP11 1927–1928). "he allowed himself to be wronged by the lowest clerics with impunity" (Life 26, NP11 1915–1916). "some of his calumniators, although very few, some of his maligners, I say, were reported to be no others than bishops!" (NP11 1939–1941). CONTEMP. Documented as Sulpicius's praise and complaint.

M19. **Death at Candes.** Sulpicius (not present; NP11 2364–2366): he went to "the church at Condate" because "the clerics of that church were at variance among themselves", "wishing to restore peace, although he well knew that the end of his own days was at hand" (Ep. III, NP11 2382–2390). When the brothers begged him not to leave them, "Martin, affected by these lamentations, ... is said to have burst into tears; and, turning to the Lord, he replied to those weeping round him only in the following words, “O Lord, if I am still necessary to thy people, I do not shrink from toil: thy will be done.”" (NP11 2425–2431). Latin: "lacrimasse perhibetur: conuersusque ad Dominum hac tantum flentibus uoce respondit: Domine, si adhuc populo tuo sum necessarius, non recuso laborem; fiat uoluntas tua." (HALM 8230–8234; OCR "lacrimasae perhi-./betur", "connersusqne", "Donunum", "re-/spondit", "I>omine", "snm", "non re-/12CUB0 laborem" {non recuso laborem}). Asked for straw beneath him: "“It is not fitting that a Christian should die except among ashes; and I have sinned if I leave you a different example.”" (NP11 2464–2466). Gregory: "Saint Martin, bishop of Tours, departed this life at Candes, a village of his diocese, and passed happily to Christ in the eighty-first year of his life and the twenty-sixth of his episcopate" (GT 1471–1477; the page heading at GT 1474 is left out). Roberts's note places Condate at "modern Cosne" (NP11 2383–2387), which differs from Gregory's Candes; see D6. CONTEMP (second-hand); LATER. Documented (death on a peace-making visit); the words are reported ("is said to have").

M20. **The funeral.** "the whole city poured forth to meet his body; all the inhabitants of the district and villages, along with many also from the neighboring cities, attended." (NP11 2488–2491). "the sorrowing monks! They are said to have assembled on that day almost to the number of two thousand,—a special glory of Martin,—through his example so numerous plants had sprung up for the service of the Lord." (NP11 2491–2495). "Then, too, there was the choir of virgins" (NP11 2499). "Martin, poor and insignificant on earth, has a rich entrance granted him into heaven." (NP11 2520–2521). CONTEMP (second-hand). Documented as reported; the number is hedged ("said", "almost").

## 3. Impact, as the record gives it (heaviest first)

I1. **His tomb at Tours became the centre of the Gallic church's devotion, and kings and bishops used his name.** (a) Later bishops built for it: Perpetuus, "fifth bishop after the blessed Martin", "saw that miracles were being worked continually at Saint Martin's tomb, and that the chapel which had been built over it was a tiny one", so "he built there a great church which remains to the present day" (GT 2194–2204). (b) The feast was kept: "The feast of the church is given sanctity by a triple virtue ; that is, the dedication of the temple, the transfer of the body of the saint, and his ordination as bishop. This feast you shall observe four days before the Nones of July, and remember that his burial is the third day before the Ides of November." (GT 2209–2213). (c) Gregory calls him "our blessed patron Martin" (GT 1416) and "our light" (GT 1438). (d) Clovis, marching on the Arian Goths, "issued an edict out of respect to the blessed Martin that no one should take anything from that country except grass for fodder, and water" (GT 2608–2611); he asked for "some omen of victory" at Martin's church (GT 2620–2627); after victory "he returned to Tours, bringing many gifts to the holy church of the blessed Martin" (GT 2705–2707), and in that church "clad himself in the purple tunic and chlamys" as consul (GT 2709–2711). (e) Kings burned tax lists for Tours "fearing the power of Saint Martin" (GT 10076–10077, 10087–10091, 10112–10113). (f) Brehaut (MODERN): "This was the chief point of Christian pilgrimage in Gaul, a place of resort for the healing of the sick and the driving out of demons, and a sanctuary to which many fled for protection" (GT 128–131); the city "had become "the religious Metropolis " of Gaul" (GT 119–120). LATER; MODERN. Documented (Gregory's own report of his church, about 180–195 years on).

I2. **The Life carried the pattern of a holy man across the Roman world and became the model for later saints' lives.** (a) Sulpicius's own claim, in his Dialogue, through Postumianus: Paulinus "was the first to bring it to the city of Rome", where "nothing commanded a readier sale" (NP11 3638–3642); it "was already generally read through all Carthage" (NP11 3643–3644); at Alexandria "it is almost better known to all than it is to yourself. It has passed through Egypt, Nitria, the Thebaid, and the whole of the regions of Memphis. I found it being read by a certain old man in the desert" (NP11 3647–3650). CONTEMP, self-report by the author through a character: Inferential-Thin as to reach; Documented as Sulpicius's claim. (b) Ep. I: the Life "was being eagerly and carefully read by multitudes" (NP11 1997–1999). (c) Later writers carried it on: Fortunatus put Sulpicius's prose Life and Dialogues into verse, "in quattuor libellis totum illud opus versu" ("that whole work in verse in four little books") (VF 24732–24736). His verse retells the cloak: "Martinique chlamys texit velamine Christum." ("and Martin's cloak covered Christ with its veil") and "militis alba chlamys plus est quam purpura regis." ("a soldier's white cloak is more than a king's purple") (VF 25048, 25051). LATER. (d) Brehaut (MODERN): "His fame in later ages is largely due to the life written about 400 by his disciple Sulpicius Severus. This work had a large circulation in Gaul and became a model for saints' lives. It is full of miracles and Gregory's Miracles of St. Martin is merely a continuation of it." (GT 11933–11936). Gregory wrote "four books" of Martin's miracles (GT 400, 397–400). Farrar, quoted by Roberts (MODERN): his name "was known from Armenia to Egypt more widely than that of any other monk or bishop of his day" (NP11 490–493). Widely Accepted (that it was copied and continued); Dominant Modern Reconstruction (that it became *the* model).

I3. **He drew others into the monastic life, and his monks became bishops.** "There were altogether eighty disciples, who were being disciplined after the example of the saintly master." (NP11 1138–1139). "many among them were such as are deemed of noble rank ... and we have seen numbers of these afterwards made bishops. For what city or church would there be that would not desire to have its priests from among those in the monastery of Martin?" (NP11 1152–1158). At his funeral, monks "almost to the number of two thousand" (NP11 2493). Clarus "betook himself to Martin" and built "an abode for himself not far from the monastery of the bishop", with "many brethren" (NP11 1716–1723). Gibson (MODERN): the Loire monasteries of Martin and Hilary came before the Provençal ones (NP11 15443–15448). CONTEMP. Documented as Sulpicius's account.

I4. **DEFENDED: that bishops should not hand a church case to an emperor, nor seek blood for heresy.** M15 and M16. Sulpicius: expulsion from the churches was "quite sufficient punishment"; "a foul and unheard-of indignity, that a secular ruler should be judge in an ecclesiastical cause" (NP11 11655–11660); afterwards "never again did he attend a synod" (NP11 5217–5219). **Gregory used it, about 190 years on:** at a council in Paris, urging silent bishops to speak to King Chilperic, Gregory said: "What of the emperor Maximus? When he forced the blessed Martin to give communion to a certain bishop who was a homicide and Martin yielded to the wicked king in order the more easily to free the condemned from death, the judgment of the eternal King pursued him and Maximus was driven from the imperial throne and condemned to the worst death." (GT 5813–5818; Gregory as speaker, GT 5796–5800). CONTEMP; LATER. Documented as their accounts. Note: Gregory does not repeat the principle. In his telling Martin was "forced" and "yielded", and his point is the punishment of Maximus. The principle itself rests on Sulpicius alone.

I5. **DEFENDED (in Sulpicius's telling): mercy for those who fall and return.** A demon reproached him "why he had taken back, on their subsequent repentance, certain of the brethren who had ... lost their baptism by falling into various errors"; Martin answered "that by-past sins are cleansed away by the leading of a better life" (Life 22, NP11 1670–1677). Inside a WONDER frame (a demon's voice heard by "some of the brethren"). Contested in the record: Roberts's note quotes Hornius calling Martin's further words "Intolerabilis hic Martini error" (NP11 1692–1695). Card only.

## 4. Links to other people

L1. **Hilary of Poitiers** — sought him out, lived with him, was made exorcist by him (NP11 872–889); followed him home from exile (NP11 977–980). CONTEMP. Census also links them (CEN 1939, 36733).
L2. **Sulpicius Severus** — visited him, questioned him, wrote his Life (NP11 1830–1840); "our master, Martin" (NP11 2357–2358).
L3. **Paulinus (of Nola)** — Martin healed his eye, Sulpicius says (NP11 1528–1534); held him up as model (NP11 1852–1861); first brought the Life to Rome (NP11 3638–3639). Roberts names him bishop of Nola (NP11 455–456).
L4. **Julian Caesar** — the commander he asked for discharge (NP11 836–842).
L5. **Emperor Maximus** — at whose court he dined and pleaded (NP11 1555–1610; 11653–11663; 5076–5219).
L6. **Priscillian; Ithacius** — Martin pleaded that Priscillian's blood not be shed and urged Ithacius to drop the charge; Ithacius accused Martin himself of heresy (NP11 11649–11655).
L7. **Clarus** — disciple and presbyter (NP11 1716–1721; 2182–2183).
L8. **Gregory of Tours** — later bishop of his see; wrote of his death, tomb and miracles; kept his feast (GT 1470–1533; 2194–2215; 400).
L9. **Venantius Fortunatus** — wrote his life in verse, for Gregory (VF 24716–24736).
L10. **Clovis** — honoured his church and lands (GT 2604–2716).

## 5. Disagreements, later material, and doubts

D1. **His age and birth year do not fit together.** Gregory: born "In the eleventh year" of Constantine (GT 1414–1416), died "in the eighty-first year of his life" (GT 1476–1477). Sulpicius: oath at fifteen (NP11 726), baptised at twenty (NP11 807), "nearly two years" more service (NP11 815–816), discharge from Julian Caesar (NP11 836–842). Gregory's birth year would make him much older than about twenty-two when he left Julian's army (Julian's dates are modern chronology, not in these files). Contested. Give no birth year and no age at discharge in the story.

D2. **When the Life was written.** Ep. I shows it was "published" while Martin lived (NP11 1996–2003). Brehaut says "written about 400" (GT 11934). Follow Sulpicius: written in Martin's lifetime. Card only.

D3. **Roberts's English mistranslates the Trier sentence.** NP11 11660–11663 says "as long as Martin survived" and "when he was about to leave this world". The Latin (HALM 5907–5910) says "quoad usque Martinus Treueris fuit" (as long as Martin was at Trier) and "mox discessurus" (when he was soon to depart). Martin was alive when Priscillian died: Dialogue III has him back at Trier "after Priscillian had been put to death" (NP11 5078). Also Roberts's "foul" renders *saeuum* (cruel, savage); one edition reads *nouum* (new) (HALM 5924).

D4. **"Sixteen years."** Dialogue III says he "lived sixteen years after this" (NP11 5217; HALM 11929). This does not fit easily with Brehaut's death in 397 and the usual modern date of Priscillian's death (not in these files). Leave the number out.

D5. **The Trier accounts differ.** Chron. II.50 (M15) has Martin delaying the trial while he was at Trier and winning a promise, with no yielding. Dial. III (M16) has a later visit where he took communion once with Ithacius's party to save lives. Gregory (I4) says Maximus "forced the blessed Martin to give communion to a certain bishop who was a homicide" and that Martin "yielded ... in order the more easily to free the condemned from death". The Dialogue and Gregory agree he gave way to save the condemned; the Chronicle does not tell that part.

D6. **Where he died.** Sulpicius: "Condate" (NP11 2383); Roberts's note guesses "modern Cosne" (NP11 2384–2387). Gregory: "Candes, a village of his diocese" (GT 1471–1476). Say "a village of his diocese" or "Candes"; do not say Cosne.

D7. **Date of death and feast.** Brehaut: died "November 8, 397" (GT 595). Gregory: "his burial is the third day before the Ides of November" (GT 2213), that is 11 November. Do not say he died on 11 November.

D8. **The wonders.** Sulpicius insists: "I have written nothing of which I had not certain knowledge and evidence" (NP11 615–617). His translator Roberts: "it is extremely difficult to decide as to the real value of his narrative" (NP11 623–624), and quotes Farrar that half the deeds "are unworthy and absurd" (NP11 685–687). Card only. Every wonder stays in Sulpicius's (or the Gaul's, or Gregory's) voice.

D9. **The body carried off by night.** Gregory tells that the people of Tours passed the body "out the window" while the men of Poitiers slept, and took it by boat down the Vienne and Loire (GT 1485–1523). LATER (about 180–195 years). Sulpicius's funeral account (M20) says nothing of this. Leave out, or tell only as Gregory's story.

D10. **Census tile wording.** The census tile says "Sulpitius Severus was not present for the events he describes" (CEN 36859). That holds for most events but not all: he met Martin and questioned him (M17). The narrator should say "met him late in life" rather than "never saw him".

D11. **Raised the dead.** Sulpicius tells two (Life 7–8, NP11 977–1049); Gregory says "three dead men" (GT 1444), two at Poitiers and one at Tours (GT 1495–1496). Leave out.
