# Outline: Martin of Tours

All references are to `Source_Sheet.md` in this folder (S = setting, M = life events, I = impact, L = links, D = doubts). Each one carries the source file and its lines.

- **The heart of their life:** Martin was a Roman soldier who asked to leave the army, saying, as Sulpicius reports it, that he was Christ's soldier and might not fight (M6, Q1). He became a monk, set up a monastery near Poitiers, and was then made bishop of Tours. As bishop he never stopped living as a monk. He lived in a wooden cell outside the city, and eighty disciples gathered round him, owning everything in common (M9, M10, M11). Cities wanted their clergy from his monastery, and Sulpicius saw many of his monks made bishops (I3). That is the pattern the tradition is named for, the monks and monk-bishops of Gaul (S5). At the emperor's court at Trier he pleaded that men already judged heretics by bishops should not be killed, and that a church's case was not for a judge of this world (M15, Q2). His friend Sulpicius Severus wrote his life while he still lived. A modern historian, Brehaut, calls that book a model for later lives of saints (I2b, I2d). His tomb made Tours a place kings and bishops honoured (I1).
  - Confidence: the soldier's refusal, the monasteries, the monk-bishop, his monks made bishops, and Trier are Documented as Sulpicius's account, and rest on him alone. The Life read in his lifetime: Documented (Sulpicius's own report). The Life as "a model for saints' lives": Dominant Modern Reconstruction (Brehaut). The tomb's honour: Documented, through Gregory of Tours about two centuries later. The monastery names Ligugé and Marmoutier, and "among the first monks of Gaul": Dominant Modern Reconstruction (Gibson); keep them off the audio.

- **Length:** about 3 minutes (400–450 words at about 150 a minute). He is a major figure (census `major: true`; card "about 3 minutes"). The life has two turns, soldier to monk and monk to bishop; Trier needs its stand and its cost explained; the afterlife needs its third. Do not pad: if it holds in 400 words, stop there. **The first 150 words carry the whole heart** (beat 1).

- **Setting, in words to say aloud:** "in the fourth century" (S3), "in Roman Gaul, in what is now France" (S3, S4), "the monks and monk-bishops of Gaul" (S5). Born in Pannonia, "in what is now Hungary" (S2). Bishop at Tours, on the river Loire (S4). Let time and place arrive inside beat 1, not as a list.

- **Shape:** Monk or ascetic. He turned from the army, chose the monk's life and kept it as a bishop, and others followed him (M6, M11, I3). Trap to avoid: a pile of wonders. Keep to the cloak dream (M4) and no other, in Sulpicius's voice.

- **Openings** (chosen: 1):
  1. **His own reported words at Worms** (M6, Q1). The Caesar Julian is paying each soldier a gift of money before a battle. Martin steps up and refuses it: "Until now I have served you as a soldier. Let me now serve God as a soldier..." It leads straight into the heart: the soldier who would not fight, and what he became instead.
  2. Short alternative: "Even as a soldier, Sulpicius says, he was seen less as a soldier than as a monk" (M2, NP11 739–740), then straight to the discharge. Use only if the writer needs a bridge; it is Sulpicius's praise and must be credited.

- **Story beats** (beat 1 is the first minute; beats 2–7 deepen it):
  1. **The heart, told through the turn at Worms (first ~150 words).** At Worms, at the donative (a gift of money to each soldier), Martin asked for his discharge, because he thought it wrong to take the money if he meant to leave. He said Q1 (M6, S7). From the army he went to Hilary, bishop of Poitiers, and later set up a monastery near Poitiers (M7, M9). The people of Tours made him their bishop, and he went on living as a monk: plain clothes, a wooden cell two miles outside the city, eighty disciples who owned nothing of their own (M10, M11). Many of his monks were made bishops in their turn (I3). Name the time and place here: Roman Gaul, the fourth century, the monks and monk-bishops of Gaul (S3–S5). Name Trier in one clause: at an emperor's court he would plead that a church's case was not for a worldly judge (M15). Name the witness and the book: his friend Sulpicius wrote his life while he lived, and a modern historian calls it a model for later saints' lives (I2b, I2d). Documented as Sulpicius's account; "model" is Brehaut's. Use Q1's last two sentences here if the full quote would push the heart past the first minute.
  2. **Born to pagans, enlisted in chains (step back in time).** Born at Sabaria in Pannonia to pagan parents, raised at Ticinum (Pavia) in Italy. At ten, against his parents' wish, he asked to become a catechumen, someone under instruction for baptism. At fifteen his father, a soldier who rose to tribune, informed on him under a law that sons of veterans must serve. He was seized, chained and made to take the soldier's oath (S2, S6, M1). Documented, as Sulpicius tells it. Keep short.
  3. **The cloak, the dream, and two more years.** At the gate of Amiens, in a winter so cold it was killing people, he cut his cloak in two with his sword and gave half to a man with no clothes. Some onlookers laughed; others groaned because they had done nothing (M3). Sulpicius says that the next night Martin saw Christ in a dream, wearing the half he had given, and heard him say Martin, "still but a catechumen", had clothed him (M4: wonder, Sulpicius's voice). He was baptised at about twenty, but stayed nearly two more years in the army because his tribune promised to leave the world with him (M5). Documented, as Sulpicius tells it.
  4. **What the refusal cost.** Julian called it cowardice. Martin offered to stand unarmed before the battle line the next day, protected by the sign of the cross. He was put back in prison. The next day the enemy sent envoys and surrendered, so there was no battle. Sulpicius credits Martin for that peace (M6). Documented as Sulpicius's account; the peace as Martin's doing is Sulpicius's reading.
  5. **Monk, beaten and driven out, then bishop against his will.** He refused to be a deacon, saying he was unworthy, and accepted the humble office of exorcist so as not to seem to scorn it (M7). Arian opponents had him publicly beaten and driven from a city, and expelled him from a monastery he had set up at Milan (M8). After the monastery near Poitiers (M9), the people of Tours wanted him as bishop. A citizen lured him out by pretending his wife was ill, and crowds lining the road brought him to the city "under a kind of guard". Some bishops objected that he was mean in looks, clothes and hair (M10). Documented, as Sulpicius tells it.
  6. **The monk-bishop at work, with force.** At the monastery the young copied books and the old prayed (M11). In the villages he tore down pagan temples and built churches or monasteries where they had stood. Once, when he set about cutting down a pine held sacred, the local people rose against him. Once a crowd drove him off and he was hurt. Often, Sulpicius says, his preaching moved people to pull down their own temples (M13). Documented as Sulpicius's account.
  7. **Trier: a stand, and a compromise.** At the court of the emperor Maximus, bishops were prosecuting Priscillian and his followers for heresy. Martin did not defend their teaching. He kept rebuking the accuser Ithacius to drop the charge and begged Maximus not to shed their blood, saying Q2 (M15). While he was at Trier the trial waited. After he left, Priscillian and his companions were put to death (M15, D3). On a later visit, as Sulpicius's Dialogue tells it through a former disciple, the emperor was about to send officers into Spain to hunt heretics. Martin won the condemned men's lives by agreeing to share communion for one day with the bishops who had pressed for the deaths. He would not sign his name to it. He grieved over it, and never went to a gathering of bishops again (M16). Documented as Sulpicius's and the disciple's accounts.
  - Optional closing beat if room: **Death on a peace mission.** Old, and knowing his end was near, he went to Candes, a village of his diocese, to make peace among quarrelling clergy (M19). When his brothers begged him not to leave them, he is said to have answered with Q3. Documented as reported; Sulpicius was not there.

- **What came of them** (about a third; heaviest first; items 2 and 3 are already opened in beat 1 and are deepened here):
  1. **His tomb at Tours.** A later bishop, Perpetuus, built a great church over his grave because miracles were reported there. Gregory, bishop of Tours almost two hundred years later, calls him "our blessed patron", and tells the faithful to keep a July feast for his church, the moving of his body and his ordination, and to remember his burial on 11 November. The Frankish king Clovis ordered his army to take nothing but grass and water from Martin's lands, brought gifts to his church after his victory, and put on a consul's purple there (I1). Documented, through Gregory. "Chief point of pilgrimage in Gaul" is Brehaut's judgment; show it through Gregory's acts.
  2. **The book.** Sulpicius's Life was read eagerly while Martin lived (I2b). Sulpicius claims, through a friend in his own dialogue, that it sold fast in Rome and was read in Carthage, Alexandria and the Egyptian desert (I2a): say it is his claim. Nearly two centuries later the poet Fortunatus put it into verse: "a soldier's white cloak is more than a king's purple" (I2c). Brehaut calls it a model for later saints' lives, and Gregory's own book of Martin's miracles "merely a continuation of it" (I2d).
  3. **Others followed him.** Cities wanted their priests from his monastery; Sulpicius saw numbers of his monks made bishops. At his funeral, it was said, almost two thousand monks gathered (I3, M20).
  4. **DEFENDED, in Sulpicius's telling: no worldly judge in a church case, and no blood for heresy** (Q2, M15, M16). Gregory later used the Trier story for a different point. At a council in Paris, when bishops sat silent before an angry king, Gregory reminded them that Maximus had forced Martin to yield, and that the eternal King's judgment drove Maximus from his throne to the worst death (I4). Gregory says nothing of heresy or a secular judge. A possible ending.

- **Quotes** (2, with a third optional; all rendered from the Latin of Halm's edition, not from Roberts's English):
  - **Q1** (soldier to Caesar; Life 4). Latin, HALM 6334–6337 (OCR cleaned): "hactenus, inquit ad Caesarem, militaui tibi: patere ut nunc militem Deo: donatiuum tuum pugnaturus accipiat, Christi ego miles sum: pugnare mihi non licet." Roberts, NP11 839–842. **Rendering:** "Until now I have served you as a soldier. Let me now serve God as a soldier. Let the man who is going to fight take your gift. I am Christ's soldier. I am not allowed to fight." Job: the opening, and the turn of the life, in his reported words. The writer may use the last two sentences alone.
  - **Q2** (Trier; reported speech, Chronicle II.50). Latin, HALM 5903–5907 (OCR cleaned): "satis superque sufficere, ut episcopali sententia haeretici iudicati ecclesiis pellerentur: saeuum esse et inauditum nefas, ut causam ecclesiae iudex saeculi iudicaret." Roberts, NP11 11655–11660. **Rendering:** "It was more than enough that men judged heretics by the bishops should be driven from the churches. It was a cruel wrong, never heard of before, for a judge of this world to try a case of the church." Introduce it as what Martin said, in Sulpicius's report. The writer may use the second sentence alone. Job: what he defended.
  - **Q3** (optional; deathbed, Ep. III, reported). Latin, HALM 8232–8234 (OCR cleaned): "Domine, si adhuc populo tuo sum necessarius, non recuso laborem; fiat uoluntas tua." Roberts, NP11 2429–2431. **Rendering:** "Lord, if your people still need me, I do not refuse the work. Your will be done." Job: the bishop who stays at the task. Keep "is said to".

- **Connection to carry into the story:** the pattern of the monk who stays a monk as bishop passed from Martin to his own monks, and through them to the churches of Gaul. Sulpicius: "we have seen numbers of these afterwards made bishops. For what city or church would there be that would not desire to have its priests from among those in the monastery of Martin?" (I3, NP11 1155–1158). The listener needs to understand what was unusual, from what the record shows: some bishops opposed his election because his looks, clothes and hair were mean (M10), yet as bishop he kept the cell, the plain clothes and the common life of monks (M11), and cities then sought out men trained that way (I3). Do not add a general claim about how other bishops lived; the sheet does not hold one. The second carrier is the book: Sulpicius's Life spread the pattern in writing, was put into verse by Fortunatus, and continued by Gregory, bishop of Martin's own see (I2b–d). Tell both through acts (monks chosen as bishops; the book read, copied, versed), never as "this matters because".

  - Sulpicius Severus: visited him, questioned him, wrote his life (L2).
  - Paulinus (of Nola): Martin held him up as a model; he took the Life to Rome (L3).
  - Emperor Maximus: Martin pleaded at his court (L5).
  - Priscillian: Martin pleaded for his life (L6).

- **Who tells it:**
  - Sulpicius Severus, a friend and admirer, wrote the Life, letters, dialogues and a chronicle. He met Martin and questioned him late in Martin's life, and published the Life while Martin was alive. He was not present for most events, nor for the death. He is a devoted admirer and fills the Life with wonders (sheet, Witness kinds). In beat 1 he enters in one clause, as the friend who wrote the book; the fuller how-we-know line comes after the first minute: **"Almost all we know of him comes from one admirer, Sulpicius Severus, who met him late in life and wrote his story while he still lived."**
  - The Trier second visit comes from Sulpicius's Dialogues, spoken by one of Martin's former disciples (M16).
  - Gregory of Tours wrote about 180 to 195 years later: **"Gregory, bishop of Tours almost two hundred years later..."**
  - Venantius Fortunatus wrote his verse Life in Gregory's generation, retelling Sulpicius.
