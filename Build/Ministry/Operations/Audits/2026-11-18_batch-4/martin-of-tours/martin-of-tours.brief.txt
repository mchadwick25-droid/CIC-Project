Before a battle at Worms, the Caesar Julian was paying each of his soldiers a gift of money. A young soldier named Martin asked for his discharge instead, since he thought it wrong to take the money if he meant to leave. As his friend Sulpicius Severus reports it, he said: "I am Christ's soldier. I am not allowed to fight."

This was Roman Gaul, in what is now France, in the fourth century. From the army Martin went to Hilary, bishop of Poitiers, and later set up a monastery nearby. Years after that, the people of Tours made him their bishop, yet he lived on as a monk, in a wooden cell outside the city with eighty disciples who owned nothing of their own. Cities wanted their priests from his monastery, and Sulpicius saw many of his monks made bishops. At an emperor's court, Martin would plead that a church's case was not for a worldly judge. Sulpicius wrote his life while he still lived, a book that a modern historian calls a model for later lives of saints.

Almost all we know comes from that one admirer, who met him late in life. Martin was born to pagan parents in what is now Hungary, and at fifteen his own father informed on him under a law that veterans' sons must serve. He was seized, chained and sworn in. At the gate of Amiens, in a winter so cold it was killing people, he cut his cloak in two and gave half to a man with no clothes. That night, Sulpicius says, Martin saw Christ in a dream, wearing that half.

At Worms, Julian called his refusal cowardice. Martin offered to stand unarmed before the battle line, and was put back in prison. The next day the enemy surrendered before any battle, and Sulpicius credits Martin with that peace.

His Arian opponents later had him publicly beaten and driven from a city. When Tours chose him, some bishops objected that his looks, clothes and hair were too poor. As bishop he tore down pagan temples and built churches where they had stood. Sometimes his preaching moved people to pull down their own, but once a crowd drove him off and he was hurt.

At Trier, bishops were prosecuting Priscillian and his followers for heresy before the emperor Maximus. Martin did not defend their teaching, but he begged that their blood not be shed, saying, in Sulpicius's report: "It was a cruel wrong, never heard of before, for a judge of this world to try a case of the church." The trial waited while he was there, but after he left, Priscillian and his companions were put to death. On a later visit, a former disciple says, Martin saved condemned men's lives by sharing communion for one day with the bishops who had pressed for the deaths. He refused to sign his name, grieved over it, and never again went to a gathering of bishops.

At his funeral, it was said, almost two thousand monks gathered. A great church rose over his grave, where miracles were reported. Gregory, bishop of Tours almost two hundred years later, tells the faithful to remember his burial day, 11 November, and tells how King Clovis let his army take nothing but grass and water from Martin's lands. By then the poet Fortunatus had put Sulpicius's book into verse, and Gregory's own book of Martin's miracles carried it on.
