# Outline check: Clement of Alexandria (batch 4)

## The heart of his life (checker's own, written before reading Outline.md)

Clement was a Christian presbyter who taught in Alexandria around the year 200 (presbyter Documented, from Alexander's letter and Jerome; teaching at Alexandria Widely Accepted). He sought teachers across the Greek world and found the last in Egypt (his own words; that it was Pantaenus is Eusebius's word). His essential work is his answer to Christians who feared Greek learning "as children are at masks" and wanted "bare faith alone": philosophy was given to the Greeks, as the Law to the Hebrews, as a guide to bring them to Christ, and a Christian should keep "whatever has been well said" by any school, though not all of it (Documented, his own books, held only in Wilson's English). He set this out in the Exhortation, the Instructor and the Miscellanies. What came of it: Origen, on Eusebius's and Jerome's word, was his pupil and followed his example (Widely Accepted; Origen's own held words name Pantaenus); Eusebius kept his books and used him as a trustworthy witness to the apostles' time; the bishop Alexander, who knew him, trusted him with a letter from prison and honored him after his death; and Jerome, about 190 years later, named him "the most learned of men" when answering a critic who said quoting pagan books defiled the church (Documented). Later judgment was mixed: Photius found parts of his books unsound.

This agrees in substance with Mark's approved heart (`Hearts.md`, 2026-11-18).

## Findings

1. **MINOR — heart places the fearful Christians in Alexandria.** Heart: "Many Christians there feared Greek philosophy". Clement says "the multitude" (ANF2 47241) and names no city, the same reason the outline gives for its martyrs Do not say. Correction: change "Many Christians there feared" to "Many Christians feared".

2. **MINOR — heart says a little more than T3.** Heart: "and threw out what men had falsified". The source says such conclusions "I would never call divine" (ANF2 28153–28154). Beat 1 has it right. Correction: change to "and would never call divine what men had falsified (T3)".

3. **MINOR — impact ranking departs from the sheet without a stated reason.** The sheet ranks Origen, Eusebius, Alexander, Jerome (I1–I4); the outline puts Jerome second, Eusebius third and Alexander fourth. Either order can be argued, but the template asks that weight be what later witnesses carried on and used. Correction: after the "What came of them" heading's parenthesis, add: "Jerome is set above Eusebius because Jerome used Clement's own idea, the heart of this story; Eusebius used him chiefly as a witness to the apostles' time (I2, I4)." Or restore the sheet's order.

4. **MINOR — sheet and card, not the outline: a held Latin witness is not in the sweep.** Jerome's Latin *Chronicle* (`cic/texts/jerome_eusebii-chronici-canones-lat_fotheringham1923.txt`, lines 43374–43377 and 43462) names "Clemens Alexandrinae ecclesiae presbyter" (OCR "ecdesiae") beside Pantaenus as most eloquent in arguing "dogmatis nostri", and "Clemens multa et uaria conscribit".
