# Outline: Clement of Alexandria

Sheet = `Source_Sheet.md` in this folder. Codes (S3, M1, T1, B1 …) are its entries.

- **The heart of their life:** Around the year 200, Clement was a presbyter, an elder of the church, who taught in Alexandria (S2, S3, M4). Many Christians feared Greek philosophy, and some wanted "bare faith alone" (S5). In his books he answered them: God gave philosophy to the Greeks as he gave the Law to the Hebrews, as a guide to lead them to Christ (T1, Q2). So a Christian need not choose between learning and faith. He kept "whatever has been well said" by the schools, and would never call divine what men had falsified (T3). What it changed: Origen, by Eusebius's and Jerome's account his pupil, followed his example and wrote his own *Miscellanies*, setting Christian and philosophers' views side by side (M4, I1, B2). Two centuries on, Jerome answered a critic who said quoting pagan books defiled the church by pointing to Clement's books, "drawn from the very heart of philosophy" (I4, B1, Q3). Confidence: his office and city, Documented; that he taught, Widely Accepted; his answer, Documented in his own words (English only, N2); Origen his pupil, Widely Accepted (Origen's own held words do not name him, D4); Jerome's use of him, Documented.

- **Essence:** A teacher in Alexandria around 200, he answered Christians who feared Greek philosophy: God gave it to the Greeks as a guide that leads to Christ. Origen followed in his path.

- **Also told:** "whatever has been well said"; Jerome using his books to defend reading pagan writers.

- **Length:** about 2 minutes 20 seconds (about 350 words at 150 a minute). He is a major figure (census `major`), and the heart has two parts that must both land in the first 150 words: his answer, and the pupil who carried it on. The record of his life is thin: no held source gives his birth, conversion or death (N1). Up to about 3 minutes for a person (about 460 words), about 4 minutes 15 for a council (about 550); craft comes before time (Mark, 2026-11-18).

- **Setting, in words to say aloud:** "Around the year 200, in Alexandria, in Egypt" (S3: his own count reaches the death of the emperor Commodus, 194 years after Jesus' birth; Eusebius and Jerome place him under the emperor Severus). Tradition: "the church at Alexandria", "the Christian teachers of Alexandria", a city of libraries and lecture halls (S6). Do not say "Catechetical School" or "Christian-Platonist" aloud (S6b, D1).

- **Shape:** Teacher or thinker.

- **Openings:**
  1. **Chosen.** The question his own books raise. He wrote that most Christians were frightened of Greek philosophy, the way children are frightened of masks, afraid it would lead them astray (S5a). Some wanted "bare faith alone", which he likened to wanting grapes without tending the vine (S5b). Then straight to his answer (beat 1). This leads into the heart at once.
  2. Held in reserve, not as the opening: his search for a teacher and "I found rest" (M1, Q1). If a writer uses it first, it must hand over to his answer within about 40 words.

- **Story beats** (beats 1 and 2 are the first minute):
  1. **The fear, and his answer.** Around 200, in Alexandria, Clement was a presbyter, an elder who led the church beside the bishop, and he taught there (S2, M4). Many Christians feared Greek philosophy "as children are at masks"; some would not touch philosophy or even the study of nature, and wanted "bare faith alone" (S5a, S5b). This is his account of them; the record holds no word of their own (S5). His answer: philosophy was given to the Greeks as the Law was given to the Hebrews, a guide to bring them to Christ (T1, Q2). Paul's word in Galatians, which Clement echoes, meant the household servant who walked a child to his teacher (T1 note: ANF2 27893 note 1853, 18697–18699, 34509–34510). He took no one school whole. He kept "whatever has been well said" by each, and would never call divine what men had "cut away and falsified" (T3). Documented. Say "taught", not "headed the school" (D1: Contested).
  2. **Who carried it on.** Eusebius says Origen was among his pupils, and Jerome says so too (M4, I1). Jerome adds that Origen followed his example and wrote ten books of his own *Miscellanies*, comparing the views of Christians and of philosophers (I1, B2). Widely Accepted (pupil); Documented as Jerome's account (the book). Origen's own held letter names Pantaenus, not Clement, as his model for Greek learning (D4): card only, but say "Eusebius tells us" or "by Jerome's account".
  3. **Where he learned.** He went from teacher to teacher: in Greece, in the Greek south of Italy, and in the East (M1). The last he tracked down "concealed in Egypt", and there "I found rest" (M1, Q1). He calls that teacher "the Sicilian bee", gathering from the meadow of the prophets and apostles. Eusebius thought the teacher was Pantaenus; say it as Eusebius's word, after the heart (M2). Documented (his memory); Dominant Modern Reconstruction (the name).
  4. **His books.** He wrote down what he had heard as "a remedy against forgetfulness" in old age (M3). He called his longest work *Miscellanies*, notes "patched together", passing from one thing to another (T6, S1). His *Exhortation* called the Greeks to Christ, the "New Song", and his *Instructor* trained Christians in how to live (M5, M6, M6a). Documented.
  5. **Faith in ordinary work.** Optional, if room. For him the whole of life was a festival kept with God: "we cultivate our fields, praising; we sail the sea, hymning" (T4). Documented.
  6. **Persecution.** He wrote that martyrs, people who died rather than deny Christ, were "burnt, impaled, beheaded" before their eyes every day (S4). He taught that no one should hand himself over to the judge (M8). Documented (his words). Do not say Clement fled, or why, and do not set any report of teachers driven from Alexandria next to this teaching (D3: Inferential-Thin).
  7. **The last we hear.** Alexander, a bishop held in prison, sent a letter to the church at Antioch by Clement's hand. He called Clement "the blessed presbyter, a man virtuous and approved", who had "strengthened and built up the Church of the Lord" (M7). In a later letter, Alexander counts Clement among the blessed men who had gone on ahead of us, that is, who had died (M9). Do not name the place of Alexander's prison (N3). Documented. The quoted phrases in this beat are McGiffert's English, given as wording from the record, not as further quotes. If one is spoken as a quote, it needs a rendering from the Greek (EUSG 2401–2403, 2490–2494).

- **What came of them** (about a third; heaviest first; items 1 and 2 are pulled forward into beats 1–2, and are deepened here): Jerome is set above Eusebius because Jerome used Clement's own idea, the heart of this story; Eusebius used him chiefly as a witness to the apostles' time (I2, I4).
  1. **Origen.** His pupil, by Eusebius's and Jerome's account. Jerome says Origen copied his example and wrote his own ten books of *Miscellanies*, setting Christian and philosophers' views side by side and backing Christian teaching with quotations from Plato and Aristotle (I1, B2). Widely Accepted. Told in beat 2; it may return here in a line. Origen's own surviving words name Pantaenus, not Clement (D4): card only.
  2. **Jerome used him to defend Christian learning.** A critic, Magnus, asked why Jerome quoted pagan books and so defiled "the whiteness of the church with the foulness of heathenism" (B1). Jerome pointed to Clement, "in my judgment the most learned of men", whose books were "drawn from the very heart of philosophy" (I4, Q3). **Defended** (his method). Documented. Tell the charge with its force, not only the praise.
  3. **Eusebius kept him and trusted him.** The church historian listed his books and used him as a witness to the earliest church: how the Gospels came to be written (I2). He copied out Clement's whole story of the apostle John and a young robber "for the benefit of my readers". **Defended:** Eusebius named him among the trustworthy who "maintained the orthodoxy of the Church" (I2). Documented.
  4. **Alexander, a bishop who knew him, honoured him and remembered him.** He trusted Clement with his prison letter (M7). Later he named "the holy Clement" among the blessed men through whom he came to know Origen (M9, I3). Clement dedicated a book to him (I3). Documented (the honour). That Alexander was Clement's pupil is a modern editor's inference (McGiffert, EUS 34358–34359): Dominant Modern Reconstruction, card only, not story.
  Suggested end: Jerome's defence against the critic (B1, I4, Q3), or Alexander's "the blessed presbyter" (M7).

- **Quotes, 2 or 3 at most:**
  - **Q2** — the heart; his one idea, in the first minute. Source: Stromata I.5, ANF2 27890–27895 (rendered from Wilson's English; Clement's Greek is not held). Verbatim: "Perchance, too, philosophy was given to the Greeks directly and primarily, till the Lord should call the Greeks. For this was a schoolmaster to bring 'the Hellenic mind,' as the law, the Hebrews, 'to Christ.' Philosophy, therefore, was a preparation, paving the way for him who is perfected in Christ." Rendering: "Perhaps philosophy was also given to the Greeks directly, as God's own gift, until the Lord should call them. For it was a guide to bring the Greek mind to Christ, as the Law brought the Hebrews. So philosophy was a preparation. It cleared the road for the one who is made complete in Christ." ("Directly" is set against what God gives "by consequence", ANF2 27888–27889. "Schoolmaster" stands for Paul's word in Galatians 3:24, which Clement echoes: the servant who led a child to the teacher. "Guide" avoids the modern sense of a teacher who runs a school. The writer may give the servant image in its own words, from beat 1.)
  - **Q3** — what came of him; a later reader's defence. Source: Jerome, Letter 70.4 to Magnus, JER 16626–16636 (Fremantle's English; Jerome's Latin of this letter is not held, so this is rendered from the printed English). Verbatim: "Clement, a presbyter of Alexandria, in my judgment the most learned of men, wrote eight books of Miscellanies and as many of Outline Sketches, a treatise against the Gentiles, and three volumes called the Pedagogue. Is there any want of learning in these, or are they not rather drawn from the very heart of philosophy?" Rendering: "Clement was a presbyter of Alexandria, and in my judgment the most learned of men. He wrote eight books of Miscellanies, and as many of Outline Sketches. He wrote a book against the pagans, and three books called the Instructor. Is learning missing from these books? Or are they not rather drawn from the very heart of philosophy?" ("Gentiles" is given as "pagans", because in modern English "Gentiles" means non-Jews. The "Pedagogue" is the book this outline calls the *Instructor*.) Job: Jerome's answer to a critic who asked why he quoted pagan books (JER 16477–16480, B1). It shows a later writer using Clement's example to defend the very idea Clement argued for.
  - **Q1** — optional, beat 3; his own voice. Source: Stromata I.1, ANF2 27489–27495 (Wilson's English; Clement's Greek is not held, so this is rendered from the printed English). Verbatim: "When I came upon the last (he was the first in power), having tracked him out concealed in Egypt, I found rest. He, the true, the Sicilian bee, gathering the spoil of the flowers of the prophetic and apostolic meadow, engendered in the souls of his hearers a deathless element of knowledge." Rendering: "Then I came upon the last of them, and he was the greatest in power. I tracked him down where he was hidden in Egypt, and I found rest. He was the true bee, the bee of Sicily. He gathered what the flowers held in the meadow of the prophets and apostles. In the souls of those who heard him, he brought into being a knowledge that does not die." Its middle sentence may stand alone.

- **Connection to carry into the story:** Clement passed on a way of using Greek learning, not a school or an office. Origen, his pupil by Eusebius's and Jerome's account, took up his example: he wrote his own *Miscellanies* and set Christian and philosophers' views side by side (I1, B2). Two centuries later Jerome, charged with defiling the church by quoting pagan books, used Clement's books as his precedent (B1, I4). The listener needs to understand that Clement's idea was disputed in his own day (some Christians feared philosophy, S5) and still disputed in Jerome's (Magnus's charge, B1), and that later teachers used his example as their answer. Tell it through what Origen and Jerome did, not through a "this matters because" sentence.

- **Who tells it:**
  - Clement's own books (*Exhortation*, *Instructor*, *Miscellanies*, *Who is the Rich Man*): his own time, in English translation only.
  - Alexander's two letters: his own time, by a bishop who knew him, kept only because Eusebius copied them.
  - Eusebius, *Church History*: about a century later. Line to say aloud: "Most of what we know of his life comes from Eusebius, a church historian writing about a hundred years later."
  - Jerome, *Lives* and Letter 70: about two centuries later. Line: "Jerome, writing two centuries on, called him the most learned of men."
  - How-we-know gets a sentence or two, after the heart. The doubt is not the story.

- **Do not say:**
  - Not 'as a young man' or any age for his search. No held source gives it (M1; ANF2 27477–27495). Say 'he went from teacher to teacher' (review, 2026-11-18).
  - Not "a long search": he never says how long it was. Say "his search for a teacher" (round-two review, 2026-11-17).
  - Not a search scene that runs on before his answer. If the search opens, it is the one line "I found rest", and it hands over to the idea within about 40 words (Heart_Test, 2026-11-18).
  - Not "Eusebius names that teacher as Pantaenus" before the heart. Put it after the heart, or leave it to the card (Heart_Test, 2026-11-18).
  - Not the "patched-together notes" before the idea. They come after it (Heart_Test, 2026-11-18).
  - Not Origen in a passing clause near the end. Origen comes in the first minute, as Eusebius's and Jerome's account (Heart_Test, 2026-11-18).
  - Not "Origen said he learned from Clement". Origen's own held letter names Pantaenus (D4). Say "Eusebius tells us Origen was his pupil".
  - Not "Origen copied Clement's book". Say "Origen followed his example and wrote his own *Miscellanies*" (B2).
  - Not "head of the school" or "he founded the school". Say "he taught in Alexandria" (D1).
  - Not "he set the course of Alexandrian Christianity" or "he shaped the whole tradition". Say what is held: Origen followed his example, and Jerome used it to defend Christian learning (I1, I4).
  - Not "the first Christian to welcome philosophy". The held record does not make him first. Say "he argued".
  - Not "philosophy was God's gift" with the "perhaps" dropped, and not "philosophy saves" or "as good as Scripture". His own words: "Perchance", and God gives the Testaments "primarily" but philosophy "by consequence" (T1, ANF2 27888–27890). Say "Perhaps, he suggested ...".
  - Not "he embraced Greek philosophy". He kept "whatever has been well said" and rejected what men had falsified (T3).
  - Not "Jerome praised him" alone. Jerome was answering a charge that quoting pagan books defiled the church (B1). Keep the charge.
  - Not "in Alexandria he watched martyrs burn". He says "before our eyes every day" without naming the city (S4).
  - Not that the Christians who feared philosophy were foolish or ignorant. This is his account of them, and they have no voice in the record (S5).

- **Leave out:**
  - "Head of the Catechetical School" as fact, and the tidy line of school heads (D1).
  - Why or when he left Alexandria; McGiffert's date of 202 (D3).
  - Birth in Athens, conversion as an adult, death in Palestine: editors' notes, not held witnesses (N1).
  - The names guessed for his six teachers (D2).
  - The details of the John and robber story, and the traditions from his lost *Outlines*: they are other people's stories (I2).
  - Rufinus's "presbyter of Antioch" (D5), and Photius's attack on the *Outlines* about 650 years later (I6): card only. The dispute over his ideas is carried in the story by the fearful Christians (S5) and Magnus's charge (B1).
  - Clement of Rome: same name, different man (S1). Only if a listener might confuse them.
