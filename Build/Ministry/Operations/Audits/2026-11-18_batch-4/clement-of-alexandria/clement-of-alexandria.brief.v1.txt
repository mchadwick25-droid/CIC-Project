Many Christians, Clement wrote, were frightened of Greek philosophy, the way children are frightened of masks. Some wanted "bare faith alone," which to him was like wanting grapes without tending the vine. This is his account; their own words have not come down to us.

Around the year 200, in Alexandria, in Egypt, Clement was a presbyter, an elder of the church, and he taught there. Perhaps, he suggested, God gave philosophy to the Greeks as he gave the Law to the Hebrews. In his words, "it was a guide to bring the Greek mind to Christ, as the Law brought the Hebrews." Paul's word for that guide meant the household servant who walked a child to his teacher. He kept "whatever has been well said" by each school, but would never call divine what men had falsified.

Eusebius tells us that Origen was among his pupils, and Jerome says so too. By Jerome's account, Origen followed Clement's example and wrote his own Miscellanies, setting the views of Christians and of philosophers side by side.

Most of what we know of Clement's life comes from Eusebius, a church historian writing about a hundred years later. By his own account, Clement went as a young man from teacher to teacher, in Greece, in southern Italy and in the East. Of the last, he wrote, "I tracked him down where he was hidden in Egypt, and I found rest." Eusebius thought this teacher was Pantaenus.

Clement wrote that martyrs were burned, impaled and beheaded in plain sight every day, yet he taught that no one should hand himself over to the judge.

A bishop named Alexander, held in prison, sent a letter to the church at Antioch by Clement's hand, praising him as a man who had built up the church.

Two centuries on, a critic charged Jerome with defiling the church by quoting pagan books. Jerome pointed to Clement, "in my judgment the most learned of men," and named his books. Then he asked, "Is learning missing from these books? Or are they not rather drawn from the very heart of philosophy?"
