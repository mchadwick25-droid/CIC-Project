# Source sheet: Clement of Alexandria

Researcher's sheet for the 2026-11-17 varied batch. The writer uses the outline and the standard only; this sheet is the evidence behind the outline.

**Files and short names used below** (all in `cic/texts/` unless marked):
- **ANF2** = `anf02_hermas-tatian-athenagoras-theophilus-clement-alexandria.xml`. Clement's own *Exhortation to the Heathen*, *The Instructor*, *The Stromata* (Miscellanies) and *Who is the Rich Man that Shall Be Saved?*, in the English of ANF's translator William Wilson (ANF2 14834, 54282, 56133).
- **EUS** = `npnf201_eusebius-church-history-life-of-constantine.xml`. Eusebius, *Church History*, tr. McGiffert.
- **EUSG** = `eusebius_werke-bd2-2-historia-ecclesiastica-vi-x-rufinus-x-xi-grc-lat_schwartz-mommsen1908.txt`. The Greek of Eusebius Books VI–X (Schwartz), with Rufinus's Latin version printed on facing pages. OCR text.
- **NP3** = `npnf203_theodoret-jerome-gennadius-rufinus.xml`. Jerome, *Lives of Illustrious Men*.
- **JER** = `npnf206_jerome-principal-works.xml`. Jerome, *Letters* (Letter 70, to Magnus).
- **PHO** = `photius_library-v1_freese1920.txt`. Photius, *Library* (Bibliotheca), tr. Freese. OCR text.
- **CEN** = `cic-website/data/world-census.json` (not in `cic/texts/`; used only for the setting and the spoken name).

**Conventions.** Line numbers are the file's own lines. English quotes are printed exactly as in the file; editors' note markers and notes are left out of quotes, and where a note matters it is given after the quote. In the OCR files (EUSG, PHO) I joined words split across lines and left out stray accent marks, line numbers and running heads. In EUSG Greek, routine OCR letter forms (χ for κ, ϑ for θ, "»" quotation marks, full stops for commas) are normalized without comment; real misreadings are listed after each quote as "OCR x = y". English OCR faults in PHO are left in place and noted the same way. ANF and EUS chapter headings (such as "Philosophy the Handmaid of Theology") are the editors', not the authors'.

**Greek.** The Library holds **no Greek text of Clement's own works**. Every rendering of Clement's words is made from Wilson's printed English, and says so. The Library does hold the Greek of Eusebius Book VI (EUSG), which contains the two letters of Alexander that name Clement; those renderings are made from the Greek. Eusebius Book V (Clement's first chapter, V.11) is held only in English.

**Time gaps.** The "about N years later" figures are my rough estimates for the card, from the usual modern dates of the writers and Clement's own setting around 200 (S3). The files do not all state them. Jerome's Letter 70 is dated 397 by its translator's summary (JER 16457–16465). They are not story material.

**Witness kinds used.** OWN = Clement's own books. CONTEMP = Alexander, bishop, who knew Clement and wrote while Clement lived or soon after (his letters survive only as quoted by Eusebius). LATER = Eusebius (about a century later), Jerome (about 190 years later), Rufinus (about 200 years later), Photius (about 650 years later). LATE-LEGEND: none found in the held record.

## 1. Setting

S1. **His name.** Eusebius gives the title Clement put on his Miscellanies: "Titus Flavius Clement's Stromata of Gnostic Notes on the True Philosophy" (VI.13.1, EUS 33868–33871). Greek: "Τίτου Φλαυίου Κλήμεντος τῶν κατὰ τὴν ἀληθῆ φιλοσοφίαν γνωστικῶν ὑπομνημάτων στρωματεῖς" (EUSG 2632–2635). Photius found the same title "in an old copy" (PHO 11152–11155). LATER, quoting the book's own title. Documented. Eusebius notes "He had the same name as the one who anciently was at the head of the Roman church" (V.11.1, EUS 28693–28694), that is, Clement of Rome; the two are different men.

S2. **His city and church.** Eusebius: "At this time Clement, being trained with him in the divine Scriptures at Alexandria, became well known" (V.11.1, EUS 28655–28693; "him" is Pantaenus). Jerome: "Clemens, presbyter of the Alexandrian church" (Lives 38, NP3 40249–40250), and in Letter 70 "Clement, a presbyter of Alexandria" (JER 16626–16628). Alexander calls him "Clement, the blessed presbyter" (S6, M7). LATER for Alexandria; CONTEMP for "presbyter". Documented. A **presbyter** is an elder: a church leader ranked beside and under the bishop. Clement's own words place his last teacher "concealed in Egypt" (ANF2 27492; M1). OWN.

S3. **Century.** (a) Clement's own count: "From the birth of Christ, therefore, to the death of Commodus are, in all, a hundred and ninety-four years, one month, thirteen days" (Stromata I.21, ANF2 30646–30648). OWN. Documented as his count. (b) Eusebius: the Stromata's table runs "down to the death of Commodus. So it is evident that that work was written during the reign of Severus" (VI.6, EUS 33080–33086). LATER. (c) Jerome: "He flourished moreover during the reigns of Severus and his son Antoninus" (NP3 40278–40279). LATER. (d) The census: "c. 190-212", "activeYear": 202 (CEN 87232, 87234). Supported spoken forms: **"around the year 200"**; **"about two hundred years after Jesus"** is supported only as Clement's own count (he reckons 194 years to Commodus's death, and he wrote after it). No held source gives his birth or death year (N1).

S4. **The danger Christians faced.** Clement: "we have exhibited before our eyes every day abundant sources of martyrs that are burnt, impaled, beheaded" (Stromata II.20, ANF2 34507–34509). OWN. Documented. Eusebius: "When Severus began to persecute the churches, glorious testimonies were given everywhere by the athletes of religion. This was especially the case in Alexandria" (VI.1, EUS 32397–32429). LATER. A **martyr** is one who died rather than deny the faith (the word means "witness").

S5. **What he was answering.** (a) Some Christians feared Greek learning: "the multitude are frightened at the Hellenic philosophy, as children are at masks, being afraid lest it lead them astray" (Stromata VI.10, ANF2 47241–47243). (b) "Some, who think themselves naturally gifted, do not wish to touch either philosophy or logic; nay more, they do not wish to learn natural science. They demand bare faith alone, as if they wished, without bestowing any care on the vine, straightway to gather clusters from the first" (Stromata I.9, ANF2 28313–28317). OWN. Documented as his account of them; the record holds no word from those he answers. Note: ANF2's note at 47243 says the translator left out two Greek words in the sentence that follows; the quoted words are not affected.

S6. **Tradition.** The census name is **Alexandrian Catechetical / Christian-Platonist Tradition**, informally "Alexandrian Christianity" (CEN 32454–32456; worldName CEN 32500). Clement himself is listed as "Named within another tradition's story", told within this one (CEN 87230, 87266). (a) Plain spoken forms a narrator may say: (1) "the church at Alexandria, in Egypt". Support: Jerome's "presbyter of the Alexandrian church" (NP3 40249–40250); Clement's own "in Egypt" (ANF2 27492); census region "Alexandria, Egypt" (CEN 32460). (2) "the Christians of Alexandria, a city of teachers". Support: the census describes Alexandria as "the ancient world's great city of libraries and lecture halls" where Christianity "took the same shape: a school" (CEN 32632), and its intro calls it "the Christian tradition of Alexandria" (CEN 32623). (3) "the Christian teachers of Alexandria". Support: census label "Alexandrian teaching tradition", noted as "a remembered line of teachers, not a proven institution" (CEN 32612–32614). (b) Do not say "Christian-Platonist" or "Catechetical" aloud. Do not present "the Catechetical School" as a settled institution: the census calls that "Eusebius's tidy picture of a formal school; historians dispute that it existed" (CEN 32602–32604), and says "Clement's own writings never once describe a formal school that he headed" (CEN 32632). See D1.

## 2. Life events

M1. **He sought out teachers across the Greek world, and found the last one in Egypt.** "Of these the one, in Greece, an Ionic; the other in Magna Græcia: the first of these from Cœle-Syria, the second from Egypt, and others in the East. The one was born in the land of Assyria, and the other a Hebrew in Palestine." / "When I came upon the last (he was the first in power), having tracked him out concealed in Egypt, I found rest. He, the true, the Sicilian bee, gathering the spoil of the flowers of the prophetic and apostolic meadow, engendered in the souls of his hearers a deathless element of knowledge." (Stromata I.1, ANF2 27483–27495). OWN. Documented. Magna Graecia is the Greek south of Italy (EUS note, 28737–28739). Clement names none of these teachers here; the ANF notes' guesses (Tatian, Theodotus) are card only (D2).

M2. **That teacher was Pantaenus — on Eusebius's word.** "In his Hypotyposes he speaks of Pantænus by name as his teacher. It seems to me that he alludes to the same person also in the first book of his Stromata" (V.11.2, EUS 28697–28703). Repeated at VI.13.2: "In them he mentions Pantænus by name as his teacher" (EUS 33981–33983). The Hypotyposes passage itself is not preserved (EUS note, 28698–28701). LATER. That Pantaenus taught Clement: Widely Accepted (Eusebius, Jerome NP3 40250, Photius PHO 11110–11111, and Alexander names both together, M9). That the "Sicilian bee" is Pantaenus: Dominant Modern Reconstruction (Eusebius's "It seems to me").

M3. **What he kept, and why he wrote it down.** "this work of mine in writing is not artfully constructed for display; but my memoranda are stored up against old age, as a remedy against forgetfulness, truly an image and outline of those vigorous and animated discourses which I was privileged to hear, and of blessed and truly remarkable men." (Stromata I.1, ANF2 27477–27481). OWN. Documented. Eusebius quotes the same (EUS 28719–28723).

M4. **He taught at Alexandria, and Origen was his pupil.** "Clement having succeeded Pantænus, had charge at that time of the catechetical instruction in Alexandria, so that Origen also, while still a boy, was one of his pupils." (VI.6, EUS 33015–33080). Greek: "Πανταῖνον δὲ Κλήμης διαδεξάμενος, τῆς κατ᾽ Ἀλεξάνδρειαν κατηχήσεως εἰς ἐκεῖνο τοῦ καιροῦ καθηγεῖτο, ὡς καὶ τὸν Ὠριγένην τῶν φοιτητῶν γενέσθαι αὐτοῦ" (EUSG 1839–1843; OCR "Hevrewor" = Πανταῖνον). Jerome: he "led the theological school at Alexandria after the death of his master and was teacher of the Catechetes" and "Origen is known to have been his disciple" (NP3 40250–40252, 40277–40278). **Catechetical instruction** means teaching people preparing for baptism (catechumens). LATER. That he taught at Alexandria: Widely Accepted. That Origen was his pupil: Widely Accepted (two later witnesses; Origen's own held words do not name him, D4). That he "succeeded" Pantaenus as head of a formal school: Contested (D1). The words "while still a boy" are missing from two manuscripts and Rufinus (EUS note, 33046–33055), card only.

M5. **His books.** Eusebius lists the eight Stromata, eight Hypotyposes (Outlines), the "Hortatory Discourse addressed to the Greeks", "three books of a work entitled the Instructor", "What Rich Man is Saved?", "the Passover", "Fasting and on Evil Speaking", "On Patience, or To Those Recently Baptized", and the "Ecclesiastical Canon, or Against the Judaizers, which he dedicated to Alexander, the bishop" (VI.13.1–3, EUS 33868–34112). LATER. Documented. Held in ANF2: the Exhortation (from 15093), the Instructor (from 18648), the Stromata I–VIII (from 27253), Who is the Rich Man (from 56141). Stromata III is printed in Latin in ANF2, not English (ANF2 35140 note; census "sourcing", CEN 32493): card only.

M6. **Three works, three jobs (his own plan).** The Instructor opens: "habits are the department appropriated by hortatory discourse ... All actions, again, are the province of preceptive discourse; while persuasive discourse applies itself to heal the passions. It is, however, one and the self-same word which rescues man from the custom of this world" (Instructor I.1, ANF2 18664–18676). "Let us then designate this Word appropriately by the one name Tutor (or Pædagogue, or Instructor)." / "The Instructor being practical, not theoretical, His aim is thus to improve the soul, not to teach, and to train it up to a virtuous, not to an intellectual life." (ANF2 18687–18693). OWN. Documented. Note: "the Word" is Christ (Greek *Logos*). ANF's note says "pædagogus" means "one who leads the child to his instructor" (ANF2 18697–18699).

M6a. **The Exhortation: the New Song.** It opens with Greek tales of singers who charmed fish, beasts and stones (ANF2 15100–15105), then: "Behold the might of the new song! It has made men out of stones, men out of beasts." (ANF2 15213–15214), and names the song: "he has been called by me the New Song. This Word, then, the Christ" (ANF2 15279–15280). OWN. Documented.

M7. **Prison letter to Antioch, carried by Clement.** Eusebius: on Serapion's death "Asclepiades, who had been himself distinguished among the confessors during the persecution, succeeded to the episcopate of the church at Antioch" (VI.11.4, EUS 33558–33596). Alexander wrote: "Alexander, a servant and prisoner of Jesus Christ, to the blessed church of Antioch, greeting in the Lord. The Lord hath made my bonds during the time of my imprisonment light and easy, since I learned that ... Asclepiades ... has undertaken the bishopric of your holy church at Antioch." (EUS 33600–33606). At the end: "My honored brethren, I have sent this letter to you by Clement, the blessed presbyter, a man virtuous and approved, whom ye yourselves also know and will recognize. Being here, in the providence and oversight of the Master, he has strengthened and built up the Church of the Lord." (VI.11.6, EUS 33612–33617). Greek: "ταῦτα δὲ ὑμῖν, κύριοί μου ἀδελφοί, τὰ γράμματα ἀπέστειλα διὰ Κλήμεντος τοῦ μακαρίου πρεσβυτέρου, ἀνδρὸς ἐναρέτου καὶ δοκίμου, ὃν ἴστε καὶ ὑμεῖς καὶ ἐπιγνώσεσθε· ὃς καὶ ἐνθάδε παρὼν κατὰ τὴν πρόνοιαν καὶ ἐπισκοπὴν τοῦ δεσπότου, ἐπεστήριξέν τε καὶ ηὔξησεν τὴν τοῦ κυρίου ἐκκλησίαν" (EUSG 2401–2403, 2490–2494; OCR "&vógoc ... xci" = ἀνδρὸς ... καὶ; "nvinotr" = ηὔξησεν). Jerome gives the same (NP3 40268–40277). CONTEMP (Alexander), quoted LATER. Documented. The letter does not name the place "here" where Alexander was held (N3). A **confessor** is one who suffered for the faith and lived.

M8. **His teaching on persecution.** "When, again, He says, 'When they persecute you in this city, flee ye to the other,' He does not advise flight, as if persecution were an evil thing; nor does He enjoin them by flight to avoid death, as if in dread of it, but wishes us neither to be the authors nor abettors of any evil to any one" (Stromata IV.10, ANF2 38875–38879). "he also who presents himself before the judgment-seat becomes guilty of his death" (ANF2 38882–38883). OWN. Documented as his teaching. Whether he himself left Alexandria for this reason: Inferential-Thin (D3).

M9. **Remembered as dead, and honoured, by Alexander.** In a later letter to Origen, Alexander writes: "For we know well those blessed fathers who have trodden the way before us, with whom we shall soon be; Pantænus, the truly blessed man and master, and the holy Clement, my master and benefactor, and if there is any other like them, through whom I became acquainted with thee, the best in everything, my master and brother." (VI.14.8–9, EUS 34330–34356). Greek: "πατέρας γὰρ ἴσμεν τοὺς μακαρίους ἐκείνους τοὺς προοδεύσαντας, πρὸς οὓς μετ᾽ ὀλίγον ἐσόμεθα, Πάνταινον, τὸν μακάριον ὡς ἀληθῶς καὶ κύριον, καὶ τὸν ἱερὸν Κλήμεντα, κύριόν μου γενόμενον καὶ ὠφελήσαντά με, καὶ εἴ τις ἕτερος τοιοῦτος· δι᾽ ὧν σὲ ἐγνώρισα, τὸν κατὰ πάντα ἄριστον καὶ κύριόν μου καὶ ἀδελφόν" (EUSG 3048–3052; OCR "ὦ ιληϑῶς" = ὡς ἀληθῶς; "ὠφελήσαν τά" = ὠφελήσαντά). CONTEMP, quoted LATER. Documented that Alexander counted Clement among those who had gone on ahead, that is, died. **κύριος** ("master" in McGiffert) is "lord", here a courtesy title: Alexander gives the same title to Origen, "κύριόν μου καὶ ἀδελφόν" (EUSG 3052), and Jerome's translator glosses the matching word in the Antioch letter as "literally 'lordly' perhaps like the conventional formula 'Lords and brethren.'" (NP3 note, 40271–40272). "Through whom" (δι᾽ ὧν) covers all the men named, not Clement alone. That Alexander was Clement's pupil is McGiffert's inference: "The epistle does not state this directly, but the conclusion seems sufficiently obvious" (EUS 34358–34359). Dominant Modern Reconstruction. When and where he died: not held (N1).

## 3. His teaching, in his own words (for the "one idea")

T1. **Philosophy as a guide to Christ.** "Accordingly, before the advent of the Lord, philosophy was necessary to the Greeks for righteousness. And now it becomes conducive to piety; being a kind of preparatory training to those who attain to faith through demonstration." (ANF2 27881–27885). "Perchance, too, philosophy was given to the Greeks directly and primarily, till the Lord should call the Greeks. For this was a schoolmaster to bring 'the Hellenic mind,' as the law, the Hebrews, 'to Christ.' Philosophy, therefore, was a preparation, paving the way for him who is perfected in Christ." (Stromata I.5, ANF2 27890–27895). OWN. Documented. The phrase echoes Paul, Galatians 3:24 (ANF2 note n.1853). "Schoolmaster" stands for Paul's word in Galatians 3:24, which Clement echoes; Clement's own Greek is not held. Support for its sense: ANF2 note on *pædagogus*, "one who leads the child to his instructor" (ANF2 18697–18699), and Wilson's "leading as a pædagogue to Christ" for the same idea (ANF2 34509–34510). "Directly and primarily" is set against God giving philosophy "by consequence" (ANF2 27888–27889). The ANF editor flags the sentence just before it, on those "justified by philosophy" (end of I.4), as "much reflected upon, in questions of Clement's Catholic orthodoxy" (ANF2 27871–27874): card only.

T2. **One road, many streams.** "The way of truth is therefore one. But into it, as into a perennial river, streams flow from all sides." (Stromata I.5, ANF2 27903–27904). OWN. Documented.

T3. **Not one school, but what was well said.** "And philosophy—I do not mean the Stoic, or the Platonic, or the Epicurean, or the Aristotelian, but whatever has been well said by each of those sects, which teach righteousness along with a science pervaded by piety,—this eclectic whole I call philosophy." and "such conclusions of human reasonings, as men have cut away and falsified, I would never call divine." (Stromata I.7, ANF2 28146–28154). OWN. Documented. He did not accept philosophy whole.

T4. **Faith in daily work.** "Holding festival, then, in our whole life, persuaded that God is altogether on every side present, we cultivate our fields, praising; we sail the sea, hymning; in all the rest of our conversation we conduct ourselves according to rule." (Stromata VII.7, ANF2 50725–50728). OWN. Documented. "Conversation" here is the old sense, "way of life".

T5. **Wealth is a tool.** "Riches, then, which benefit also our neighbours, are not to be thrown away." ... "Such an instrument is wealth. Are you able to make a right use of it? It is subservient to righteousness." (Who is the Rich Man 14, ANF2 56499–56507). "the Saviour by no means has excluded the rich on account of wealth itself" (ANF2 56838–56839). OWN. Documented.

T6. **Why "Miscellanies".** "Let these notes of ours ... be of varied character—and as the name itself indicates, patched together—passing constantly from one thing to another" (Stromata IV.2, ANF2 37560–37564). OWN. Documented.

## 4. What came of him (ranked heaviest first)

I1. **Origen, his pupil.** Eusebius (EUS 33043–33080) and Jerome (NP3 40277–40278) make Origen his pupil. Jerome: "Imitating his example Origen wrote ten books of Miscellanies, in which he compares together the opinions held respectively by Christians and by philosophers" (Letter 70, JER 16636–16641). Jerome also: Origen "presided over the school of the church, succeeding a man of great learning the presbyter Clement" (JER 19286–19288). LATER (about 190 years). Widely Accepted. See D4: Origen's own held letter names Pantaenus as his model, not Clement.

I2. **Eusebius kept his books and used him as a witness to the apostles' time — and defended him.** Eusebius lists his works (VI.13, EUS 33868–34266) and draws on him for the order of the Gospels and how Mark came to write: "The Gospels containing the genealogies, he says, were written first" ... "last of all, John ... composed a spiritual Gospel." This is the account of Clement." (VI.14.5–7, EUS 34306–34328); for James chosen bishop of Jerusalem (II.1.3, EUS 9975–9981). He copies in full Clement's tale of John and the young robber from *Who is the Rich Man* (III.23.5–19, EUS 17100–17372; Clement's own text ANF2 57246–57249 onward) and says "This extract from Clement I have inserted here for the sake of the history and for the benefit of my readers" (EUS 17379–17381). **DEFENDED:** Eusebius names him a trustworthy witness: "They should be trustworthy who have maintained the orthodoxy of the Church; and such indeed were Irenæus and Clement of Alexandria." (III.23.2, EUS 17044–17049). LATER (about a century). Documented.

I3. **Alexander, a bishop who knew him, trusted him, honoured him and remembered him.** Carrier of Alexander's prison letter to Antioch, with praise that he "strengthened and built up the Church of the Lord" (M7). Later "the holy Clement, my master and benefactor" (McGiffert; Greek κύριος, a courtesy title), named among the men through whom Alexander came to know Origen (M9). The pupil link is a modern inference (M9). Clement dedicated his *Ecclesiastical Canon* to Alexander (EUS 34056–34112). CONTEMP. Documented.

I4. **Jerome used his example to defend Christian use of pagan learning.** Magnus asked Jerome "why it is that sometimes in my writings I quote examples from secular literature" (Letter 70.2, JER 16477–16479). Jerome answers with a list of Christian writers, including: "Clement, a presbyter of Alexandria, in my judgment the most learned of men, wrote eight books of Miscellanies and as many of Outline Sketches, a treatise against the Gentiles, and three volumes called the Pedagogue. Is there any want of learning in these, or are they not rather drawn from the very heart of philosophy?" (JER 16626–16636). **DEFENDED** (his method, as Jerome's precedent). Also *Lives* 38: "notable volumes, full of eloquence and learning, both in sacred Scripture and in secular literature" (NP3 40252–40254). LATER (about 190 years). Documented.

I5. **His books kept others' words.** Eusebius: he refers to Tatian, Cassianus, and the Jewish writers Philo, Aristobulus, Josephus, Demetrius and Eupolemus (VI.13.7, EUS 34151–34223); in his Passover book he wrote down "the traditions which he had heard from the ancient presbyters" and "mentions Melito and Irenæus" (EUS 34260–34266). LATER. Documented. (Census: his Stromata keep words of Valentinus's letters; not checked line by line here, card only.)

I6. **Not everyone approved (contested reception).** Photius, about 650 years later, read the Outlines, the Miscellanies and the Tutor. Of the Outlines: "Although in some cases whit he says appears orthodox, in others he indulges in impious and legendary fables" and "He talks much other blasphemous nonsense, either he or some one else under his name" (PHO 11083–11086, 11103–11105; OCR "whit" = what). Of the Tutor: "entirely free from idle and blasphemous opinions. The style is florid" (PHO 11131–11133). Of the Miscellanies: "The work in some parts is unsound, but not like the Outlines" (PHO 11162–11163). LATER. Documented as Photius's judgment. McGiffert's note that his name was later dropped from the roll of saints (EUS 28676–28678) is a modern editor's remark, not a held primary source: card only, not story.

## 5. Links to other people (by kind)

- **Pantaenus** — heard him teach (Clement's own unnamed "Sicilian bee", M1; named as teacher per Eusebius, M2). Widely Accepted.
- **Origen** — taught him (M4, I1). Widely Accepted.
- **Alexander, bishop (later of Jerusalem)** — met him; Clement carried his letter (M7); Clement dedicated a book to him (I3; also NP3 40259–40261). CONTEMP. Documented. "Taught him" is a modern inference (M9): Dominant Modern Reconstruction.
- **Asclepiades, bishop of Antioch** — the letter Clement carried rejoiced at his appointment (M7).
- **Eusebius of Caesarea** — kept his books, quoted his books, defended him (I2).
- **Jerome** — wrote his life (*Lives* 38), praised and quoted his example (I4). Also his Latin *Chronicle* (`cic/texts/jerome_eusebii-chronici-canones-lat_fotheringham1923.txt`): "Clemens Alexandrinae ecclesiae presbyter et Pantaenus stoicus philosophus in disputatione dogmatis nostri disertissimi habentur" (lines 43374–43380; OCR "ecdesiae" = ecclesiae; words split across lines joined), and "Clemens multa et uaria conscribit" (line 43462). LATER. A corroborating witness for the card step; it adds no new claim to the story.
- **Photius** — read and judged his books (I6).
- **Irenaeus, Melito, Tatian** — he quoted their books (I5).
- **Clement of Rome** — same name only, not the same man (S1).

## 6. Where sources disagree, or are later than the life

D1. **A school, or teachers?** Eusebius says Clement "succeeded" Pantaenus and "had charge ... of the catechetical instruction" (M4), and Jerome that he "led the theological school" (NP3 40250–40251). The census says historians dispute that a formal school existed, and that "Clement's own writings never once describe a formal school that he headed" (CEN 32602–32604, 32632). I found no passage in ANF2 where Clement calls himself head of a school. Say: "he taught in Alexandria"; if the succession is told, give it as Eusebius's account. Contested.

D2. **Who the six teachers were.** Clement names none (M1). ANF's note guesses Tatian and Theodotus (ANF2 27483–27484 note); McGiffert calls such guesses "mere guesses" (EUS 28728–28730). Leave out.

D3. **Why and when he left Alexandria.** No held source says Clement left or why. Eusebius says only that when Origen began to teach "there was no one at Alexandria to give instruction in the faith, as all were driven away by the threat of persecution" (VI.3.1, EUS 32670–32674; Greek "πάντων δ᾽ ἀπεληλαμένων ὑπὸ τῆς ἀπειλῆς τοῦ διωγμοῦ", EUSG 1216–1218). Clement is not named there. McGiffert reads Clement into it and dates it to 202 (EUS notes at 28668–28670, 33035–33040): modern inference. His later presence with the imprisoned Alexander (M7) is Documented; that he left Alexandria because of persecution is Inferential-Thin. Do not tell as fact.

D4. **Origen's silence.** In his own letter quoted by Eusebius, Origen says of using Greek learning: "in this we have followed Pantænus, who benefited many before our time ... and also Heraclas" (VI.19.13, EUS 35266–35272; Greek EUSG 3734–3737). He does not name Clement. The pupil link rests on Eusebius and Jerome. Widely Accepted, but say "Eusebius tells us" if it matters.

D5. **Presbyter of which church?** Eusebius's Greek calls him "the blessed presbyter" without a city (EUSG 2403). Rufinus's Latin version, about 200 years later, writes that Alexander sent the letter "per Clementem Antiochenae ecclesiae presbyterum", by Clement, presbyter of the church of Antioch (EUSG 2464–2465). Jerome (NP3 40249–40250) and Eusebius's own context make him of Alexandria. Card only.

D6. **Pantaenus's dates.** Jerome says Pantaenus "taught in the reigns of the emperor Severus and Antoninus surnamed Caracalla" (NP3 40220–40221), but also that Clement led the school "after the death of his master" in the same reigns (NP3 40250–40251, 40278–40279). McGiffert calls the first "erroneous" (EUS 28508–28512). Card only.

D7. **Orthodoxy.** Eusebius defends him (I2); Photius, much later, attacks the Outlines while doubting they are all his (I6). Contested reception; not a story point beyond one line, if any.

## 7. Not held, or not found

N1. Birth place, birth year, conversion, family, death year and place. McGiffert's note (EUS 28656–28674) and Freese's note (PHO 11078, 11119–11125: "he fled to Palestine, where he died") are modern editors' statements, not held witnesses. Do not use.
N2. Clement's own Greek text. Not held; all renderings of his words are from Wilson's English.
N3. Where Alexander was imprisoned when Clement was with him. The letter says "here" (EUS 33615) without naming it. Do not name Jerusalem or Cappadocia as Clement's location.
N4. The Hypotyposes passage naming Pantaenus (lost; EUS note 28698–28701).

## Added for batch 4

B1. **The critic's charge Jerome answered, in full.** Magnus asked "why it is that sometimes in my writings I quote examples from secular literature and thus defile the whiteness of the church with the foulness of heathenism." (Letter 70.2, JER 16477–16480). LATER (Jerome, about 190 years after Clement, reporting Magnus's question). Documented as Jerome's report of the question. It shows the force of the objection that Jerome met with Clement's example (I4).

B2. **What Origen's copy of the Miscellanies did, as Jerome tells it.** "Imitating his example Origen wrote ten books of Miscellanies, in which he compares together the opinions held respectively by Christians and by philosophers, and confirms all the dogmas of our religion by quotations from Plato and Aristotle, from Numenius and Cornutus." (Letter 70.4, JER 16636–16642; the translator's note markers left out). LATER. Widely Accepted for the pupil link (I1); Documented as Jerome's account of Origen's book. Origen's *Miscellanies* is not held in the Library (the translator's note, JER 16637–16639: "Of Origen's Miscellanies only a few fragments remain"), so the book's content rests on Jerome alone.
