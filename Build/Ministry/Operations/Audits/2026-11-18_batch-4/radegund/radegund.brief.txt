In the sixth century, in the kingdom of the Franks, now France, a queen who had become a nun wrote to the bishops of Gaul about the years after her death. "Because I could not come in person," wrote Radegund, "I bow down through this letter in my place, as if fallen at your feet."

In it she set out what she had built. With King Lothar's backing and the wealth he had given her, she founded a monastery for women at Poitiers and gave it everything she owned. She made Agnes, a girl she had raised, its abbess. She gave the house a written Rule that Caesarius, bishop of Arles, had made for his sister's convent, and under it a nun stayed inside the walls for life. Women came from the bishops' own dioceses to live by it.

She sent to the emperor in the East for a piece of the wood of the Cross, and the house came to be called Holy Cross. Gregory, a bishop who knew her, says the bishop of Poitiers would not receive it. So the king sent the bishop of Tours, who brought it in with psalms, candles and incense.

Her letter asked the bishops to guard her sisters from any tyrant. It made a second request as well: if anyone, even the city's own bishop, tried after her death to break the Rule or replace the abbess, they must stop him.

She had been born a princess of the Thuringians. When the Franks destroyed her people's kingdom, she was taken as spoil and married to Lothar, who later had her brother killed. She left the court for Medard, bishop of Noyon, and begged him to consecrate her to God.

Her friend the poet Fortunatus tells what followed. Medard hesitated because she was a married woman, and the king's nobles dragged him from the altar. She put on a nun's dress and charged him. "If you put off consecrating me, and fear a man more than God, then, shepherd, may the soul of your sheep be demanded from your hand." Medard consecrated her a deaconess.

She died in 587. Gregory buried her, and about two hundred nuns stood around her body.

Soon after, forty or more nuns walked out in revolt. The abbess read Radegund's letter aloud and sent copies to the bishops nearby. Before the bishops, the abbess said she had brought in no new custom, only what was done under the lady Radegund. Gregory copied the whole letter into his History, and that is how we still have her own words.
