# Outline: Radegund

Sheet references (S, L, Q, I, D, 4b, A) point to `Source_Sheet.md` in this folder. File short names: DAL = Gregory, *History*, tr. Dalton; GRL = Gregory, Latin works; VR-F / VR-B = the Latin Lives by Fortunatus / Baudonivia; VF = Fortunatus's poems.

- **The heart of their life:** A queen turned nun founded a monastery for women at Poitiers, with the king's backing, and gave it everything the king had given her (her own letter, L11; Documented). She gave it a written Rule, the one Caesarius of Arles wrote for his sister's convent, under which a nun stayed inside its walls for life (her letter and the bishops' letter, L13; Documented). She sent to the emperor in the East for a piece of the wood of the Cross, and the house came to be called Holy Cross (Gregory and Baudonivia, L16; Documented; the name rests on the editors, I3; Widely Accepted). Women came to her from the bishops' own dioceses, about two hundred by her death (the bishops' letter and Gregory, I2, L25; Documented). And she wrote a letter to the bishops to stand guard over the house after she died: protect the Rule, the abbess and the property against anyone, even the city's own bishop. When the house was torn by revolt after her death, the abbess read that letter aloud and sent copies to the bishops (her letter and Gregory, L19, I1; Documented).

- **Essence:** A captive queen who became a nun, she founded a house for women at Poitiers, gave it a written Rule and a relic of the Cross, and wrote the letter that guarded it after she died.

- **Also told:** all the king had given her; the name Holy Cross; the bishop of Poitiers refusing the relic; about two hundred women; the letter read in the revolt.

- **Length:** 2 minutes 30 seconds (about 375 words, Neo). The heart now opens the story and takes the first minute, so the captive princess and the altar at Noyon, which give the founding its stakes, need room after it, and the letter's use after her death carries a third of the story. The record is rich and close to her, so this is room the story needs, not filler. She is not a major figure on the tree. Up to about 3 minutes for a person (about 460 words), about 4 minutes 15 for a council (about 550); craft comes before time (Mark, 2026-11-18).

- **Setting, in words to say aloud:** "In the sixth century, in the kingdom of the Franks, in what is now France." "The church of Frankish Gaul." She came from Thuringia, a kingdom the Franks conquered (S1, S2, S6). Her house was at Poitiers (S4). She died in 587 (S5; the year is the editors' reckoning from Gregory's dating, Widely Accepted). Do not give a birth year or age.

- **Shape:** Founder or leader.

- **Openings:**
  1. **Chosen. Her own letter.** A queen who had become a nun writes to the bishops of Gaul, in her lifetime, for the time after her death: "Because I could not come in person, I bow down through this letter in my place, as if fallen at your feet" (Q2, L19). It leads straight into the heart, because the letter itself sets out what she founded, what she gave it and what she asked the bishops to guard. Say first who is writing and to whom.
  2. The altar at Noyon, in one sentence only, turned at once to Poitiers: the queen who put on a nun's dress herself and faced the bishop (L8; Fortunatus tells it). Use only if the writer can reach the founding within about 40 words.

- **Story beats** (beats 1–3 are the first minute, about 150 words):
  1. **The letter.** She wrote to the bishops while she still lived, sending the letter in her place (Q2). In it she tells what she had done. (L19. OWN, Documented. The year of the letter is not held; say only "in her lifetime", L19.)
  2. **The house at Poitiers.** Backed by King Lothar, and with the wealth he had given her, she built a monastery for women at Poitiers and gave it everything she owned; her nuns did the same, keeping nothing back. She made Agnes, a girl she had raised, its abbess, and obeyed her. She gave it a Rule, a written law of life, which Caesarius, bishop of Arles, had written for his sister's convent. Under it a nun stayed inside the walls for life. Women came from the bishops' own dioceses to live under it. (L11–L14, I2. Her own letter and the bishops' letter: Documented. Do not say she went to Arles, D2.)
  3. **The Cross from the East, and the guard for after her death.** With King Sigibert's letters she sent clerics to the East for the wood of the Cross; the emperor sent it, set in gold and gems. The house came to be called Holy Cross. In her letter she asked the bishops to protect the sisters from any tyrant; and, as a separate request, if after her death anyone, even the city's own bishop or a royal officer, tried to trouble the sisters, break the Rule or replace the abbess, to stop him. (L16, L19, I3. Cross: Documented; the gold and gems are Baudonivia's. The name: the editors, Widely Accepted. Keep the two requests apart: she names the bishop as a possible threat, never as a tyrant.) Keep, in one or two clauses, in Gregory's voice: Gregory says the bishop of Poitiers would not receive the Cross, so the king sent the bishop of Tours, who brought it in with psalms, candles and incense (L16, D5; DAL 19606–19625). This is the record's conflict with her own bishop, and it lets the listener hear the letter's naming of the city's bishop as a real danger.
  4. **Who she had been: taken as spoil.** A princess of the Thuringians. When the Franks destroyed her people's kingdom, the victorious kings quarrelled over who should have her. She fell to King Lothar, who had her raised at a royal estate and taught to read, then married her. As queen she set up a house where she bathed poor women and tended their sores herself; at court it was said the king had married a nun, not a queen. (S1, S2, L1–L5. Capture and marriage: Documented, Gregory. The quarrel and the schooling: Documented as Fortunatus's account. Care for the poor: Widely Accepted. The saying: "it was said", D4.)
  5. **She leaves.** Lothar had her brother killed. She left the court and went to Medard, bishop of Noyon, and begged him to consecrate her to God. He hesitated, because she was a married woman; the king's nobles dragged him from the altar. She put on a nun's dress, came to the altar and charged him (Q1). He laid his hand on her and consecrated her a deaconess, a woman set apart for the church's service. (L7, L8. Brother: Documented, Gregory. The altar scene: Fortunatus's account; Gregory does not tell it. Do not say whether the king sent her, D3.)
  6. **Death.** She died on 13 August 587. The local bishop was away, so Gregory of Tours came and buried her. About two hundred nuns stood round her body, some from senators' and kings' families (Q3). (S5, L24, L25. Documented; Gregory was there. The year 587 is the editors' reckoning from Gregory's dating: Widely Accepted (S5).)

- **What came of them** (about a third of the story; item 1 is foreshadowed in beat 3 and told here):
  1. **Her letter defended the house. DEFENDED: the Rule, the abbess, the property.** Soon after her death, forty or more nuns walked out in revolt. Gregory warned them with the bishops' letter to her. The abbess read Radegund's own letter aloud and sent copies to the bishops near by. At the bishops' hearing the abbess said she had brought in no new custom, only what was done under the lady Radegund. Gregory copied the whole letter into his *History*, which is how it survives. (I1, A1, A2. Documented.)
  2. **Women followed her.** The bishops wrote that young women flocked from their dioceses to take up her Rule; at her death there were about two hundred. (I2. Documented. Told in beats 2 and 6; here only if not already said.)
  3. **The Cross drew people.** While she lived, Gregory saw a lamp before it overflow with oil and wrote: from then on he preached the power of the Cross. Baudonivia says the blind and sick were healed there, and asks who could say how great a gift she gave the city. Gregory and Baudonivia both compare her to Helena, the emperor Constantine's mother, said to have found the Cross. (I3, L17, A3. Wonders: in Gregory's and Baudonivia's own voices.)
  4. **Her friends wrote her life twice.** Fortunatus, her friend the poet, wrote it after her death. Later the abbess and the nuns asked Baudonivia, a nun Radegund had raised, to write it again. (I5. Documented.)
  Possible ending, in the record's words: Gregory's "from then on I preached the power of the holy Cross" (I3), or Baudonivia's question about the gift to the city (I3).

- **Quotes, 2 or 3 at most:**
  1. **Q2, her letter (the opening).** Source text (Latin; GRL 31516–31517; raw lines in Sheet §8; OCR "Ef" read as Et, "epistidae" as epistulae; the sentence runs on, "coniurans per Patrem...", and is cut here): "Et quia praesens non valui, quasi vestris provoluta vestigiis, epistulae vicarietate prosternor". Rendering, mine, from the Latin: "Because I could not come in person, I bow down through this letter in my place, as if fallen at your feet." Printed English for comparison: Dalton, "And since I cannot in person throw myself at your feet, I make prostration vicariously through this letter" (DAL 19878–19879). Job: her own words; the letter that stands in for her, and the door into what she built.
  2. **Q1, at the altar.** Source text (Latin; VR-F 28806, 28808; raw lines in Sheet §8; editor's marks removed): "Si me consecrare distuleris et plus hominem quam Deum timueris, de manu tua, pastor, ovis anima requiratur." Rendering, mine, from the Latin: "If you put off consecrating me, and fear a man more than God, then, shepherd, may the soul of your sheep be demanded from your hand." Job: the turn of her life, in her own voice as Fortunatus gives it. Say before it that Fortunatus tells it.
  3. **Q3, the nuns at her bier.** Source text (Latin; GRL 61581–61582; raw lines in Sheet §8; OCR "suraus" read as sumus): "Cui nos orfanas, mater sancta, relinques? Cui nos desolatas conmendas? Reliquimus parentes facultatesque ac patriam et te secutae sumus." Rendering, mine, from the Latin: "Holy mother, to whom will you leave us orphans? To whom do you entrust us, forsaken? We left our parents, our goods and our homeland, and we followed you." Job: shows who followed her and what they gave up. Gregory reports it in a set form used for other saints (Q3 note), so give it as "Gregory heard them cry". Optional: drop it if the length is tight; the two hundred can be told without it.

- **Connection to carry into the story:** Her letter passed her authority to the house after her death. She wrote it to the bishops in her lifetime, asking them to stop anyone, the city's bishop included, who would break the Rule, replace the abbess or diminish its property (DAL 19884–19888, 19910–19919). In the revolt after her death the abbess read it aloud and sent copies to the neighbouring bishops (DAL 19821–19824), and defended the house's ways as what was done under the lady Radegund (A1). The listener needs to understand that the letter was written for exactly this: a founder who could no longer speak still spoke for her house, through bishops she had bound to it in writing. Carry it through what the abbess did with the letter, not through a "this matters because" sentence. Gregory's copying of the letter is the second link: it is why we have her own words at all.

- **Links:**
  - Gregory of Tours: met her; buried her; kept her letter; wrote of her (4b).
  - Venantius Fortunatus: her friend; by her wish he stayed near her (his own line, damaged in the held text), and was often her guest (Dalton); wrote her life; wrote a poem thanking the emperor for the Cross (4b).
  - Baudonivia: wrote her life. (She was a nun Radegund raised, 4b. Baudonivia has her own entry and story; do not tell it here.)
  - Caesarius of Arles and Caesaria: she used his Rule, written for Caesaria's convent (4b).
  - Martin of Tours: she visited his shrine; the bishops compared her to him; she entrusted her sisters to him (4b).
  - Hilary of Poitiers: she entrusted her sisters to him (4b).

- **Who tells it:**
  - Her letter: her own words, kept by Gregory (OWN).
  - Gregory of Tours: a bishop who knew her and buried her; wrote within a few years (CONTEMP).
  - Venantius Fortunatus: her close friend, a poet; wrote her Life after her death (CONTEMP).
  - Baudonivia: a nun of her house whom she raised; wrote after 600 (CONTEMP).
  - Line the writer can say aloud: "Her friend the poet Fortunatus, and Baudonivia, a nun she had raised, both wrote her life; they wrote as people who loved her." How-we-know gets this sentence or two; the doubt is not the story.

- **Do not say:**
  - Do not say how the revolt ended ("the Rule and the abbess held"): the outline does not give it. Say only what the abbess did with the letter and what she said at the hearing. (Round-two review, 2026-11-17.)
  - Say "forty or more" nuns, not "more than forty". (Round-two review, 2026-11-17.)
  - Do not add revolt details beyond the outline: not Clotild's name, her claim to be a king's daughter, or the nuns' complaint of insults and humiliation. Gregory's text holds them (DAL 19406–19416), but this story keeps only the letter's use. (Heart test, 2026-11-18.)
  - Tell the altar at Noyon once, not as an opening and again later. (Heart test, 2026-11-18.)
  - Do not say "the custom of the lady Radegund" as her phrase or Gregory's: it is Dalton's English. Say "no new custom, only what was done under the lady Radegund" (A1).
  - Do not call the abbess in the revolt Agnes. She was Leubovera (A2); "the abbess" is enough.
  - Do not place Gregory's lamp wonder after her death: he saw it while she lived (A3).
  - Do not say she went to Arles for the Rule (D2), or that the king sent her to Medard (D3).
  - Do not call the bishop a tyrant: she names him only as one who might trouble the house (L19).
  - Do not say the bishop of Poitiers refused the Cross as fact: "Gregory says" (D5).
  - Do not say the bishop's refusal caused her letter, or give his reason: no held line says either (D5). Gregory gives only that she failed to win his good grace and turned to the king for protection (DAL 19625–19632).
  - Say 'about two hundred' nuns, not 'two hundred' (GRL 61578, L25).
  - Say "consecrated her a deaconess", not "ordained her a deacon" (L8).
  - Say "the house came to be called Holy Cross", not that she named it so (I3).
  - Do not say whether she and Lothar had children, or give her brother a name (Sheet §6).

  - Do not hold back any part of the heart. The two hundred, the letter's guard against anyone, even the city's bishop, and the abbess reading it aloud in the revolt are all heart, not only "what came of them". Tell the revolt reading in full later. (Review, batch 4, 2026-11-18.)
  - Do not say "the king" for the Cross's journey or its bringing-in: name King Sigibert (DAL 19605, 19610–19620). "The king" unnamed is heard as Lothar. (Review, batch 4, 2026-11-18.)

- **Leave out:**
  - Childhood stories (wanting to be a martyr, play processions): told by a friend about years he did not see (L2).
  - The ascetic feats (iron chains grown into her flesh, ash beds): Fortunatus's, and they swamp the founding story (L15).
  - The burning of a Frankish shrine and the king's attempt to take her back: told only by Baudonivia, side stories (L6, L10).
  - Her peace letters to the warring kings: told only by Baudonivia (L18); the first minute needs the room. Use one clause at most, in Baudonivia's voice, only if the length allows.
  - Wonders at her death (the tax official's dream, the stone-cutters' angel, her shining face): side material (L26).
  - Whether she went to Arles for the Rule: contested (D2).
  - "Radegund of Poitiers" versus "Radegund, a sinner" in her letter's greeting: card only (D7).
  - The poems in her voice, such as the lament for Thuringia: Fortunatus wrote them in her name (D1).
  - Naming the hymn "Vexilla regis" as hers: the link rests on a modern editor, and the held text is unreadable (I4).
  - The nuns' revolt in detail: only her letter's use in it belongs here (I1).
  - Baudonivia's own story: she has her own entry; here she is a witness and the link "wrote her life".
