# Review: Radegund

Reviewer, batch 4, 2026-11-18. Brief read once, straight through, before the outline or sheet. Census: none given.

## The test, from one hearing

1. **Who:** Radegund, a Thuringian princess taken as war spoil and married to the Frankish king Lothar, who left him, became a nun and founded a monastery for women at Poitiers in the sixth century.
2. **What happened:** She gave the house all she had, an abbess she had raised, Caesarius's Rule of lifelong enclosure and a relic of the Cross from the East. She wrote to the bishops to guard it after her death, even against the city's own bishop, and after she died the abbess read that letter aloud in a revolt.
3. **Why the connection matters:** Her letter let her go on speaking for her house after her death, through the bishops she had bound to it. Gregory copied it into his *History*, which is why we have her own words.

All three lines come clear. I did not get lost. Nothing reads as a children's book or talks down.

## Verdict: REVISE

## Findings

**1. BLOCKING: the heart's second half arrives late.** The two hundred (w364), the letter's guard against the city's bishop (w209–235) and the abbess's reading of the letter in the revolt (w379–385) all arrive well past about 170 words. *Change:* keep the opening quote, then tell in order: the house, the gift, Agnes, the Rule and the enclosure; the Cross and the name in one sentence; and the letter's two requests (now paragraph 4). Then add one sentence before word 150–170 carrying the following and the revolt, for example: "By her death about two hundred women had followed her, and when the house was torn by revolt, the abbess read this letter aloud." To make room, Gregory's account of the bishop refusing the Cross can move after the heart. It still works there as the record's conflict with her bishop, and the letter's naming of him gains force when the revolt is told in full at the end. Keep the revolt's details (forty or more, the copies, the hearing) where they are. The outline left room for this, so add to its Do not say list:
> - Do not hold back any part of the heart past the first minute (about 150 words). The two hundred, the letter's guard against anyone, even the city's bishop, and the abbess reading it aloud in the revolt are all heart, not only "what came of them". Name the revolt reading in the first minute and tell it in full later. (Review, batch 4, 2026-11-18.)

**2. BLOCKING: "the king" at the Cross reads as Lothar.** "So the king sent the bishop of Tours" (paragraph 3). The only king the listener has heard named is Lothar, but it was King Sigibert. The clerics carried Sigibert's letters, and the queen "sent a fresh message to King Sigibert", who "enjoined the blessed Eufronius" (DAL 19605, 19610–19620). *Change:* "So King Sigibert sent the bishop of Tours" (or name Sigibert when the clerics set out). The outline's beat 3 says "the king" here, so add:
> - Do not say "the king" for the Cross's journey or its bringing-in: name King Sigibert (DAL 19605, 19610–19620). "The king" unnamed is heard as Lothar. (Review, batch 4, 2026-11-18.)

**3. MINOR: the abbess at the revolt.** The length note asked for this check. The brief does not call her Agnes; it says only "the abbess", as the outline's Do not say allows (A2; DAL 19409–19411 confirms Leubovera). But the listener has heard "She made Agnes... its abbess", and then the letter's plea to stop anyone who would "replace the abbess". So "the abbess" in the revolt is easily heard as Agnes. *Optional change:* name her once, "the abbess, Leubovera,". Do not explain how Agnes was succeeded: no held line gives it.

**4. MINOR: length.** At 430 words (about 2:48), the brief is over the outline's 375 words and Mark's 2:30, but under the 460 cap. I found no real padding to cut. Every paragraph carries an outline must-keep. Finding 1 should be done by moving material, not by adding to it.
