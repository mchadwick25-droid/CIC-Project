# Outline: Radegund

Sheet references (S, L, Q, I, D, 4b) point to `Source_Sheet.md` in this folder. File short names: DAL = Gregory, *History*, tr. Dalton; GRL = Gregory, Latin works; VR-F / VR-B = the Latin Lives by Fortunatus / Baudonivia; VF = Fortunatus's poems.

- **Length:** 2 minutes (about 300 words). The record is rich, but it holds one clear arc: from captive queen to founder, and a house that her own letter defended. That fits two minutes without crowding. She is not a major figure on the tree, and the extra material (wonders, ascetic feats, the nuns' revolt in detail) is side story, not room the story needs.

- **Setting, in words to say aloud:** "In the sixth century, in the kingdom of the Franks, in what is now France." "The church of Frankish Gaul." She came from Thuringia, a kingdom the Franks conquered (S1, S2, S6). Her house was at Poitiers (S4). She died in 587 (S5; the year is the editors' reckoning from Gregory's dating, Widely Accepted). Do not give a birth year or age.

- **Thread:** A captive queen turned her royal wealth into a house of women at Poitiers, gave it a written Rule and a relic of the Cross, and wrote the letter that defended it after she died.

- **Shape:** Founder or leader.

- **Openings:**
  1. The altar at Noyon: the king's nobles drag the bishop back from the altar; she goes into the sacristy, puts on a nun's dress herself, and walks out to face him (L8, Q1). Fortunatus tells it.
  2. Gregory of Tours at her bier: about two hundred nuns round her body, some from senators' and kings' families, crying out to her (L25, Q3).

- **Story beats:**
  1. **Taken as spoil.** A princess of the Thuringians. When the Franks destroyed her people's kingdom, the victorious kings quarrelled over who should have her. She fell to King Lothar, who had her raised at a royal estate and taught to read, then married her. (S1, S2, L1, L2, L3. Capture and marriage: Documented, Gregory. The quarrel and the schooling: Fortunatus's account, Documented as his.)
  2. **A queen who served.** She set up a house where she bathed poor women and tended their sores herself, and rose at night to pray. At court it was said the king had married a nun, not a queen. (L4, L5. Care for the poor: Widely Accepted, Fortunatus and Baudonivia agree. Night prayer and the saying: Documented as Fortunatus's account. Tell the saying as "it was said", D4.)
  3. **She leaves.** Lothar had her brother killed. She left the court and went to Medard, bishop of Noyon, and begged him to consecrate her to God. He hesitated, because she was a married woman; the king's nobles dragged him from the altar. She put on a nun's dress, came to the altar and charged him (Q1). He laid his hand on her and consecrated her a deaconess, a woman set apart for the church's service. (L7, L8. Brother: Documented, Gregory. The altar scene: Fortunatus's account; Gregory does not tell it. Do not say whether the king sent her, D3.)
  4. **The house at Poitiers.** Backed by the king and with the wealth he had given her, she built a monastery for women at Poitiers. She gave it everything she owned, and her nuns did the same. She made Agnes, a girl she had raised, abbess, and obeyed her. She gave the house a Rule, a written law of life, which Caesarius, bishop of Arles, had written for his sister's convent. Under it a nun stayed inside the walls for life. (L11–L14. Her own letter: Documented. Do not say she went to Arles, D2.)
  5. **The Cross from the East.** With King Sigibert's letters she sent clerics to the East for wood of the Cross. The emperor sent it, set in gold. The bishop of Poitiers would not receive it, so the king sent the bishop of Tours, who brought it in with psalms, candles and incense. Gregory and Baudonivia both compare her to Helena, the emperor Constantine's mother, said to have found the Cross. (L16, L17. Documented. Who refused is told two ways, D5: say "Gregory says" for the bishop's refusal.)
  6. **A letter for after her death.** She wrote to the bishops: protect us from any tyrant. And after my death, if anyone, even the city's own bishop or a royal officer, tries to trouble the sisters, break the Rule or replace the abbess, stop him (Q2). Keep these two requests apart: she names the bishop as a possible threat, never as a tyrant. Her nun Baudonivia adds that when the kings turned on each other, she wrote to each of them alike, begging them to make peace, not war. (L19; L18. Letter: OWN, Documented. Peace letters: Baudonivia only.)
  7. **Death.** She died on 13 August 587. The local bishop was away, so Gregory of Tours came and buried her. About two hundred nuns stood round her body, some from senators' and kings' families (Q3). (S5, L24, L25. Documented; Gregory was there.)

- **What came of them** (about a third of the story):
  1. **Her letter defended the house. DEFENDED: the Rule, the abbess, the property.** Soon after her death, forty or more nuns walked out in revolt. Gregory warned them with the bishops' letter to her. The abbess read Radegund's own letter aloud and sent copies to the bishops near by. At the bishops' hearing the abbess defended the house's ways as "the custom of the lady Radegund". Gregory copied the whole letter into his *History*. (I1. Documented.)
  2. **Women followed her.** The bishops wrote that young women flocked from their dioceses to take up her Rule; at her death there were about two hundred. (I2. Documented.)
  3. **The Cross drew people.** Gregory saw a lamp before it overflow with oil and wrote: from then on he preached the power of the Cross. Baudonivia says the blind and sick were healed there, and asks who could say how great a gift she gave the city. The house came to be called Holy Cross. (I3. Wonders: in Gregory's and Baudonivia's own voices. The name: the editors.)
  4. **Her friends wrote her life twice.** Fortunatus, her friend the poet, wrote it after her death. Later the abbess asked Baudonivia, a nun Radegund had raised, to write it again. (I5. Documented.)
  Possible ending, in the record's words: Gregory's "from then on I preached the power of the holy Cross" (I3), or Baudonivia's question about the gift to the city (I3).

- **Quotes, 2 or 3 at most:**
  1. **Q1, at the altar.** Source text (Latin; VR-F 28806, 28808; raw lines in Sheet §8; editor's marks removed): "Si me consecrare distuleris et plus hominem quam Deum timueris, de manu tua, pastor, ovis anima requiratur." Rendering, mine, from the Latin: "If you put off consecrating me, and fear a man more than God, then, shepherd, may the soul of your sheep be demanded from your hand." Job: the turn of the story, in her own voice as Fortunatus gives it. Say before it that Fortunatus tells it.
  2. **Q2, her letter.** Source text (Latin; GRL 31516–31517; raw lines in Sheet §8; OCR "Ef" read as Et, "epistidae" as epistulae; the sentence runs on, "coniurans per Patrem...", and is cut here): "Et quia praesens non valui, quasi vestris provoluta vestigiis, epistulae vicarietate prosternor". Rendering, mine, from the Latin: "Because I could not come in person, I bow down through this letter in my place, as if fallen at your feet." Printed English for comparison: Dalton, "And since I cannot in person throw myself at your feet, I make prostration vicariously through this letter" (DAL 19878–19879). Job: her own words; shows the letter as the thing that will stand in for her.
  3. **Q3, the nuns at her bier.** Source text (Latin; GRL 61581–61582; raw lines in Sheet §8; OCR "suraus" read as sumus): "Cui nos orfanas, mater sancta, relinques? Cui nos desolatas conmendas? Reliquimus parentes facultatesque ac patriam et te secutae sumus." Rendering, mine, from the Latin: "Holy mother, to whom will you leave us orphans? To whom do you entrust us, forsaken? We left our parents, our goods and our homeland, and we followed you." Job: shows who followed her and what they gave up. Gregory reports it in a set form used for other saints (Q3 note), so give it as "Gregory heard them cry".

- **Links:**
  - Gregory of Tours: met her; buried her; kept her letter; wrote of her (4b).
  - Venantius Fortunatus: her friend; by her wish he stayed near her (his own line, damaged in the held text), and was often her guest (Dalton); wrote her life; wrote a poem thanking the emperor for the Cross (4b).
  - Baudonivia: raised by her; wrote her life (4b).
  - Caesarius of Arles and Caesaria: she used his Rule, written for Caesaria's convent (4b).
  - Martin of Tours: she visited his shrine; the bishops compared her to him; she entrusted her sisters to him (4b).
  - Hilary of Poitiers: she entrusted her sisters to him (4b).

- **Who tells it:**
  - Her letter: her own words, kept by Gregory (OWN).
  - Gregory of Tours: a bishop who knew her and buried her; wrote within a few years (CONTEMP).
  - Venantius Fortunatus: her close friend, a poet; wrote her Life after her death (CONTEMP).
  - Baudonivia: a nun of her house whom she raised; wrote after 600 (CONTEMP).
  - Line the writer can say aloud: "Her friend the poet Fortunatus, and Baudonivia, a nun she had raised, both wrote her life; they wrote as people who loved her."

- **Leave out:**
  - Childhood stories (wanting to be a martyr, play processions): told by a friend about years he did not see (L2).
  - The ascetic feats (iron chains grown into her flesh, ash beds): Fortunatus's, and they swamp the founding story (L15).
  - The burning of a Frankish shrine and the king's attempt to take her back: told only by Baudonivia, side stories (L6, L10).
  - Wonders at her death (the tax official's dream, the stone-cutters' angel, her shining face): side material (L26).
  - Whether she went to Arles for the Rule: contested (D2).
  - "Radegund of Poitiers" versus "Radegund, a sinner" in her letter's greeting: card only (D7).
  - The poems in her voice, such as the lament for Thuringia: Fortunatus wrote them in her name (D1).
  - Naming the hymn "Vexilla regis" as hers: the link rests on a modern editor, and the held text is unreadable (I4).
  - The nuns' revolt in detail: only her letter's use in it belongs here (I1).

- **Do not say** (added from the round-two review, 2026-11-17): Do not say how the revolt ended ("the Rule and the abbess held"): the outline does not give it. Say only that the abbess defended the house's ways as "the custom of the lady Radegund". Say "forty or more" nuns, not "more than forty".
