# Outline: Macrina the Younger

"Sheet" = `Source_Sheet.md` in this folder, by line number.

- **Essence:** The eldest of the family of Basil and Gregory of Nyssa, she turned her family's household into a community of prayer and work, where her mother lived as an equal with the slave women. Dying, she taught Gregory about death and rising again, and he wrote it down with her as "the Teacher".

- **Also told:** the women's and men's sides, with Peter; the famine babies and the young widow; Ferrara 1438.

- **The heart of their life:** Macrina, the eldest of the family of Basil of Caesarea and Gregory of Nyssa, turned her family's household into a community given to prayer and work. She led her mother to live as an equal with the slave women of the house, in the same food and the same kind of bed, with prayer and hymns by night and day (sheet 81-85, 235). Over time it became one community with a women's side, which she led, and a men's side led by her youngest brother Peter, whom she had raised; it took in babies abandoned in a famine, and a young widow (sheet 89-97, 153-158, 229-232, 242-247). A visitor called it "the school of virtue" (sheet 229-230). The second half: as she lay dying, she taught her brother Gregory about the soul, death and rising again, and he wrote that teaching down as a dialogue with her as "the Teacher". More than a thousand years later it was still being quoted and argued over, at Ferrara in 1438 (sheet 107-110, 165-177). Confidence: the house and its life, Documented (Gregory's eyewitness account, sheet 81-85, 153-158); that she taught and that Gregory wrote the dialogue, Documented; how much of the dialogue's argument is hers, Contested (sheet 215); Ferrara, Documented (sheet 170-177); "double monastery" and the house's later weight as an early example, Dominant Modern Reconstruction, card only (sheet 156, 182-184).

- **Length:** 2 minutes (about 300 words). Thin in kind: one witness (her brother) for the whole life, and none of her own writing. But that witness is close and detailed, and her afterlife is real. Up to about 3 minutes for a person (about 460 words), about 4 minutes 15 for a council (about 550); craft comes before time (Mark, 2026-11-18). The heart must be told by about word 150.

- **Setting:** "In the fourth century, in Pontus, a region on the Black Sea, in what is now Turkey" (sheet 38-43). Do not place her house on a river aloud: the Iris is Gregory's placing for Naucratius, and Clarke's for her (sheet 38-41). Tradition: "the churches of Cappadocia and Pontus", or "the family of Basil of Caesarea and Gregory of Nyssa" (sheet 45). She died in 379, as scholars date it, some nine months after her brother Basil (sheet 43).

- **Shape:** Monk or ascetic. The life she chose (equal with the slave women, prayer and work), the others drawn after her (mother, brothers, foundlings, a young widow), and the turn that began it (refusing marriage), told after the house, not before it. Keep wonders out (see Leave out).

- **Openings** (chosen: 1; it leads straight into the house she made):
  1. Gregory arrives at the house after eight years apart. The men of the community stream out to meet him; the women wait in the church. He is led to his sister. She is lying on the floor, on a sack spread over a board, with another board for a pillow. She lifts herself on one elbow but cannot rise to meet him (sheet 101-105, 153). Next sentence: this house was her making, with Peter, the brother she had raised, as her chief helper (LIFE 164). Keep the scene to about 35 words.
  2. A soldier and his wife visit "the school of virtue", as he called it. He goes to the men's side, led by Peter; she goes to the women's side, to Macrina (sheet 153-154, 229-232). Use only if opening 1 is not used.

- **Story beats** (beats 1-3 are the first minute, about 150 words):
  1. **The scene, and who she is.** Opening 1. She is the eldest of the family of Basil and Gregory; fourth century, Pontus (sheet 33-34, 38-45, 101-105). One clause of place, not a sentence. Documented.
  2. **The house she made.** She had led her mother to live as an equal with the slave women of the house: the same food, the same kind of bed, prayer and hymns day and night. Gregory says that for them this prayer was their work, and work as others call it was rest. It grew over years into one community, women on one side under her and men on the other under her youngest brother Peter, whom she had raised from a baby. In a famine she picked up babies left by the roadside to die, and raised them; a young widow came to live under her care (sheet 81-85, 89-97, 153-158, 235-247). Gregory calls this "philosophy", meaning a life given wholly to God; do not say the word aloud (sheet 50-53). Documented.
  3. **Her teaching, and how we know.** In those last days, fevered and dying, she talked with Gregory about the soul, death and the journey from death to life again. Gregory, the brother who sat with her, is almost our only witness: he wrote her life, and he wrote her teaching down as a dialogue with her as "the Teacher". Join the how-we-know to this sentence; do not give it a sentence of its own earlier. One clause may look ahead: more than a thousand years later her words were still being quoted (sheet 107-110, 165-169, 221). Documented / Contested (how much is hers).
  4. **The turn that began it.** Her grandparents had lost their goods, and one grandfather his life, for confessing Christ; she was named for her father's mother, who had stood firm in the persecutions (sheet 33-34, 47-48). At twelve her father chose her a husband; the young man died before the marriage. She refused every other offer. There is one marriage, she said, as there is one birth and one death, and her promised husband was not dead but only away, because of the hope of rising again (sheet 66-70). She would not leave her mother. After her father died she helped her mother carry the cares of an estate spread over three districts, and often prepared bread for her mother with her own hands (sheet 72-77; the bread is in the Greek, PG46 70301-70302). Documented (the family's memory, as Gregory tells it).
  5. **Her brothers.** Gregory says that when Basil came home from his studies, proud of his speaking, she drew him to a simple life of work with his hands. Basil's friend Gregory of Nazianzus says Basil had already chosen that life. Tell it as Gregory's account (sheet 78-79, 213). Contested. Peter looked to her "as the model of all good" (sheet 188). Documented.
  6. **The deathbed.** In 379, some nine months after Basil's death, as scholars date it. When Gregory told her of his troubles and exile, she told him to see the gift in his fame (Quote 3) (sheet 101, 112-117). At evening, her couch turned to the east, she prayed (Quote 1). A lamp was brought, and she wanted to sing the evening thanksgiving for the lighting of the lamps. Her voice failed, so she finished it in her heart, moving her hands and lips. She made the sign of the cross, drew a deep breath, and her life and her prayer ended together (sheet 118-128; LIFE 232-234). Documented as Gregory's eyewitness report.
  7. **What she left.** Lampadia, the deaconess who led the sisters, said that all she owned was a cloak, a head-covering and worn shoes (Quote 2) (sheet 129-132). Crowds kept an all-night vigil "as in the case of martyrs' festivals", and she was buried beside her mother (sheet 142-145). Documented.

- **What came of them** (about a third of the story; heaviest first; item 1 is named in beat 3 and told in full here):
  1. Her teaching, in her voice. Gregory wrote a dialogue, *On the Soul and the Resurrection*, in which she is "the Teacher" and he the grieving questioner. **She defends** the soul's life after death and the body's rising, and tells him not to grieve like those "without hope". In Greek the dialogue was also called by her name, "the Macrinia" (sheet 165-169). Say plainly that the words are Gregory's writing of their talk; how much is hers is not known (sheet 215). Documented / Contested (how much is hers).
  2. More than a thousand years later, in 1438, Latin and Greek church leaders met at Ferrara to argue over what happens to souls after death. The Latin side quoted "Macrina speaking" from Gregory's book. The Greek side answered that Gregory was a man and could err. The Latins replied that he was the brother of Basil the Great and of blessed Macrina (sheet 170-178). Documented.
  3. The house she made: mistress and slave women living as sisters, women and men in one community, prayer by night and day. Later scholars use Gregory's account as an early picture of how such a house was run (sheet 153-161, 182-184). Documented (the house) / Dominant Modern Reconstruction (its later weight). Told in beat 2; here, at most one clause.
  4. Her brothers: Peter, who took her as "the model of all good"; Gregory, who called her "the great Macrina"; Basil, as Gregory tells it (sheet 186-190). Documented, Basil Contested.
  5. Gregory wrote her life so it would not be "unknown to our time" (sheet 192-193). Possible last line: the end of item 2, or Gregory's own purpose.

- **Quotes** (rendered from Gregory's Greek in Migne, PG 46, checked against Clarke's 1916 English; Greek printed with the OCR's letter errors corrected, as listed in the sheet):
  1. **Her dying prayer.** Greek, PG46 71687, 71690, 71730-71732: «Σὺ,» φησὶν, «ἔλυσας ἡμῖν, Κύριε, τοῦ θανάτου τὸν φόβον. Σὺ ζωῆς ἀληθινῆς ἀρχὴν ἡμῖν ἐποίησας τὸ τέλος τῆς ἐνταῦθα ζωῆς. Σὺ πρὸς καιρὸν διαναπαύεις ὕπνῳ τὰ σώματα, καὶ πάλιν ἀφυπνίζεις ἐν τῇ ἐσχάτῃ σάλπιγγι. (Clarke: LIFE 230, sheet 118-121.)
     **Rendering:** "You, Lord," she said, "have set us free from the fear of death. You have made the end of this life the beginning of true life for us. For a time you give our bodies rest in sleep. And you wake them again at the last trumpet."
     **Job:** her own faith at the moment it was tested; the teaching of the heart, in her own prayer.
  2. **Lampadia on her wealth.** Greek, PG46 72082-72085: Ποίοις, εἶπεν, ἀποκειμένοις; Ἐν χερσὶν ἔχεις πᾶν τὸ ἀπόθετον· ἰδοὺ τὸ ἱμάτιον, ἰδοὺ τῆς κεφαλῆς ἡ καλύπτρα, τὰ τετριμμένα τῶν ποδῶν ὑποδήματα· οὗτος ὁ πλοῦτος· αὕτη ἡ περιουσία. (Clarke: LIFE 284, sheet 129-132.)
     **Rendering:** "What store?" she said. "You have in front of you everything she put by. Here is the cloak, here is the head-covering, and the worn-out shoes on her feet. This is her wealth. This is what she owned."
     **Job:** her poverty shown by a witness who lived with her, not told by the narrator.
  3. *(Optional, if room.)* **To Gregory.** Greek, PG46 71505-71506, 71536: «Σὺ δὲ,» φησὶ, «πόλεσι, καὶ δήμοις, καὶ ἔθνεσιν ὀνομαστὸς εἶ· καὶ σὲ πρὸς συμμαχίαν τε καὶ διόρθωσιν Ἐκκλησίαι πέμπουσι, καὶ καλοῦσι· καὶ οὐχ ὁρᾷς τὴν χάριν; (Clarke: LIFE 218, sheet 112-116.)
     **Rendering:** "But you," she said, "are known by name in cities, peoples and nations. Churches send you out and call you in, to help them and set them right. And do you not see the gift?"
     **Job:** the elder sister teaching a bishop, even from her deathbed.

- **Connection to carry into the story:** From Macrina to Gregory, and through his book to Ferrara. Gregory sat with her for her last two days, heard her teach on the soul and rising again, and wrote that teaching as a dialogue in which she is the Teacher (sheet 107-110, 165-168). The listener must understand that her words survive only because her brother wrote them, so they are his writing of her voice, not her own book (sheet 215). In 1438 the Latin side at Ferrara quoted "Macrina speaking" from that book, the Greek side said Gregory was a man and could err, and the Latins answered that he was the brother of Basil and of Macrina (sheet 170-177). The writer must make plain that her family tie was used as an argument for trusting the book; that is why her name was still being spoken in a quarrel between the churches. The second connection, told in beat 2: her mother and her brother Peter were drawn into the life she chose, and Peter led the men's side of the house (sheet 81-92, 154).

- **Links:**
  - Macrina the Elder: grandmother; named for her (sheet 33-34, 201).
  - Emmelia: mother; lived with her, led her into the ascetic life, and was buried beside her (sheet 202).
  - Basil of Caesarea: brother; drew him to the ascetic life, as Gregory tells it (contested) (sheet 78, 203).
  - Peter of Sebaste: brother; raised and taught him; he led the men's side (sheet 89-92, 205).
  - Gregory of Nyssa: brother; heard her teach, wrote her life, and gave her the teacher's voice in his dialogue (sheet 165-169, 206).
  - The Latin side at Ferrara, 1438: quoted her words, through Gregory's book (sheet 170-177, 209).

- **Who tells it:**
  - Gregory of Nyssa, her younger brother: an eyewitness of her last two days, writing within a few years of her death, in her honour (sheet 27, 221). Say aloud, inside beat 3: "Almost all we know of her comes from her brother Gregory, who sat with her as she died." Gregory's dating is Clarke's reading; "a few years later" only with "as scholars date it".
  - The soldier who called the house "the school of virtue": a visitor whose words Gregory reports (sheet 229-230).
  - The Ferrara debate, 1438, more than a thousand years later (sheet 170). Say aloud: "More than a thousand years later, her words were still being quoted."
  - Clarke, Moore and Wilson, Morison, Delehaye: modern scholars; card only.

- **Do not say:**
  - Not "sharing their beds": they slept on "the same kind of bed" (from the round-two review, 2026-11-17).
  - Not a scene, a how-we-know sentence, a place line and the betrothal all before the house: the house she made and her teaching come within the first 150 words; the betrothal follows them (Heart_Test, 2026-11-18).
  - Not "she freed her slaves": she led her mother to live with them "as if they were sisters" (sheet 238-239).
  - Not "she founded a monastery" on a day: it grew over years from the household (sheet 238-240).
  - Not "a double monastery" as fact, and not "the first" or "a new kind of" house: "double monastery" is Clarke's reading, and no held source calls it first or new (sheet 156, 182-184, 195).
  - Not that they farmed or ran a trade together: Gregory names their work as prayer and hymns, and her own handwork (sheet 234-236).
  - Not "Macrina wrote" or "her book": the dialogue is Gregory's; say "Gregory wrote her teaching down as a conversation, with her as the teacher" (sheet 215).
  - Not "she taught purgatory": say her words were quoted and argued over (sheet 178).
  - Not "she turned Basil to the monastic life" as fact: "Gregory says..." and Basil's friend says otherwise (sheet 213).
  - Not "in 379" as Gregory's own date: "in 379, as scholars date it" (sheet 43). The dialogue's date, 380, belongs to the writing, not to her death (sheet 215).
  - Not "Peter ran the house": the women's side was hers; Peter led the men's side (sheet 154, 242-245).
  - Not "widows" in number: one young widow, Vestiana, is named (sheet 247).
  - Not a story that never looks ahead to the afterlife: the approved heart includes her words still being quoted at Ferrara in 1438, so beat 3's look-ahead clause is required, not optional (Review, 2026-11-18).
  - Not the house without the young widow: she is part of the approved heart, so beat 2 must name her (Review, 2026-11-18).

- **Leave out:**
  - The wonder stories: the healed tumour, the soldier's daughter's eye, the grain that did not run out, Gregory's dream of shining relics (sheet 136-140). They are reports Gregory heard after her death, and Gregory himself held most back. If one is wanted, tell only that he chose to leave many out.
  - The secret name Thecla from her mother's dream (sheet 57-60); the iron cross and the ring with a relic (sheet 134); the opening of the family grave: side stories.
  - "Purgatory" as her teaching (sheet 178); "Phaedo"; "double monastery"; the estate's name Annesi; the exact number of children (sheet 219, 223): modern terms or disputed details.
  - Gregory of Nazianzus as a link to her: no held text of his names her (sheet 207).
  - Any feast day: none found in the held texts (sheet 197).
