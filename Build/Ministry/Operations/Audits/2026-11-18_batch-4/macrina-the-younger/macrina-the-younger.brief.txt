After eight years apart, Gregory came to his sister's house. Its men streamed out to meet him, while the women waited in the church. He was led to Macrina, who lay on a sack spread over a board on the floor. She lifted herself on one elbow, but could not rise.

This house was her making, in fourth-century Pontus, in what is now Turkey. Macrina, the eldest of the family of Basil of Caesarea and Gregory of Nyssa, had led her mother to live as an equal with their slave women, with the same food, the same kind of bed, and prayer and hymns night and day. Over the years it grew into one community, the women under her and the men under Peter, the brother she had raised. In a famine she took in babies left by the roadside to die.

In her last days she talked with Gregory about the soul, death and rising again. Almost all we know of her comes from her brother Gregory, who sat with her as she died, and he wrote her teaching down as a conversation, with her as the Teacher.

It had begun with a refusal. When the young man her father chose for her died before the wedding, she turned down every other offer. There is one marriage, she said, as there is one birth and one death. Her promised husband was not dead but only away, because of the hope of rising again.

She died in 379, as scholars date it. At evening she prayed. "You, Lord," she said, "have set us free from the fear of death." When a lamp was brought, her voice failed in the evening thanksgiving, so she finished it in her heart, and her life and her prayer ended together.

How much of Gregory's book is truly hers, no one can say. Yet more than a thousand years later, her words were still being quoted. At Ferrara in 1438, Latin and Greek church leaders argued over what happens to souls after death, and the Latins quoted Macrina from Gregory's book. When the Greeks replied that Gregory was a man and could err, the Latins answered with his family: he was the brother of Basil the Great and of blessed Macrina.
