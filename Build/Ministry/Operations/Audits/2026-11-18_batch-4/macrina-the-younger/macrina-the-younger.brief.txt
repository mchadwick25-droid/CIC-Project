After eight years apart, Gregory came to his sister's house and was led to Macrina, lying on a sack spread over a board. She lifted herself on one elbow but could not rise.

The house, in fourth-century Pontus in what is now Turkey, was her making. She was the eldest of the family of Basil of Caesarea and Gregory of Nyssa. She had led her mother to live as an equal with the slave women, eating the same food, sleeping on the same kind of bed, and praying night and day. Over the years it grew into one community, the women under her and the men under Peter, the brother she had raised, and in a famine she took in babies left to die.

As she lay dying, she talked with Gregory about the soul, death and rising again. Almost all we know of her comes from her brother Gregory, who sat with her as she died, and he wrote her teaching down as a conversation, with her as the Teacher.

It had begun with a refusal. When the man her father chose for her died before the wedding, she would take no other. There is one marriage, she said, as there is one birth and one death, and he was not dead but only away, because of the hope of rising again.

She died in 379, as scholars date it. At evening she prayed. "You, Lord," she said, "have set us free from the fear of death." When her voice failed in the evening thanksgiving, she finished it in her heart, and her life and her prayer ended together.

How much of Gregory's book is hers, no one can say, but more than a thousand years later her words were still being quoted. At Ferrara in 1438, Latin and Greek church leaders argued over what happens to souls after death, and the Latins quoted Macrina from Gregory's book. When the Greeks replied that Gregory was a man and could err, the Latins answered with his family: he was the brother of Basil the Great and of blessed Macrina.
