After eight years apart, Gregory came to his sister's house and was led to her. She lay on a sack spread over a board, and she lifted herself on one elbow but could not rise.

The house, in fourth-century Pontus in what is now Turkey, was the making of Macrina, the eldest of the family of Basil of Caesarea and Gregory of Nyssa. She had led her mother to live as an equal with the slave women, eating the same food, sleeping on the same kind of bed, and praying night and day. Over the years it grew into one community, the women under her and the men under Peter, the brother she had raised, and in a famine she took in babies left to die.

On her deathbed she taught Gregory about the soul, death and rising again. Almost all we know of her comes from her brother Gregory, who sat with her as she died, and he wrote her teaching down as a conversation, with her as the Teacher. How much of it is hers, no one can say.

Her life had turned on a refusal. When the man her father chose for her died before the wedding, she would take no other. He was not dead, she said, but only away, because of the hope of rising again.

She died in 379, as scholars date it, and at evening she prayed. "You, Lord," she said, "have set us free from the fear of death." When her voice failed in the evening thanksgiving, she finished it in her heart, and her life and her prayer ended together.

More than a thousand years later, her words were still being quoted. At Ferrara in 1438, Latin and Greek church leaders argued over what happens to souls after death, and the Latins quoted Macrina from Gregory's book. When the Greeks replied that Gregory was a man and could err, the Latins answered with his family: he was the brother of Basil the Great and of blessed Macrina.
