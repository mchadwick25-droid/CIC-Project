# Outline: the Council of Nicaea (325)

Sheet = `Source_Sheet.md` in this folder; tags (K1, S1, M5, I3, B1...) point to its entries, which give file and lines. Rebuilt for batch 4 from `Outline.r50.md`: its lines, renderings and line references are kept; the beats are reordered so the heart comes first.

- **The heart of their life:** The church was split over Jesus: was the Son God as the Father is, or a being with a beginning? Arius said, in his own letter, "the Son has a beginning, but God has no beginning" (D-3, Q1; Documented). The council's letter charged him with saying "there was a time when he was not" and that the Son is "a creature" (D-4; Documented as the council's charge; keep the two voices apart). In 325 the emperor Constantine called bishops from across the empire to Nicaea (S1, S2, S4; year Widely Accepted). They set out a creed calling the Son "God of God, Light of Light", "begotten, not made", "of one substance" (of one being) with the Father, and cursed anyone who said there was a time when he was not (M8; Documented, the council's own text). Arius and two bishops were condemned (M10; Documented), and the emperor sent Arius into exile (M11; Widely Accepted, a historian a century later). It also settled a split in Egypt, set one day for Easter and made twenty rules for church order (M12, M13, M14; Documented). What it changed: later councils, meeting over new disputes, set its creed first: "shall not be set aside" (Constantinople) and "pre-eminent" (Chalcedon) (I1, B1; Documented). But it did not hold at once; for decades the word "of one being" divided the church (I3; Documented). Its rules travelled as church law, read aloud in Carthage (I4; Documented) and accepted in Persia (I4; Widely Accepted, through Braun).
- **Length:** about 450–500 words, about 3½ to 4 minutes in Daniel's voice (Mark, 2026-11-17: may run long). Reason: a major council that did five kinds of work, with three voices to follow (Constantine, Arius, Eusebius) and an after-history on each decision. The record is rich; do not fill beyond it. The heart (beats 1–2) takes the first 130 words or so.
- **Setting:** 325 (S2: the year; the days are Contested, say only "in 325"). The city of Nicaea, in today's Turkey (S1; CEN). Bishops from across the Roman Empire, called by the emperor Constantine (S4, S5), in the churches of the empire newly free of persecution: in Constantine's words, the tyrants' hostility had been "forever removed" (S3). Do not give a number of years.
- **Shape:** Council. The major issue leads and stays the spine; the other business is told as "it also settled"; the impact is told in brief in the first minute and in full at the end.
- **Openings:** (1) Arius's own complaint, from his letter before the council: "We are persecuted because we said, 'The Son has a beginning, but God has no beginning.'" (D-3, Q1). Chosen: it is the stake, in the condemned man's voice, and it leads straight to what the council said back. (2) Second choice: the emperor telling the bishops that strife inside the church was worse to him than any war (M2, Q2); if used, the question about the Son must follow in the next sentence. Do not open on Easter.

**Story beats** (beats 1–2 carry the heart in the first minute; beats 3–7 deepen it in Mark's spine order, with Arius already told in beat 1: what called it (beats 3–4), the debate (5), the creed and its condemnation (6), then the other business (7); then What came of it.)

1. *The major issue, and who met.* Bishop Alexander of Alexandria had questioned his presbyters (priests); Arius held that the Son had a beginning and God had none, and Alexander drove him out. Arius wrote that he was persecuted for it (Q1). The council's letter puts his teaching harder: the Son came from nothing, "there was a time when he was not", he is "a creature". Reason it mattered: whether the Son is God as the Father is, or a being with a beginning. In 325 the emperor Constantine called bishops "from all quarters" to Nicaea. D-1, D-3, D-4, S4, Q1. Documented (Arius's words his own; the charge the council's).
2. *Its decision, and what it changed, in brief.* The bishops answered with a creed: the Son is "begotten, not made", of one being with the Father; anyone saying "there was a time when he was not" is cursed (declared cut off). Arius and two bishops were condemned; the emperor sent Arius into exile, a historian a century later says. Later councils set this creed first, though for decades it divided the church. M8, M10, M11, I1, I3. Documented (exile Widely Accepted). The full creed wording and the after-history come later (beat 6, What came of it); here, one or two sentences each.
3. *What called it.* Two troubles reached the emperor. In Egypt, Eusebius writes, bishops clashed with bishops in every city, and some even insulted the emperor's statues. And the churches kept Easter on different days: one party held to the Jewish reckoning, so some fasted while others feasted. K1, K2. Documented. (Eusebius also names a "Theban and Egyptian schism" in Egypt; do not identify it with Melitius, K1.) Easter is told here as the second trouble, not as an equal cause beside the question of the Son.
4. *What called it, and the meeting.* Constantine's letter to Alexandria failed, so he called the bishops, lent them the public post and horses, and met them at Nicaea. He told them, in Latin through an interpreter, that strife inside the church was worse than war (Q2). K3, S4, M1, M2, M3. Documented as Eusebius's account. Optional detail: he waited for the bishops to beckon before he sat (M1). Who came: more than 250 bishops by Eusebius's count, from Syria to Spain, a bishop from Persia, two priests for Rome's aged bishop (C1, C2, C3; count Contested).
5. *Its story.* The debate, told by three men who were there, each differently: Eusebius read his church's creed, and the emperor asked for one word, homoousios, "of one being"; Athanasius says Arius's friends read the bishops' words, like "from God" and "like the Father", as true of all creatures too, and were caught "whispering ... and winking"; Eustathius says a statement from that party was torn up "in the sight of all". M5, M6, M7. Each Documented as its teller's account; Contested as one story (sheet D2).
6. *Its decision, in full.* The creed: "God of God, Light of Light", "begotten, not made", of one being with the Father, with its curse. Eusebius signed, he told his church, with peace as his aim (Q3). The two bishops were Theonas and Secundus (M10). The emperor backed the sentence with force: Sozomen says he ordered banishment for any who resisted, and Constantine's own letter, as Socrates gives it, set death as the penalty for hiding a book by Arius and not burning it. M8, M9, M10, M11, H12. Documented (exile Widely Accepted; the death penalty as Constantine's own letter in Socrates).
7. *What else it did, and decided ("it also settled").* The council's letter turns next to Egypt's other trouble: Melitius, who had ordained clergy of his own, kept "the bare title of his rank" but could ordain no one, and his clergy served under Alexander's. Then Easter, one of the two troubles that called it: one day everywhere, the day kept at Rome and in Alexandria. It also set down twenty rules (canons) for the order of the church: how a bishop is made, councils twice a year in every province, mercy, after years of penance, for those who had given way under Licinius without being forced, the last communion never refused to the dying, clergy forbidden to lend at interest. M12, M13, M14, K2. Documented.

**What came of it** (about a third of the story; heaviest first. Item 1 and the first sentence of item 2 are first told in brief in beat 2.)
1. The creed became the fixed reference: Constantinople, which met in 381 (editor's date) and condemned new heresies by name, ruled that the faith of the 318 fathers "shall not be set aside"; Chalcedon, in 451 (editor's date, NC 18942), called it "pre-eminent". I1, B1. Documented. **Defended**: Athanasius wrote a whole book defending its words "of the essence" and "of one being". I2.
2. It did not hold at once. Eusebius of Nicomedia and Theognis were brought back, and Arius was recalled. At Rimini, in 359 by the editor's date, Jerome wrote, "the whole world groaned, and was astonished to find itself Arian." I3. Documented. It divided those who held the word from those who would not.
3. Its rules travelled as church law: about ninety years later in Carthage, bishops read aloud the creed and "the statutes ... in twenty heads" from the copy Caecilian had brought home, and wrote east for exact copies; a Persian synod, dated 410 in the work Braun cites (MAR 894), swore to accept the canons, and its acts set out the Nicene creed. I4. Documented (Carthage); Widely Accepted (Persia).
4. Easter did not settle at once either: a later council at Antioch (341, by the editor's date) threatened to cast out anyone who set aside "the decree of the holy and great Synod" on Easter. I5. Documented.
End there: on Carthage reading the copy aloud, or on the later councils setting the creed first.

**Connection to carry into the story**
- Nicaea to Constantinople (381, editor's date) and Chalcedon (451, editor's date, NC 18942): its creed passed to them as the standard they had to keep, "shall not be set aside" (NC 13970–13972) and "pre-eminent" (NC 20217–20220). The listener needs to hear that these later councils met to deal with newer heresies (NC 13972–13977; NC 20221–20223; B1) and still named Nicaea's faith first; say "318 fathers" is their name for Nicaea (I1). This connection is first named in the first minute (beat 2), and told in full at the end.
- Nicaea to Carthage: Caecilian, who sat at Nicaea, carried home a written copy of the creed and the twenty canons; ninety years later the African bishops read that copy aloud as law and, when a disputed rule was not in it, wrote to Antioch, Alexandria and Constantinople for exact copies (NC 32523–32545; I4). The listener needs to understand that the canons travelled as written copies, and copies could be checked.
- Nicaea to Persia: Marutha read "the volume of the canons" to Persian bishops, who accepted them under oath (MAR 857–866; I4). Tell it as Braun's report of the Syriac acts.
- Nicaea to Antioch (341): the Easter ruling was enforced by a later council against those who still kept the older day (NC 9438–9452; I5).

**Who tells it**
- The creed, rules and letter to Egypt are the council's own; its minutes are lost (not held).
- Eusebius of Caesarea was there and admired the emperor; he was slow to accept the word. Aloud: "Eusebius, a bishop who was there and admired the emperor". Place this at beat 3 or 4, not in the first minute.
- Eustathius was there; Athanasius was there as a deacon, a later historian says; both were Arius's foes. Athanasius wrote years later. Aloud: "Athanasius, who was there as a deacon and wrote later in its defence".
- Arius's letter dates from before the council; it is kept in the history of Theodoret, who was against him. One clause near the opening quote is enough: "in a letter written before the council".
- Socrates, Sozomen and Theodoret wrote over a century later. Aloud, for the exile, the emperor's banishment order or the scars: "a historian writing a century later". Constantine's death-penalty letter is his own, as Socrates gives it.
- How-we-know is a sentence or two; the doubt is not the story.

**Do not say**
- "318 bishops came." → "More than 250, by the count of Eusebius, who was there; others who were there said about 270 or about 300. Later tradition fixed the number at 318."
- "The council was unanimous." → "All but a few signed; Arius and two bishops were condemned." Do not give a number of refusers (two or five: the witnesses differ).
- "Constantine presided" / "Constantine wrote the creed" / "the pope presided." → Eusebius says the emperor approved his creed and asked for one word to be added; the record does not say who presided. Rome's bishop did not come.
- "Ossius presided" / "Eustathius presided." → Eusebius speaks only of "those who presided" and names no one (EUS 64501).
- "The 318 fathers came to Nicaea." → When quoting Constantinople or Chalcedon, say "318 fathers" is the name later councils used for Nicaea, not a count.
- "Constantine made Christianity the empire's religion at Nicaea" / "Constantine was baptized there." → Nothing in the record. He called the council, sat in it and spoke at it.
- "Arius said Jesus was only a man" / "a mere creature." → Arius called the Son "perfect God" but said that before he was begotten "he was not", that he "has a beginning". The council's letter charged him with calling the Son a creature. Keep the two voices apart.
- "Homoousios means equal" / "the same as" / "similar." → "of one being (one substance) with the Father". This holds in the first-minute summary too: not "equal to the Father" as shorthand.
- "The creed churches say today was written at Nicaea." → The Nicaea creed is shorter: its statement of belief stops at "and in the Holy Spirit", and its curses follow; the creed said in churches today is the longer one tied to the council of Constantinople. If unsure, do not mention it. This holds in the first-minute line about later councils: they set Nicaea's creed first; do not say they handed it on as today's creed.
- "The council decided which books belong in the Bible." → Nothing in the record. Do not say it.
- "The council fixed Easter by the full moon and the spring equinox." → It set one day for Easter everywhere, so that those in the East who had followed the Jewish custom would keep it on the same day as Rome and Alexandria.
- "Easter was settled for the sake of unity." alone → If a reason is given, the council's decision is credited to the council; the open contempt for the Jews is in Constantine's letter and is credited to Constantine, never carried forward into the narrator's voice or quoted. Eusebius's plainer reason (K2: some fasting while others feasted) may be used.
- "The council said the Jews ..." / "the bishops condemned the Jews." → The council's own letter says only that those in the East who followed the Jewish custom would now keep Easter with Rome and Alexandria (NC 6402–6406). The hostile words are Constantine's.
- "The council fixed the date of Easter for ever." → It set one day; a later council at Antioch had to enforce it (I5).
- "The Theban and Egyptian schism" = Melitius. → Eusebius does not say so; keep them apart (K1).
- "Twenty canons on the Trinity" / "rules about doctrine." → The canons are about church order: making bishops, provincial councils, penance, the dying, clergy moving and lending at interest (M14). The faith is in the creed.
- "Canon 6 made Rome the head of the church." → It upholds old customs for Alexandria, Rome and Antioch side by side (M14); do not rank them.
- "Arius was executed" / "burned" / "excommunicated and killed." → He was condemned and sent into exile, as a historian a century later reports; later he was recalled. The death penalty in Constantine's letter is for hiding Arius's books, not for Arius.
- "The council ordered Arius's books burned" / "the bishops threatened death." → The book-burning order and its death penalty are Constantine's own letter (H12), not the council's decree. Do not tie the letter to a day of the council; Socrates gives it among the documents after the council, undated.
- "This ended the Arian controversy." → Conflict went on for decades (Jerome on Rimini, 359 by the editor's date; Constantinople, 381).
- "Athanasius led the bishops" / "Athanasius won the debate." → He was there as a deacon, a later historian says, and opposed Arius's friends; he wrote his account years later.
- Eusebius of Caesarea and Eusebius of Nicomedia are two different men. Eusebius of Caesarea signed; Eusebius of Nicomedia was Arius's friend and was exiled and later restored.
- "Eusebius of Nicomedia was exiled with Arius at the council." → Only Socrates says so, and his editor says Socrates gives the wrong reason for it (sheet D4). Say he was exiled and later restored.
- "Eustathius tore up Arius's creed." → Eustathius says "the formulary of Eusebius" was torn up "in the sight of all"; he does not say who tore it, and does not name which Eusebius. Theodoret, later, says it was a statement by Arius's friends.
- "The council met for two months, from 20 May to 25 August." → Say only "in 325"; the days are disputed.
- "Bishops with gouged eyes and burnt hands filled the hall." → Only Theodoret, a century later, says so. If used, name him.
- "Bishops wept / cheered / the emperor was moved." → No feelings beyond what Eusebius writes.
- "A Persian bishop and a Scythian" are Eusebius's words; do not name them.
- Do not give the Antioch council's year as settled (from the re-thread check, 2026-11-17). The editor dates it to 341; he reports, and Hefele rejects, a theory that its canons came from a synod of 332. Say "a later council at Antioch", or "in 341, by the editor's date".
- "At stake was whether the Son is eternal like the Father." alone → Not wrong, but narrower than the stake. Prefer Mark's form: whether the Son is God as the Father is, or a being with a beginning (Heart_Test, 2026-11-18).
- "Later councils met to confirm Nicaea." → They met over newer disputes and named Nicaea's faith first (B1). Do not say they were called for Nicaea's sake.
- The first minute is not spent on witness framing (Heart_Test, 2026-11-18): Eusebius's credentials, the theaters detail and the run-in to Constantine's quote come after the creed and its impact, not before.
