"There was a time when he was not." That, the council's own letter said, was what Arius, a priest of Alexandria, taught about the Son. It charged him with more: that the Son came from nothing, and that he is "a creature". At stake was whether the Son is God as the Father is, or a being with a beginning.

In 325 the emperor Constantine called bishops to Nicaea. They answered with a creed that calls the Son "begotten, not made", of one being with the Father, and curses anyone who says there was a time when he was not. Arius and two bishops were condemned. Later councils set this creed first, yet for decades it divided the church.

Two troubles had reached the emperor. Eusebius, a bishop who was there and admired the emperor, writes that in Egypt bishops clashed with bishops in every city, and the churches kept Easter on different days. When his letter to Alexandria failed, Constantine called the bishops to Nicaea, in today's Turkey, and let them travel by the imperial post, with horses. More than 250 came by Eusebius's count, though other witnesses gave higher numbers. The emperor told them, as Eusebius reports it: "For I count civil strife in the church of God worse than any war or fierce battle."

Three men who were there tell the debate, each differently. Eusebius says the emperor asked for one word to be added: homoousios, "of one being". Athanasius, who was there as a deacon and later wrote in the council's defense, says Arius's friends read the bishops' words, like "from God", as true of every creature too. They were caught "whispering ... and winking". A third, who also opposed Arius, says a written statement was torn up "in the sight of all".

All but a few signed the creed, and the emperor backed its sentence with force. He sent Arius into exile, a historian a century later says. Constantine's own letter made hiding a book by Arius, rather than burning it, a crime punished by death.

It also settled other business. In Egypt, a leader who had ordained clergy of his own kept his title but could ordain no one. Easter would fall on one day everywhere. And twenty rules, called canons, set the order of the church. They ran from how a bishop is made to mercy for those who had fallen away without being forced.

In 381 a council at Constantinople, meeting over newer heresies, ruled that the faith of the 318 fathers, its name for Nicaea, "shall not be set aside". In 451 Chalcedon called it "pre-eminent". But it did not hold at once. Arius was recalled, and the word "of one being" divided those who held it from those who would not.

The rules traveled as written law, and a Persian synod, a modern scholar reports, swore to accept them. About ninety years after Nicaea, the bishops of Africa, meeting at Carthage, found a disputed rule missing from their copy, and wrote east for exact copies. Their own copy held the creed and the twenty rules, and they read it aloud as law. One of their own bishops had sat at Nicaea and carried it home.
