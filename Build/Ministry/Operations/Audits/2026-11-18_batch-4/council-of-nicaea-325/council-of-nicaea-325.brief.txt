"We are persecuted because we said, 'The Son has a beginning, but God has no beginning.'" Arius, a priest of Alexandria, wrote this in a letter before the council, after his bishop had driven him out. The council's own letter charged him with more: that the Son came from nothing, that "there was a time when he was not", and that he is "a creature". At stake was whether the Son is God as the Father is, or a being with a beginning.

In 325 the emperor Constantine called bishops to Nicaea, in today's Turkey. They answered with a creed that calls the Son "begotten, not made", of one being with the Father, and curses anyone who says there was a time when he was not. Arius and two bishops were condemned, and the emperor sent Arius into exile, a historian a century later says. Later councils set this creed first, yet for decades it divided the church.

Eusebius, a bishop who was there and admired the emperor, writes that in Egypt bishops clashed with bishops in every city, while the churches kept Easter on different days. When his letter to Alexandria failed, Constantine called the bishops and lent them the public post. More than 250 came by Eusebius's count, though other witnesses gave higher numbers. The emperor told them, as Eusebius reports it: "For I count civil strife in the church of God worse than any war or fierce battle."

Three men who were there tell the debate differently. Eusebius says the emperor asked for one word to be added: homoousios, "of one being". Athanasius, who was there as a deacon and later wrote in the council's defense, says Arius's friends read the bishops' words, like "from God", as true of every creature too, and were caught "whispering ... and winking". Eustathius, who like Athanasius opposed Arius, says a written statement was torn up "in the sight of all".

All but a few signed the creed, and the emperor backed its sentence with force. A later historian says he ordered banishment for any who resisted, and Constantine's own letter made hiding a book by Arius, rather than burning it, a crime punished by death.

The council also settled other business. Melitius, who had ordained clergy of his own in Egypt, kept his title but could ordain no one. Easter would fall on one day everywhere. And twenty rules, called canons, set the order of the church, from how a bishop is made to mercy for those who had given way under persecution without being forced.

In 381 a council at Constantinople, meeting over newer heresies, ruled that the faith of the 318 fathers, its name for Nicaea, "shall not be set aside". In 451 Chalcedon called it "pre-eminent". But it did not hold at once. Arius was recalled, and of a council in 359 Jerome wrote, "the whole world groaned, and was astonished to find itself Arian."

The rules traveled as written law, and a Persian synod, by its own Syriac record, swore to accept them. About ninety years after Nicaea, the bishops of Carthage read aloud the creed and the twenty rules from the copy their bishop Caecilian had carried home. When a disputed rule was missing, they wrote east for exact copies.
