# Outline: Antony the Great

Sheet references (S, E, I, L, D, G, A) point to `Source_Sheet.md` in this folder, which gives each file and line.

- **The heart of their life:** Antony heard one Gospel reading as spoken to him, gave away his inheritance, and went ever further into the Egyptian desert to live by prayer, work with his hands, and fasting (E2, E3, S7, E6–E8). Documented as the *Life*'s account. Others came after him; he directed them "as a father", and in the *Life*'s Greek "the desert became a city of monks" (E8, E9). Documented as the *Life*'s account. The *Life* written soon after his death was sent abroad as "a sufficient pattern" for monks, soon put into Latin, and changed lives far from Egypt: two court officials at Trier, and Augustine in Milan (I1a, I1c, I2). Documented: the *Life*'s own purpose, the Latin version, and Augustine's own account. Gregory of Nazianzus, about twenty-five years on, called it the laws of the monastic life set out as a story (A1; Documented as Gregory's words). A historian writing nearly a century later says his disciples flourished in Egypt, Libya, Palestine, Syria and Arabia (I3b; Widely Accepted as Sozomen's report). The book that tells his story is also the book that carried his way of life east and west.

- **Length:** stretch toward 3 minutes, about 440 words. Reason: a major figure (census marks him major, CEN 88288), and the heart is a spread across the Roman world that needs room (I1, I2, I3). The record of his own words is thin (G1), so the life itself stays tight. The first minute (about 150 words) must carry all three parts of the heart: the turn, the followers he guided, and the book that carried him.

- **Setting:** Egypt, its villages and then its desert (S1, S2, E7, E11). Plain words: "a village in Egypt", "the Egyptian desert". Time: the three-hundreds; he lived on into the time of the emperor Constantine and his sons (S4, E16), who wrote to him late in life. Tradition: "the desert monks of Egypt", the men and women later called "the Desert Fathers and Mothers" (S6). No birth year as fact (D5); "the first monk" is not said (D3).

- **Shape:** Monk or ascetic.

- **Openings:**
  1. **Chosen.** A young man walks into church thinking of the apostles who left everything, and hears the Gospel read "as though ... the passage had been read on his account" (E2). Tell it in about 40 words, then go straight on: the desert, the followers, the book.
  2. In reserve: twenty years walled in a ruined fort; friends force the door, and others follow him out into the desert (E8). Usable if the writer prefers to open on the desert filling and give the reading in the next sentences, still inside the first minute.

- **Story beats** (beats 1–3 are the first minute, about 150 words; beats 4–7 deepen it):
  1. **The reading** (about 40 words). Less than six months after his Christian parents died, on his way to church, he was thinking of the apostles who left all to follow Jesus. Inside, he heard: "go and sell that thou hast and give to the poor." He took it as meant for him. He gave his family's fields to the villagers and sold the rest for the poor (E1, E2). Documented as the *Life*'s account.
  2. **The desert, and the many who followed** (about 50 words). He took up a life of training, like an athlete's: work with his hands, prayer, bread and salt once a day (S7). He went further out, in the end walling himself into a deserted fort for nearly twenty years. When many wanted to live as he did, friends forced the door. He persuaded many to take up the solitary life, and he directed them "as a father". In the *Life*'s Greek, "the desert became a city of monks" (E7, E8, E9; rendered from VAG 70548–70549; not the 1892 "colonised", whose modern sense misleads). Documented as the *Life*'s account. "Discipline" = a life of training (S7).
  3. **The book that carried him** (about 60 words). Soon after he died, a *Life* of him was written, long credited to Athanasius, bishop of Alexandria (D1). It was written for monks abroad, as "a sufficient pattern of discipline" (I1a), and it was soon in Latin (I1c). The *Life* itself asks how a man hidden on a mountain came to be heard of in Spain, Gaul, Rome and Africa (I1b: the *Life*'s own claim). Gregory of Nazianzus called it the laws of the monastic life set out as a story (A1). Point ahead: it would reach Augustine in Milan (I2). Documented as each witness's words.
  4. **What the desert held.** Before him there were hermits only near the villages; he learned from an old hermit in the next village and from every good man he heard of (S5, E4). He left his sister with trusted women who had vowed to stay unmarried, and gave away the last of his goods (E3). He shut himself first in a tomb; the *Life* says he fought demons there, and that is kept in its voice: "the *Life* tells how..." (E6). When the door of the fort was forced, he came out, says the *Life*, neither fat nor wasted, the same as before (E8). Documented as the *Life*'s account; the demons are a wonder in the witness's voice.
  5. **In the city of the martyrs.** When persecution came under the emperor Maximinus, he went to Alexandria to serve Christians held in prisons and mines for their faith. He wanted to be martyred but would not hand himself in. When monks were banned from the court, he washed his cloak and stood in plain view of the governor (E10). Documented as the *Life*'s account. "Confessors" = Christians imprisoned or sent to the mines for the faith.
  6. **The inner mountain.** Crowds and healings drove him three days into the desert, to a mountain with a spring and a few palms. He grew his own grain so as to burden no one, and wove baskets to give visitors in return for what they brought him. When people came for healing, he told them it was not his but the Lord's (E11, E12). Emperors wrote to him; he told his monks not to be amazed, since an emperor is a man (Q1, E16). When a commander begged him to stay, he gave the image of the fish (Q2, E17). Documented as the *Life*'s account.
  7. **Defending the faith; the hidden grave.** DEFENDED: when Arians claimed he agreed with them, he came down to Alexandria and taught that the Son of God was not created (E14; the Arians taught that the Son was created). Near a hundred and five, he asked his two companions to bury him secretly, so no one would keep his body in a house as the Egyptians did their holy dead. He left his sheepskins to two bishops, Athanasius and Serapion (E19, E20, E21). Documented as the *Life*'s account.

- **What came of him** (ranked heaviest first; items 1 and 3 are pulled forward into beat 3, and told in full here):
  1. **The book became the pattern.** Written for monks abroad as "a sufficient pattern" (I1a); "read these words ... to the rest of the brethren that they may learn what the life of monks ought to be" (I1a, VA §94). Soon in Latin, by Evagrius of Antioch (I1c). Gregory of Nazianzus: the laws of the monastic life, in the form of a story (A1). Spain, Gaul, Rome and Africa: the *Life*'s claim (I1b).
  2. **Augustine.** At Trier, two officials of the emperor's court found the *Life* in a monks' cottage, read it, and left the court that day to serve God (I2). Pontitianus, a fellow official who was at Trier that day, told the story to Augustine in Milan. In a garden there, in tears, Augustine remembered Antony and that Gospel reading. He opened Paul's letters, read, and his doubt was gone (I2, Q3; AUG 13125–13134). End here, or on the reach in item 1.
  3. **The desert filled, and the way spread east.** Disciples in Egypt, Libya, Palestine, Syria and Arabia (Sozomen, nearly a century later, I3b). Hilarion came from Palestine, stayed about two months, went home and chose "to begin as Antony had begun" (Jerome, about thirty-five years later, I3c).
  4. DEFENDED: his stand for Nicaea's faith and for his bishop (I4). Told in beat 7; one clause at most here.

- **Quotes (3 at most):**
  - **Q1** — job: shows his plain, unimpressed voice and what he thought truly amazing. Source: VA §81, Greek VAG 77108–77112; printed English NP4 33193–33195: "Do not be astonished if an emperor writes to us, for he is a man; but rather wonder that God wrote the Law for men and has spoken to us through His own Son." Greek: "Μὴ θαυμάζετε, εἰ γράφει βασιλεὺς πρὸς ἡμᾶς, ἄνθρωπος γάρ ἐστιν· ἀλλὰ μᾶλλον θαυμάζετε, ὅτι ὁ Θεὸς τὸν νόμον ἀνθρώποις ἔγραψε, καὶ διὰ τοῦ ἰδίου Υἱοῦ λελάληκεν ἡμῖν." (OCR corrected as in E16). **Rendering (from the Greek):** "Do not be amazed if an emperor writes to us, for he is a man. Be amazed instead that God wrote the Law for human beings. And he has spoken to us through his own Son."
  - **Q2** — job: why he would not stay among people; the image his tradition is remembered by. Source: VA §85, Greek VAG 77522–77527 and 77605; printed English NP4 33352–33355: "Fishes, if they remain long on dry land, die. And so monks lose their strength if they loiter among you and spend their time with you. Wherefore as fish must hurry to the sea, so must we hasten to the mountain. Lest haply if we delay we forget the things within us." Greek as in E17. **Rendering (from the Greek):** "Fish that linger on dry land die. In the same way, monks who linger with you and spend their days among you lose their strength. So, as the fish must hurry to the sea, we must hurry to the mountain. If we linger, we may forget what is within." (The last clause is read across a column break in the OCR, E17.)
  - **Q3** — job: the impact, in Augustine's own words. Source: Augustine, *Confessions* VIII.12.29, Latin AUGL 13470–13476, 13501; printed English AUG 13117–13124 (Pilkington). Latin: "audieram enim de Antonio, quod ex euangelica lectione, cui forte superuenerat, admonitus fuerit, tamquam sibi diceretur quod legebatur: uade, uende omnia, quae habes, da pauperibus et habebis thesaurum in caelis; et ueni, sequere me, et tali oraculo confestim ad te esse conuersum." **Rendering (from the Latin):** "For I had heard this of Antony. He had come in by chance during the Gospel reading, and was urged on by it. It was as though the words read were spoken to him. 'Go, sell all that you have, give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven. And come, follow me.' By such a divine word he was at once turned to you." ("You" is God; Augustine's book is addressed to God. The writer should make that clear in the sentence that introduces it. "Divine word" renders *oraculum*; "oracle" would mislead.)

- **Connection to carry into the story:** a book passed his way of life on. The *Life* was written to be read to monks abroad as a pattern (I1a, VA §94), put into Latin (I1c), and found at Trier by two court officials who left the court that day (I2). Their colleague told the story to Augustine, who in a Milan garden remembered how Antony had taken one Gospel reading as spoken to him, and then took up a reading of his own (I2, Q3). What the listener must understand: Augustine never met Antony. He met him through the book and through a story told by someone who knew the book. The same act, hearing Scripture as addressed to oneself, passes from Antony to Augustine. Let the story show the chain (book, officials, Pontitianus, Augustine), not state that it matters.

- **Who tells it:**
  - The *Life of Antony*: written within a few years of his death (editor's dating, 356–362), long credited to Athanasius, bishop of Alexandria, who says he saw him many times (D1). It is a book of praise and full of wonders. Line to say aloud, inside the first minute (beat 3): "Most of what we know comes from one book, a Life written soon after his death, which the church has long credited to Athanasius, bishop of Alexandria." Turn it at once from source to subject: this book is also what carried him abroad.
  - Augustine, *Confessions*: about forty years after Antony's death, telling his own experience. He calls Antony's deeds "so recent, and almost in our own" times (AUG 12565–12566). If that is used, credit it to him.
  - Gregory of Nazianzus (about twenty-five years later, a sermon on Athanasius), Jerome (about thirty-five years later) and Sozomen (about ninety years later): later writers. Say "a historian writing nearly a century later" for Sozomen.
  - How-we-know gets a sentence or two; the doubt is not the story.

- **Do not say:**
  - (round-two review, 2026-11-17) Do not drop his stand against the Arians (beat 7): it is what he defended.
  - (Mark's source question, 2026-11-17) Do not attach "in the time of the emperor Constantine and his sons" to his youth or the church visit. Those rulers wrote to him late in life (VA §81). Say instead that he "lived on into the time of the emperor Constantine and his sons".
  - (heart test, 2026-11-18) Do not bring in the *Life* only as a source ("his story is told in one book"). In the first minute it is also the book that carried his way of life abroad (I1a–c).
  - (heart test, 2026-11-18) Do not reduce the followers to "many followed". Say he persuaded many to take up the life and directed them "as a father" (E8, E9).
  - (heart test, 2026-11-18) Do not leave out the spread east. Say disciples in Palestine, Syria and Arabia, credited to "a historian writing nearly a century later" (I3b); Hilarion is Jerome's account (I3c).
  - (heart test, 2026-11-18) Do not spend the first minute on the emperors' letters or the early biography; they come after the heart (beats 4, 6).
  - Do not say his fame reached Spain, Gaul, Rome and Africa as a fact; it is the *Life*'s own claim: "the *Life* itself asks how..." (I1b).
  - Do not say "within twenty years" as a fact; the date of the Latin is an editor's (I1c). Say "soon put into Latin".
  - Do not give Robertson's "kindled the flame of monastic aspirations ... all over the West" as any ancient writer's view; it is the 1892 editor's judgment (I1e), card only. Likewise "the founder of asceticism" in the note to Gregory is the editor's (A1).
  - Do not say Athanasius wrote the *Life* as settled. Say "long credited to Athanasius" (D1). Do not say the writer was Antony's attendant (D1, Contested).
  - Do not say "convent"; say "trusted women who had vowed to stay unmarried" (E3).
  - Do not say "the desert was colonised"; say "the desert became a city of monks" (E8).
  - Do not say the officials at Trier, or Augustine, became monks because of Antony alone; the record gives the book and the story as what moved them, in Augustine's account (I2).

- **Leave out:**
  - "The first monk" or "father of all monks" as fact: the *Life* shows hermits before him, and Jerome backs Paul of Thebes (D3).
  - Exact birth or death years, and the village name Coma, unless clearly marked as later report (D4, D5).
  - The long run of demon fights, healings and foreknowledge (E5, E6, E12; §§57–66, 82, 86): one at most, and only in the *Life*'s voice. The beast visions and the hyenas are side stories.
  - Balacius and the letter he spat on: the two accounts disagree (D2).
  - "He could not read or write" as flat fact (D7). Say, with the *Life*, that he had not learned letters and needed an interpreter for Greek.
  - The seven Coptic letters as texts we have: none is held (G1, I6).
  - The *Paradise* sayings: compiled centuries later (I5).
  - Paul of Thebes and Antony's visit to him: Jerome's later story (L11).
  - Burial-custom detail beyond one sentence (E20): it explains the hidden grave, nothing more.
