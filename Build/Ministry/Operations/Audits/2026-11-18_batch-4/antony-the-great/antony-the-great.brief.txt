A young man named Antony, in a village in Egypt, was walking to church, thinking of the apostles who left everything to follow Jesus. Inside, he heard the Gospel tell a man to sell what he had and give to the poor, and he took it as spoken to him. He gave his family's fields to the villagers and sold the rest for the poor.

He took up a life of training, like an athlete's, with work, prayer, and bread and salt once a day. He went ever further into the Egyptian desert, and in the end walled himself into a deserted fort for nearly twenty years. When many wanted to live as he did, his friends forced the door. He persuaded many to take up the solitary life, and he directed them as a father, until the desert became a city of monks.

Most of what we know comes from one book, a Life written soon after his death, which the church has long credited to Athanasius, bishop of Alexandria. That book, from the middle of the three-hundreds, also carried him abroad. It was written to be read to monks in other lands, as a pattern for their life, and it was soon put into Latin. Later in the same century, Gregory of Nazianzus called it the laws of the monastic life, set out as a story.

When the emperor Maximinus persecuted the church, Antony went to Alexandria to serve Christians held in prisons and mines. He wanted to be martyred, and though he would not hand himself in, he stood in plain view of the governor.

As crowds came to him for healing, he moved three days further into the desert, to a mountain. He lived on into the time of the emperor Constantine and his sons. When Arians claimed he agreed with them, he came down to Alexandria and taught that the Son of God was not created.

A historian writing nearly a century later says his disciples flourished in Egypt, Libya, Palestine, Syria and Arabia. And at Trier, two officials of the emperor's court found the Life in a monks' cottage, read it, and resolved that very hour to leave the court and serve God. A fellow official who was there that day told the story to Augustine in Milan. Augustine never met Antony, but in a garden there, in tears, he remembered him. Later he wrote of it in a book addressed to God.

"For I had heard this of Antony. He had come in by chance during the Gospel reading, and was urged on by it. It was as though the words read were spoken to him. 'Go, sell all that you have, give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven. And come, follow me.' By such a divine word he was at once turned to you."

Then Augustine opened Paul's letters and read, and his doubt was gone.
