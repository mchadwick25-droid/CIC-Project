"There is a certain Marcion, from Pontus. Even now he is still teaching the people he has won over. He teaches them to believe in some other god, greater than the Creator." Justin, a Christian opponent, wrote this to the emperor while Marcion lived, in the middle of the second century. Marcion came from Pontus, on the Black Sea, a shipmaster as Tertullian calls him, and he taught at Rome. Everything we know of him comes from the men who opposed him.

Irenaeus, a generation later, agrees that for Marcion the God of the law of Moses and the prophets, who made the world, was not the Father of Jesus. "Separating the law from the gospel is Marcion's own work, and his chief one," Tertullian writes of his book, the Antitheses, which is now lost.

Marcion kept his own Scripture, one Gospel and Paul's letters, which his opponents say he cut down from Luke and Paul. His claim, in Tertullian's report, was that "the defenders of Judaism" had added to the Gospel and he was restoring it. Justin says that, with the demons' help, he had led many "in every race of people", and the church's writers answered him in book after book.

At first, Tertullian says, Marcion believed as the church at Rome did, and gave it two hundred sesterces in the first warmth of his faith. Then Rome expelled him more than once, Tertullian says, naming Valentinus with him, for a restless curiosity that spread to others, and his gift was cast out with him.

Still his churches spread. Tertullian says with scorn that the Marcionites make churches as wasps make combs. Two centuries later, Epiphanius still found them from Rome and Italy through Egypt, Syria and Cyprus, and even in Persia.

Against him, writer after writer argued that the God who made the world is the Father of Jesus. In a book that Irenaeus quotes, Justin said he would not have believed the Lord Himself if He had announced any God but our maker. Tertullian's own Against Marcion ran to five books. Irenaeus argued that the Gospel is fourfold, and he named Marcion among those who make it fewer.

Irenaeus tells, too, of a single meeting between Marcion and Polycarp, the bishop of Smyrna. Marcion asked, "Do you know me?" Polycarp answered, "I do know you: the firstborn of Satan."
