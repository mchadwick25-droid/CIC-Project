"But whatever kind of man he was, this much about him we hold as certain. He left successors known for great self-control, for the love of God, and for life under a rule." So wrote Bede, an English monk, more than a century after Columba died.

Born to a noble family in Ireland early in the sixth century, Columba founded a monastery at Durrow. In about 563 he sailed to Britain with twelve disciples "to make a pilgrimage for Christ", leaving home for good. On Iona, a small island off what is now Scotland, he founded a monastery.

From those two houses, Bede says, his disciples founded many more in Britain and Ireland, and Iona held first place among them. In Bede's day its abbot was a priest, yet even the bishops were under him. This was, Bede says, "according to the example of their first teacher", a priest and monk, not a bishop.

Bede says, too, that Columba turned the northern Picts to the faith. In 635, about forty years after his death, King Oswald of Northumbria asked the Irish for a bishop, and Iona sent its monk Aidan to Lindisfarne. Irish monks who followed him taught the English the monks' rule of life.

Our fullest account comes from Adomnán, abbot of Iona about a hundred years later, who wrote to honor him. He tells how Brude, king of the Picts, first kept his gates shut against Columba and then honored him for life, though he never says the king was baptized.

On a Saturday in 597, by the usual reckoning, the old and weary abbot was copying the Psalms. He stopped at a verse promising that those who seek the Lord will lack no good thing, and named the disciple who would succeed him to finish the page. "Here I must stop, at the bottom of this page. Let Baithene write what comes next." That night he knelt at the altar, and when his attendant Diormit raised his right hand, Columba moved it as far as he could to bless his monks, and died.

His heirs kept Easter by an older Irish table, so it could fall on a different Sunday from Rome's. At the synod of Whitby in 664, Colman, a bishop from Iona's line, defended "our most reverend Father Columba and his successors". The king ruled for Rome, and Colman went back to the Irish with those who would not change. Iona held out until about 716, when the English monk Egbert brought it to the Roman date, correcting what Bede calls "their error".
