# Outline: Columba (Colum Cille)

Sheet items (S, E, I, D, A) are in `Source_Sheet.md`, which gives the file and line for each. A-items are under "Added for batch 4".

- **The heart of their life:** Columba, an Irish monk, left Ireland for good as a pilgrim for Christ and founded a monastery on the small island of Iona (S6, E4, E5; Documented as Adomnán's and Bede's statements; year Contested, D1). From his two houses, Durrow in Ireland and Iona, many more grew in Britain and Ireland, and Iona was the head of them all. Its abbot was a priest, and even bishops were under him, "according to the example of their first teacher", who was a priest and monk, not a bishop (I1, S7, A2, A3; Documented as Bede's account of Iona in his own day, about 130 years later). From Iona the faith went out: Bede says Columba himself turned the northern Picts to the faith (E8, D6; Bede's claim, Contested in scope), and in 635 his monastery sent Aidan to the English, who set up on Lindisfarne and taught the English the monks' way of life (I2, A4; Documented as Bede's account; the monastery's act, not his own). His heirs held to his way of keeping Easter, defended it at Whitby in 664 and lost, and Iona kept it until about 716 (I3, I4, A5; Documented as Bede's account).
- **Length:** about 2½ minutes (about 375 words). Why: a major figure, with two independent later witnesses (Adomnán and Bede) and the annals, and a heart with three parts (the founding, the family of houses, the mission outward) that must all land in the first minute, about the first 150 words. The rest is room for how he lived and died, for Whitby, and for one plain line on how late the record is. Not 3: no word of his own and no witness from his lifetime is held, so nothing more should be built.
- **Setting:** the sixth century (S4); Ireland, then Iona, a small island off northern Britain, in what is now Scotland (S3; "what is now Scotland" Widely Accepted, editor's note only); the Irish monastic church, which was led by abbots rather than city bishops (S5, S7). Plain spoken words: "the sixth century", "Ireland", "the island of Iona, off the coast of what is now Scotland", "the monasteries of Ireland".
- **Shape:** Founder or leader. Trap: titles in place of acts. Keep to what he built on Iona, how it lived, and what outlasted him. The heart is what Iona became, not only that he founded it.
- **Openings:**
  1. **Chosen.** Bede's question, which leads straight into the heart. Bede, an English monk writing more than a century later, says he is not sure what kind of man Columba was, but he is sure of the men Columba left behind (Quote 1; I6; BEDL 18882–18885, BEDE 5866–5869). The next sentences say who he was, what he founded, and what those heirs ran: the family of houses led from Iona.
  2. Not used as the opening this time: the old abbot copying the Psalms on his last day and leaving the rest of the page to Baithene (E12; AD 10783–10789). It moves to the death beat, where it also belongs.

**Story beats** (beats 1 to 3 are the first minute, about 150 words; beats 4 to 7 deepen it)

1. **Who he was, and the founding.** Born to a noble family in Ireland, early in the sixth century (S2, D5; Documented as Adomnán's statement; year Inferential-Thin). Before he left Ireland he had founded Durrow, "the Field of Oaks" (E2; Documented, Bede). In his forty-second year, two years after a great battle in Ireland, he sailed to Britain with twelve disciples, "to make a pilgrimage for Christ". Pilgrimage here means leaving home for good for God's sake (E4, S6; Documented; gloss Widely Accepted). The year is about 563 by the usual reckoning; the annals say 562 and Bede 565 (D1, Contested). He founded a monastery on Iona; Bede says the Picts gave him the island, the Irish annals a king named Conall (E5, D4; founding Documented, giver Contested).
2. **What Iona became** (What came of them 1, pulled forward). Bede: many monasteries in Britain and Ireland grew from his two houses through his disciples, and Iona, where his body lies, held first place among them all (I1, A2). For a long time it was the chief of almost all the houses of the northern Irish and of all those of the Picts (A3). Iona's abbot was a priest, and the whole province, even its bishops, were under him, "according to the example of their first teacher" (S7). Bede marks this as unusual, "contrary to the usual method". Documented as Bede's account of his own day.
3. **The faith carried out from it** (What came of them 2, pulled forward). Bede says Columba came to preach to the northern Picts and turned that nation to the faith (E8, I7; Bede's claim, Contested in scope, D6). About forty years after his death, in 635, King Oswald of Northumbria asked the Irish for a bishop, and Iona sent its monk Aidan, who set up on the tidal island of Lindisfarne. Monks from the Irish houses preached, churches and monasteries were built, and the English were taught the monks' rule of life (I2, A4; Documented as Bede's account). This is his monastery's act, a generation on, not his own.
4. **How Iona lived.** Adomnán says he never let an hour pass without prayer, reading, writing or manual work, and that he slept on bare rock with a stone for a pillow. His monks copied the Psalms (E6, E7; Documented as Adomnán's portrait, written in praise by his successor).
5. **Among the Picts, in Adomnán's telling.** He went to Brude, king of the Picts, in his fortress. Adomnán says the king first kept his gates shut against him, then honoured him for the rest of his life. He preached through an interpreter: Adomnán tells of a countryman baptized "with all his household", and of an old chief. Adomnán never says the king was baptized (E8, E9, D6; Documented as Adomnán's account). This beat is where the writer sets Adomnán's smaller picture beside Bede's claim in beat 3.
6. **His last day.** A Saturday, in 597 by the usual reckoning (D2; Widely Accepted, annals differ; the Irish annals give 9 June). Old and weary, he blesses the barn and says his monks will have enough grain for the year. He copies the Psalms and stops at "they that seek the Lord shall not want any good thing", leaving the rest of the page to his disciple Baithene, who succeeded him (Quote 2; E12; Documented as Adomnán's account).
7. **His death.** That night he gave his last words for the monks, to love one another and keep peace; only his attendant Diormit heard them (Quote 3; E13). At the midnight bell he ran ahead into the church and knelt at the altar. Diormit found him lying there and held his head, then raised his right hand, and Columba moved it as much as he could to bless the monks, and died (E14; Documented as Adomnán's account).

**What came of them** (about a third of the story; heaviest first. Items 1 and 2 are told in beats 2 and 3, inside the first minute; items 3 and 4 close the story)

1. **A family of monasteries, led from Iona.** Bede: many houses in Britain and Ireland grew from his, and Iona was "the principal of them all"; its abbot, a priest, was over even bishops, "according to the example of their first teacher". Bede calls them "the monasteries of Columba". Baithene succeeded him, and later abbots were called his heirs (I1, S7, A1, A2, A3). Told in beat 2.
2. **The English.** In 635 Iona sent Aidan, "a monk of the island called Hii" (Iona), to Northumbria; he set up on Lindisfarne, and Irish monks taught the English their rule (I2, A4). Told in beat 3.
3. **Defended at Whitby, 664, and lost.** DEFENDED. His heirs reckoned the date of Easter by an older Irish table, so their Easter could fall on a different Sunday from Rome's. At the synod of Whitby, Colman, the bishop from Iona's line, defended "our most reverend Father Columba and his successors". Wilfrid, for the Roman side, asked whether Columba could be put before Peter. The king ruled for Rome, and Colman went home to the Irish with those who would not change. Iona itself kept its custom until about 716, when the English monk Egbert brought it to the Roman date; Bede calls this correcting "their error" (I3, I4, A5; Documented as Bede's account; Bede writes for the side that won).
4. **His memory.** About a century after him, Iona's ninth abbot, Adomnán, wrote his life, and claimed his name had reached Spain, Gaul, Italy and Rome itself (I5; Documented as Adomnán's claim; the reach Inferential-Thin). End here, on the Whitby defence, or on his death.

**Quotes** (3)

1. Verbatim (BEDL 18882–18885, Bede's Latin, Plummer 1896): "Uerum qualiscumque fuerit ipse, nos hoc de illo certum tenemus, quia reliquit successores magna continentia ac diuino amore regularique institutione insignes" (Giles's English, BEDE 5866–5869: "But whatsoever he was himself, this we know for certain, that he left successors renowned for their continency, their love of God, and observance of monastic rules."). Rendering (from the Latin): "But whatever kind of man he was, this much about him we hold as certain. He left successors known for great self-control, for the love of God, and for life under a rule." Job: the opening. An outsider's doubt about the man that turns the story at once to what he left, the heart.
2. Verbatim (AD 10787–10789, Huyshe's English, 1905): ""Here", he says, " I must stop at the foot of this page, and what follows let Baithene write."" Rendering (from Huyshe's printed English; the Latin is not held): "Here I must stop, at the bottom of this page. Let Baithene write what comes next." Job: the handing on of his work, in the death beat.
3. Verbatim (AD 10815–10817, Huyshe's English): "These my last words I commend to you, O my sons, that ye have mutual and unfeigned charity among yourselves, with peace" (the sentence goes on with a promise; this is the first clause). Rendering (from Huyshe's English): "My sons, I entrust these last words to you. Love one another without pretence, and live in peace." ("Charity" is rendered "love": its modern sense misleads.) Job: his own voice at the end, as Adomnán reports it, and the one thing he asked them to keep. Optional if words run short.

**Connection to carry into the story:** what passed from Columba was a house and a way of life that others copied and obeyed. His disciples founded many monasteries from Durrow and Iona, and Iona ruled them, its abbot over bishops because their first teacher had been a priest and monk (I1, S7, A2). The listener must understand that this is why Aidan matters: Aidan was not a stray missionary but a monk sent by Iona, so the faith and the monastic rule the English received at Lindisfarne came from Columba's house (I2, A4). And it is why Whitby matters: Colman was defending the custom of Columba and his heirs, and losing meant the English church turned from Iona's way to Rome's (I3, A5). Tell it through these acts, not with a "this matters because" sentence.

**Links**

- Baithene: his disciple; succeeded him as abbot; finished his page.
- Diormit: his attendant; heard his last words.
- Brude (Bridius), king of the Picts: met him; honoured him.
- Finnian: his teacher.
- Aidan of Lindisfarne: monk of his monastery; sent by it to the English.
- Colman: defended his custom at Whitby.
- Wilfrid: argued against his heirs' custom at Whitby.
- Egbert: brought Iona to the Roman Easter.
- Adomnán: wrote his life.
- Bede: wrote of him.

**Who tells it**

- Adomnán, ninth abbot of Iona, wrote about a hundred years after Columba died, from older writings and the memories of old monks. He was Columba's successor and writes to honour him; his book is arranged by kinds of wonder (D8). Say aloud, at beat 4 or 5: "Our fullest account comes from Adomnán, abbot of Iona about a hundred years later."
- Bede, an English monk, finished his history in 731, more than a century later, and wrote as a critic of Iona's Easter. He is the witness for the whole heart: the family of houses, the abbot over bishops, the Picts, Aidan and Whitby. Name him in the opening, where his doubt is the hook.
- The Irish and Welsh annals: year-by-year records kept and copied over centuries; their dates differ by a few years.
- No writing by Columba and no witness from his own lifetime is held. How-we-know gets a sentence or two; the doubt is not the story.

**Do not say**

- The gates opening "of their own accord" is Adomnán's wonder: if it is used, say "Adomnán says the king kept his gates shut against him until they opened", never as the narrator's fact. (From the round-two review, 2026-11-17.)
- Not "he converted the Picts" or "he brought the faith to the Picts" as fact. Say "Bede says he turned the northern Picts to the faith", and set beside it what Adomnán shows: a household, an old chief, a king who honoured him. (Heart test, 2026-11-18.)
- Not "Columba sent Aidan" or "Columba brought the faith to the English". Columba had been dead almost forty years. Say "his monastery sent Aidan". (Heart test, 2026-11-18.)
- Not "Oswald asked Iona for a bishop". Bede says he sent to "the elders of the Scots", the Irish. Say "asked the Irish for a bishop, and Iona sent Aidan". (A4.)
- The abbot over bishops is Bede's account of Iona in his own day, about 130 years on: say "in Bede's day" or "Bede says", not "Columba ruled over bishops". Columba himself was "a priest and monk", not a bishop. Keep "even the bishops". (Heart test, 2026-11-18.)
- Keep Bede's qualifiers: "almost all" the houses of the northern Irish, and "for a long time", not "all the churches of Ireland and Britain" or "for centuries".
- Not "in 563" as settled. Say "about 563", or "in his forty-second year, two years after a great battle in Ireland". The annals give 562 and Bede 565. (Heart test, 2026-11-18.)
- Not "the Picts gave him Iona" or "King Conall gave him Iona" alone. Say "Bede says the Picts gave him the island; the Irish annals say a king named Conall did", or simply "he founded a monastery on Iona". (Heart test, 2026-11-18.)
- Not "he fled Ireland after a battle", "exile" or "penance". The only held reason is "a pilgrimage for Christ"; the battle only dates the voyage.
- Not "the family of houses ruled the Irish church". Bede says Iona "had the direction of their people", for "almost all" the houses of the northern Irish and all those of the Picts (A3).
- Not "the Celtic Church", "Insular", or "from Scotland". He came from Ireland.
- At Whitby, not "the two sides agreed" or "Iona was persuaded". The king ruled against Columba's heirs; Colman left with those who would not change; Iona held out about fifty more years. "Error" is Bede's word: credit it to him.
- Not "he wrote the Book of Kells" or any other book. No book of his is held.

**Leave out**

- Any reason for his leaving Ireland beyond "a pilgrimage for Christ". Adomnán names the battle of Cooldrevny only to date the voyage. The one held link between the battle and his life is a guess in Huyshe's 1905 note, and it is about the excommunication, not the voyage (E3, E4). The annal that the battle was won "through the prayers of Colum-Cille" (AU 4539–4540) is a side story and doubtful.
- The excommunication at Teltown (E3): real in Adomnán, but its cause is unknown, so it needs more room than it earns here.
- The white horse at his last day, and the gates wonder at Brude's fortress: wonders that took a third of the old first minute. Leave both out this time; if one is wanted, use only the gates, in Adomnán's voice, in beat 5.
- The Book of Lismore's numbers (45 years, twenty bishops and fifty students): fifteenth-century legend (D7).
- The books later credited to him (the Cathach, the Book of Durrow, "three hundred books") and the hymns: later tradition, in Huyshe's introduction only (E7).
- The other wonders: the floating stone, the raised boy, the thunder voice, the angel's book of glass, the visions at his death.
- Aedhan's ordination and the Convention of Drumceatt (E10, E11): side stories.
- Do not join him to Columbanus, a different Irish monk.
