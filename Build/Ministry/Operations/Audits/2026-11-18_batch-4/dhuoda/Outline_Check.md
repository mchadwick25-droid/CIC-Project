## 1. My account of the heart, written before reading `Outline.md`

Dhuoda was a Frankish noblewoman, wife of Bernard, duke of Septimania, kept at Uzès "at my lord's command" while both her sons were away from her: the baby taken to his father before baptism, the elder, William, sent to commend himself to King Charles after the war of 841. From 30 November 841, when William had just turned fifteen, she dictated for him a handbook of Christian life, and worked on it for more than a year (E8–E11, A4). Its own table of seventy-three chapters sets out a course for a young layman at court: love and seek God, faith, hope and love, short and pure prayer, honour for his father, faithful service to his lord, honour for priests, virtues against vices, the Beatitudes, help for the poor, widows, orphans and pilgrims, just judging, prayer for every rank of the Church, for kings and for the dead (A2, T5–T7). She meant it to stand in her place: a mirror in which he would see her and what he owed her, taught with "a heart burning" as no other teacher would, to be shown in turn to his little brother (A1, T4, T8). It also carries her own life: illness, debts, her family's dead, her epitaph. What came of it: it was copied (a Carolingian copy and a 17th-century one) and kept, printed in part from 1677 and whole in 1887, and later named among early works of Christian instruction; it survives as a noblewoman's voice of faith, not a cleric's (census "very few lay voices" as framing only). No held record shows it used between her time and 1677.

**Comparison with the outline's heart:** the same in substance, and the same as Mark's approved heart in `Hearts.md`. Every major contribution is there, with the right confidence levels. The outline adds kings and pilgrims, both held (A2 LV, DH 2478; T7, DH 11364–11366). Beats 1–3 are marked for the first minute and the length line puts the heart in the first 150 words. No difference from the sources.
