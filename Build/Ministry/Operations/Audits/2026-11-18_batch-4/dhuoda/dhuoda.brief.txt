"I see many people in this world who rejoice in their children with them. But I, Dhuoda, see myself kept far away from you, my son William." So begins a handbook of faith that a Frankish noblewoman dictated for her son in November 841, at Uzès in the south of France. William had just turned fifteen, and he had been sent to serve King Charles.

In seventy-three chapters she set out a Christian life for a young man at court. He must love God first and then his father, and pray in short, pure words. He must serve Charles faithfully, help widows, orphans and pilgrims, and judge without taking gifts.

She meant the book to stand in her place. Reading it and praying, she wrote, he could see her, "mind and body, as in a picture in a mirror." Other teachers would teach him more, "but they will not teach on equal terms, with a heart burning in the breast, as I do, your mother." She was a noblewoman, not one of the clergy, and William was to show the book to his little brother when the boy could read.

She was apart from both her sons in a time of war. When the emperor Louis, Charlemagne's son, died in 840, his sons fought over the empire. In March 841 Dhuoda bore a second son. Before he was baptized, her husband Bernard, duke of Septimania, had the baby brought to him, with the bishop of Uzès, while she stayed on at Uzès, as she says, at her lord's command.

Almost all we know of her comes from her own book. The chronicles of her day name her husband and her son, but not her. She writes of illness and says her end is near. She pictures herself as a persistent puppy among the clergy under its master's table, snatching the falling crumbs. And she asks William to have her epitaph carved on her grave, so that passers-by will pray for her.

She worked on the book for more than a year. In 844 the Franks judged Bernard guilty of treason, and on Charles's order he was put to death. In 848 William seized Ampurias and Barcelona by trickery, and in 850 he was captured by greater trickery and killed there. No record tells when or where Dhuoda died.

Her book outlived them all. It was copied and kept, and printed in part in 1677. In 1887 its editor printed it whole, calling it the oldest writing by a woman born in France to reach us. In 1923 two translators of the Catholic catechism named her manual among the works of Christian instruction, to answer the claim that the catechism began with Luther.
