"I see many people in this world who rejoice in their children with them. But I, Dhuoda, see myself kept far away from you, my son William." So begins the handbook of faith that a Frankish noblewoman dictated for her son at Uzès, in the south of France, in November 841. William had just turned fifteen, and he had been sent to serve King Charles.

In seventy-three chapters she set out a Christian life for a young man at court. He must love God first and then his father, and pray in short, pure words. He must serve Charles faithfully, help widows, orphans and pilgrims, and judge without taking gifts.

She meant the book to stand in her place. Reading it and praying, she wrote, he could see her, "mind and body, as in a picture in a mirror." Other teachers would teach him more, "but they will not teach on equal terms, with a heart burning in the breast, as I do, your mother." She was a noblewoman, not one of the clergy, and William was to show the book to his little brother when the boy could read.

When the emperor Louis, Charlemagne's son, died in 840, his sons went to war over the empire. In March 841 Dhuoda bore that second son, and before he was baptized, her husband Bernard, duke of Septimania, had the baby brought to him with the bishop of Uzès. She stayed on at Uzès, as she says, at her lord's command.

Almost all we know of her comes from her own book. The chronicles of her day name her husband and her son, but not her. In it she pictures herself as a persistent puppy among the clergy under its master's table, snatching the falling crumbs. And she asks William to have her epitaph carved on her grave, so that passers-by will pray for her.

She worked on the book for more than a year. In 844 the Franks judged Bernard guilty of treason, and on Charles's order he was put to death. In 848 William seized Ampurias and Barcelona by trickery, and in 850 he was captured by greater trickery and killed there. No record tells when or where Dhuoda died.

Her book outlived them all. It was copied and kept, printed in part in 1677, and printed whole in 1887 by an editor who called it the oldest writing by a woman born in France to reach us. In 1923 two translators of the Catholic catechism named her manual among the works of Christian instruction, to answer the claim that the catechism began with Luther.
