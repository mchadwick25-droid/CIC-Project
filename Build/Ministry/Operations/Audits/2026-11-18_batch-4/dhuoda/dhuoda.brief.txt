"I see many people in this world who rejoice in their children with them. But I, Dhuoda, see myself kept far away from you, my son William." So begins a handbook of faith that a Frankish noblewoman dictated for her son, at Uzès in the south of France. She began it in November 841, when William had just turned fifteen and had been sent to serve King Charles.

In seventy-three chapters she set out a Christian life for a young man at court: love God first and then your father, pray in short, pure words, serve Charles faithfully, help widows, orphans and pilgrims, and judge without taking gifts.

She meant the book to stand in her place. Reading it and praying, she wrote, he could see her, "mind and body, as in a picture in a mirror." Other teachers would teach him more, "but they will not teach on equal terms, with a heart burning in the breast, as I do, your mother." She was a noblewoman, not one of the clergy, and William was to show the book to his little brother when the boy could read.

She was apart from both her sons. In 824 she had married Bernard, duke of Septimania, at the emperor's palace in Aachen. When Louis, Charlemagne's son, died in 840, his sons went to war over the empire. In March 841 Dhuoda bore a second son, but before he was baptized, Bernard had the baby brought to him, with the bishop of Uzès. She did not yet know his name when she wrote. She stayed at Uzès, as she says, at her lord's command, while Bernard sent William to Charles, to commend himself as his sworn man if Charles would grant the lands and offices held in Burgundy.

Almost all we know of her comes from her own book. The chronicles of her day name her husband and her son, but not her. She writes of illness and says her end is near. She had borrowed from Christians and from Jews to support Bernard on the frontier, so that he would not part from her and William. She pictures herself as a persistent puppy among the clergy under its master's table, snatching the falling crumbs. And she asks William to have her epitaph carved on her grave, so that passers-by will pray for her.

She worked on the book for more than a year. In 844 the Franks judged Bernard guilty of treason, and on Charles's order he was put to death. In 848 William seized Ampurias and Barcelona by trickery, and two years later he was captured by greater trickery and killed there. No record tells when or where Dhuoda died.

Her book outlived them all. It was copied and kept, and the scholar Mabillon printed parts of it in 1677. In 1887 Édouard Bondurand printed the whole book, calling it the oldest writing by a woman born in France to reach us. And in 1923, two translators of the Catholic catechism named her manual among the works of Christian instruction, to answer the claim that the catechism began with Luther.
