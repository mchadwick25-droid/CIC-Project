# Outline: Dhuoda

Sheet references (S, E, T, I, D, and A for "Added for batch 4") point to `Source_Sheet.md` in this folder. File short names: DH = Bondurand's 1887 edition of her book; NIT = Nithard; AB = *Annals of Saint-Bertin*; TRE = 1923 Roman Catechism translators; CEN = the world census.

- **The heart of their life:** Dhuoda, a Frankish noblewoman kept apart from both her sons, dictated a handbook of Christian life for her elder son William. She began it on 30 November 841, when he had just turned fifteen and had been sent to serve King Charles, and worked on it for more than a year (E8, E9, E10, E11, A4). Documented. It holds a whole course of faith for a young layman at court, in her own plan of seventy-three chapters: love and seek God; pray in short, pure words; honour his father and serve his lord faithfully; honour priests; set virtues against vices; help the poor, widows, orphans and pilgrims; judge fairly; pray for the Church, for kings and for the dead (A2, T5, T6, T7). Documented. It also holds her own life: her verses, her illness, her debts, her family's dead, and the epitaph she asks him to carve (T9, E13–E17). Documented. She meant the book to stand in her place: a mirror in which he could see her and what he owed her, taught "with a heart burning" as no other teacher would, and passed on to his little brother (A1, T4, T8). Documented. What it changed: it was copied and kept, printed in part in 1677 and whole in 1887 (I1, I2, I4), Documented. It survives as a noblewoman's voice of faith, not one of the clergy's, from the church of Charlemagne's heirs (S7): "not clergy" Documented; "one of very few lay voices" is the census's framing, Widely Accepted at most. No held record shows it read or used between her time and 1677 (sheet section 7).
- **Essence.** Kept apart from her sons, a Frankish noblewoman wrote a handbook of Christian life for her son William, meant as a mirror where he would see her. It survives as the voice of a lay mother, not a cleric.
- **Also told:** what it asks of him (to love God, pray simply, honor his father, help the poor, honor priests, pray for the Church and the dead); "with a heart burning"; passing it to his little brother; more than a year's work.
- **Length:** about 2½ minutes (about 375 words). The census marks her a major figure, and her own book is a full, self-dated record. Her life outside the book is thin: two lines in the chronicles, and nothing on her death. The extra half-minute goes to the book's contents and voice, not to her life. The heart (what the book is, what it asks, why she wrote it, a mother as teacher) is told in the first 150 words.
- **Setting:** in the 840s (S6); at Uzès, a town in the south of France (S2, S3); in the Frankish empire that Charlemagne had built, now split by his grandsons' war (S4, S5, S7). Her own word for the times: kingdom and homeland "in discord" (S4, DH 2078). Tradition: the church of Charlemagne's heirs; do not say "Carolingian Reform Christianity" aloud (S7).
- **Shape:** Teacher or thinker. Her question: how to shape a son's faith when she cannot be with him. What she taught is the book. Its later readers are its copyists and editors.
- **Openings:** (1) **Chosen.** Her first words (Q1): many people have the joy of their children, but she is kept far from hers, so she sends him this little book. It leads straight into what the book is, and Q2 follows within the first minute. (2) Alternative: the mirror (Q2) as the opening image, with Q1 after it.
- **Story beats:**
  1. **The book and why** (first minute). She sends William a book written in her name, so that, though absent in body, it will be with him (T1, Q1). She dictated it and had it written out and sent (E9). She calls it a handbook, to be read as often as one looks in a mirror or plays a board game (T2). She began it on Saint Andrew's day, 30 November 841; William had just turned fifteen (E8, A4). Documented.
  2. **What it asks of him** (first minute). Her own table of chapters: love God, seek God, faith, hope and love, reverence in prayer, his father, his lord, honour for priests, virtues against vices, the Beatitudes, help for the poor, prayer for the Church, kings and the dead (A2). In her words elsewhere: pray in short, pure words (T5); love God first and then his father (T7); serve Charles faithfully, in mind and not only to please the eye (T6); help widows, orphans and pilgrims, and judge without taking gifts (T7). When she hears he has been commended to Charles, she tells him to serve well, but first to seek the kingdom of God (A3). Documented. Give it as a few concrete asks, never a list of chapter titles.
  3. **A mother as teacher** (first minute). The book is to be his mirror: reading it and praying, he can see her, mind and body, and see what he owes her (A1, Q2). Other teachers will teach him more, but not with a heart burning as hers (T4, Q3). He must teach his little brother and show him the book when the boy can read (T8). She was a noblewoman, not one of the clergy (S7). Documented.
  4. **Why she was apart.** In 824 she married Bernard, duke of Septimania, in the emperor's palace at Aachen, as his lawful wife; William was born in 826 (E1, E2, S3). Louis, Charlemagne's son, died in 840, and his sons fought over the empire (S4, S5). In March 841 she bore a second son at Uzès. Before the baby was baptized, Bernard had him brought to him in Aquitaine, with Elefantus, bishop of Uzès (E4). She did not yet know the baby's name (E6). She stayed on at Uzès "at my lord's command" (E5). After the battle of 841, Bernard sent William to King Charles, to commend himself to Charles as his sworn man if Charles would grant the lands and offices held in Burgundy; Nithard, writing in those same years, records it (E7). She heard of it at Uzès (E7, DH 2303–2304). Documented.
  5. **Her own life in the book.** She writes of illness and says her end is near (E13). She took on debts to support Bernard in the March, so that he would not part from her and William, borrowing from Christians and from Jews; she asks William to repay any left (E14). She asks him to pray for his father's dead kin, named in a list (E15). She pictures herself as a persistent puppy under its master's table, snatching the falling crumbs, among the clergy who are the other puppies (T3; paraphrase from the checked rendering below). Its opening verses spell, in their first letters, a greeting from Dhuoda to William (T9). She writes her own epitaph and asks him to have it carved on her grave, so that passers-by will pray for her (E17). Documented as her words.
  6. **The end of the book and of the family.** She finished her verses when William turned sixteen, on Saint Andrew's day 842, and the book on 2 February, more than a year after starting (E10, E11). In 844 the Franks judged Bernard guilty of treason, and on Charles's order he was put to death (E19). In 848 William seized Ampurias and Barcelona by trickery; in 850 he was himself captured by greater trickery and killed at Barcelona (E20). No record tells when or where she died (E18). Documented, except her death: not recorded.
- **What came of them** (about a third; item 4's "not clergy" point is pulled forward into beat 3):
  1. **The book was copied and kept.** An early copy, from the age of Charlemagne's heirs, survives in fragments found at Nîmes in 1885. Another, made in the 1600s, came from an old manuscript of La Grasse abbey. The two agree, and her editors used them to show the text is hers (I1). *Defended:* its authenticity.
  2. **Scholars printed and praised it.** Mabillon printed parts in 1677. Eighteenth-century Benedictine scholars called it "a respectable monument of literature and piety" and regretted it was not printed whole (I2). *Defended:* its worth.
  3. **The whole book was printed at last in 1887.** Its editor, Édouard Bondurand, called it the oldest writing by a woman born in France to reach us (I4). Say it as his claim.
  4. **It is read as a voice from outside the clergy.** The census sets her among very few lay voices to survive from the period, and an aristocratic one (S7, I6). The project's framing, not a witness: say "a noblewoman, not one of the clergy".
  5. **It was used in an argument.** In 1923 two translators of the Catholic catechism named "the manual of Dhuoda" among the works of Christian instruction from the ninth century on. They used it to answer the claim that the catechism began with Luther (I5). *Defended:* instruction for ordinary people before Luther.
  6. **An old error was put right.** One copy has her call the emperor Louis "my brother", so some made her Charlemagne's daughter. Earlier scholars had already doubted it. The older copy reads "the emperor", and the idea was given up (I3). *Defended:* the true reading. Optional; drop first if time is short.
- **Quotes, 2 or 3 at most:**
  - **Q1. Why she writes** (opening). DH 1893–1899, Latin, P only: "Cernens plurimos cum suis in sgeculo gaudere proies, et me Dodanam, ofili Willelme, a te elongatam conspiciens procuLobid quasi anxia, et utilitatis desiderio plena, hoc opusculum ex nomine meo scriptum in tuam specie tenus formam legendum dirigo, gaudens quod, si absens sum cor- pore, iste prsesens libellus tibi ad mentem reducat quid ergame, cum legeris, fili, debeas agere." Rendering (mine, from the Latin): "I see many people in this world who rejoice in their children with them. But I, Dhuoda, see myself kept far away from you, my son William. For this reason I am, as it were, anxious, and full of the wish to do you good. So I send you this little work to read, written in my name and shaped for you. I am glad of this: though I am absent in body, this little book is here with you. When you read it, my son, it will bring to your mind what you ought to do for me." ("Shaped for you" renders the obscure "in tuam speciem tenus formam"; Bondurand leaves it untranslated, DH 1886–1890.) Job: opens the story in her voice and leads straight into what the book is.
  - **Q2. The mirror** (new for batch 4). DH 3163–3168, Latin, P only: "Ilortatrix tua D[huoda] semper adest, fili, et si defuerim deflciens, qiiod fiiturum est, habes hic memoriale libellum, 0''. M'^. R." are unexplained and left unrendered, A1]. Rendering (mine, from the Latin): "Your Dhuoda, who urges you on, is always with you, my son. If I fail and am gone, as will happen, you have this little book to remember me by. Reading it and praying to God, you can look on me, mind and body, as in a picture in a mirror. And you can see in full what you owe me." Job: what she meant the book to be, her presence in his hands; it carries the heart.
  - **Q3. No teacher like a mother.** DH 3168–3171, Latin, P only, the next sentence after Q2: "Fili, habebis doctores qui te plura et ampliora utilitatis doceant documenta, sed non jfiquali conditione, animo ardentis in pectore, sicut ego genitrix tua." [jfiquali = aequali]. Rendering (mine, from the Latin): "My son, you will have teachers who will teach you more lessons, and greater ones, for your good. But they will not teach on equal terms, with a heart burning in the breast, as I do, your mother." Job: her claim to teach as a layperson and a mother. Q2 and Q3 may be given together, as they stand in the book.
  - **For paraphrase only, not quoted (T3).** DH 2620–2626, Latin, P only. Checked rendering (mine, from the Latin): "I too, unworthy and frail as I am, seek him in the shadows, as best I can. As far as I know and understand, I ask for his help without ceasing. I need it greatly, in everything. For this is a thing that happens now and then. A persistent puppy, among the other puppies under its master's table, manages to snatch the falling crumbs and eat them." She goes on to call the clergy "the puppies, that is, the ministers of the holy altar", from whose crumbs she gathers for herself and William (T3).
- **Connection to carry into the story:** what passed from her was the book itself, to William and, through him, to his brother: she tells William to read it, to do what it says, and to show it to the little boy and urge him to read it when he is old enough (T8, DH 3172–3178). The listener needs to understand that this was a mother passing the faith to her sons in writing because she could not do it face to face: the book was to be her presence (Q1, Q2), and a mother's teaching, she says, has a warmth no other teacher's has (Q3). The writer must not imply that William or his brother did read it: no record says so (E18, section 7). What the record does show is that the book itself outlived the family. It was copied, kept, printed in part in 1677, printed whole in 1887, and in 1923 named among the Church's early works of Christian instruction (I1, I2, I4, I5). Carry it through those acts, not a "this matters because" line.
- **Links:** Bernard of Septimania (husband; married at Aachen). William (son; the book is written to him). The younger son (born at Uzès; she did not yet know his name; editors call him Bernard). King Charles (William's lord; she urges loyalty to him). Louis the Pious (the emperor in her dates). Elefantus, bishop of Uzès (the baby was brought to Bernard with him). Theodoric (William's godfather; on her prayer list). Nithard (wrote of Bernard sending William). Prudentius of Troyes (his annals record Bernard's and William's deaths). Prudentius the poet (she quoted his book). Mabillon (printed parts of her book). Léopold Delisle (read her acrostic; corrected the "daughter of Charlemagne" error). Édouard Bondurand (edited her whole book).
- **Who tells it:** Dhuoda herself, in 841–843, for almost everything (OWN). Several key passages, including Q1, Q2 and Q3, survive only in a 17th-century copy (sheet, "The two manuscripts"); card only. Nithard, a grandson of Charlemagne writing in those same years, wrote of Bernard sending William to Charles (CONTEMP). The *Annals of Saint-Bertin*, kept year by year at the time, record the deaths of Bernard and William (CONTEMP). The editors and scholars wrote 800 to 1,080 years later (LATER). Line to say aloud, after the heart: "Almost all we know of her comes from her own book. The chronicles of her day name her husband and her son, but not her."
- **Do not say:**
  - "Bernard had the baby taken from her" (Heart_Test side note), "snatched", "stole", "tore the baby from her" → **"Bernard had the baby brought to him, with the bishop of Uzès, before he was baptized."** She makes no complaint (E4).
  - "a book" alone, at the opening, for what she sent → **"a handbook of faith for William, which she dictated"** (Heart_Test; E9, A2).
  - "a cat" or "a cat among the cats" → **"a puppy", "a little dog"**. The Latin is *catula*. Bondurand's French summary wrongly says *chatte* (D3).
  - "she wrote it with her own hand" → **"she dictated it and had it written out"** (E9).
  - "she never knew his name" → **"she did not yet know his name when she wrote, and she never names him in the book"** (E6). Do not call him Bernard in her voice. "Later writers call him Bernard" is allowed.
  - "banished / exiled to Uzès" as fact → **"she stayed at Uzès at her lord's command"**. "She calls herself an exile in her verses" is allowed (D7, T9).
  - "William, aged fifteen, was sent..." → **give no age for the sending.** "He had just turned fifteen when she began the book" is allowed (A4); so is "she gives his age as sixteen when she finished her verses" (D2, E10).
  - "if Charles would give William his father's lands" → **"the lands and offices held in Burgundy"**. The Latin does not settle whose they were (E7).
  - "Bernard was beheaded" → **"In 844 the Franks judged Bernard guilty of treason, and on Charles's order he was put to death"** (E19, D4).
  - "William died avenging his father" → **"William seized Ampurias and Barcelona by trickery. In 850 he was himself captured and killed at Barcelona."** No held annal gives a motive (E20).
  - "she died in 843", "she died soon after", "she died before her husband" → **"No record tells when or where she died"** (E18).
  - "her epitaph is carved on her tomb" or "her grave at Uzès" → **"she asked for it to be carved"**. No record says it was, or where she lies (E17).
  - "she borrowed from Jews" alone → **"from Christians and from Jews"**. Give both, and give her reason: to support Bernard in the March and to keep him from parting from her and William (E14).
  - "she finished it in 843" → **"she worked on it for more than a year"**. The year of completion is missing from the manuscript (E11, D1).
  - "chess" → **"a board game"** (T2).
  - "a hostage" → **"sent to commend himself to Charles, as his sworn man"** (E7). No held source calls William a hostage.
  - "she never saw them again" / "she was dying as she wrote" → **"she writes of illness and says her end is near"** (E13). Nothing records whether she saw her sons again (E18).
  - "William treasured / read / lived by the book", "his brother learned from it" → **say only what she asks of them** (T8). No record says what either son did with it.
  - "Charlemagne's daughter" or "the emperor's sister" → **no. Her family is unknown** (I3, D5).
  - "the first woman writer in France" → **"her editor called it the oldest writing by a woman born in France to reach us"** (I4).
  - "she taught William to honour his father even in this", or any motive for her loyalty to Bernard or to Charles → **say only what she asks.** The book gives no motive and no complaint.
  - "an ordinary believer" or "a common voice" → **"a noblewoman", "not one of the clergy"** (S7).
  - "the only lay voice", "the only woman's voice" of the age → **"a noblewoman's voice, not one of the clergy"**; "one of very few" only as the census's view (S7, I6).
  - "read for centuries / used to teach children" → **"copied, kept, and printed in part in the 1600s"**. No held record shows it in use between her time and then (section 7 of the sheet).
  - "a catechism" for her book → **"a handbook"**. The 1923 translators list it among works of Christian instruction; they do not call it a catechism (I5).
  - "seventy-three chapters" may be said: it is the book's own table (A2) as well as the editors' count (DH 290–292).
  - "she told him to seek first the kingdom of God" as her own words → **"she reminds him, 'as Scripture says', to seek first the kingdom of God"** (A3, DH 2307; Matt. 6:33, DH 2348).
  - "the baby's removal, or the little brother, told only after the first minute" → **"name both sons' absence, and her charge to show the book to the little brother, in the opening."**
- **Leave out:** Henri Martin's story of Bernard and the empress Judith (D6), a 19th-century gossip-history with nothing in her book. The legend of Bernard stabbed by the king at Toulouse (D4). The drowning of Bernard's sister and the blinding of his brother: real events, but side stories. The younger son's later revolt and death (864, 872): the annals do not name his mother. Bondurand's guesses about her family (D5). Her number-lore (God's name in Greek and Latin numerals, finger-counting): rich, but it needs explaining and would crowd the story. The 842 vs 843 completion debate (card). Her slip on Louis's reign years (E3). The unexplained sigla in Q2 (A1). Never leave out the force in the record: the baby brought away before baptism, her stay at her lord's command, the war, Bernard's execution, and William's killing are told as plainly as the book's virtues.
