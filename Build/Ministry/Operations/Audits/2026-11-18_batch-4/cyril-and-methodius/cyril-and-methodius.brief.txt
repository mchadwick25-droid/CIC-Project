The emperor asked a tired scholar to go to the Slavs. He would go gladly, the scholar said, "if they have letters for their language." Without them, he asked, "who can write words on water, and who wants to be called a heretic?"

The request had come in the 860s from Rastislav, prince of Moravia, in what is now the Czech Republic. His people had teachers from Italy, Greece and Germany, but none in their own language. So the emperor sent the scholar, Constantine, and his brother Methodius, from Thessalonica, a Greek city where Slavic was spoken too. The Slavs had no letters, so Constantine made them. With those letters the brothers put the Gospel, the Psalms, the readings from the apostles and chosen services into Slavic. In Moravia they taught disciples the prayers and the Mass in their own tongue.

When Latin clergy said God could be praised only in Hebrew, Greek and Latin, Constantine answered that every people may praise God in its own language. In Venice, his Life says, he asked them: "Does God's rain not fall on all alike? Does the sun not shine the same on everyone?" A pope would one day agree, and their disciples would carry the letters to Bulgaria and the Rus.

Most of what we know comes from two Lives written by their own followers. They survive only in copies made centuries later. They tell how Constantine fell ill in Rome, became a monk named Cyril, and died in 869. His last words told Methodius, who loved the mountain of monks, not to give up their teaching for its sake.

Methodius went back as a bishop, and his Life says Frankish bishops held him prisoner for two and a half years. A Salzburg writer of his day complained that this "Greek" with "newly invented Slavic letters" had partly cheapened, in the people's eyes, the Latin Masses and Gospels. Pope John VIII repeated a ban on Slavonic Mass in 879, but in 880 he allowed it, with the Gospel read first in Latin. He wrote: "For he who made the three chief languages, Hebrew, Greek and Latin, also himself created all the others, for his praise and glory."

After Methodius died in 885, a Life of Clement, written in Bulgaria a generation or more later, says his younger disciples were sold as slaves. The teachers were chained and jailed, then stripped and driven out by soldiers. Clement, Naum and Angelarius crossed the Danube to Bulgaria, where Prince Boris welcomed them, and Clement became "the first bishop of the Bulgarian tongue." Writing in Kiev more than two centuries later, the chroniclers said this Slavic writing was found among the Rus.
