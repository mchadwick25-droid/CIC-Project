The emperor asked a tired scholar to go to the Slavs. He would go gladly, the scholar said, "if they have letters for their language." Without them, he asked, "who can write words on water, and who wants to be called a heretic?"

The request had come in the 860s from Rastislav, prince of Moravia, in what is now the Czech Republic. His people had teachers from Italy, Greece and Germany, but none who taught in their own language. So the emperor sent two brothers from Thessalonica, a Greek city where Slavic was spoken too. The Slavs had no letters, so Constantine made them. With those letters the brothers put the Gospel, the Psalms, the readings from the apostles and chosen services into Slavic, and in Moravia they taught disciples the prayers and the Mass in it. When Latin clergy said God could rightly be praised only in Hebrew, Greek and Latin, Constantine answered that every people may praise God in its own language. A pope would one day agree, and their disciples would carry the letters to Bulgaria and the Rus.

Most of what we know comes from two Lives written by their own followers, which survive only in copies made centuries later. In Venice, Constantine's Life says, Latin clergy gathered against him "like crows against a falcon." He answered: "Does God's rain not fall on all alike? Does the sun not shine the same on everyone? Do we not all breathe the air alike? Then how are you not ashamed to fix on three languages only? You would order every other people and tribe to be blind and deaf."

In Rome, Constantine fell ill, took the monk's name Cyril, and died in 869. To his brother he said, "Look, brother, we were partners ploughing a single furrow. And I fall in the field, having finished my day." He told Methodius not to give up their teaching for the mountain of monks he loved.

Methodius went back as a bishop. His Life says Frankish bishops held him prisoner for two and a half years, and a Salzburg writer of his day complained that this "Greek" with "newly invented Slavic letters" had partly cheapened the Latin Masses in the people's eyes. In 879 Pope John VIII repeated a ban on Slavonic Mass, yet in 880 he allowed it, with the Gospel read first in Latin. He wrote: "For he who made the three chief languages, Hebrew, Greek and Latin, also himself created all the others, for his praise and glory."

Methodius died in 885. A Life of Clement, written in Bulgaria a generation or more later, says his younger disciples were then sold as slaves, while soldiers drove the teachers out of Moravia in chains. Clement, Naum and Angelarius crossed the Danube to Bulgaria, where Prince Boris thanked God for sending them, and Clement became "the first bishop of the Bulgarian tongue." Writing in Kiev more than two centuries later, the chroniclers said this Slavic writing was found among the Rus.
