The emperor asked a tired scholar to go to the Slavs. He would go gladly, the scholar said, "if they have letters for their language." Without them, he asked, "who can write words on water, and who wants to be called a heretic?"

The scholar was Constantine, and the Slavs had no letters, so he made them. With his brother Methodius he put the Gospel, the Psalms, the readings from the apostles and chosen services into Slavic. When Latin clergy said God could be praised only in Hebrew, Greek and Latin, Constantine answered that every people may praise God in its own language. Their work began in Moravia, in what is now the Czech Republic. After Methodius died, a Life of their disciple Clement says, the younger disciples were sold as slaves, and the teachers were chained, stripped and driven out. Three of them, Clement, Naum and Angelarius, carried the letters to Bulgaria.

That Life was written in Bulgaria a generation or more later. In it Prince Boris thanks God for sending them, and Clement becomes "the first bishop of the Bulgarian tongue." He wrote plain sermons for every feast, because Bulgarian priests could barely follow Greek. Writing in Kiev more than two centuries later, the chroniclers said this Slavic writing was found among the Rus.

It had begun in the 860s, when Rastislav, prince of Moravia, sent word to the emperor in Constantinople. His people had teachers from Italy, Greece and Germany, but none in their own language. The emperor chose the two brothers because they came from Thessalonica, a Greek city where Slavic was spoken too. Methodius had governed a Slavic district for the emperor, then left it to become a monk on a mountain of monks called Olympus.

Most of what we know comes from two Lives written by their own followers. They survive only in copies made centuries later. They say that after prayer God showed Constantine the letters, and that the first words he wrote were "In the beginning was the Word." In Venice, his Life says, Latin bishops, priests and monks gathered against him "like crows against a falcon." He asked them: "Does God's rain not fall on all alike? Does the sun not shine the same on everyone?"

In Rome, the Lives tell, Constantine fell ill, became a monk named Cyril, and died in 869. He told his brother: "Look, brother, we were partners ploughing a single furrow. And I fall in the field, having finished my day. You love the mountain very much. Do not give up your teaching for the mountain's sake."

Methodius went back as a bishop, and his Life says Frankish bishops held him prisoner for two and a half years. A Salzburg writer of his day complained that this "Greek" with "newly invented Slavic letters" had partly cheapened, in the people's eyes, the Latin Masses and Gospels. Pope John VIII repeated a ban on Slavonic Mass in 879, but in 880 he allowed it, with the Gospel read first in Latin. He wrote: "For he who made the three chief languages, Hebrew, Greek and Latin, also himself created all the others, for his praise and glory."

Methodius died in 885. His Life says his disciples held the funeral in Latin, Greek and Slavonic, and crowds came, rich and poor, free and slave.
