# Outline: Peter of Alexandria

Sheet items (S, E, M, D, I, L, X) are in `Source_Sheet.md`, which gives the file and line for each. ANF6 is the Ante-Nicene Fathers English of his canons and letter; PED the Greek of the canons (Pedalion, 1886); EUS Eusebius's *Church History* in McGiffert's English, EUS-G its Greek; EPI Epiphanius's *Panarion* in Greek; ATH Athanasius in English; BELL and HEF the two modern historians.

- **The essence:** Peter was bishop of Alexandria, leading the churches of all Egypt, through the Great Persecution that began in 303 (S1–S3; Documented). In 306 he set rules for taking back Christians who had given way under it, measuring each case by what the person had suffered and chosen, and in 311 he was beheaded (E1, E3–E12, D1, D2; Documented). In those same years a bishop of his, Melitius, broke away; the rival church that split left lasted beyond his death and came before the Council of Nicaea (M1–M11, I2; Documented that it happened; why it began, Contested, X1).
- **Also told:**
  - **The rules.** Those tortured until the body failed, who had mourned their fall for three years: forty more days, then communion. Prison without torture: a year more. Those who gave way from fear alone: longer, by the parable of the barren fig tree. Those who tricked the officials, by walking past the altar, handing in a written statement or sending a pagan in their place: six months. Slaves forced by their masters: a year; the masters who forced them: three years. Those who paid to be left alone, or fled, are not to be blamed. Clergy who fell do not return to their office, but keep communion. Those who rushed forward to be arrested are blamed, but kept in communion (E3–E12; Documented, his own words).
  - **The break, told from both held sides.** Letters of the time: four imprisoned bishops rebuked Melitius for ordaining in their churches; after they died he came to Alexandria, put out Peter's appointed men and ordained his own; Peter told his people to hold no communion with him (M1–M3; Documented as the letters' account). Athanasius, decades later and a foe of the Melitians, says Peter's council deposed him for many crimes, among them sacrifice to idols (M5; Documented as his charge; Contested as fact). Epiphanius, some seventy years on, says the two were in prison together and split over the fallen: Peter for mercy, Melitius for strictness (M7–M10; Documented as his account; Contested). Historians still weigh them (M12, M13; Contested).
  - **The death.** Eusebius, who lived through it, says he was seized for no cause and beheaded without warning, with many bishops of Egypt, in the ninth year of the persecution (D2, D3; Documented).
  - **What came of him.** His rules became church law, sealed by a council in 692 and kept with commentaries in the Greek books of church law (I1; Documented). Nicaea left Melitius only the title of bishop, but the split did not heal (I2; Documented).
- **Length:** about 3 minutes 15 seconds (about 490 words). Count first: the three quotes, with the plain sense of "the marks of Jesus", about 75 words; the words to explain (the lapsed, communion, penance, ordain, the fourth Easter as a count of years, schism) about 45; the witnesses it must credit (Eusebius, the four bishops' letter, Peter's own letter, Athanasius, Epiphanius, one modern historian, Nicaea's letter) about 55: some 175 words that cannot go. The rest carries the canons as substance (the heart), the two-sided break, the death and a third on what came. It runs past three minutes because the break must be told from both sides, each credited, and the rules must be heard as rules, not a list of numbers. Cut first, if needed: E11 (paying and fleeing), Beat 2's Libya letter, What came 3.
- **Setting:**
  - Time: bishop from about 300 (S1); the Great Persecution from 303 (S3); the rules in 306 (E1); beheaded in 311 (D1, D4). Plain words: "the Great Persecution", "the early three-hundreds".
  - Place: Alexandria, in Egypt; its bishop led the churches of all Egypt, the Thebaid (Upper Egypt) and Libya (S2). Lycopolis, Melitius's city, is in Upper Egypt (M5; `melitius-of-lycopolis`).
  - Tradition: the church of Alexandria; the tree's name is "Alexandrian Christianity". Do not make him head of the catechetical school (S4).
  - Language: he wrote in Greek. The canons survive in Greek; his letter about Melitius only in a Latin translation (sheet, Language).

**Words to explain** (the first time each is heard; plain word or short gloss, worked into the sentence)

- **the lapsed / those who lapsed**: "Christians who had given way, who had sacrificed to the gods or denied Christ under threat". Prefer "those who had given way".
- **communion**: "communion, sharing the Lord's Supper with the church". Taking someone back means letting them share it again.
- **penance**: "a set time of repentance, fasting and prayer before they could share communion again". Do not use "penance" bare.
- **"the fourth Easter of the persecution"** (E1): the narrator says it means the fourth year since it began, so 306.
- **"the marks of Jesus"** (Quote 1): Paul's phrase for the scars of suffering for Christ; the narrator gives the plain sense, "the scars of their torture" (Zonaras's gloss, E3).
- **sacrifice to the gods**: "to burn incense or make an offering to the gods, as the emperors' officials demanded". The test of loyalty in the persecution.
- **ordain / ordination**: "to make priests and deacons". A bishop was to ordain only in his own church (M1).
- **schism / split**: use "split" or "rival church". Do not use "schism" bare.
- **confessor**: if used, "someone who suffered for the faith and survived". Better avoided.
- **deposed**: "removed from office".
- **Thebaid**: "Upper Egypt". Do not use the word.
- **Melitians**: "the followers of Melitius".

- **Shape:** Martyr. The life comes to a stand in 311. The trap is the death swallowing the story: here the rules of 306 and the split carry the heart, and the death is one beat. The impact is in how the church remembered him and used what he left: his rules as law, and the split as business for Nicaea.

- **Openings:**
  1. **Chosen.** His own rules (Quote 1; E1, E3). In about three sentences: the fourth Easter of the persecution had come, and the bishop of Alexandria wrote about Christians tortured until they gave way: "They did not come to this by their own choice. The weakness of the flesh betrayed them. For they show the marks of Jesus in their bodies." The narrator gives the plain sense, the scars of their torture. Then the name, Peter, and the essence.

**Story beats** (the opening, the essence and beat 1 are the first minute, about 150 words; beats 2 to 5 deepen it)

1. **Bishop before the storm.** Peter followed Theonas as bishop of Alexandria about 300, and led the church less than three years before the persecution broke out; for the rest of his life, Eusebius says, he kept a stricter discipline over himself and cared, not in secret, for the good of all the churches (S1; Documented, Eusebius, who lived then). The bishop of Alexandria led the churches of all Egypt and Libya (S2). In 303 the emperors ordered the churches pulled down, and Christians were made to sacrifice to the gods; many bishops of Egypt were jailed (S3, D3).
2. **The rules of 306.** Some gave way under torture, some in prison, some from fear, some by tricks; now they asked to come back. In a sermon on repentance, three years in, Peter set rules case by case (E1, E2). The tortured who had mourned three years: forty more days, the days Christ fasted, then communion. Prison only: a year more. Fear alone: longer, by the parable of the barren fig tree. Those who walked past the altar, handed in a written statement or sent a pagan in their place: six months. Slaves sent by their masters: a year; the masters: three, for forcing them (E3–E8; Documented, his words). Those who paid to be left alone, and those who fled, he would not blame, since Christ said to flee from city to city (E11). Clergy who fell were not to return to office, though they kept communion (E10). Those whose hands were forced onto the sacrifice and burned, martyrs wrote to him from prison, were to be counted among those who stood firm (E12). Optional, cut first: the Libya letter.
3. **The break, from the letters.** During the persecution, Melitius, bishop of Lycopolis in Upper Egypt, began making priests in other bishops' churches. Four bishops in prison wrote to rebuke him: no bishop may ordain outside his own church, and he should have waited for Peter, whom they call their great bishop and father (M1; Documented as their letter, in Latin translation; the words are paraphrase here, not a quote). After they died for the faith, an account kept with the letter says, he came to Alexandria, put out the men Peter had left to visit the faithful, and ordained his own (M2). Peter, then probably in hiding (M4, Bell's and Hefele's reading), wrote to his people to hold no communion with Melitius until he could meet him with wise men and learn his aims (M3; Documented, his letter). Athanasius, bishop of Alexandria decades later and Melitius's bitter foe, says Peter's council of bishops removed him for many crimes, among them sacrifice to the gods (M5; Documented as Athanasius's charge).
4. **The break, from Epiphanius, and the weighing.** Epiphanius, writing some seventy years later, tells it another way: the two were in prison together and split over the fallen. Melitius would take them back only after the persecution, and not as clergy; Peter begged to receive them now (M7, M8). Then Quote 2. Most of the bishops, monks and priests, he says, went with Melitius (M10; the cloak scene in one clause at most, or not at all). Then one modern weighing, credited: a historian of the councils (Hefele) answered that Peter's own rules kept fallen clergy out of office, the very strictness Epiphanius gives Melitius (M13); a scholar of the Melitians' papyri (Bell) thought Epiphanius had good sources and doubted the sacrifice charge (M12). Give each by role, both if room, Hefele first if only one. Do not settle it (X1).
5. **The death.** Persecution eased, and then returned (BELL 3198–3200, 3210–3211; Bell's reading). In the ninth year of the persecution, 311, Peter was seized and beheaded; Eusebius, who lived through it, tells it in Quote 3, and adds that many other bishops of Egypt died with him (D1–D3; Documented). The day, 25 November, comes from the Egyptian church's later reckoning (D4); say "in 311", or "late in 311".

**What came of him** (about a third of the story; heaviest first)

1. **His rules became law.** Later churches kept them. In 692 a council at Constantinople, the Council in Trullo, set its seal on the rules of earlier fathers, naming Peter of Alexandria among them (I1; Documented). Two Greek church lawyers of the twelfth century, Balsamon and Zonaras (by role), wrote commentaries on them, and a Greek book of church law printed them again with notes in 1886, saying that by the council's seal they carry, in a way, the force of a council of the whole church (I1; Documented; the editor Nikodemos's view, told in paraphrase, not quoted). The listener must hear that the rules he wrote in a persecution outlived it, as law for the whole church.
2. **The split reached Nicaea.** The rival church did not end with him. In 325 the Council of Nicaea let Melitius keep only the title of bishop, with no power to ordain, and his clergy were taken in under Alexandria's bishop (I2; Documented, the council's letter). Within months, by Athanasius's account, the Melitians were troubling the churches again, and they joined Athanasius's enemies (I2; Documented as his account). The rival church lasted in Egypt for centuries (I2; Bell). The listener must hear that a quarrel over church order and the fallen, begun in the persecution, became business for the first great council of the whole church.
3. **Remembered as a martyr.** Eusebius called him one of the finest teachers of the faith (D3). Athanasius, who fought the Melitians all his life, still called him blessed, bishop and martyr (I3; paraphrase, not a quote: the three quotes are spent). Alexandria kept the day of his memory (I3, Socrates retelling Rufinus).

**Quotes, 3**

1. **"They did not come to this by their own choice. The weakness of the flesh betrayed them. For they show the marks of Jesus in their bodies."** Rendered from the Greek of Canon 1 (PED 49722–49723: "οὐ γὰρ κατὰ προαίρεσιν ἐν τούτῳ ἐληλύθασιν, ἀλλὰ καταπροδοθέντες ὑπὸ τῆς ἀσθενείας τῆς σαρκός, ἐπειδὴ καὶ τὰ στίγματα τοῦ Ἰησοῦ ἐνδείκνυνται ἐν τοῖς σώμασιν αὐτῶν"). **The Greek is held only in a damaged OCR scan** (PED reads "σίγματα" for στίγματα); the English of ANF6 26616–26618 confirms every clause ("for they did not come to this of their own will, but were betrayed by the frailty of the flesh; for they show in their bodies the marks of Jesus"). No second Greek print is held. Job: the opening; the mercy of the rules in his own words. The narrator gives the plain sense of "the marks of Jesus": the scars of their torture.
2. **"Peter's case was for mercy and kindness. The case of Melitius and those with him was for truth and zeal."** Rendered from the Greek of Epiphanius, *Panarion* 68.3 (EPI 43852–43853; confirmed by a second print, EPI-PG 13795–13798: "Καὶ ἦν μὲν τοῦ Πέτρου ὁ λόγος ὑπὲρ ἐλέους καὶ φιλανθρωπίας, καὶ τοῦ Μελητίου καὶ τῶν ἅμα αὐτῷ ὑπὲρ ἀληθείας καὶ ζήλου"). Job: the other side, in the voice of the witness who tells it, giving each man his due. Credit Epiphanius beside it.
3. **"He was seized for no cause, when no one expected it. All at once and with no reason given, as though on Maximinus's order, he was beheaded."** Rendered from the Greek of Eusebius, *Church History* 9.6.2 (EUS-G 17556–17560: "ἐξ οὐδεμιᾶς ἀνάρπαστος γεγονὼς αἰτίας, μηδεμιᾶς προλαβούσης προσδοκίας, ἀθρόως οὕτως καὶ ἀλόγως, ὡς ἂν Μαξιμίνου προστάξαντος, τὴν κεφαλὴν ἀποτέμνεται"). **The Greek is held only in a damaged OCR scan** (it reads "διαξιμίνου" for Μαξιμίνου); McGiffert's English (EUS 47993–47998) confirms every clause ("being seized for no cause and quite unexpectedly, was, as if by command of Maximinus, immediately and without explanation, beheaded"). Job: the death, from a witness who lived through it. The narrator gives him as "the emperor Maximinus" (BELL 3210–3211).

**Connection to carry into the story**

What passed on was a way of judging the fallen, and a quarrel. The rules: Peter judged each person by what they had suffered and chosen, and later churches kept his judgments as law; in 692 a council at Constantinople sealed them with the rules of other fathers (I1; NPNF14 26731–26744). The listener must hear that these were rules written inside a persecution, for people asking to come back to communion, and that they became law for churches long after it. The quarrel: the rival church of Melitius outlived Peter and came before the Council of Nicaea in 325, which let Melitius keep only his title (I2; SOC 3203–3211). The listener must be told, not left to infer, that the council's ruling on Melitius is the same split that began under Peter; that the council could not end it; and that its followers later fought Athanasius. Carry it through acts: Peter's letter, the council's terms, Athanasius's charge.

**Ending**

1. **Chosen.** His memory kept. Once the connection has landed: Athanasius, the Melitians' great foe, still called him blessed, bishop and martyr (ATH 22465; in paraphrase), and Alexandria kept the day of his memory each year (SOC 4007–4008). End on Peter, not on the council.
2. The opening image seen again: the rules for those who "show the marks of Jesus in their bodies", written by a bishop who would himself be killed five years later (E1, D1). Use only if it does not repeat Quote 1 word for word.

**Who tells it**

- **Peter himself** (the canons, 306; the letter about Melitius): his own words, the canons in Greek, the letter in Latin translation. Credit the rules to him (Beat 2), the letter to him (Beat 3).
- **The four bishops' letter and the account kept with it** (contemporary; Latin translation; anonymous account): credit as "a letter of the time" and "an account kept with it" (Beat 3).
- **Eusebius of Caesarea** (contemporary, wrote in the 310s and 320s, lived through the persecution in Palestine): credited beside the beheading and the praise (Beats 1, 5; What came 3). He says nothing of Melitius.
- **Athanasius** (bishop of Alexandria from 328, writing decades after the split; the Melitians' lifelong opponent): credited beside the sacrifice charge and the five-months line, and named as an opponent where his charge is told (Beat 3; What came 2).
- **Epiphanius** (wrote 374–377, some seventy years later; a hunter of heresies; editors think he drew on the Melitians' own account): credited beside the prison story and Quote 2 (Beat 4).
- **Hefele and Bell** (modern historians): by role, credited beside their weighing (Beat 4).
- **The council's letter** (325, kept by Socrates and Theodoret): credited beside Nicaea's terms (What came 2).
- The doubt is part of the story: Beat 4 is the scene where it is told, by setting the witnesses side by side. No stock how-we-know sentence.

**Do not say**

- "Peter deposed Melitius for sacrificing to idols" as fact. Say: "Athanasius, his foe, says Peter's council removed him for many crimes, among them sacrifice to the gods."
- "Peter and Melitius were in prison together" as fact. Say: "Epiphanius says they were in prison together." Hefele denies it (X2).
- "Peter was lenient" or "Melitius was a hard-liner" as the narrator's verdict. Those are Epiphanius's terms; Peter's own rules kept fallen clergy out of office.
- "Peter was hiding" as fact. Say "probably in hiding", or leave it out (M4).
- "Peter fled, so he excused those who fled." No source links his rule on flight to his own conduct.
- A number for those who gave way from fear alone: the commentators give three years and four (X6). Say "longer".
- "Fourteen canons" or "fifteen canons": the counts differ (X7). Say "rules".
- "Head of the catechetical school": not held (S4).
- "Beheaded on 25 November 311" as Eusebius's date. Eusebius gives "the ninth year of the persecution"; the day is the Egyptian church's later reckoning (D4).
- "Killed after the emperor's edict of toleration" as Eusebius's words. That is McGiffert's inference (D4). "Persecution eased, then returned" is enough.
- "The split began in 306" or any single year: 301, 304–305 and 305 are all held (X3). Say "during the persecution".
- "Melitius was a heretic." Epiphanius says he did not change the faith (M7); Robertson, that no doctrine was at stake (M14).
- "Arius" in the story (see Links).
- "Schism", "the lapsed", "penance", "Thebaid" without their plain sense (Words to explain).
- "Meletius" → "Melitius" (the census spelling), everywhere.
- Any quote from `melitius-of-lycopolis`: "ordained in the prison several unto himself", "offering sacrifice to idols", "a nominal dignity", "Church of the Martyrs".
- Do not tell the cloak scene at length: it opens `melitius-of-lycopolis`. One clause at most.
- Do not quote the four bishops' letter or Peter's letter: their Latin is not held and Hefele calls the text corrupt. Paraphrase.

**Leave out**

- *The Genuine Acts of Peter* (his last night, the vision of Christ's torn robe and Arius, prayer at Mark's tomb, the soldier's sword) and the heavenly voice "Peter the beginning of the apostles, and Peter the end of the martyrs": late legend; McGiffert calls the Acts "spurious and historically quite worthless" (D5).
- Birth, family and schooling: no early source (S5).
- Arius (Mark, 2026-11-20): left out. Sozomen, a century later, says Peter made Arius a deacon and then cast him out for siding with Melitius; Robertson, editor of Athanasius, says such tales "deserve little credit" (L4).
- The boy-bishop story of Athanasius on Peter's feast day: doubted by Robertson; it is Athanasius's story, not Peter's (I3).
- Coxe's and Neale's verdict that Melitius was wicked: an editor's partisanship (M15).
- Canon 15 (weekday fasts): not about the fallen (E13).
- The doctrinal fragments (On the Godhead, the soul not existing before the body): real but thin, and kept only through later collections (S4). They would add a second subject.
- Who succeeded Peter (Achillas or Alexander): sources differ and the story does not need it (X4).
- Not left out: the force of the persecution (torture, forced sacrifice, masters forcing slaves), the hostility between the two churches, and Athanasius's charge. Those are told plainly.
