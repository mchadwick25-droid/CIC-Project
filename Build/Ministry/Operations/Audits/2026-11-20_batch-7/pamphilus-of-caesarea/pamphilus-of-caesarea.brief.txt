About eighty years after Pamphilus of Caesarea died, the scholar Jerome found twenty-five volumes of Origen's commentaries, his books explaining Scripture, copied, he wrote, in Pamphilus's own hand. "If it is a joy to have one letter of a martyr," he wrote, "how much more to have so many thousand lines! He seems to me to have marked them with the traces of his own blood."

Pamphilus was a presbyter, one of the elders who served the church beside its bishop, in Caesarea, a port in Roman Palestine. Origen, the great Bible scholar of the century before, had taught there. Pamphilus gathered a library of sacred books for his church, and Jerome says he copied most of Origen's books himself. Arrested in the last and greatest persecution of Christians by Rome's emperors, he worked from prison with his pupil and friend Eusebius on a book defending Origen. He was put to death for his faith about 310.

Eusebius writes that he gave his inheritance to the poor; Jerome, that he bought every book Origen had written, at great cost, for the church.

In a Life of him, now lost but quoted by Jerome, Eusebius wrote: "Who among those eager to learn was not a friend of Pamphilus? If he saw any of them lacking what they needed to live, he gave freely what he could. He gave the holy Scriptures too, most readily, not only to read but to keep. He gave them not only to men, but also to women he saw devoted to reading. So he kept many books ready, to give to those who wanted them whenever need arose."

Late in 307, the governor of Palestine ordered him to offer sacrifice to the Roman gods. When he refused, the governor had his sides scraped in long torture and jailed him for two years.

Eusebius writes that the two worked on this Defense of Origen "jointly", Pamphilus from prison. The Defense speaks of the ideas that came to Origen from the Scriptures. "When he explains these things, he often adds and declares that he is not pronouncing them as a final verdict, or settling them as fixed doctrine. He is searching as far as his strength allows, and examining the sense of the Scriptures. He does not claim to have grasped it wholly and perfectly."

In February of 309 or 310, a new governor condemned the prisoners who still would not obey. Twelve died in all. Pamphilus, the only presbyter among them, was put to death by the sword.

About 392, Jerome listed the Defense under Pamphilus's name. In 397 Rufinus of Aquileia put its first book, all that survives, into Latin as Pamphilus's, though he called it the book Pamphilus "is said to have written". Around 400, Jerome began to doubt it was Pamphilus's, and Rufinus answered that Jerome no longer spared even the martyrs. Jerome then named Eusebius as the author, and charged that Rufinus had put a martyr's name on it so that readers would trust its praise of Origen. His proof was Eusebius's own Life, which says Pamphilus wrote nothing but letters to friends.

Almost all we know of Pamphilus rests on that same Eusebius, who had called the Defense their joint work. Five centuries later, a scholar in Constantinople who read all six books said Pamphilus wrote five in prison, working with Eusebius, and Eusebius the sixth alone. Modern editors read it as joint work.

The library outlived him. Eusebius drew much of his history from it, his English editor judged, and Jerome worked from it. The Defense became a weapon in the quarrel over Origen. Jerome complained that readers ignored the bishops who condemned Origen, "since they think a martyr praised him." The book defended the good name of a teacher half a century dead.

And at Caesarea lay the books themselves, many thousand lines in Pamphilus's hand, which Jerome had once said seemed marked with his blood.
