# Source sheet: Pamphilus of Caesarea

Researcher's sheet for batch 7 (2026-11-20). The job: the presbyter who copied Origen's books by hand and built the church library at Caesarea, wrote a Defense of Origen from prison with Eusebius, and died a martyr about 310; the dispute over who wrote the Defense is told with each witness credited. Martyr. New card (no `Card.existing.json`). The writer uses the outline, the shapes and the standard only; this sheet is the evidence behind the outline.

**Original language.** Eusebius wrote in Greek: HE VI–X and both forms of MP are held in Greek (GRK, damaged OCR); his *Life of Pamphilus* is lost, and its one surviving passage is held only in Jerome's Latin (JER-L) and English (JER). Jerome wrote in Latin (JER-L held). The Defense was written in Greek; the Greek is **not held** (it does not survive); Book I survives only in Rufinus's Latin (APOL-L, APOL-M). Photius's Greek is **not held**; Delarue quotes one sentence of it (APOL-M 40541–40544). No English translation of the Defense itself is held (JER gives only Rufinus's preface).

**Witness kinds.**
- OWN-JOINT = the **Defense**, Book I (APOL-L, APOL-M), in Rufinus's Latin of 397. Whose words they are is the dispute (D1). The Library counts it as his own words (CEN `sourcing`).
- CONTEMPORARY = **Eusebius**, his pupil and friend, who worked with him: HE VI–VIII (finished in the years after the persecution), both forms of MP, and the lost Life (quoted by Jerome). Admiring; the only first-hand witness to his life and death.
- LATER, about 80–95 years = **Jerome** (DVI, 392/3; Letter 84, 400; Apol., about 401–402) and **Rufinus** (397, and his Apology about 400). Both had used the library at Caesarea; both are parties to the quarrel over Origen.
- LATER, about 130 years = **Socrates** (5th c.).
- LATE, about 550 years = **Photius** (9th c.), who says he read the whole six-book Defense.
- EDITORS = EUS-McG (McGiffert, 1890, citing Lightfoot), JER-ed (Fremantle, 1892), JER-L-ed (Martianay), APOL-M-ed (Delarue), ANF6 translator. Marked wherever used.

## 1. Setting

S1. **Place: Caesarea in Palestine.** "Pamphilus, a presbyter, who was the great glory of the parish of Cæsarea, and among the men of our time most admirable." (EUS 44791–44792, HE VIII.13.6; Greek GRK 15464–15466). Caesarea is "Caesarea Maritima", today's Israel (CEN `modernCountries`). Plain words: "Caesarea, a port on the coast of Roman Palestine". Documented.

S2. **Birthplace and family: Berytus (Beirut).** MP-long, Greek only: "ὡρμᾶτο μὲν οὖν ἐκ τῆς Βηρυτίων πόλεως, ἔνθα τὴν πρώτην ἡλικίαν τοῖς αὐτόθι τέθραπτο παιδευτηρίοις" ("He came from the city of Berytus, where he was brought up in his early years in the schools there"; GRK 24841–24842). Of noble family, distinguished in the public offices of his home city: "τοῦ μὲν Παμφίλου ἐξ εὐπατριδῶν κατάγοντος τὸ κατὰ σάρκα γένος ἐπισήμως τε ταῖς κατὰ τὴν πατρίδα πολιτείαις διαπρέψαντος" (GRK 24682–24683). CONTEMPORARY (Eusebius, longer form). That the longer form is Eusebius's own: "That Eusebius himself was the author of the original cannot be doubted" (EUS-McG 3041–3042); Widely Accepted. The claim itself: Documented in the longer form only; damaged OCR, no other held print. Plain words: "Beirut".

S3. **Study at Alexandria: a late, doubted report.** Photius: "Pierius was the teacher of Pamphilus, the head of the catechetical school at Alexandria." (PHOT 11506–11507). McGiffert: "He pursued his studies in Alexandria (according to Photius, under Pierius, more probably under Achillas …)" (EUS-McG 43013–43015), and judges Photius's report on Pierius likely "false and rests upon a combination of the accounts of Eusebius and Jerome" (EUS-McG 43070–43071). CEN `relationsSummary`: "He is said to have studied at Alexandria." LATE (Photius); Contested.

S4. **Tradition.** CEN `toldWithin` = `alexandria-catechetical`; its spoken name, `informalName`, is "Alexandrian Christianity" (CEN). Origen's live story uses the same name. The tree's own entry for his city is `palestinian-church-pre-constantinian`, "The Church of Roman Palestine before Constantine" (CEN), a possible future world. Plain words: "Origen's line of Alexandrian Christianity" (one clause); "the church of Caesarea".

S5. **Time.** The Great Persecution began in 303; in Palestine Eusebius counts its years from spring 303 (EUS-McG note, 46640–46651). Arrested in the fifth year, late 307 (EUS-McG 47123–47126). Died in February, after two whole years in prison: "309" in the common reckoning, "310" by McGiffert's (EUS-McG 47126–47131); Martianay gives "an. 309, 16 Februarii" (JER-L-ed 58607–58608); the tree's Palestine entry says 310 (CEN). Plain words: "about 310", "in February of 309 or 310". The census gives no birth year (CEN `sourcing`).

S6. **Rome's officials.** Urbanus, governor of Palestine, arrested and tortured him (EUS 46700–46709, MP 7.5–6); Firmilian, his successor, condemned him (EUS 47193–47198, MP 11.14; EUS-McG 43027–43029). Plain words: "the Roman governor", "a new governor". Documented.

## 2. Events (in time order)

E1. **Gave away his inheritance.** MP-long, Greek: "μακρὰ μὲν χαίρειν εἰπὼν τρυφῆι καὶ πλούτου περιουσίαι, ὅλον δὲ ἑαυτὸν ἀναθεὶς τῶι τοῦ θεοῦ λόγωι. ἀποδόμενός γέ τοι τὰ εἰς αὐτὸν ἐκ προγόνων ἥκοντα γυμνοῖς, πηροῖς καὶ πένησιν τὰ πάντα διένειμεν, αὐτὸς δὲ ἐν ἀκτήμονι διῆγε βίωι" (GRK 24836–24840): "He bade a long farewell to luxury and plenty of wealth, and gave himself wholly to the word of God. He sold what came to him from his forebears and gave it all to the naked, the maimed and the poor, and lived a life without possessions." The shorter form, in English, confirms the sense: "for renouncing and despising the world, for sharing his possessions with the needy, for contempt of earthly hopes" (EUS 47074–47076, MP 11.2). CONTEMPORARY. Documented (the giving: both forms; "sold … all": longer form only, damaged OCR).

E2. **Presbyter at Caesarea, and his school.** "In his time we became acquainted with Pamphilus, that most eloquent man, of truly philosophical life, who was esteemed worthy of the office of presbyter in that parish." (EUS 43009–43010, 43036–43038, HE VII.32.25; Greek GRK 12922–12923). Eusebius wrote, in a separate work, "all the particulars of his life, and of the school which he established, and the trials which he endured in many confessions during the persecution, and the crown of martyrdom" (EUS 43041–43045). Students: Apphianus, "being instructed by that perfect martyr, Pamphilus" (EUS-McG 1433–1435, quoting MP 4; Greek GRK 23296–23297, "ὑπὸ Παμφίλωι τῶι μεγάλωι μάρτυρι"); Aedesius "took part for a long time in the school of Pamphilus" (GRK 23752–23753, "τῆς τε Παμφίλου διατριβῆς πλείονι χρόνωι μετέσχηκεν"). The bishop at Caesarea then was Agapius (EUS 42997–43010); not needed. CONTEMPORARY. Documented.

E3. **The library: gathered for the church.** Eusebius: in the Life "we give … a catalogue of the library which he collected of the works of Origen and of other ecclesiastical writers. Whoever desires may learn readily from this which of Origen's works have reached us." (EUS 36890–36894, HE VI.32.3; Greek GRK 5152–5156). Jerome, in a letter to Marcella kept by Rufinus: "The blessed Martyr Pamphilus … wished to rival Demetrius Phalereus and Pisistratus, in his zeal to establish a library of sacred books: he sought out all through the world representative works of great minds … but most of all he acquired at great expense all the books written by Origen, and gave them to the church at Cæsarea." (JER 49611–49618). Jerome: the Hebrew Matthew "has been preserved until the present day in the library at Cæsarea which Pamphilus so diligently gathered" (JER 39360–39361, DVI 3; Latin JER-L 48160, "bibliotheca quam Pamphilus martyr studiose confecit"). CONTEMPORARY (catalogue); LATER (Jerome). Documented that he gathered it and gave it to the church; "the best Christian library in the world" (CEN tradition entry) is the tree's own wording, not a held source's.

E4. **Copying Origen by hand.** Jerome, DVI 75 (392/3): "Pamphilus the presbyter, patron of Eusebius bishop of Cæsarea, was so inflamed with love of sacred literature, that he transcribed the greater part of the works of Origen with his own hand and these are still preserved in the library at Cæsarea." (JER 40958–40962). Latin: "Pamphilus presbyter, Eusebii Caesariensis episcopi necessarius, tanto Bibliothecae divinae amore flagravit, ut maximam partem Origenis voluminum sua manu descripserit, quae usque hodie in Caesariensi bibliotheca habentur." (JER-L 58486–58490; "Bibliothece divine … Cesariensi" as printed). Martianay: church writers so call holy Scripture (JER-L-ed 58553–58554). Photius: "is said to have copied most of Origen's commentaries on Scripture with his own hand" (PHOT 11510–11512). Eusebius's own lost Life is cited by McGiffert as the ground: "devoting himself chiefly to the study of Scripture, and to the transcription of mss. of it and of the works of Origen" (EUS-McG 43021–43024, editor's summary). LATER (Jerome, about 80 years); Documented as Jerome's report; "the greater part", not "all".
- **Quote candidate (Q1)**, DVI 75 continued: "I have twenty-five volumes of Commentaries of Origen, written in his hand, On the twelve prophets which I hug and guard with such joy, that I deem myself to have the wealth of Croesus. And if it is such joy to have one epistle of a martyr how much more to have so many thousand lines which seem to me to be traced in his blood." (JER 40962–40968). Latin: "Sed et in duodecim Prophetas viginti quinque Ἐξηγήσεων Origenis volumina, manu ejus exarata reperi, quae tanto amplector et servo gaudio, ut Croesi opes habere me credam. Si enim laetitia est, unam epistolam habere martyris, quanto magis tot millia versuum, quae mihi videtur sui sanguinis signasse vestigiis!" (JER-L 58490–58496; printed "vinginti", "Cresi", "letitia"). Sophronius's Greek beside it confirms the last clause: "μᾶλλον τοσαύτας χιλιάδας στίχων, δι' ὧν, ὡς ἐμοὶ δοκεῖ, τὰ ἴχνη τοῦ οἰκείου καταλέλοιπε πάθους" (JER-L 58501–58502). Variant: "per quae mihi videtur sui sanguinis signasse vestigia" (JER-L-ed 58587–58588). The same report in Rufinus's age: APOL-L 6641 and APOL-M 93628 quote "Origenis volumina manu Pamphili exarata". The Latin print is readable with minor OCR faults; two other held texts (the NPNF English and Sophronius's Greek) confirm the reading.

E5. **Copies of Scripture from Caesarea (editor-reported).** A colophon in a Vatican manuscript, as Lightfoot translates it: "Pamphilus and Eusebius corrected [this copy]" (EUS-McG 3822–3830); Taylor: "many copies of its fifth column alone were issued from Cæsarea under the direction of Pamphilus the martyr and Eusebius" (EUS-McG 3834–3838). The manuscript is **not held**; the report rests on editors. For the card, not the story.

E6. **What Eusebius wrote of him as a reader and giver of books (the lost Life).** Jerome quotes Book III of Eusebius's Life of Pamphilus (Apol. I.9): "What lover of books was there who did not find a friend in Pamphilus? If he knew of any of them being in want of the necessaries of life, he helped them to the full extent of his power. He would not only lend them copies of the Holy Scriptures to read, but would give them most readily, and that not only to men, but to women also if he saw that they were given to reading. He therefore kept a store of manuscripts, so that he might be able to give them to those who wished for them whenever occasion demanded. He himself however, wrote nothing whatever of his own, except private letters which he sent to his friends, so humble was his estimate of himself. But the treatises of the old writers he studied with the greatest diligence, and was constantly occupied in meditation upon them." (JER 51429–51443). Latin: "Quis studiosorum amicus non fuit Pamphili? Si quos videbat ad victum necessariis indigere, praebebat large quae poterat. Scripturas quoque sanctas non ad legendum tantum, sed et ad habendum, tribuebat promptissime. Nec solum viris, sed et feminis, quas vidisset lectioni deditas. Unde et multos codices praeparabat, ut cum necessitas poposcisset, volentibus largiretur. Et ipse quidem proprii operis nihil omnino scripsit, exceptis epistolis, quas ad amicos forte mittebat; in tantum se humilitate dejecerat." (JER-L 31255–31266). CONTEMPORARY (Eusebius), held only in Jerome's Latin, quoted by Jerome as evidence against Pamphilus's authorship (D1). The Life is lost (EUS-McG 43032–43033). Documented as Eusebius's words in Jerome's quotation.

E7. **Persecution, arrest and torture, late 307.** "Among these was my dearest friend Pamphilus, who was by reason of every virtue the most illustrious of the martyrs in our time. Urbanus first tested him in rhetorical philosophy and learning; and afterwards endeavored to compel him to sacrifice. But as he saw that he refused and in nowise regarded his threats, being exceedingly angry, he ordered him to be tormented with severest tortures. And when the brutal man, after he had almost satiated himself with these tortures by continuous and prolonged scrapings in his sides, was yet covered with shame before all, he put him also with the confessors in prison." (EUS 46694–46709, MP 7.4–6; Greek GRK 24045–24050). The date: "Pamphilus was thrown into prison in the fifth year of the persecution and as late as November of that year, i.e. between November, 307, and April, 308" (EUS-McG 47123–47126). CONTEMPORARY. Documented.

E8. **The Defense, written in prison with Eusebius, 307–309/10.** Eusebius: "whatever it has seemed necessary to record about him can be found in the Apology in his behalf written by us and Pamphilus, the holy martyr of our day. We prepared this carefully and did the work jointly on account of faultfinders." (EUS 36972–36976, HE VI.33.4). Greek: "ἐκ τῆς ὑπὲρ αὐτοῦ πεπονημένης ἡμῖν τε καὶ τῷ καθ' ἡμᾶς ἱερῷ μάρτυρι Παμφίλῳ ἀπολογίας πάρεστιν ἀναλέξασθαι, ἣν τῶν φιλαιτίων ἕνεκα συμπονήσαντες ἀλλήλοις διὰ σπουδῆς πεποιήμεθα" (GRK 5258–5261). Photius: "It is in six books, five of which were written by Pamphilus when in prison in the company of Eusebius. The sixth is the work of Eusebius alone, after the martyr, having been deprived of life by the sword, was removed to God" (PHOT 11437–11442). Photius's Greek, as Delarue quotes it: "Πάμφιλος σὺν Εὐσεβίῳ καθειργμένος τῷ οἰκήματι συνεγράψατο" ("shut up in the room with Eusebius", APOL-M 40541). McGiffert: Eusebius "spent a great deal of time with him"; that Eusebius was himself imprisoned has "no other evidence … and in the face of Eusebius' own silence it is safer perhaps to assume (with most historians) that he simply visited Pamphilus in his prison" (EUS-McG 1479–1489). Documented that Pamphilus was in prison and that the two wrote it together (Eusebius's own word); where Eusebius was, Contested.

E9. **To whom it was written.** Rufinus's Latin heading: "Praefatio ad Confessores ad metalla Palaestinae damnatos" (APOL-L 14112; APOL-M 41939). Photius: "addressed to those who were condemned to the mines for the sake of Christ, the chief of whom was Patermythius, who shortly after the death of Pamphilus ended his life at the stake with others" (PHOT 11502–11506). Eusebius tells of confessors condemned "to the copper mines in Phæno in Palestine", their ankles disabled with hot irons (EUS 46667–46680, MP 7.2–3). Documented. Plain words: "Christians who had confessed the faith and been sent to forced labor in the copper mines of Palestine".

E10. **What the Defense argues (Book I).** It opens by telling the confessors that some condemn Origen out of ignorance, others out of malice, and treat anyone who reads him as a heretic (APOL-L 14118–14166; APOL-M 41940–42045). Its first argument: Origen spoke "with great fear of God and with all humility", and asked pardon for what came to him in deep searching of the Scriptures.
- **Quote candidate (Q3)**, Latin: "quae cum exponit, frequenter addere solet, et profiteri se non haec quasi definitiva pronuntiare sententia, nec statuto dogmate terminare, sed inquirere pro viribus, et sensum discutere Scripturarum, nec tamen profiteri quod integre perfecteque comprehenderit" (APOL-L 14211–14215). APOL-M reads the same with OCR faults ("detinitiva", "stalulo dogmate tertinare", "iutegre", APOL-M 42051–42056). Next: "sed cum omni humilitate et veritate non erubescit fateri haec sibi non liquere" (APOL-L 14221–14222; APOL-M 42091–42093). And: "ut si quis melius de his locis … dixerit, vel exposuerit, illi potius, qui rectius dixerit, quam sibi esset auscultandum" (APOL-L 14225–14229; APOL-M 42096–42099). Both prints are rough OCR; they agree with each other; no English is held. Documented as the Defense's words in Rufinus's Latin; whose words (Pamphilus's or Eusebius's) is D1. Jerome charged that Rufinus altered the passages on the Son and the Spirit (JER 51386–51390); this passage is not on those subjects.
- Photius's summary of the whole: fifteen charges against Origen "which he declares to be mere slanders, proving it by quotations from his writings in his fourth book, and refuting them by the evidence of others on his behalf in the fifth" (PHOT 11400–11404); the Defense on the attack on Origen at Alexandria (PHOT 11490–11499). Photius judged it "not a refutation of the charges … but rather supports the accusation" (PHOT 11378–11380). LATE; a hostile reader's view.

E11. **The death, February 309 or 310.** "It is time to describe the great and celebrated spectacle of Pamphilus, a man thrice dear to me, and of those who finished their course with him. They were twelve in all … Of these the leader and the only one honored with the position of presbyter at Cæsarea, was Pamphilus" (EUS 47068–47074, MP 11.1–2). "After these persons had continued in prison for two entire years" (EUS 47106–47107). Brought with five Egyptians before Firmilian (EUS 47131–47134); "he proceeded against Pamphilus and his companions. And having learned that already under former tortures they had manifested an unchangeable zeal for the faith, he asked them if they would now obey. And receiving from every one of them only this one answer, as their last word of confession in martyrdom, he inflicted on them punishment similar to the others." (EUS 47193–47198, MP 11.14). The manner: MP-long, "παραπλησίως κεφαλικὴν τιμωρίαν κατ' αὐτῶν ἐξενεγκάμενος ἐπάγει" ("he passed the same sentence of beheading on them", GRK 25242–25243); Photius, "deprived of life by the sword" (PHOT 11440–11441). Documented (beheading: longer form and Photius; the shorter English says only "similar").
- **Porphyry.** "a young man, one of the household servants of Pamphilus, who had been educated in the noble life and instruction of such a man, learning the sentence passed upon his master, cried out from the midst of the crowd asking that their bodies might be buried" (EUS 47200–47204); tortured and burned "before the death of his earthly master" (EUS 47205–47226). MP-long: "Πορφύριος ἦν ὁ μακάριος, θρέμμα γνήσιον Παμφίλου οὐδ' ὅλων ὀκτωκαίδεκα ἐτῶν" ("a true foster-son of Pamphilus, not quite eighteen", GRK 25247–25248); "τὸ μὲν δοκεῖν τοῦ Παμφίλου γεγονὼς οἰκέτης, διαθέσει γε μὴν ἀδελφοῦ καὶ μᾶλλον γνησίου παιδός" ("in name Pamphilus's servant, but in affection a brother, or rather a true son", GRK 24696–24697). Documented.
- **The bodies (a wonder).** "kept as food for the wild beasts for four days and as many nights. But since, strange to say, through the providential care of God, nothing approached them … they were taken up uninjured" (EUS 47317–47323). In Eusebius's voice only, or left out.
- Eusebius's grief: "my dearest friend" (EUS 46694); "thrice dear to me" (EUS 47069); MP-long: "ὁ ἐμὸς δεσπότης (οὐ γὰρ ἑτέρως προσειπεῖν ἔστι μοι θέμις τὸν θεσπέσιον καὶ μακάριον ὡς ἀληθῶς Πάμφιλον)" ("my master: for it is not right for me to call the divine and truly blessed Pamphilus otherwise", GRK 24670–24672).

E12. **Eusebius takes his name and writes his Life.** "after his death Eusebius showed his affection and respect for him by adopting his name, styling himself Eusebius Pamphili" (EUS-McG 43011–43013, editor). Jerome: "His surname Pamphilus arose from his friendship for Pamphilus the martyr." (JER 41076–41077, DVI 81). The Life in three books (EUS 47082–47085, MP 11.3); lost (EUS-McG 43032–43033). Documented; the Eusebius story carries the name.

## 3. Impact, heaviest first (what later witnesses carried on and used)

I1. **The library, used by those after him.** Eusebius sent readers to its catalogue for Origen's works (E3). McGiffert: "Pamphilus is chiefly celebrated for the library which he collected at Cæsarea and to which Eusebius owes a large part of the materials of his history. Jerome also made extensive use of it." (EUS-McG 43017–43020; editor). Jerome used it: the Hebrew Matthew (JER 39360–39361), the twenty-five volumes (E4), the six volumes of Eusebius's Defense (JER 54979–54981). After him: "This library was afterwards partly destroyed; but Acatius and later on Euzoius, Bishops of that church, endeavoured to reestablish it in parchment volumes. The last of these recovered a great many works, and left us an inventory of them" (JER 49618–49622, Jerome's letter in Rufinus). Documented as each witness says; "Eusebius owes a large part": editor.

I2. **The Defense, a weapon in the quarrel over Origen about 400 (defended).** Rufinus put Book I into Latin in 397, under Pamphilus's name (JER-ed 45085, 45110–45114; JER 45144–45147). Jerome, about 401: "You thought that men would believe the martyr, though they would have turned in abhorrence from the chief of the Arians." (JER 51402–51404); "The consciences of a great many persons have been wounded by the book which you have published under the name of the martyr; they give no heed to the authority of the bishops who condemn Origen, since they think that a martyr has praised him." (JER 51454–51459). Only Book I survives, in Rufinus's Latin (EUS-McG 3661–3663; CEN). What it defended: Origen's faith and good name (E10). Jerome's live story tells his break with Rufinus over Origen (CEN `jerome`). Documented.

I3. **Socrates kept him as a witness for Origen.** "That holy man Pamphilus, and Eusebius who was surnamed after him, are trustworthy witnesses on this subject: both these witnesses in their joint life of Origen, and admirable defense of him in answer to such as were prejudiced against him" (SOC 10542–10546, III.7; Latin SOC-L 29891, "testes sunt locupletissimi S. ## 5. Where sources disagree or come late

D1. **Who wrote the Defense.** Each witness, in time order:
- **Eusebius** (HE VI.33.4), the co-author: "written by us and Pamphilus … We prepared this carefully and did the work jointly" (E8). And in the lost Life, as Jerome quotes it: "He himself however, wrote nothing whatever of his own, except private letters" (E6).
- **Jerome, DVI 75** (392/3, "the fourteenth year of the Emperor Theodosius", JER 38785): "He wrote an Apology for Origen before Eusebius had written his" (JER 40968–40969; Latin "Scripsit, antequam Eusebius Caesariensis scriberet, Apologeticum pro Origene", JER-L 58496–58498).
- **Rufinus** (397): translates Book I as Pamphilus's, but writes of "the book which he is said to have written in his defence in Greek" (JER 45146–45147; Latin "librum eius, quem pro Origene in Graeco scripsisse traditur", APOL-L 13961–13962, APOL-M 41897).
- **Jerome, Letter 84** (400): "shew me any other work of Pamphilus; you will nowhere find any, this is his only one. How then can I know that it is by Pamphilus?" (JER-E 19399–19401); the book "contains nearly the first thousand lines of Eusebius's sixth book in defence of Origen" (JER-E 19408–19410); "the book belongs not to Pamphilus but to Didymus or somebody else" (JER-E 19423–19425); and, if Pamphilus wrote it, "only by Pamphilus not yet a martyr … that he might efface his error by a martyr's death, and wash away his one fault by shedding his blood" (JER-E 19426–19431).
- **Jerome, Apology against Rufinus** (about 401–402): Eusebius, "the standard bearer of the Arian faction, wrote a large and elaborate work in six books in defence of Origen … The first of these six books you have translated and assigned it to the martyr" (JER 51379–51384); Rufinus changed "a few statements about the Son of God and the holy Spirit" (JER 51386–51388); citing the Life (E6) (JER 51418–51443); "I never saw in writing the book which was known as the work of Pamphilus till I read it in your own manuscript" (JER 53964–53966); he admits that "ten years ago" he listed it under Pamphilus's name in his catalogue, "supposing the matter to be as it had been brought before the public by you" (JER 53975–53981; Latin JER-L 34746–34752); "in the library of Cæsarea I found the six volumes of Eusebius' Apology for Origen" (JER 54979–54981; Latin "in Caesariensi Bibliotheca Eusebii sex volumina reperi Ἀπολογίας ὑπὲρ Ὠριγένους", JER-L 36197–36215).
- **Rufinus's answer** (Apology, about 400): Jerome says the martyr's works "are either not to be considered genuine or if genuine, to be treated with contempt … Now even the Martyrs fail to gain any respect from him" (RUF, JER 50158–50166); and even if it were "not that of the martyr Pamphilus, but of some other unknown member of the church", the book "sets out quotations from the works of Origen himself" (JER 50210–50214).
- **Socrates** (5th c.): their "joint" defense; also "the books which he wrote" (I3).
- **Photius** (9th c.): six books, five by Pamphilus in prison with Eusebius, the sixth by Eusebius alone after his death (E8).
- **Editors.** McGiffert: "it is certain that Pamphilus was a joint author of it, and it is quite probable that Rufinus was true to his original in ascribing to Pamphilus all the explanations which introduce and connect the extracts from Origen" (EUS-McG 3672–3677); "Jerome's unfairness and dishonesty in this matter have been pointed out by Lightfoot" (EUS-McG 3671–3672). Lightfoot reconciles the Life's "wrote nothing of his own" with joint work: "the very form of the expression in the original … was probably chosen so as to exclude this work of compilation and partnership" (JER-ed note, JER 51419–51424). Fremantle: "There seems however no reason to doubt, notwithstanding Jerome's violent protestations, that Pamphilus was associated with Eusebius throughout the work" (JER-ed 45129–45132). Editors' views; Dominant Modern Reconstruction in the held editions.
- Jerome's two counts differ: the first of six books (Apology) and "the first thousand lines of … the sixth book" (Letter 84); the Letter 84 translator notes "Jerome is again misled" if the text is sound (JER-E 19410–19412). Card only.
- Confidence: that both men worked on it: Documented (Eusebius's own word), with Jerome's denial Documented as his charge; how the work was shared: Contested.

D2. **Was Eusebius in prison with him?** Photius implies it (E8); Eusebius never says so; McGiffert prefers that he visited (E8). The charge that Eusebius came out of prison unharmed belongs to the Eusebius story. Contested; the story says only "with Eusebius".

D3. **309 or 310.** S5. Say "about 310" or "309 or 310".

D4. **Study at Alexandria.** S3. Late and doubted.

D5. **Beheading.** The shorter form (English) gives only "punishment similar to the others"; the longer form and Photius give the sword (E11). Documented in two held witnesses; the shorter is silent, not contrary.

D6. **The "Exposition of the Chapters of the Acts" (ANF6 16856–16871).** Ascribed to Pamphilus in ANF; "Others give them to Euthalius, so they are not counted" (CEN `sourcing`). Not used.

D7. **The census's "Pamphilus wrote nothing except some short letters to his friends"** (CEN `legacy`) is Jerome, Apol. II.23 (JER 53981–53983), Jerome's report of the Life. The story tells the Life's own sentence (E6) in paraphrase.
