# Outline: Pamphilus of Caesarea

Sheet items (S, E, I, L, D) are in `Source_Sheet.md`, which gives the file and line for each. EUS is McGiffert's English of Eusebius's *Church History* (HE) and *Martyrs of Palestine* (MP); GRK the Greek of both, with the longer form of MP (MP-long); JER Jerome and Rufinus in English, JER-L Jerome's Latin; JER-E Jerome's Letter 84; APOL-L and APOL-M the Defense, Book I, in Rufinus's Latin (two prints); PHOT Photius in English; SOC Socrates in English.

- **The essence:** Pamphilus, a presbyter of the church at Caesarea in Roman Palestine, gathered a library of sacred books for his church and, Jerome says, copied most of Origen's books with his own hand; Jerome held twenty-five of those volumes about eighty years later (E3, E4; the library Documented by Eusebius and Jerome; the copying Documented as Jerome's report, with Photius's "is said"). Arrested in the Great Persecution, he wrote a Defense of Origen in prison, working with his pupil and friend Eusebius, and was put to death for his faith about 310 (E7, E8, E11; Documented, Eusebius's own account).
- **Also told:**
  - **The library and the giving of books.** He gave away his inheritance to the poor; he bought Origen's books at great cost and gave them to the church; he copied them; he gave copies of the Scriptures to men and to women who loved to read (E1, E3, E4, E6; Documented, Eusebius and Jerome; "sold all he had": Eusebius's longer form only).
  - **The Defense.** Written to Christians condemned to the copper mines, it argued that Origen had searched the Scriptures as an inquirer, not laid down doctrine, and that his accusers misread him. Only its first book survives, in Rufinus's Latin (E9, E10, I2; Documented as the book's words; its authorship Contested, D1).
  - **The death.** Tortured, held two years, condemned with eleven others and beheaded, in February 309 or 310; his young servant Porphyry died first, asking for their bodies to be buried (E7, E11; Documented; year Contested, D3).
  - **The dispute, each witness credited.** Eusebius said the two wrote it together. Jerome first listed it under Pamphilus's name; about ten years later he said it was Eusebius's, citing Eusebius's own Life that Pamphilus wrote nothing but letters, and accused Rufinus of putting a martyr's name on it. Rufinus answered that Jerome no longer spared even the martyrs. Photius, five centuries later, says Pamphilus wrote five books with Eusebius, and Eusebius the sixth alone. Modern editors hold it joint work (D1; each Documented as that witness's words; the sharing Contested).
  - **What came of him.** The library fed Eusebius's history and Jerome's work, and later bishops restored it; the Defense became a weapon in the quarrel over Origen about 400 (I1, I2; Documented as each says).
- **Length:** about 3 minutes 45 seconds (about 560 words). Counted first: the three quotes, about 200 words (about 180 with Q2's second sentence dropped); the plain sense of the words to explain, about 55 words (presbyter, Origen, the Great Persecution, confessors in the mines, Croesus, martyr, Arians); the witnesses credited, about 80 words (Eusebius twice, Jerome three times, Rufinus twice, Photius, the editors once). That is about 300 words before any event is told. Why past three minutes: the job names four turns (the copying and library, the Defense from prison, the death, the authorship dispute), and the last is a scene with four witnesses who differ; a third goes to what came of him. Why not longer: one surviving book, and a life known almost wholly from one friend. Cut first, if needed: Porphyry down to one clause (beat 5), the Photius clause (beat 6), Socrates (What came 3), Berytus and the inheritance down to one clause (beat 1).
- **Setting:**
  - Time: arrested late in 307, in the fifth year of the Great Persecution (E7; S5); two years in prison; died in February 309 or 310 (S5, D3; say "about 310"). No birth year is known (S5). The dispute: Jerome's catalogue 392/3 (D1), Rufinus's Latin 397, Jerome's attack about 400–402.
  - Place: Caesarea, a port on the coast of Roman Palestine, in today's Israel (S1). Born at Berytus, today's Beirut (S2, Eusebius's longer form). A later report has him study at Alexandria (S3; doubted).
  - Tradition: told within Alexandrian Christianity, Origen's line (S4); one clause at most, for the tie to Origen. The church of Caesarea is the place.
  - Language: he and Eusebius wrote in Greek; the Defense survives only in Latin (sheet, Original language).

**Words to explain** (the first time each is heard; plain word or short gloss, worked into the sentence)

- **presbyter**: "a presbyter, one of the elders who served the church beside its bishop". After that, "presbyter" bare.
- **Origen**: "Origen, the great Bible scholar of the century before, who had taught at Caesarea" (CEN `origen`). Origen died about 253, before Pamphilus's time; never imply they met.
- **the Great Persecution**: "the last and greatest persecution of Christians by Rome's emperors, which began in 303".
- **confessors / condemned to the mines** (E9): "Christians who had refused to give up the faith and been sent to forced labor in the mines of Palestine".
- **the Defense of Origen** (the job's name): "a book defending Origen". Use "Apology" only if a quote needs it; say once that "apology" here means a defense.
- **sacrifice** (E7): "to offer sacrifice to the Roman gods".
- **Croesus** (Q1): "Croesus, a king of old famous for his riches". The narrator gives it in the same breath.
- **commentaries** (Q1): "Origen's commentaries, his books explaining Scripture".
- **the twelve prophets** (Q1): "the twelve short books of prophecy at the end of the Old Testament", in the same breath.
- **Arian / chief of the Arians** (Jerome's charge, D1): "those who sided with Arius, a priest who taught that the Son had a beginning". Never "Arian" bare.
- **heretic** (Jerome): "a teacher of false doctrine", in Jerome's voice only.
- Do not use: "Hexapla", "Septuagint", "recension", "menologion", "catechetical school" (Leave out).

- **Shape:** Martyr. A life that comes to a stand, and what the stand cost: he kept and copied a dead teacher's books, defended that teacher from prison, and died for his own faith. The impact is in how the church remembered it and what it did with that memory: Jerome treasured his copies as if traced in his blood, and then fought over whether a martyr could be named as Origen's defender. The trap is the death swallowing the story: the library and the Defense get as much room as the end, and the dispute after his death is part of what came of him.
- **Sibling:**
  - `eusebius-of-caesarea` (batch 7, outline read; job: the bishop-historian, his history, the persecution he watched, Constantine, Nicaea, the charges at Tyre). It carries Pamphilus as one clause (his teacher and friend, whose name he took, who died a martyr) and leaves the library and the Defense to this story. Here: Eusebius acts as pupil, co-worker and witness; his own life, his history and the Tyre charge are his story's and are not told. His taking of Pamphilus's name is one clause. That story's quotes ("a lonely and untrodden path", "with our own eyes", Potamo's question, "not without inquiry") are not used.
  - `origen` (live text read). It already says "Pamphilus of Caesarea and Eusebius wrote in his defense", and holds the quotes "Be careful not to change your mind because of us" and "the hardworking Origen" (barred). Here: what Origen taught is one clause; this story tells the Defense's argument (Q3) and the books' survival.
  - `jerome` (live text read). It tells his break with Rufinus over Origen about 400 and calls Origen's teachings poisonous in his voice. Here: Jerome acts twice, as the collector who prized the volumes (Q1) and as the witness who denied the Defense to Pamphilus (beat 6). His Bible work and the rest of the quarrel are his story's.
  - `methodius-of-olympus` (live text read): the Defense's sixth book answered him. Not told here (Leave out).
- **Openings:**
  1. **Chosen.** Jerome with the volumes (Q1; E4). In about three sentences: around 392 (D1; "about eighty years after his death" is safer than a place), the scholar Jerome wrote that he had found, and kept, twenty-five volumes of Origen's commentaries copied in one man's hand; then Q1, from "I embrace" (or the whole); then straight into the essence: the man was Pamphilus, a presbyter of Caesarea, who copied most of Origen's books and died a martyr. Name Pamphilus inside the first sentence or the second ("copied in the hand of Pamphilus"), not as a held-back reveal. Give "Croesus" its plain sense in the same breath.
  2. Not chosen: the governor first testing him on rhetoric and philosophy before ordering him to sacrifice (E7). Strong, but it belongs in time order at beat 4.

**Story beats** (the opening, the essence and beat 1 make the first minute, about 150 words. The opening reaches ahead to Jerome; the rest follows the life once, in order. Jerome's later attack comes only at beat 6, after the death)

1. **From Beirut to Caesarea.** He came from Berytus, today's Beirut, of a noble family, and he had himself held public office with honor in his home city (Eusebius's longer form; damaged OCR, read by hand), and was schooled there; he sold what came to him from his forebears and gave it all to the poor, Eusebius writes (S2, E1; Documented in Eusebius's longer form; the shorter form confirms "sharing his possessions with the needy"). A much later writer says he studied at Alexandria (S3; optional, one clause, credited; Contested). At Caesarea he was made a presbyter, and he set up a school (E2; Documented). Eusebius came to know him there; Eusebius later called him "my master" (E11, MP-long, damaged OCR) (Greek read by hand; no other held print; the card gives the English alone), and his editor calls him Eusebius's teacher (E12; editor). Say "his pupil and friend Eusebius" once; Widely Accepted. Reason for the act: the record gives none beyond Eusebius's "he gave himself wholly to the word of God" (E1); add no motive.
2. **The library and the copying.** Origen had taught at Caesarea in the century before (CEN `origen`). Pamphilus set out to gather a library of sacred books for the church: he searched the world for the best works, and above all bought, at great cost, every book Origen had written, and gave them to the church at Caesarea, Jerome writes (E3; credit Jerome). He copied most of Origen's works with his own hand (E4; Jerome; "most", not "all"). Eusebius later wrote a catalogue of the library in his Life of Pamphilus, so readers could learn which of Origen's works survived (E3; one clause).
3. **What he did with the books.** Quote 2, from Eusebius's Life, as Jerome kept it (E6): he gave the Scriptures not only to read but to keep, to women as well as men. The listener must hear that the Life is lost and this passage survives only because Jerome quoted it, for a reason the story tells at beat 6 (Do not say: no more of beat 6 here). Reason for the act: the record's own, "so that … when need arose" (Q2).
4. **Arrest, torture, prison: late 307.** In the fifth year of the Great Persecution the governor of Palestine first tested him in rhetoric, philosophy and learning, then tried to force him to sacrifice; when he refused, he had his sides scraped in long torture, then put him in prison with other confessors, Eusebius writes (E7; Documented; keep the torture plain, D: force). He stayed there two years (E11).
5. **The Defense, from prison.** In prison Pamphilus worked on a Defense of Origen, "jointly", Eusebius writes, with Eusebius himself (E8; credit Eusebius; never say Eusebius was in prison, D2). It was addressed to confessors condemned to the copper mines of Palestine, who, it says, had come to think of Origen what some others did (E9, E10; APOL-L 14118–14121). Its first argument, Quote 3: Origen wrote as one searching, not laying down doctrine (E10; credit "the Defense", in Rufinus's Latin). Then the death. In February of 309 or 310, a new governor brought him and his companions out, asked if they would now obey, heard from each the same answer, and condemned them; twelve died in all, Pamphilus the only presbyter among them, and he was beheaded (E11; Documented; "beheaded": Photius, "by the sword"; Eusebius's longer form, "a capital sentence"). Optional (cut first): his young servant Porphyry, not yet eighteen, cried out from the crowd to ask that the bodies be buried, and was tortured and burned before his master died (E11). Eusebius called him "my dearest friend" (one phrase, optional) and took his name, calling himself Eusebius of Pamphilus (E12; one clause; the Eusebius story's).
6. **The dispute over the Defense (the scene).** Told after the death, in time order, each witness credited, the narrator taking no side:
   - Eusebius, the co-worker: "written by us and Pamphilus", "jointly" (E8; already heard at beat 5; refer back in one clause, do not retell).
   - Jerome, about 392: listed the Defense under Pamphilus's name, written "before Eusebius had written his" (D1).
   - Rufinus, in 397: put its first book into Latin under Pamphilus's name, calling it the book "he is said to have written" (D1). His Latin is the only form that survives (I2).
   - Jerome, about 400, in a letter (Letter 84): he began to doubt the book was Pamphilus's: "How then can I know that it is by Pamphilus?" (JER-E 19399–19401; paraphrase, or that one question).
   - Rufinus answered him (his Apology, about 400) that Jerome would now not spare even the martyrs (JER 50158–50167, 50208–50210; paraphrase).
   - Jerome, in his books against Rufinus (about 401–402), about ten years after his first list: the work was Eusebius's, whom he called the chief of the Arians (gloss); he had found Eusebius's six books in the library at Caesarea, and the first was the one Rufinus had put under the martyr's name; Rufinus, he said, had changed its words on the Son and the Spirit, so that readers would trust a martyr's praise of Origen (D1). His proof: Eusebius's own Life, which says Pamphilus wrote nothing of his own except letters to friends (E6; the next lines after Q2; paraphrase). He admitted he had once believed it Pamphilus's himself (D1).
   - Optional, one clause: Photius, in Constantinople five centuries later, who read all six books, says Pamphilus wrote five in prison with Eusebius, and Eusebius the sixth alone after his death (E8, D1).
   - The narrator ends the scene plainly: Eusebius's two statements stand side by side, one that the Defense was joint work and one that Pamphilus wrote nothing of his own; modern editors read the Defense as joint work, mostly Origen's own words strung together with comments (D1; credit "modern editors"). Do not settle it beyond that.

**What came of him** (about a third of the story; heaviest first)

1. **The library outlived him.** Eusebius wrote his history with it, his English editor judged (I1; credit the editor once); Jerome worked from it, and found there both the volumes in Pamphilus's hand and Eusebius's six books of the Defense (I1, E4, D1). Later the library was partly destroyed, and two bishops of Caesarea tried to restore it on parchment, Jerome writes (I1; one clause; the bishops by role).
2. **The Defense became a weapon (what he defended).** About 400 it stood at the center of the quarrel over Origen: Jerome complained that readers no longer heeded the bishops who condemned Origen, "since they think a martyr praised him" (I2; a short phrase in the narrator's sentence, or paraphrase). What it defended, the good name and faith of a teacher a century dead, is the point to land (E10, I2). The Jerome story tells the break with Rufinus; here one clause.
3. **Remembered as a witness.** Socrates, a historian a century and more later, still called Pamphilus and Eusebius trustworthy witnesses for Origen in their joint defense (I3; optional, cut first).

**Connection to carry into the story:** what passed from Pamphilus was Origen's written work itself, copied and kept in a church library, and a public defense of Origen's good name made by a man who then died a martyr. The listener must be told three things. First, how the books passed: Origen was dead; Pamphilus bought his books, copied most of them by hand, and gave them to the church, so that a later generation, Eusebius and then Jerome, could read and work from them (E3, E4, I1). Second, why the Defense mattered after his death: it carried a martyr's authority, and that is why its authorship became a fight, with Jerome saying Rufinus had used a martyr's name to protect Origen and Rufinus saying Jerome would not spare even the martyrs (D1, I2). Third, that the record is mostly one friend's: Eusebius tells almost all of his life, and the same Eusebius both said they wrote the Defense together and, in his Life, that Pamphilus wrote nothing of his own; say this once, inside the beat 6 scene. Tell it through acts: the buying, the copying, the giving, the writing in prison, the quarrel over the name. Not as a "this matters because" sentence.

**Ending:** close on the person. Either (a) the opening's image seen again, in paraphrase, not the quote repeated: the many thousand lines in his hand that Jerome had once said seemed marked with his blood; or (b) his death, with Eusebius's line that on that day "the chief gate was opened" to the martyr Pamphilus (EUS 47276–47278, MP 11.23; a phrase in Eusebius's voice), placed after What came only if the death has not been told; it has, so (b) needs care. Recommended: (a), placed after What came, with no moral added (no "his true legacy was …").

**Links**

- Jerome: kept twenty-five volumes in his hand; used his library; later denied him the Defense (L3).

**Who tells it**

- **Eusebius**, his pupil and friend, in his own time: the family, the giving away, the school, the arrest, the torture, the prison, the death, and the joint work on the Defense (beats 1, 2, 4, 5). Admiring: he calls him "my master" and "thrice dear". The whole life rests on him; say so once, inside the beat 6 sentence about his two statements.
- **Jerome**, about 80 to 95 years later, a collector who used the library, then a party to the quarrel over Origen: the library and the copying (beat 2, Q1), and the attack on the Defense's authorship (beat 6). Credit him every time; his attack is a hostile view, told openly.
- **Rufinus**, a party on the other side: the Latin of the Defense, "said to have written", and his reply (beat 6).
- **Photius**, five centuries later, who says he read all six books (beat 6, optional).
- **Modern editors** (McGiffert, Lightfoot), by role, once: the Defense was joint work (beat 6); Eusebius owed much of his history to the library (What came 1).
- **The doubt is itself the story** at beat 6: the scene is Jerome's list, Rufinus's Latin, Jerome's reversal with his proof from the Life, and Rufinus's answer.

**Do not say**

- Any word in "Words to explain" used without its plain sense the first time it is heard.
- Not "Pamphilus founded the greatest library of the ancient world" or "the best Christian library in the world". Say he gathered a library of sacred books and gave it to the church (E3).
- Not "he copied all of Origen's works". Say "most of" or "the greater part" (E4).
- Not "Pamphilus studied under Origen" or "met Origen". Origen died about 253; Pamphilus came later (CEN `origen`).
- Not "he studied at Alexandria under Pierius" as fact. Only "a much later writer says", or leave out (S3).
- Not "Eusebius was in prison with him" or "they shared a cell". Say "with Eusebius" or "working with Eusebius" (E8, D2).
- Not "Pamphilus wrote the Defense" or "Eusebius wrote the Defense" in the narrator's voice. Give each witness (D1).
- Not "Jerome lied", "Jerome was dishonest" or "Rufinus forged it". The charges are each man's; "unfairness and dishonesty" is McGiffert's word and is left out of the story (D1).
- Not "Rufinus changed the Defense" as fact. It is Jerome's charge (D1).
- Not "Jerome changed his mind out of spite" or any motive for his reversal. Jerome gives his own reason: he read the six books at Caesarea (D1).
- Not "Pamphilus wrote nothing" as fact. It is Eusebius's sentence in the Life, as Jerome quotes it (E6).
- Not "the Defense survives" or "we have the Defense". Only its first book, in Rufinus's Latin (I2).
- Not "Pamphilus says" before Quote 3. Say "the Defense says" (D1).
- Not "he died in 309" or "in 310" as fact. Say "about 310", or "in February of 309 or 310" (D3).
- Not "burned" or "crucified". Say "put to death by the sword", or "beheaded, a later writer says" (E11, D5). Porphyry was burned; one of the twelve was crucified; do not mix them.
- Not "the beasts would not touch the bodies" in the narrator's voice. In Eusebius's voice only, or left out (E11).
- Not "Pamphilus taught Origen's errors" or "Pamphilus was an Origenist heretic". Jerome's "heretic" is for Origen and in Jerome's voice only.
- Not "Eusebius took the name after his death" as Eusebius's own statement. The timing is his editor's (E12); say only "took his name".
- Not "Porphyry was his son". Eusebius says servant, "in affection a brother, or rather a true son" (E11).
- Not "he gave the Scriptures to women, which was unheard of". The record says only that he did (Q2); add no comparison.
