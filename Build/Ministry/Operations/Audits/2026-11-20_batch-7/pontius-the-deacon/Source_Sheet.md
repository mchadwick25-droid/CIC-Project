# Source sheet: Pontius the Deacon

Researcher's sheet for batch 7 (2026-11-20). The job: the deacon who shared Cyprian's exile and wrote his Life and Passion, which some call the oldest surviving Christian biography; why he wrote it, what he chose to tell and leave out, the dream at Curubis, and his grief at being left behind. Credited to him by Jerome; the text itself is unsigned. Shape: Teacher. The death and the plague are clauses here; `cyprian-of-carthage` (this batch, done) tells them. The writer uses the outline, the shapes and the standard only; this sheet is the evidence behind the outline.

## Witness kinds

- OWN = the *Life* itself, written by Cyprian's companion in exile, about a year after the death (the date is the scholars' view, below). Open praise throughout.
- OWN-reported = Cyprian's own words as the *Life* reports them (the dream).
- CONTEMP record = the *Acta* (KNOPF), and Cyprian's own last letter (EP Ep. 81).
- LATER = Jerome, about 392, some 130 years after (JE). Medieval martyrologies (9th century on) and the Roman Martyrology, via AASS: late legend for Pontius's own end.
- MODERN = Rigault (17th c., via AASS), the Bollandists (AASS), Coxe (VE-ed), Benson 1897, Monceaux 1902, Harnack 1913, Delehaye 1921, the handbook SHK 1922 and the scholars it reports.

## Setting

**S1. Time.** Cyprian's exile at Curubis began after his hearing of 30 August 257 (KNOPF 4823–4824, "tertio kalendarum Septembrium"; 4836, 4850–4851). He died 14 September 258 (KNOPF 4881, 4955–4956). The *Life* was written about 259: "Man wird daher nicht irren, wenn man das Jahr 259 als Jahr der Abfassung feststellt (dies ist auch die herrschende Ansicht)" (HAR 379–381), because the martyrdom seems recent (HAR 373–376). HAR-view, "the prevailing view": Dominant Modern Reconstruction. J. Martin argued for a later date (SHK 31633–31634). Plain words: "a year or so after Cyprian's death".

**S2. Place.** Carthage, in today's Tunisia (census). Curubis, Cyprian's place of exile, in the province of Africa (HAR 1319–1320: "sie lag in Africa procons."). Plain words: "a small town on the coast, Curubis" (BEN 27288–27290: "little coast town").

**S3. Tradition.** Census `toldWithin`: `latin-pastoral-congregational-christianity`; the tree's spoken name, as in the Cyprian outline, "the Latin Pastoral Congregations". Do not say "Latin pastoral congregational Christianity" aloud.

## Events

**E1. Who he was, and who says so.** The *Life* never names its author. Harnack: "In keiner der uns erhaltenen Handschriften ist m. W. der Name des Pontius als Verfasser verzeichnet und auch im Cheltenh. fehlt er" (HAR 331–333), "In none of the manuscripts we have, as far as I know, is Pontius's name given as author, and it is missing in the Cheltenham list too." Jerome supplies it: "Pontius, deacon of Cyprian, sharing his exile until the day of his death, left a notable volume On the life and death of Cyprian" (JE 40833–40835; JL 57618–57620: "Pontius, diaconus Cypriani, usque ad diem passionis ejus cum ipso exsilium sustinens, egregium volumen vitae et passionis Cypriani reliquit"). LATER: Jerome's preface says he wrote to "the fourteenth year of the Emperor Theodosius" (JE 38785), that is 392 (the editor's note misprints 492), about 130 years after. Harnack: Jerome "followed a tradition whose source we do not know, but whose reliability we have not the least reason to doubt" (HAR 340–343; HAR-view). Delehaye: "Rien ne s'oppose à ce que nous adoptions le nom que lui donne S. Jérôme" (DEL 3907–3910). Most scholars accept Jerome (SHK 31626–31628). The office of deacon is also only Jerome's: the text gives neither the name nor the office (HAR 337–340). **Confidence: Widely Accepted** (one late witness, accepted by most). Plain words: "a deacon of Cyprian's, Pontius, as Jerome names him; the book itself is unsigned."

**E2. He went with Cyprian into exile, by choice.** "On that day whereon we first abode in the place of banishment (for the condescension of his love had chosen me among his household companions to a voluntary exile: would that he could also have chosen me to share his passion!)" (VE 28055–28059; VL 41012–41015: "nam et me inter domesticos comites dignatio caritatis eius delegerat exulem uoluntarium, quod utinam et in passione licuisset"; HAR 1298–1300, same). OWN. Documented as the text's own claim. Harnack: here a "we-passage" begins; the writer was present (HAR 1322–1323; HAR-view). The handbook reports that Reitzenstein held the eyewitness role invented (SHK 31630–31631: "hält Reitzenstein die Augenzeugenschaft für fingiert"); Delehaye answers that a forger would not have been so sparing (DEL 4266–4277). **Contested** only in that one scholar's view; the text's claim is Documented.

**E3. The place of exile.** He first imagines a filthy, waterless wilderness, then says God gave Cyprian "a sunny and suitable spot, a dwelling, secret as he wished", with "the number of the brethren who visited him, and then the kindness of the citizens themselves" (VE 28032–28051). OWN. Documented as Pontius's account. Benson (BEN 27383–27392) reads the passage as "affected" but takes it as describing a pleasant retreat. His one thought on exile, maybe Cyprian's own: "to the Christian, the whole of this world is one home" (VE 28015–28016; VL 40912). Benson thinks such lines are "fragments of [Cyprian's] conversation" (BEN 27397–27402; Benson's view).

**E4. The dream at Curubis.** On the first day there, before he fell asleep, Cyprian told them, a young man of more than human size led him to the governor's court. The governor wrote a sentence on his tablet without questioning him. The young man behind him read it, and since he could not speak it, he showed it: "with hand expanded and flattened like a blade, he imitated the stroke of the accustomed punishment ... I understood the future sentence of my passion" (VE 28062–28074; VL 41006–41029; HAR 1301–1313). Cyprian begged a delay of one day to put his affairs in order; the judge seemed moved; the young man signalled by twisting his fingers that the delay to the next day was granted; Cyprian woke glad of the delay but trembling at the doubt of its meaning (VE 28075–28090; HAR 1313–1355). OWN-reported (Cyprian's words, given by Pontius as "said he"). **Documented** as Pontius's report of what Cyprian told him; what the dream was is not for the narrator to judge. Harnack takes the young man as an angel (HAR 1326–1327; HAR-view) and sees no reason to doubt the dream was really dreamt (HAR 2867–2868). **Quote 2.**

**E5. Pontius's reading of the dream.** "This one day signified a year" (VE 28098–28099): Cyprian was put to death a year later, on the same day of the year that the dream came to him (VE 28099–28102; HAR 1361–1365). He adds that no one knew why it had been shown until the day it was fulfilled (VE 28113–28115). He also says the delay Cyprian asked for was to settle church business, above all care of the poor: "what affairs or what will had he to arrange, except ecclesiastical concerns?" (VE 28125–28126; HAR 1400–1402). OWN, Pontius's interpretation. Documented as his reading. Harnack: "Es folgt die Auslegung des Pontius, die nicht überzeugend ist" ("Pontius's interpretation follows, which does not convince"), and on "what could be more plain": "Das war nicht Cyprians Meinung" ("That was not Cyprian's view") (HAR 1379–1380); he thinks Cyprian meant his private affairs, which Pontius found awkward (HAR 1335–1337). HAR-view. **Check for the writer:** the year and day line up only roughly by the *Acta*: the hearing was 30 August 257 and the death 14 September 258 (KNOPF), and Pontius says the dream came on the day they reached Curubis, not the hearing day. Pontius gives no date. So "a year later, to the day" is Pontius's claim, not a fact the narrator states.

**E6. The last year and the last night.** A messenger came from Rome with news of Xistus's death (VE 28137–28139). Friends of high rank urged him to withdraw and offered places; Pontius says "he did not consent" (VE 28142–28149). He wished to die "in the very act of speaking about God" (VE 28158–28160). After his arrest at his gardens, held overnight in the officer's house, "we, his associates and friends, were as usual in his company" (VE 28183–28184). Next morning a soldier who had once been a Christian offered him dry clothes; Cyprian answered, "We apply medicines to annoyances which probably to-day will no longer exist" (VE 28219–28226; HAR 6110: "medellas adhibemus querelis"). OWN. Documented as Pontius's account. Delehaye: apart from the dream, this is the only saying of Cyprian's the companion records from all that time (DEL 4259–4263; Delehaye's view, and a fair reading of the text).

**E7. The trial and death, in one line each.** Of the trial: "he is interrogated as to his name. He answers who he is, and nothing more" (VE 28228–28230). The *Acta* hold the whole exchange (KNOPF 4882–4896). Pontius reads the governor's sentence as a prophecy fulfilled: he had been called "a standard-bearer" and "an enemy of the gods", and so he was (VE 28233–28251). Of the death he gives a few lines (VE 28252–28266, told in the Cyprian outline). Harnack: the death itself is not told in detail "aus Scheu" (out of awe) (HAR 1785; HAR-view). **Here a clause only**: Cyprian was beheaded at Carthage on 14 September 258, and Pontius was there (VE 28183–28184; KNOPF 4955–4956).

**E8. Why he wrote.** His stated aim: "placuit summatim pauca conscribere, non quo aliquem uel gentilium lateat tanti uiri uita, sed ut ad posteros quoque nostros incomparabile et grande documentum in immortalem memoriam porrigatur" (VL 40267–40270; HAR 459–462, "non quod [quo?]"; VE 27673–27677: "I have thought it well to prepare this brief and compendious narrative. Not that the life of so great a man can be unknown to any even of the heathen nations, but that to our posterity also this incomparable and lofty pattern may be prolonged into immortal remembrance"). VL then adds "et ut ad exemplum sui litteris dirigantur" (40270–40271); Harnack strikes these words, which are missing or misplaced in the manuscripts (HAR 445–449). **Quote 1** stops before them. OWN. Documented. Then his reason: "our fathers have given such honour even to lay-people and catechumens who have obtained martyrdom ... as to record many, or I had nearly said, well nigh all, of the circumstances of their sufferings ... the passion of such a priest and such a martyr as Cyprian should be passed over, who, independently of his martyrdom, had much to teach" (VE 27678–27687; VL 40271–40277). OWN. Documented. Who the "lay-people and catechumens" were is the scholars' reading: the *Passion of Perpetua and Felicity* above all (HAR 486–488; DEL 3998–4009). Dominant Modern Reconstruction. Plain words for catechumens: "people still learning the faith before baptism". And his hearers' demand: "you also are anxious to hear very much, or if it be possible every thing, about him ... although now his living words are silent" (VE 27692–27695; VL 40316–40319; HAR 511–514). OWN. Documented. **Opening.**

**E9. What he chose to tell.** He starts at Cyprian's baptism: "the doings of a man of God should not be reckoned from any point except from the time that he was born of God" (VE 27703–27706). Harnack: "Diese erste christliche Biographie setzt bei der Wiedergeburt ein und schafft damit das Vorbild für alle späteren" ("This first Christian biography begins at the rebirth and so sets the pattern for all later ones", HAR 543–544; HAR-view). He tells, in order: chastity and the sale of estates as a learner; made elder and bishop while new; the people's choice; his bearing as bishop; the withdrawal under Decius, defended as God's command and not fear; the plague care; the exile and the dream; the last days and the death; first bishop-martyr of Carthage (Harnack's outline, HAR 1953–2082). OWN. Documented. Harnack: no wonder stories, no demons or angels, besides the dream (HAR 2864–2868); "miraculum" in the text means only great virtue (HAR 2877–2890). HAR-view, checkable in the text. His sources, in his own words: what he was present for, and what he learned "de antiquioribus" (VL 40364; HAR 559; VE 27710–27712 renders "if I have discerned any of his more illustrious labours", from a different reading: AASS 196801–196803 "si quae de antiquioribus ejus operibus comperi"). Harnack takes it as "from older men", meaning he was an eyewitness but not from the start (HAR 589–592; HAR-view). The text is uncertain here: paraphrase only, "what he saw himself and what he learned from others".

**E10. What he chose to leave out.**
- Cyprian's life before conversion: "He may have had pursuits previously, and liberal arts may have imbued his mind while engaged therein; but these things I pass over" (VE 27706–27708; VL 40330–40332). OWN. Documented.
- The first hearing of 257: "And what God's priest replied to the interrogation of the proconsul, there are Acts which relate" (VE 28001–28003; VL 40900–40901: "sunt acta quae referant"). OWN. Documented. Benson notes Pontius does not report it (BEN 27192–27193).
- "I omit many other matters, and, indeed, many important ones, which the necessity of a limited space does not permit" (VE 27976–27978). OWN. Documented.
- Cyprian's councils and dealings with Rome, including the baptism quarrel. Delehaye: a "strange silence" on "son action dans les conciles, ses relations avec Rome" (DEL 4168–4173). Harnack: these held no "opera ac merita" in the writer's sense and did not belong in it (HAR 2087–2093). Monceaux: "d'étranges lacunes" ("strange gaps"), with "quelques allusions à l'affaire des lapsi"; Pontius wished to paint "moins le politique, le chef d'Eglise, que le chrétien, le clerc accompli" ("less the politician, the church leader, than the Christian, the accomplished cleric") (MON 11092–11103). The silence is Documented; the reason is each scholar's view.
- The final hiding. Pontius says Cyprian "did not consent" to friends' urging to withdraw (VE 28142–28149). Cyprian's own last letter says that on friends' advice he did leave his gardens for a while, so as not to be taken to Utica and to confess at Carthage (EP Ep. 81, 17370–17377: "consilio carissimorum persuasum esset ut de hortis nostris interim secederem, iusta interueniente causa consensi"). CONTEMP, his own words. Documented. Rigault, 17th century, faulted the *Life* for passing over this hiding in particular (AASS 196858–196866: "Atque hoc praecipue queritur omissum ... se ... tantisper latuisse"). Delehaye notes the friends' advice is also in Ep. 81 (DEL 4182–4198). **Credit each:** Pontius tells the refusal; Cyprian's letter tells the hiding.
- Monceaux faults him for setting the exile straight after the plague, though "quatre ans au moins" ("four years at least") lay between (MON 11124–11131; reported at HAR 2041–2043), and Harnack answers that Pontius there means Cyprian's merits as bishop at large (HAR 2043–2046); Harnack also notes the *Life* never tells Cyprian's return from Curubis (HAR 2869–2876). Modern critics' points; for Do not say.

**E11. His grief at being left behind.** The book ends: "What shall I do now? Between joy at his passion, and grief at still remaining, my mind is divided ... Shall I grieve that I was not his associate? But yet I must triumph in his victory. Shall I triumph at his victory? Still I grieve that I am not his companion. Yet still to you I must in simplicity confess, what you also are aware of, that it was my intention to be his companion. Much and excessively I exult at his glory; but still more do I grieve that I remained behind" (VE 28294–28303; VL 41402–41410; HAR 1806–1813; AASS 196784–196790). OWN. Documented. The three Latin prints agree. Harnack calls the close "frosty" and "conventional" in its feeling, "but it may still be true" (HAR 1839–1841; HAR-view). **Quote 3.** It echoes his aside at Curubis, "would that he could also have chosen me to share his passion" (E2).

**E12. His own end: not known.** No held early source tells how or when Pontius died. Richardson: "Died about 260" (JE-ed 40833, no source given). Coxe: "Pontius is said to have followed his beloved bishop, a.d. 258, dying a martyr" (VE-ed 28060, no source; and 258 is Cyprian's year, which cannot be right for a man writing after it). The Roman Martyrology, 8 March: "Carthagine S. Pontii, Diaconi B. Cypriani Episcopi, qui usque ad diem mortis illius cum ipso exilium sustinens, vitae et passionis ejus egregium volumen reliquit, atque in suis passionibus Dominum semper glorificans, coronam vitae promeruit" (AASS 196819–196824), following Usuard, Ado and Notker (9th century) "and many old martyrologies" (AASS 196817–196819). LATE legend, built on Jerome's line. Inferential-Thin for any martyrdom.

## Impact (heaviest first)

**I1. The book survived beside Cyprian's works.** It stands at the end of Cyprian's works in the Cheltenham list of 359 (HAR 260–263), and is kept in 23 manuscripts (HAR 264). Von Soden, quoted by Harnack: "in Rome about the middle of the fourth century the *Vita* was attached to the body of Cyprian's writings" (HAR 314–316, "in Rom hat man um die Mitte des 4. Jahrhunderts ... an das Corpus Cypriani die Vita angehängt"); Harnack adds it may have happened earlier in Africa (HAR 344). Documented (the list and the manuscripts); the place is von Soden's view. Most manuscripts break off in chapter 15, where the *Acta* take over (HAR 293–295). Documented.

**I2. Jerome read it and named its author** (E1), about 392. His one line is the only reason the book has a name on it. Documented.

**I3. Called the first Christian biography, and disputed.** Harnack, 1913: "die älteste christliche Biographie, die wir besitzen" (HAR 1909–1910, "the oldest Christian biography we possess"); its rivals do not count, since Gregory the Wonderworker's speech on Origen is a speech of praise and the Life of Polycarp by Pionius is a fourth-century work (HAR 1916–1919). It "founded a new kind of literature, Christian biography, and at once set a type" which, he says, still rules the ascetic Lives of the Western church (HAR 175–181; HAR 4157–4170). HAR-view. Against him: Reitzenstein, as the handbook and Delehaye report him, saw no reason to call it the "first Christian biography"; he set it with the Roman accounts of famous men's deaths, and thought the writer a "poor rhetorician" (SHK 31630–31633; DEL 4079–4083). Corssen saw a speech of praise and a defence of Cyprian's election and conduct under Decius (DEL 4095–4102, which Delehaye thinks "much exaggerated"; DEL 4055–4058 quotes him that it was meant to be read aloud). Delehaye: Pontius wanted a book for Cyprian not less than the *Passion of Perpetua*, made the dream its centrepiece after that model, and blended biography and praise (DEL 4012–4036, 4312–4320). Monceaux, 1902: "non une véritable biographie, mais un panégyrique" ("not a true biography, but a panegyric", MON 11030–11032). **Contested**: "the oldest Christian Life that survives" is Harnack's claim; others call it a speech of praise. The corpus map's own note says "often counted the earliest Christian biography" (`cic/corpus-map/latin-pastoral-congregational-christianity.yaml`, 18698), a builder's note, not a source.

**I4. Kept as a saint.** Medieval martyrologies and the Roman Martyrology keep him at Carthage on 8 March (E12; AASS 196261–196265, 196817–196824). LATE. Documented that they keep him; that he died a martyr is not held.

**I5. Its reliability argued over.** Rigault (17th c.) said Pontius invented much "pio erga suum Episcopum adfectu, Rhetorica libertate" ("from loving devotion to his bishop, with a rhetorician's freedom") and showed more "rhetoricae dictionis gratiam ... quam narrationis historicae diligentiam" (AASS 196852–196861). The Bollandists answered him at length, defending the text (AASS 196880–196990). Harnack: its plain facts are reliable (HAR 1820–1824, citing Monceaux and Goetz); Benson calls him "devoted grandiloquent" (BEN 27384). Each a view; for the card, or one sentence at most.

## Where sources disagree or come late

- **D1. Authorship.** The text is unsigned (HAR 331–333); Jerome names Pontius (E1). Most accept him (SHK 31626–31628; DEL 3907–3910; HAR 340–343). Dessau tried to identify him with a Pontius in an inscription at Curubis; Corssen showed this was ill-founded (DEL 3936–3942; SHK 31628–31630). Card.
- **D2. Eyewitness or not.** The text says he was there (E2, E6). Reitzenstein held this invented (SHK 31630–31631); Delehaye rejects that (DEL 4266–4303). Tell the text's claim as his; one clause on the doubt at most.
- **D3. What kind of book.** Biography (Harnack), speech of praise and defence (Corssen), the deaths-of-famous-men form (Reitzenstein), a blend modelled on the *Passion of Perpetua* (Delehaye). See I3. Credit each if told.
- **D4. Did he know the *Acta*?** He points to them (E10). Monceaux and Harnack: he knew the record of the first hearing and the sentence, not the *Acta* as we have them (HAR 1829–1833). Delehaye: more likely he knew them (DEL 4251–4252). Von Soden: perhaps meant to replace them (SHK 31640–31641). Card.
- **D5. The last withdrawal.** Pontius: he refused to withdraw; Cyprian's Ep. 81: he did leave his gardens for a while (E10). Credit each.
- **D6. The dream's meaning.** Pontius's "one day meant a year" and "church affairs"; Harnack: not convincing, not Cyprian's view (E5). Keep the meaning in Pontius's voice.
- **D7. When written.** About 259, Harnack's "prevailing view" (S1); Martin: later (SHK 31633–31634).
- **D8. The "older men" phrase.** "de antiquioribus comperi" (Hartel, Harnack) or "de antiquioribus ejus operibus comperi" (AASS; VE "more illustrious labours"). E9. Paraphrase only.
- **D9. His own end.** Unknown; "about 260" (Richardson), "said to have died a martyr" (Coxe), martyrologies 9th century on (E12).
- **D10. The garden sale.** Card's note (Ritschl and Benson) stands; not needed here.
