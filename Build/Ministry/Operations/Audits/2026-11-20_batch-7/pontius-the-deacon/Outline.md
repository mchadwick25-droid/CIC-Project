# Outline: Pontius the Deacon

Sheet items (S, E, I, D) are in `Source_Sheet.md`, which gives the file and line for each. VE is the English of the *Life* (ANF 5); VL is Hartel's Latin and HAR is Harnack's 1913 print of it with his commentary (both damaged scans that agree with each other); JE is Jerome in English; AASS is the Acta Sanctorum for 8 March; DEL is Delehaye (1921); SHK is a 1922 handbook; EP is Cyprian's letters in Latin; KNOPF is the court record of Cyprian's hearings and death; MON is Monceaux (1902); BEN is Benson (1897); JL is Jerome in Latin.

- **The essence:** Pontius, Cyprian of Carthage's deacon, went with him into exile in 257 by choice, and was with him on his last night in 258 (E2, E6; Documented, the book's own claims; his name and his office are Jerome's, E1). Soon after, he wrote *The Life and Passion of Cyprian*, so that Cyprian's example would reach later generations (E8; Documented, his own words; written about 259, Dominant Modern Reconstruction). It tells a bishop's life from his baptism to his death; a leading historian called it the oldest Christian biography that survives, and others call it a speech of praise (I3; Contested). The book is unsigned; Jerome, about 130 years later, gives the deacon's name (E1; Widely Accepted).
- **Also told:**
  - **Why he wrote.** Earlier Christians had written down the trials and deaths of martyrs who were ordinary believers, or not yet baptized, in great detail. It would be hard, he says, if the death of so great a bishop were passed over, when he had much to teach even apart from it (E8; Documented). Then Quote 1.
  - **What he chose to tell and leave out.** He begins at Cyprian's baptism, the day he was "born of God", and passes over everything before it (E9, E10; Documented). He points readers to the court record for the first hearing and gives the last trial in one line (E10, E7; Documented). He tells nothing of Cyprian's councils, his dealings with Rome or the baptism quarrel, the quarrel with Rome over whether those baptized outside the church must be baptized again (E10; Documented that they are missing). Scholars give different reasons for the gap (E10; each scholar's view). He tells no wonders except the dream (E9; Harnack's observation, which can be checked in the text). Where Cyprian's own last letter says he hid for a while, Pontius says he refused to leave (D5; credit each).
  - **The dream at Curubis.** On their first day in exile, Cyprian told them a dream: he was brought before the governor. A tall young man behind the governor read the unspoken sentence and showed it with his flat hand like a blade. Cyprian begged for a day's delay and it was granted (E4; Documented as Cyprian's words, reported by Pontius). Pontius read the one day as one year, and says that Cyprian died a year later, on the same day (E5; Pontius's reading, in his voice). Then Quote 2.
  - **His grief at being left behind.** At Curubis he had wished Cyprian had chosen him to share his death too (E2). The book ends torn between joy and grief: he grieves more that he "remained behind" (E11; Documented). Quote 3. How or when Pontius himself died, no early source says (E12).
- **Length:** about 3:45 (about 560 words at 150 a minute). Counted first: three quotes (about 150 words), the plain sense of five words to explain and of the baptism quarrel (about 60) and the witnesses credited (Jerome, Cyprian's own last letter, the court record, a German historian, a Belgian scholar, a French historian: about 70). That is about 280 words before the story is told, which leaves about 280 for it. Why this length: the record is one book and one line of Jerome. But the job asks for four things inside that book, the reason, the choices, the dream and the grief, and each needs its own scene. The death and the plague are clauses, so the room goes to the book. Cut: the soldier's dry clothes in beat 4; Rigault in What came 3; the calendars (What came 4); Perpetua by name in beat 5. Beat 6's scholars are folded into one clause. Never cut the dream or the three quotes. Never cut the gap between his account and Cyprian's own letter on the last hiding (beat 4). That is the record's conflict, and it shows what he chose.
- **Setting:**
  - Time: the exile, from late August 257 (S1, KNOPF 4823–4824). Cyprian's death, 14 September 258 (S1). The book, about 259, by the scholars' prevailing view (S1; D7). Plain words: "in the middle of the third century"; "soon after Cyprian's death".
  - Place: Carthage, in what is now Tunisia (S2). Curubis, a small town on the coast, his place of exile (S2; BEN 27288–27290, "little coast town"). Cyprian was sent away there by the governor (E2; KNOPF 4836, 4850–4851). Not "under guard": the held texts do not say how he was kept.
  - Tradition: the Latin-speaking churches of North Africa; the tree's name is "the Latin Pastoral Congregations" (S3). Do not say "Latin pastoral congregational Christianity" aloud.

**Words to explain** (the first time each is heard; plain word or short gloss, worked into the sentence)

- **Life** (as a kind of book): "*The Life and Passion of Cyprian*, as Jerome titles it (JL 57618–57622, 'vite [vitae] et passionis Cypriani'): the story of his life, and of his suffering and death as a martyr". Say *The Life and Passion of Cyprian* once, with "passion" glossed.
- **passion** (in the title and the martyr accounts): "the account of a martyr's suffering and death". Never "passion" bare in its modern sense.
- **catechumens**: "people still learning the faith before baptism" (E8). Never the word bare.
- **proconsul**: "the Roman governor of the province" (E4).
- **the Acts** (VE "there are Acts which relate"): "the court record of the hearing". Not "the Acts" bare, which a listener will hear as the Acts of the Apostles.

- **Shape:** Teacher or thinker. The question he tried to answer was how to remember a bishop who had died a martyr: whether only his death should be written down, as with earlier martyrs, or his whole life. What he taught is in his book's choices: begin at baptism, leave out what did not show God at work, read the dream and the sentence as prophecy. His readers were Jerome and the copyists who kept the book with Cyprian's works. The trap is a list of books; here there is one book, so the trap is a list of its contents. Show the idea through his own words (Quotes 1 and 3) and one scene (the dream).
- **Sibling:** `cyprian-of-carthage` (in this batch, done; job: the rhetorician made bishop, the hiding under Decius and the charge it brought, the plague sermon, the *Unity* and its "mother" line, the quarrel with Stephen, and his trial and death in 258). That story tells the death scene, the plague care, the crowd at the door, the people in the trees, the bound eyes and the executioner's shaking hand, each credited to Pontius. Here: the death is one clause, and the plague is one clause at most (beat 6, as a thing he chose to tell). None of those scenes is retold here. The Decius hiding and the Roman clergy's letter are Cyprian's. Here Pontius's defence of the withdrawal is one clause in beat 6, as a thing he chose to tell, without the Roman letter. The second hiding of 258 is told in Cyprian's story from his own letter. Here it is one clause in beat 4, set beside Pontius's version. The dream and "I remained behind" are this story's (batch README). Live `jerome` is a link: one clause, his line naming Pontius.
- **Openings:**
  1. **Chosen. His readers' longing** (E8; VE 27692–27695; VL 40316–40319). In about three sentences: soon after the bishop of Carthage was put to death, the Christians there wanted to hear everything about him, now that his living voice was silent. So says the man who set out to write it, and he writes that he felt pressed on both sides: by the greatness of the bishop and by their asking (VE 27697–27699). Then his name, credited to Jerome in a clause, and the essence. This moment comes after the beats in time. The hook may reach ahead; beat 5 must not tell this request again.
  2. Not chosen: the dream's hand like a blade (Quote 2). It is strong, but it belongs in time order at beat 2, and opening on it would tell it twice.

**Story beats** (the opening, the essence and the start of beat 1 are the first minute, about 150 words; beats 1 to 6 deepen it)

1. **A willing exile** (E2, E3). In 257, after his first hearing, Cyprian was sent away to Curubis (S1; one clause; the hearing is Cyprian's story). Pontius went with him. Cyprian had chosen him, he writes, among his household companions, as "a willing exile" (VL 41014–41015, "exulem uoluntarium"; may stand as a short phrase). In the same breath he adds that he wished Cyprian could have chosen him to share his death as well (E2; Documented; paraphrase only, since Quote 3 carries the grief). He says they found a sunny, fit place, with many visitors and kind townspeople (E3; Pontius's account). To a Christian, he writes, the whole world is one home (E3; may stand as a short phrase).
2. **The dream** (E4). On their first day there, before he fell asleep, Cyprian saw a young man of more than human size lead him to the governor's court. The governor wrote a sentence on his tablet without asking him anything; the young man behind him read it. Then Quote 2. Cyprian begged for one day's delay, to put his affairs in order. The judge seemed moved, and the young man signalled with twisted fingers that the delay was granted till the next day. Cyprian was glad, but still trembling at the doubt of what it meant (E4; Documented as Cyprian's words, told by Pontius). Say once that these are Cyprian's own words as Pontius gives them.
3. **How Pontius read it** (E5). Pontius read the one day as one year. Cyprian, he says, was put to death a year later, on the very day he had seen the dream (E5; Pontius's claim; the narrator does not state the date as fact). He says the delay was asked for church business, above all the care of the poor, and that Cyprian spent the year on it (E5; Pontius's reading). A German church historian, Adolf Harnack, writing in 1913, found this reading unconvincing and thought it was not Cyprian's own (E5; credit him; may be cut).
4. **The last days** (E6, D5). News came from Rome that its bishop had been put to death (E6; the bishop by role). Friends of high rank urged Cyprian to withdraw and offered him places to go, and Pontius says he refused (E6; Pontius's account). Cyprian's own last letter says that, on friends' advice, he did leave his gardens for a while, so that he would not be taken to another city to die (D5; EP Ep. 81; Documented, his own words; credit each, side by side). On the night after his arrest, Pontius writes, "we, his companions and friends", were with him as usual (E6; may stand as a short phrase). Then one clause: he was beheaded that day, 14 September 258, and Pontius's book tells the death in a few lines (E7; KNOPF 4955–4956; told in Cyprian's story).
5. **Why he wrote** (E8). He wrote soon after, about 259 by most scholars' reckoning (S1). The church's forebears, he says, had honoured martyrs who were ordinary believers, and some not yet baptized. They had written down almost every detail of their sufferings, so that people not yet born would know them. It would be hard, he says, if the death of so great a bishop and martyr were passed over, when he had much to teach even apart from it (E8; Documented, his own words; paraphrase). Scholars take him to mean above all the account of an earlier group of martyrs, put to death in 203 (E8; DEL 3998–4009, 4029; scholars' reading; by role only, not named). Then Quote 1.
6. **What he told, and what he left out** (E9, E10, E7). He began at Cyprian's baptism. A man of God's deeds, he wrote, should be counted only from the day he was born of God (E9; paraphrase). Whatever Cyprian had studied before, "these things I pass over" (E10; may stand as a short phrase). He told the bishop's care for the poor, his defence of the hiding under Decius as God's command and not fear (one clause), and his care in the plague (one clause, no detail). For the first hearing, he sent readers to the court record (E10; "there are Acts which relate", glossed). Of the last trial he wrote only that Cyprian was asked his name, and "he answers who he is, and nothing more" (E7; may stand as a short phrase). He says nothing of the councils Cyprian called, his dealings with Rome or the baptism quarrel, the quarrel with Rome over whether those baptized outside the church must be baptized again (E10). Then one clause on why: scholars differ: one found the silence strange, one thought such things did not belong in a book about God at work in one man, one thought Pontius wished to praise the Christian and the cleric, not the church leader (Delehaye, DEL 4168–4173; Harnack, HAR 2087–2093; Monceaux, MON 11092–11103). Credit each, or say "scholars differ" in one clause. He tells no wonders but the dream (E9; Harnack's observation).

**What came of it** (about a third of the story; heaviest first)

1. **It was kept with Cyprian's own works.** By the year 359 it stood at the end of a list of Cyprian's writings, and it was copied with them; 23 manuscripts hold it (I1; Documented). **The connection**: tell it here, at its strongest, with What came 2.
2. **Jerome named its author.** About 392 Jerome wrote that Pontius, Cyprian's deacon, who shared his exile until the day of his death, "left a notable volume" on his life and death (I2, E1; Documented; "a notable volume" may stand as a short phrase). Without that one line the book would have no name on it.
3. **"The oldest Christian biography."** Harnack called it the oldest Christian biography that survives, and said its pattern, beginning at the new birth, shaped the saints' Lives of the Western church (I3; his view, credited). A French historian of African Christian writing, in 1902, called it not a true biography but a speech of praise (I3; MON 11030–11032; his view, credited by role). Delehaye thought Pontius wanted a book for Cyprian as great as the earlier martyr account, and blended a life with a speech of praise (I3; his view). Others, as Delehaye reports, called it a speech of praise and a defence (I3). Tell that they differ, credit each, and do not decide it.

Defended: that the whole life of a bishop, not only his death, deserved to be written down for later generations (E8, Quote 1).

No held source says who keeps his feast today: do not say "today".

**Connection to carry into the story:** what passed from Pontius was one book: an account of a bishop's whole life as a Christian, from his baptism to his death. Harnack judged it the oldest Christian biography that survives; others dispute what kind of book it is (I3). It passed on in two ways. First, by 359 it had been joined to Cyprian's own writings and was copied with them; the manuscripts that keep it are manuscripts of Cyprian (I1, HAR 260–264, 314–316). Second, Jerome read it and put Pontius's name to it (I2, JE 40833–40835). The listener must understand three things. First, what he set out to do differently, in his own words: earlier writers had told martyrs' trials and deaths, and he set out to tell the bishop's life too, from baptism (E8, E9). Whether that makes it the first Christian biography is disputed (I3). Second, that much of what we know of Cyprian's last year comes from this one companion, beside the court record and Cyprian's letters (E2–E7; HAR 1822–1827). That is why what he left out matters, such as the final hiding, which Cyprian's own letter tells (D5). Third, that scholars still argue over what kind of book it is (I3): say who holds which view, and do not settle it. Tell it through these acts, the copying, the naming and the arguing, not as a "this matters because" sentence.

**Ending:** close on the person, in his own last words: Quote 3, the end of his book (E11; VE 28294–28303). Hold it back from beat 6 and tell it as the last thing, after What came of it. It answers the opening: the people wanted to hear everything about their bishop; the man who told them wished he had died with him. Do not end on Harnack, Jerome or the calendars.

**Who tells it**

- **Pontius himself**, in the *Life*, written soon after the death as open praise of his bishop (E2–E11). The whole story rests on this one book. Say so once, inside the sentence that brings him in, or at beat 5: what we know of him is what he wrote, and he writes as an admirer.
- **Cyprian**, in his own words as Pontius reports them: the dream (E4). And in his own last letter, on the final hiding (D5; EP Ep. 81).
- **The court record** of the hearings (KNOPF): one clause, that it holds what Pontius left out (E7, E10).
- **Jerome**, about 130 years later, who gives Pontius's name and office (E1). Say the book itself is unsigned.
- **Harnack** (1913), **Delehaye** (1921) and **Monceaux** (1902), modern scholars who disagree about the book (I3, E5, E10). Credit each by name or role.
- **Rigault**, a 17th-century editor, and the Jesuit editors of the saints' lives who answered him (I5): cut, not told.
- **The medieval calendars**, late (E12, I4): cut, not told.

**Do not say**

- Any word in "Words to explain" used without its plain sense the first time it is heard.
- Not "Pontius wrote the Life" as a bare fact on first mention. Say "a deacon whom Jerome names as Pontius", or "the book is unsigned; Jerome, about 130 years later, gives the name" (E1).
- Not "the first Christian biography" or "the first Life of a bishop" in the narrator's voice. Say "the oldest Christian biography that survives, a leading historian judged" and give the other view (I3, D3).
- Not "Pontius was Cyprian's secretary", "his closest friend", "a young man" or "ordained deacon by Cyprian". No held text says so; Harnack's guess that he was a younger man ordained after 251 is his inference (E9, HAR 589–592).
- Not any birth year or age (Harnack infers after 202 from "we who were not yet born", HAR 494).
- Not "Pontius was converted by Cyprian" or any family or life outside the book.
- Not "he died a martyr" or "he was killed soon after". No early source says how or when he died. Coxe's "said to have died a martyr" and the calendars are late and unsourced (E12, D9).
- Not "Cyprian died exactly a year after the dream" in the narrator's voice. That is Pontius's reading, and the court record's dates (30 August 257, 14 September 258) do not settle it (E5).
- Not "an angel appeared to Cyprian". Cyprian says "a young man of more than human size"; that it was an angel is Harnack's reading (E4).
- Not "God revealed his death to him" or "the dream came true" in the narrator's voice. Keep the dream in Cyprian's words and its meaning in Pontius's voice (E4, E5, D6).
- Not "the delay was to care for the poor" as fact. Cyprian asked to put "my affairs" in order; that it meant church affairs and the poor is Pontius's reading (E5).
- Not "he refused to hide" or "Cyprian never hid in 258" as fact. Pontius says he did not consent to his friends' advice; Cyprian's own letter says he left his gardens for a while (D5). Give both.
- Not "Pontius was at the trial" or "Pontius saw him die". He says the companions were with him the night before (E6); the book tells the trial and death without an "I" or "we". Say "his book tells the death".
- Not "Pontius invented the eyewitness parts" or "the book is a fake". One scholar held the eyewitness role invented; Delehaye rejected that (D2). If told, credit each, in one clause.
- Not "he did not know the court record" or "he copied the court record". Scholars differ (D4). Leave out.
- Not "the exile came straight after the plague". Pontius sets them side by side; Monceaux says years lay between, "four years at least" (E10, MON 11124–11131); Harnack answers that Pontius there means Cyprian's merits as bishop at large, not the plague alone (HAR 2043–2046).
- Not "the book was read aloud at his feast" or "it was a sermon". One scholar thought it was meant to be read aloud; the book calls itself a volume (DEL 4055–4058; HAR 1936–1940). Leave out.
- Not "Possidius modelled his Life of Augustine on it", or any named later Life that followed it. No held source shows one (Links).
- Not "Pontius left out the quarrel to protect Cyprian's name". No held source gives his motive; the scholars' reasons are theirs (E10).
- Not "the dream at Curubis" placed on the night after the hearing. It was the first day at Curubis (E4).

- Dessau's inscription at Curubis (D1), the debate over the court record (D4), the deaths-of-famous-men form (D3): for the card.
- Pontius's comparison of Cyprian with Tobias, and with Job: side matter.

- Benson's "grandiloquent" and Delehaye's "a barren mind": scholars' judgments of style, for the card if anywhere.
