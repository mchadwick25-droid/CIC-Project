# Outline check: Anatolius of Alexandria and Laodicea

## My account of the heart (written before reading `Outline.md`)

From the census entry (`knownFor`, `datesNote`, `role`), the source sheet and what he is known for.

**The essence.** A Christian of Alexandria famed for his learning in mathematics, astronomy and philosophy, who during a siege of the city's palace quarter talked its council into letting the useless mouths go, and under that cover got almost all the besieged out by night, his own church first and many men dressed as women (Documented as Eusebius's report, "they say"). Kept as bishop at Laodicea in Syria as he passed through on his way to the synod at Antioch over Paul of Samosata, he wrote an Easter reckoning on a nineteen-year cycle, the first Christian known to use one (McGiffert, "so far as we know": Dominant Modern Reconstruction); it became the basis of Easter reckoning at Alexandria, and at Whitby in 664 both sides cited him (Documented, Bede).

**Also told.** His learning, as Eusebius and Jerome praise it, and the report that Alexandria asked him to found a school of Aristotle's philosophy (a report). The deacon Eusebius outside the walls, who won safe passage from the Roman general and received the refugees "like a father and physician". The speech, with its hard words about old women, children and old men. Theotecnus of Caesarea first making him a fellow bishop, meant as his successor. What his extract says: the cycle's first new moon at the spring equinox and the error of those who put the first month before it; the rule claimed as known to the Jews before Christ. Whitby: Colman's claim rests on a Latin book under his name (an early translation to McGiffert, a forgery to Harnack), Wilfrid's on his own cycle; Bede doubted the Irish claim. Rufinus's later version gives Anatolius the outside role (credited). His death is not known; Stephen followed him before the persecution.

Then the outline was read.

**Comparison.** The outline's essence and Also told match mine in substance: the siege rescue, Laodicea on the road to the synod, the nineteen-year Easter reckoning with McGiffert's "so far as we know", and Whitby, where both sides claimed him. Nothing major is left out. It keeps Mark's approved heart (`Hearts.md`, 2026-11-20) and the README job as the hearts entry corrected it ("both sides claimed him", not "both sides named the reckoning"). The beats give the heart in the first minute, then follow the life once, in order. The ending is not told early.

## BLOCKING

**B1. The Connection and What came 1 present "his cycle became Alexandria's" as settled, but the held sources differ.** The Connection says: "His cycle, so far as is known the first a Christian used, became Alexandria's (I1)." What came 1 says "it was soon taken up at Alexandria". The sources say three different things:
- McGiffert says the nineteen-year (Metonic) cycle was "soon adopted in the Alexandrian church" (EUS-ed 40911–40912).
- Hefele says "This cycle of nineteen years was soon subjected to different modifications, after which it was generally adopted in Alexandria". The chief change was moving the equinox from 19 to 21 March (HEF 20656–20661).
- Bucherius, quoted in the PL67 notes, held that Anatolius's own table was used for only a few years, at Laodicea or nearby, and was soon pushed out by Eusebius of Caesarea's cycle (PL67-ed 36039–36045; the sheet already holds this at D7).

Under Mark's ruling of 2026-11-19, each must be credited. **Correction.** In the Connection, replace "His cycle, so far as is known the first a Christian used, became Alexandria's (I1)" with: "The nineteen-year cycle, which he is the first Christian known to have used, was soon taken up at Alexandria, the editors say, though Hefele adds that it was first changed, with the equinox moved to 21 March; one early editor, Bucherius, thought Anatolius's own table was used only a few years, at Laodicea (I1; HEF 20656–20661; PL67-ed 36039–36045)." In What came 1, after "it was soon taken up at Alexandria", add: "after changes, Hefele says (HEF 20656–20661); one early editor thought his own table was soon set aside (PL67-ed 36039–36045). Credit each in a clause, or put the qualifier on the card." Add to Do not say: "Not 'Alexandria adopted his table' as fact. The editors say the nineteen-year cycle was taken up there, Hefele says after changes, and one early editor thought his own table was soon set aside (I1, D7)."

**B2. The gloss for "the Scots" is wrong.** The outline glosses it as "the Irish monks and their English followers, whom Bede calls the Scots". Bede's "Scots" are the Irish. He sets the English who sided with them apart from that word: "the Abbess Hilda and her followers were for the Scots" (BEDE 7966–7967). In the passage where the story would hear the word, the Scots are "the northern province of the Scots" kept apart from "all the nation of the Picts" (BEDE 5752–5754). The live Hild story uses "the Irish monks of Iona, whom Bede calls the Scots". **Correction.** Replace the entry with: "**the Scots** (Bede's word): 'the Irish, whom Bede calls the Scots' (BEDE 5749–5757, 7966–7967; as in the live Hild story). Bede never uses the word for their English followers. At Bede's doubt (What came 3), say 'the northern Irish and the Picts' (BEDE 5752–5754)."

## MINOR

**M1. The opening plans a held-back name reveal.** Opening 1 says "Then the turn, with the name: he was Anatolius". That is the "The man was ..." move that `roles/writer.md` bars. It also says "the people he named were the ones he got out", which is short of the record. The decree let out those not needed for the army, and under its cover he got out church members first and then "a multitude of others" (E4). "A man rose in the city council" adds a detail: Eusebius has him rise only to cast the first vote (EUS 42746). **Correction.** Replace Opening 1's three sentences with: "In a besieged quarter of Alexandria, Eusebius heard, the bread had run out, and hunger was harder to bear than the enemy outside. Anatolius, a Christian whom Eusebius ranked first among the learned men of his time, told the city council it should send out the old women, children and old men who would soon be dead. The people he called useless were among those he got out, and many more besides." Change Quote 1's job line to match: "...the listener must hear at once, from what he did next, that those he called useless were among the many he got out."

**M2. The length count undercounts the quotes, so the time does not fit the beats.** Quote 1's rendering is 91 words and Quote 2's is 73, so the quotes carry 164 words, not 145. Add 70 for the glosses and 75 for the credited witnesses, and 500 words leaves about 190 words. Those 190 would have to carry the opening, five beats, three What came items and the ending, which comes to under 20 words each. That is the batch 6 overrun again. **Correction.** Replace the Length line's count with: "the quotes carry about 165 words (Quote 1 about 90, Quote 2 about 75)". Then either (a) set the length to "about 3 minutes 45 seconds (about 560 words)", with about 250 words for the events and the same cut-first list, or (b) keep 3:20 and quote Quote 1 only from "Why keep them here for nothing", with the first three sentences in paraphrase. Recommended: (a). The siege scene and the Whitby paragraph are the craft, and 3:45 is under the four-minute line. Mark's hearts page carried 3:20 from the miscounted figure, so the change should be named to him.

**M3. The damaged scans (Quotes 1 and 3).** The only held print of the Greek is Schwartz's OCR (EUS-gr). McGiffert's English is a translation, not a print of the original, so it cannot stand as the confirming print. I checked the Greek by hand at EUS-gr 12483–12492 and 12597–12599. Every word of Quote 1 is certain: the damage is glyph substitution only (`xci`, `oc δ᾽`, `ποϑ᾽`, `οὕπω`). In Quote 3, the numeral is garbled (`Φαμενὼϑ' xc` for κϛʹ). Schwartz's apparatus ("ἕχτη xal εἰχάδι ATER", EUS-gr 12625) and McGiffert ("twenty-sixth", EUS 42793) confirm it, but Quote 3 is not "legible" as the outline says. **Correction.** Beside Quote 1, add: "No other held print of the Greek; the card prints the English alone. (Greek hand-checked against the scan at the outline check, 2026-11-20: every word certain.)" In Quote 3, replace "Legible." with: "The numeral is OCR-damaged (`xc` for κϛʹ), confirmed by Schwartz's apparatus (EUS-gr 12625) and McGiffert (EUS 42793); no other held print of the Greek, so the card prints the English alone."

**M4. Quote 2 drops "even".** "cum nec eius decreta seruetis" means "when you do not even keep his decrees" (BEDE-lat 21583). Giles drops it, but the outline's own What came 2 has it ("did not even keep"). **Correction.** "But what have you to do with him, when you do not even keep his rules?" The rest of the rendering matches mine.

**M5. Beat 5 paraphrases Anatolius's rule loosely.** The beat says "whoever fixes the fourteenth day before it ... makes 'no small' mistake". What he faults is those who "place the first month in" the segment before the equinox "and determine by it the fourteenth" (EUS 42815–42816; EUS-gr 12608–12612). **Correction.** "His rule: the first month must not be counted from a new moon before the spring equinox; those who place the first month there, and take the fourteenth day from it, he wrote, make 'no small' mistake (E6)."

**M6. "Jewish writers before Christ" is not accurate.** Anatolius says the rule "was known to the Jews of old, even before Christ". He then cites Philo and Josephus, who wrote in and after Christ's time, alongside older writers (EUS 42817–42867). **Correction.** In Also told, beat 5 and Leave out, replace "claimed Jewish writers before Christ for the rule" and "say 'Jewish writers before Christ'" with "said the Jews kept the rule even before Christ, and cited their writers for it".

**M7. Harnack's view of the Latin book is narrowed.** Also told has "a forgery made during that very quarrel", and What came 3 has "perhaps in the sixth century, during the British and Roman Easter quarrel itself". Harnack says "im 6. Jahrhundert oder schon früher im Zusammenhang mit der britisch-römischen Osterkontroverse" (HARN 4871–4873). Columbanus cited the book before Whitby (HARN 4832–4836), so "that very quarrel" would read as the quarrel of 664. **Correction.** In both places: "Harnack, as a forgery made in the sixth century or earlier, in the long Easter quarrel between the British and Irish churches and Rome."

**M8. Where held sources differ but the outline cuts one side first, the card must carry it.** Two places:
- Rufinus's version of the siege, where Anatolius himself goes to the general and receives the refugees (D1), is optional and first to be cut.
- Ceolfrid's letter in Bede, which traces the nineteen-year cycle to the apostles' own time (D7), sits only in the optional Picts clause.

Under Mark's ruling of 2026-11-19, the story or the card credits each. **Correction.** Add to Leave out: "If cut from the story, for the card, each credited: Rufinus's version of who went to the general and who received the refugees (D1), and Ceolfrid's claim that the cycle began in the apostles' time (D7)."

**M9. The outline leaves room for an unsupported claim about when he wrote.** Beat 5 comes after Laodicea, and the essence reads "he was kept there as its bishop, and he wrote". Together they let a writer say he wrote the reckoning as bishop. No source says where or when he wrote it. Harnack thinks it was "wohl noch in Alexandrien" (HARN 4855–4856). **Correction.** Add to Do not say: "Not 'as bishop he wrote', 'at Laodicea he wrote' or 'then he wrote'. No source says where or when he wrote it; Harnack thinks while still at Alexandria (E6)." Also add: "Not that his table ended Alexandria's yearly Easter notice. Ceolfrid's letter gives that change to Eusebius of Caesarea's later system (BEDE 14743–14748)."

**M10. Words to explain and What came.**
