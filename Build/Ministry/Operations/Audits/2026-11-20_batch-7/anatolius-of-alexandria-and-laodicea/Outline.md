# Outline: Anatolius of Alexandria and Laodicea

Sheet items (S, E, I, L, D) are in `Source_Sheet.md`, which gives the file and line for each. EUS is Eusebius's *Church History* VII.32 in McGiffert's English; EUS-gr the Greek (Schwartz), with Rufinus's Latin (RUF) on the facing pages; BEDE Bede's *History* in English (Giles), BEDE-lat in Latin (Plummer); LATCAN the Latin Easter book under his name, in the ANF English.

- **The essence:** Anatolius, a learned Christian of Alexandria, saved most of the starving people trapped in a besieged quarter of the city: he talked its council into letting the "useless" go, and under that cover got out almost everyone, his own church first (E2–E4; Documented as Eusebius's report, "they say"). Passing through Laodicea in Syria on his way to a synod, he was kept there as its bishop, and he wrote a reckoning of Easter on a cycle of nineteen years, the first Christian known to use one; almost four centuries later, at Whitby in England, both sides of the Easter quarrel claimed him (E5, E6, I1, I2; Documented; "first known", Dominant Modern Reconstruction).
- **Also told:**
  - **The learned man.** Eusebius says he stood first among the ablest men of his time in arithmetic, geometry, astronomy, logic, physics and the art of speaking, and that the citizens, it is reported, asked him to found a school of Aristotle's philosophy (S1; Documented as Eusebius's praise; the school, a report only).
  - **The siege and the stratagem.** Bread had failed in the palace quarter, which held out against a Roman army. He had a deacon outside the lines win from the Roman general a promise of safety for anyone who came over. Then he told the council to send out the old women, children and old men who would soon die anyway; when it voted that all not fit to fight might leave, he sent out, under that cover, a multitude of others beyond the decree, secretly dressed in women's clothes, by night to the Roman camp (E1–E4; Documented as Eusebius's report).
  - **Bishop on the road.** The bishop of Caesarea in Palestine made him a bishop to succeed him, and for a short time they led that church together. Called to the synod at Antioch on Paul of Samosata, he passed through Laodicea, whose bishop, the same deacon, had died, and the believers there kept him (E5; Documented).
  - **The Easter reckoning.** Easter's date hangs on the Passover moon. His rule was that the first month, whose fourteenth day is the Passover, must not begin before the spring equinox, and he set it on a cycle of nineteen years; he claimed Jewish writers before Christ for the rule (E6; Documented, his words).
  - **What came of it.** The nineteen-year cycle became Alexandria's and spread (I1; Dominant Modern Reconstruction). At Whitby in 664, Colman cited him for keeping Easter from the fourteenth day of the moon to the twentieth, and Wilfrid answered that Colman's side did not even keep his nineteen-year cycle (I2; Documented as Bede's account). The Latin book that holds Colman's rule may not be his: McGiffert takes it as an early translation, Harnack as a forgery made during that very quarrel (D4; Contested).
- **Length:** about 3 minutes 20 seconds (about 500 words). The count first: the quotes carry about 145 words (Quote 1 about 85, Quote 2 about 60); the plain sense of the words to explain about 70 (the palace quarter, the city council, the synod, the Passover moon and the spring equinox, the nineteen-year cycle, Bede's "Scots"); the witnesses credited about 75 (Eusebius's "they say", Bede writing two generations after Whitby, McGiffert's "so far as we know", McGiffert against Harnack on the Latin book). That leaves about 210 for the events. The record is one chapter of Eusebius and two chapters of Bede; but the siege is a scene worth its room, and Whitby, where both sides claim him, is the connection and needs a full paragraph. Cut first: Quote 3, the Rufinus variant (beat 3), the Picts (What came 1), What came 3.
- **Setting:**
  - Time: the second half of the third century. No year for his birth or death; bishop around 268 to 270 (S2). The siege year is disputed (D2): say "in the 260s or early 270s; the sources disagree on the year".
  - Place: Alexandria, in Egypt; Caesarea in Palestine; Laodicea, a port on the coast of Syria, not the Laodicea in Phrygia, in today's Turkey (S3). Antioch, the great city of Roman Syria, for the synod.
  - Tradition: the tree's spoken name is "Alexandrian Christianity" (S4). He is a Christian of Alexandria by birth, a bishop in Syria.
  - Language: he wrote in Greek (sheet, Language).

**Words to explain** (the first time each is heard; plain word or short gloss, worked into the sentence)

- **Pyrucheium / Brucheium**: "the palace quarter of the city", which held the royal palaces and the citadel (E1, McGiffert's note). Do not use either name.
- **the senate of Alexandria / the assembly**: "the city council" (E3; Greek βουλή).
- **synod**: "a meeting of bishops". The first time: "a meeting of bishops at Antioch, called to judge its own bishop, Paul of Samosata, for his teaching about Christ" (E5).
- **ordained him bishop … to make him his successor**: "made him a bishop beside himself, meaning him to take over when he died". Do not say "coadjutor" or "assistant bishop" bare.
- **Passover / the fourteenth of the moon**: "the Passover, kept on the fourteenth day of the first spring month, counted by the moon, at full moon" (E6, E7). Do not add a theology of the date, or a reason the two feasts are linked beyond what the outline holds.
- **the vernal equinox**: "the spring equinox, the day in March when day and night are equal" (LATCAN 14983 speaks of "equality between light and darkness"; by Anatolius's count the sun is four days past it on 22 March, E6).
- **cycle of nineteen years**: "a table of nineteen years, after which the moon's phases fall again on the same days of the year". The mechanism is the editors' (HEF 20562–20565 on how such tables work); keep it to that clause.
- **Phamenoth** (Quote 3, if used): "an Egyptian month"; the narrator gives "about the twenty-second of March", as he himself also gives it in the Roman way (E6).
- **the Scots** (Bede's word): "the Irish monks and their English followers, whom Bede calls the Scots" (BEDE 5749–5757; as in the live Hild story).
- **decrees** (Quote 2): "rules", already in the rendering.
- **Aristotelian**: "the philosophy of Aristotle". Do not say "Peripatetic".
- **Quartodeciman, computus, Metonic**: do not use.

- **Shape:** Founder or leader. What he led: a city council in a siege, then a church in Syria. How it ran: by persuasion, a vote and a stratagem; then by a rule for the year's greatest feast. Who it served: the starving of the palace quarter, his own church first; then the churches that needed to find Easter. The impact is in what outlasted him: the nineteen-year reckoning, and a name both sides wanted on their side at Whitby. The trap is titles in place of acts; here the acts are the message, the speech, the vote, the night exit, and the book.
- **Sibling:**
  - `polycrates-of-ephesus` (live text, read; job: the Easter quarrel of the 190s over the day of the week, his letter to Victor). Here one clause at most: an older quarrel had been over which day of the week; Anatolius's question was how to find the right moon (L1; Hefele's view, HEF 20653–20654). No Polycrates quote or detail is reused. Do not retell Victor.
  - `bede-of-jarrow` (live text, read; job: Bede's life and death). Here Bede is the witness to Whitby, credited once, and his own doubt (BEDE 5749–5757) is told in a clause. None of the Bede story's quotes is reused.
  - `hild-of-whitby` (live text, read; job: Hild and her house). It already tells that the kings met in her house in 664, that Colman led the Irish side and Wilfrid Rome's, and that the king ruled for Peter. Here: one clause that the synod met at her monastery, and the king's ruling in one clause; the Whitby paragraph is about the two claims on Anatolius only. No Hild quote reused.
  - `wilfrid-of-ripon`, `columba`, `cedd-and-chad` (live, read for overlap): each tells Whitby without Anatolius. Do not use Wilfrid's "in a corner of the remotest island" or Colman's "our most reverend Father Columba and his successors": they are held by those stories.
  - `eusebius-of-caesarea` (batch 7, outline not yet written): here Eusebius is the witness only. `dionysius-of-alexandria` (batch 7): no held source ties him to Anatolius; not told (L7).
- **Openings:**
  1. **Chosen.** The famine and the speech (E2, E3; Quote 1, first four sentences). In about three sentences: in a besieged quarter of Alexandria, Eusebius heard, the bread had run out, and hunger was harder to bear than the enemy outside. A man rose in the city council and asked why they should keep old women, children and old men who would soon be dead. Then the turn, with the name: he was Anatolius, a Christian whom Eusebius ranked first among the learned men of his time, and the people he named were the ones he got out. ("Meant to save them" is the writer's inference from the act; say what he did, not what he meant.) Then the essence.
  2. Not chosen: Whitby, 664, two churchmen claiming the same long-dead bishop (I2). Strong, but it is the story's connection and belongs at the end in time order; told first, it would be told twice.

**Story beats** (the opening, the essence and beat 1 are the first minute, about 150 words; the rest deepen it)

1. **The learned man.** Born in Alexandria; first among the ablest men of his time, Eusebius says, in arithmetic, geometry, astronomy, logic, physics and the art of speaking; and, it is reported, the citizens asked him to found a school of Aristotle's philosophy (S1; Documented as Eusebius's praise, the school a report). Eusebius knew of "many other" brave acts in the siege, for which those in high office honored him, and tells only this one (E1). One clause on who tells it: Eusebius, writing a generation later, tells the siege as he heard it ("they say").
2. **The siege and the message.** The palace quarter held out against a Roman army; the rest of the city was on Rome's side and not besieged (E1, E2). Bread failed. Anatolius sent word to a deacon of the church outside the lines, a man so well known that his name had reached the Roman general. The deacon asked the general, as the greatest favor, to spare anyone who came over from the enemy, and got his promise (E2; Documented as Eusebius's report). Give the deacon by role (Names).
3. **The council and the vote.** He called the city council and first urged them to make peace with Rome; they were angry. Then he made the proposal of Quote 1 (the rest of it, or in paraphrase if the opening used it). He rose first and voted that all not needed for the fighting, men or women, should leave the city, since if they stayed they would die of hunger; all agreed (E3, E4). Under cover of the decree he got out almost everyone: the members of the church first, then others of every age, and with them a multitude more, secretly dressed in women's clothes. By night they went out of the gates to the Roman camp, where the deacon took them in "like a father and physician" and nursed them back from the long siege (E4; Documented as Eusebius's report). The listener must hear the force: a vote won by an argument about who was useless, a disguise that took a multitude beyond the decree's classes past it, and Christians sent out first. Optional, one sentence, credited: in his Latin version a century later, Rufinus gives the deacon's part, going to the general and receiving the refugees, to Anatolius himself (D1).
4. **Caesarea, then Laodicea.** After the war he left Alexandria (E5). At Caesarea in Palestine, its bishop, Theotecnus (by role, Names), made him a bishop beside himself, meaning him to take over when he died, and for a short time the two led that church together (E5). Then the synod on Paul of Samosata called him to Antioch. On the way he passed through Laodicea, whose bishop, the same deacon from Alexandria, had died, and the believers there kept him as their bishop (E5; Documented). Eusebius: "one good man, as they say, following another" (paraphrase or a phrase in the narrator's sentence). Bishop around 268 to 270 (S2).
5. **The Easter reckoning.** He did not write many works, Eusebius says, but in them he set out his views on the Passover (E6). Why it mattered: Easter's date hangs on the Passover moon, kept on the fourteenth day of the first spring month (E6, E7). His rule: the first month must not begin before the spring equinox; whoever fixes the fourteenth day before it, he wrote, makes "no small" mistake (E6; his words, paraphrased). He set it on a cycle of nineteen years (Quote 3, optional; or in paraphrase) and claimed Jewish writers before Christ for the rule (E6). He also wrote ten books on arithmetic (E6). Where and when he wrote it, no source says (E6).

**What came of him** (about a third of the story; heaviest first)

1. **The nineteen-year cycle.** McGiffert, who translated Eusebius, calls him the first Christian, "so far as we know", to use the old nineteen-year cycle for Easter; it was soon taken up at Alexandria, and from there, the editors say, won its way everywhere (I1; Dominant Modern Reconstruction, editor's). Optional, one clause: in 710 the king of the Picts (BEDE 14315–14318) had the nineteen-year tables copied through his kingdom, Bede says, but the letter that persuaded him traced the cycle to the apostles' own time and does not name Anatolius (I1, D7). Do not say he invented the cycle (D7).
2. **Both sides at Whitby.** In 664 the Easter quarrel came to a synod at Hild's monastery at Whitby, in Northumbria (I2, L3). Bede, writing about two generations later, gives the speeches. Colman, for the Irish side, asked whether Anatolius, a holy man much praised in the Church History, had gone against the law or the gospel when he wrote that Easter should be kept from the fourteenth day of the moon to the twentieth (I2; paraphrase). Wilfrid, for Rome's side, answered with Quote 2. He added that Anatolius counted his days in the Egyptian way, from evening, so that his fourteenth was really the fifteenth, and his twentieth the twenty-first (I2; optional). The king ruled for Rome (one clause; Hild's story holds it). The listener must hear that the same man was the authority for both sides, and why: Colman's rule is in the Latin book under his name; Wilfrid's cycle is in his own words in Eusebius (I2, D5).
3. **Whose book?** Bede had already doubted the Irish claim: they believed they followed Anatolius, he wrote, and any skilled reader could tell whether that was true (I2). Scholars still disagree about the Latin book: McGiffert, following Zahn, takes it as an early translation of his genuine work; Harnack, with Ideler and others, holds it was forged, perhaps in the sixth century, during the British and Roman Easter quarrel itself (D4; Contested). Credit each; the narrator does not settle it. Optional (cut first): later readers kept using his Greek extract: in the eleventh century, Elias, a bishop of the Church of the East at Nisibis, read in it where the spring equinox fell in Anatolius's day (I4).

**Quotes** (2, with a third optional)

Only these are quoted aloud. "One good man following another" and "no small mistake" may stand as short phrases in the narrator's sentence.

1. EUS-gr 12485–12492 (VII.32.9), rendered from the Greek; checked against EUS 42735–42744. The council's own hard logic, which he used to get the weak out; the listener must hear at once, from what he did next, that the people he named were the ones he got out.
2. BEDE-lat 21581–21587 (III.25), rendered from the Latin; checked against BEDE 8134–8141. The Latin OCR is legible for this passage. Rendering: "It is agreed that Anatolius was a most holy and most learned man, most worthy of praise. But what have you to do with him, when you do not keep his rules? For he followed the rule of truth in his Easter, and set down a cycle of nineteen years. Either you do not know it, or you know it and treat it as nothing, though the whole church of Christ keeps it." Job: What came 2. The connection in a voice from Whitby: both sides honor him, and the cycle is what Rome's side says passed from him to the whole church. Credit it as Wilfrid's words in Bede's account.

**Connection to carry into the story:** what passed from Anatolius was a way to fix Easter: a rule that the Passover month must not begin before the spring equinox, set on a nineteen-year cycle (E6). Two things must be clear to the listener. First, why a date needs such a table: Easter follows the Passover moon, so its date moves and had to be worked out each year; for a time, Ceolfrid's letter in Bede says, the bishop of Alexandria sent it round to the churches (E6, E7; BEDE 14743–14748); a nineteen-year table lets anyone look ahead, because after nineteen years the moon's phases fall on the same days again (E6, E7; the mechanism is the editors', HEF 20562–20565). Second, what happened to his name. His cycle, so far as is known the first a Christian used, became Alexandria's (I1). By 664 his name had become an authority people fought over: at Whitby, Colman claimed him for the Irish rule, from the fourteenth day of the moon to the twentieth, and Wilfrid claimed him for the nineteen-year cycle that, he said, the whole church kept (I2). The two claims rest on two different texts: Wilfrid's on Anatolius's own words in Eusebius, Colman's on a Latin book under his name that may not be his (D4, D5). Tell it through these acts: the book, the speeches at Whitby, the king's ruling. Not as a "this matters because" sentence.

**Ending:** close on the person. Either (a) the opening image seen again: the weak and the disguised going out of the gates by night to the Roman camp, and of his own death no early writer says anything; before the Great Persecution, another man led his church (E4, E8); or (b) Wilfrid's words, if Quote 2 is not used in What came 2 (not recommended: it ends on a later voice). Recommended: (a), after What came, so the last thing heard is the night at the gates and the silence after him.

**Who tells it**

- **Eusebius**, writing early in the fourth century, about a generation later. The siege rests on him, and he tells it from report ("they say"). Say so once, in beat 1, inside a sentence about Eusebius. Credit him beside the stratagem and the disguise, where the record's force sits.
- **Anatolius himself**, in the Easter extract Eusebius quotes: the nineteen-year cycle and the equinox rule are his own words (E6). His speech is his only as Eusebius heard it.
- **Rufinus**, about 402, in Latin: the variant of the siege (D1). Optional, credited.
- **Bede**, finishing his History in 731, about 67 years after Whitby: the only held account of the synod and its speeches (I2). Credit Colman's and Wilfrid's words to them "in Bede's account" once.
- **McGiffert** and **Harnack**, modern scholars, on the Latin book (D4); McGiffert's "so far as we know" on the cycle (I1). The narrator does not settle it.
- **Duchesne** and **Harnack** guess that Anatolius had a part in the revolt itself (D1). Not told in the story; for the card.

**Do not say**

- Any word in "Words to explain" used without its plain sense the first time it is heard.
- Not "he saved the city" or "he ended the siege". Eusebius: he "saved almost all the besieged" by getting them out (E4). Rufinus's claim that he freed the city from famine and war is Rufinus's (D1).
- Not "he saved everyone". Say "almost all", Eusebius's words (E4).
- Not that he meant the speech's words, or that he despised the old and the weak. Eusebius gives it as the argument he used to persuade an angry council (E3, E4). Do not explain his motive beyond what follows in the record.
- Not "he tricked the Romans". The general had promised safety to all who came over (E2). The disguise took others past the council's decree. Eusebius does not say they were men of fighting age, though the decree let out only those not needed for the fighting; do not say "soldiers" or "fighting men". Say only what Eusebius says: "under cover of these", "secretly clothed in women's garments" (E4).
- Do not leave out that the church's members went first (E4), or that a multitude beyond the decree went out disguised. That is the record's force.
- Not "Anatolius led the rebels" or "he was a leader of the revolt". That is Duchesne's view (D1). Not "he was a senator" or "a member of the city council": Harnack's reading; Eusebius says only that he called the council and voted first (E3, E4).
- Not "he ran the school of Aristotle" or "he headed the Alexandrian school". Eusebius reports only that the citizens, it is said, asked him to found one (S1, D3). Not the catechetical school: no held source puts him there.
- Not "in 262", "in 260" or any single year for the siege (D2). Not "in 270" or "in 268" as fact for his becoming bishop: say "around 268 to 270" (S2).
- Not "the deacon was his old friend": McGiffert's phrase, not Eusebius's (E5).
- Not "Dionysius sent him to Antioch": an editor's guess (L7).
- Not "he took part in condemning Paul" or "he attended the synod". Eusebius says only that the synod called him and that he was kept at Laodicea on the way (E5, D6).
- Not "the Laodiceans forced him" or "kidnapped him". Eusebius: "he was detained by the brethren there" (E5). "Kept him" is the plain word.
- Not "Laodicea in Asia Minor" or in Phrygia. Say "Laodicea, on the coast of Syria" (S3).
- Not "he invented the nineteen-year cycle". It was old among the Greeks; he is the first Christian known to use it for Easter, "so far as we know" (I1, D7).
- Not "the Council of Nicaea adopted his cycle" (D8).
- Not "he wrote that Easter should be kept from the fourteenth to the twentieth" in the narrator's voice. That is Colman's claim, and the Latin book's; Wilfrid read him otherwise (D5).
- Not "the Irish forged his book" or "the book is a forgery" as fact. Harnack's view, against McGiffert's (D4).
- Not "Anatolius's cycle was adopted at Whitby", "the Picts adopted Anatolius's cycle" or "England took his tables". Wilfrid says the whole church keeps the nineteen-year cycle; the Roman tables that reached Britain came through Alexandria's later bishops and Dionysius Exiguus (BEDE 14738–14762), and Ceolfrid's letter does not name him (D7).
- Not "Bede says" for Colman's or Wilfrid's words without "in Bede's account", once.
- Not "Easter was decided at Whitby for all England" or more of the ruling than one clause: Hild's story holds it.
- Not Wilfrid's "a corner of the remotest island", or Colman's line on Columba (Sibling).
- Not "he died a martyr", or any death, date or grave (E8).
