# Source sheet: Anatolius of Alexandria and Laodicea

Researcher's sheet for batch 7 (2026-11-20). The job: the learned man of Alexandria who got the weak out of a besieged quarter of the city, became bishop of Laodicea on the road to a synod, and wrote the nineteen-year Easter reckoning both sides named at Whitby four centuries later. Shape: Founder or leader. The writer uses the outline, the shapes and the standard only; this sheet is the evidence behind the outline.

## Setting

S1. **Alexandria, and his learning.** EUS 42684–42690 (VII.32.6): "He also was an Alexandrian by birth. In learning and skill in Greek philosophy, such as arithmetic and geometry, astronomy, and dialectics in general, as well as in the theory of physics, he stood first among the ablest men of our time, and he was also at the head in rhetorical science. It is reported that for this reason he was requested by the citizens of Alexandria to establish there a school of Aristotelian philosophy." Greek EUS-gr 12392–12399 (λόγος ἔχει, "the report has it"). Documented (birth, learning, as Eusebius's praise); the school, Documented as a report only. Jerome, *Lives* 73: "a man of wonderful learning in arithmetic, geometry, astronomy, grammar, rhetoric, and dialectic" (JER 40932–40937; PL23 58316–58319). Contested: the list (D3).

S2. **Time.** No held source gives his birth or death. Jerome: "flourished under the emperors Probus and Carus" (JER 40933–40934; Probus 276–282, Carus 282–283, PL23 notes 58345, 58348). Jerome's *Chronicle*, second year of Probus: "Anatolius Laodicenus episcopus philosophorum disciplinis eruditus plurimo sermone celebratur" (JCHRON 44129–44130). Richardson: "Born about 230, bishop 270, died about 283" (JER-ed 40932, no source given); Salmond: "[a.d. 230–270–280.]", with "The dates which are known suggest conjectural dates of our author's birth and death" (ANF6-ed 14711–14714). Harnack: bishop "im J. 268 oder um dieses Jahr"; the Chronicle's dates are "wertlos", too late by about ten years; when he died "wissen wir nicht" (HARN 4797–4808). Duchesne puts the last council of Antioch in 268 (DUCH 17808). He did not live to the Great Persecution: by then Stephen was bishop of Laodicea (EUS 42942–42949; HARN 4811). Plain words: "in the second half of the third century"; bishop "around 268 to 270". Contested (all years).

S3. **Laodicea.** "Laodicea in Syria" (JER 40933), distinct from Laodicea in Phrygia (`npnf214_seven-ecumenical-councils.xml` 10477–10480, Percival). Loofs: "im syrischen Laodicea (am Meere)" (LOOFS 13957–13958). CEN `modernCountries`: Syria. Plain words: "Laodicea, a port on the coast of Syria". (The port is Loofs's "am Meere"; no other held source says it.)

S4. **Tradition.** CEN `toldWithin` `alexandria-catechetical`, spoken name "Alexandrian Christianity" (CEN `informalName`). The corpus map files his works there as "provisional because his episcopate was Syrian" (`cic/corpus-map/alexandria-catechetical.yaml` 105–107). CEN `statusWord`: "Named within another tradition's story".

## Events

E1. **The siege of the palace quarter.** EUS 42696–42717 (VII.32.7): "They relate of him many other eminent deeds during the siege of the Pyrucheium […] in Alexandria, on account of which he was especially honored by all those in high office; but I will give the following only as an example." McGiffert: the Pyrucheium or Brucheium "was one of the three districts of Alexandria and was inhabited by the royal family and by the Greeks. It was the finest and most beautiful quarter of the city, and contained, besides the royal palaces, many magnificent public buildings. Comprising, as it did, the citadel as well, it was besieged a number of times" (EUS-ed 42702–42708). Jerome's *Chronicle*, first year of Claudius: "In Alexandria Bruchium quod per multos annos fuerat obsessum tandem destruitur" (JCHRON 44299–44301). Documented as Eusebius's report ("They relate"); the year Contested (D2).

E2. **The famine and the deacon outside.** EUS 42718–42730 (VII.32.8–9): "They say that bread had failed the besieged, so that it was more difficult to withstand the famine than the enemy outside; but he being present provided for them in this manner. As the other part of the city was allied with the Roman army, and therefore was not under siege, Anatolius sent for Eusebius,—for he was still there before his transfer to Syria, and was among those who were not besieged, and possessed, moreover, a great reputation and a renowned name which had reached even the Roman general,—and he informed him of those who were perishing in the siege from famine. When he learned this he requested the Roman commander as the greatest possible favor, to grant safety to deserters from the enemy. Having obtained his request, he communicated it to Anatolius." Greek EUS-gr 12402–12404, 12474–12482. Eusebius the deacon: "a deacon in the church of Alexandria, who distinguished himself by his good offices during the persecution of Valerian" (EUS-ed 42642–42645). Documented as Eusebius's report ("They say").

E3. **The council, and his speech.** EUS 42732–42744 (VII.32.9): "As soon as he received the message he convened the senate of Alexandria, and at first proposed that all should come to a reconciliation with the Romans. But when he perceived that they were angered by this advice, he said, 'But I do not think you will oppose me, if I counsel you to send the supernumeraries and those who are in nowise useful to us, as old women and children and old men, outside the gates, to go wherever they may please. For why should we retain for no purpose these who must at any rate soon die? and why should we destroy with hunger those who are crippled and maimed in body, when we ought to provide only for men and youth, and to distribute the necessary bread among those who are needed for the garrison of the city?'" Greek EUS-gr 12483–12492: "βουλὴν τῶν Ἀλεξανδρέων συναγαγών, τὰ μὲν πρῶτα πάντας ἠξίου φιλικὴν δοῦναι Ῥωμαίοις δεξιάν, ὡς δ᾽ ἀγριαίνοντας ἐπὶ τῷ λόγῳ συνεῖδεν, 'ἀλλ᾽ οὐ τούτῳ γε,' φησίν, 'ἀντιλέξειν ποθ᾽ ὑμᾶς οἴομαι, εἰ τοὺς περιττοὺς καὶ ἡμῖν αὐτοῖς οὐδαμῇ χρησίμους, γραΐδας καὶ νήπια καὶ πρεσβύτας, ἐκδοῦναι πυλῶν ἔξω βαδίζειν ὅποι καὶ βούλοιντο, συμβουλεύσαιμι. τί γὰρ δὴ τούτους εἰς μάτην, ὅσον οὔπω τεθνηξομένους, παρ᾽ ἑαυτοῖς ἔχομεν; τί δὲ τοὺς ἀναπήρους καὶ τὰ σώματα λελωβημένους τῷ λιμῷ κατατρύχομεν, τρέφειν δέον μόνους ἄνδρας καὶ νεανίας καὶ τὸν ἀναγκαῖον πυρὸν τοῖς ἐπὶ φυλακῇ τῆς πόλεως ἐπιτηδείοις ταμιεύεσθαι;'" (OCR normalized; the held lines read `xci` for καί, `oc δ᾽` for ὡς δ᾽, `ποϑ᾽` for ποθ᾽.) "Senate" = βουλή, the city council (EUS-gr 12483, 12498). Documented as Eusebius's report of his words; the words are hearsay set down a generation later.

E4. **The decree, the escape, and the disguise.** EUS 42745–42763 (VII.32.10–11): "With such arguments he persuaded the assembly, and rising first he gave his vote that the entire multitude, whether of men or women, who were not needful for the army, should depart from the city, because if they remained and unnecessarily continued in the city, there would be for them no hope of safety, but they would perish with famine. As all the others in the senate agreed to this, he saved almost all the besieged. He provided that first, those belonging to the church, and afterwards, of the others in the city, those of every age should escape, not only the classes included in the decree, but, under cover of these, a multitude of others, secretly clothed in women's garments; and through his management they went out of the gates by night and escaped to the Roman camp. There Eusebius, like a father and physician, received all of them, wasted away through the long siege, and restored them by every kind of prudence and care." Greek EUS-gr 12492–12502, 12570–12578 (τῇ δὲ τούτων προφάσει καὶ μυρίους ἄλλους, λεληθότως γυναικείαν στολὴν ἀμπισχομένους, νύκτωρ; πατρὸς καὶ ἰατροῦ δίκην). Documented as Eusebius's report. The force: the decree covered only those "not needful for the army"; under its cover he got out "a multitude" of others in women's clothes, Christians first.

E5. **To Caesarea, then Laodicea.** EUS 42764–42767 (VII.32.12): "The church of Laodicea was honored by two such pastors in succession, who, in the providence of God, came after the aforesaid war from Alexandria to that city." EUS 42925–42940 (VII.32.21): "Theotecnus, bishop of Cæsarea in Palestine, first ordained him as bishop, designing to make him his successor in his own parish after his death. And for a short time both of them presided over the same church. But the synod which was held to consider Paul's case called him to Antioch, and as he passed through the city of Laodicea, Eusebius being dead, he was detained by the brethren there." EUS 42683–42684: Anatolius "was appointed his successor; one good man, as they say, following another" (EUS-gr 12391–12392: ἀγαθός, φασίν, ἀγαθοῦ). The deacon Eusebius had gone to Syria "on this account", "the affair of Paul", and "was restrained from returning home by those there who were zealous in divine things" (EUS 42661–42664). Documented.
- McGiffert: "When on his way to (possibly on his return from) the synod of Antioch" (EUS-ed 42673–42676); he calls the deacon "his old friend" (EUS-ed 42677), which Eusebius does not say. McGiffert and Valesius: Theotecnus and Anatolius are among the first known "assistant bishops" (EUS 33475–33480, EUS-ed 42672–42673). Harnack: the Laodiceans "nötigte" him (HARN 4796–4798).
- Paul's case: the final synod at Antioch condemned Paul of Samosata, bishop of Antioch, and "his false doctrine clearly shown before all, and he was excommunicated from the Catholic Church under heaven" (EUS 41950–41954, VII.29.1). Whether Anatolius reached the synod: D6.

E6. **His writing on Easter.** EUS 42769–42773 (VII.32.13): "Anatolius did not write very many works; but in such as have come down to us we can discern his eloquence and erudition. In these he states particularly his opinions on the passover." Own words, the extract (EUS 42789–42816; EUS-gr 12598–12612): "There is then in the first year the new moon of the first month, which is the beginning of every cycle of nineteen years, on the twenty-sixth day of the Egyptian Phamenoth; but according to the months of the Macedonians, the twenty-second day of Dystrus, or, as the Romans would say, the eleventh before the Kalends of April. On the said twenty-sixth of Phamenoth, the sun is found not only entered on the first segment, but already passing through the fourth day in it. […] But they call the one preceding this the last of months, and the twelfth segment […] Wherefore we maintain that those who place the first month in it, and determine by it the fourteenth of the passover, commit no slight or common blunder." Greek EUS-gr 12598–12599: "Ἔχει τοίνυν ἐν τῷ πρώτῳ ἔτει τὴν νουμηνίαν τοῦ πρώτου μηνός, ἥτις ἁπάσης ἐστὶν ἀρχὴ τῆς ἐννεακαιδεκαετηρίδος, τὴν κατ᾽ Αἰγυπτίους μὲν Φαμενὼθ κϛʹ"; 12608–12612: "δι᾽ ὃ καὶ τοὺς ἐν αὐτῷ τιθεμένους τὸν πρῶτον μῆνα καὶ τὴν τεσσαρεσκαιδεκάτην τοῦ πάσχα κατ᾽ αὐτὴν λαμβάνοντας οὐ μικρῶς οὐδ᾽ ὡς ἔτυχεν ἁμαρτάνειν φαμέν" (Schwartz flags κατ᾽ αὐτὴν as corrupt, apparatus 12625–12626). Rufinus's Latin: "decem et novem annorum circuli principium" (EUS-gr 12660–12661). Phamenoth 26 = 22 March (EUS-ed 42797–42803).
- He claims the rule is old: "it was known to the Jews of old, even before Christ […] Philo, Josephus, and Musæus […] the two Agathobuli […] and the famous Aristobulus" (EUS 42817–42867): the Passover sacrifice "after the vernal equinox, in the middle of the first month" (EUS 42868–42871). Then: "I refrain from demanding this sort of demonstration for matters from which the veil of the Mosaic law has been removed, so that now at length with uncovered face we continually behold as in a glass Christ and the teachings and sufferings of Christ" (EUS 42890–42897).
- Also "the Institutes of Arithmetic, in ten books, and other evidences of his experience and proficiency in divine things" (EUS 42918–42925). Documented (his words).
- Where and when he wrote it: no source says. Harnack: "Die betitelten beiden Schriften sind wohl noch in Alexandrien abgefaßt" (HARN 4855–4856, editor's). Hefele: "He discovered the Easter cycle of nineteen years, and began it with the year 277, probably because in that year his calculation was established"; "He considers the 19th March as the equinox"; "He says nothing about the old question relating to the fast" (HEF 20638–20654, editor's).
- What a cycle does (editor's): Hefele on Hippolytus's sixteen-year table, "after sixteen years, the Easter full moon falls on the same day of the month" (HEF 20562–20565).

E7. **Why Easter needed a reckoning** (for the writer's gloss). Passover is kept "on the fourteenth of the month, beginning with the evening", at the full moon, "after the vernal equinox" (EUS 42868–42882, his extract). The Easter fast question of the second century: see the Polycrates outline (batch 6), and LATCAN 15099–15113 below.

E8. **The end.** "After Anatolius had departed this life, the last bishop of that parish before the persecution was Stephen" (EUS 42942–42944). No held source tells his death or burial. Documented (the succession); death, not known.

## Impact (ranked: what later witnesses carried on and used)

I1. **The nineteen-year cycle.** McGiffert: "Anatolius was, so far as we know, the first Christian to employ the old Metonic nineteen-year cycle for the determination of Easter" (EUS-ed 42791–42793). Fuller: "the first Christian to make use of it for the computation of Easter, so far as we know, was Anatolius […]. It was soon adopted in the Alexandrian church, and already in the time of Athanasius had become the basis of all Easter calculations […]. From about the time of the Nicene Council on, Alexandria was commonly looked to for the reckoning of the date of Easter, and although an older and less accurate cycle remained in use in the West for a long time, the nineteen-year cycle gradually won its way everywhere" (EUS-ed 40901–40915). Hefele: the cycle "was soon subjected to different modifications, after which it was generally adopted in Alexandria from the time of Diocletian" (HEF 20658–20661). Dominant Modern Reconstruction (editors; "so far as we know").
- Its later road, without his name: Bede's copy of Abbot Ceolfrid's letter to the Pictish king (about 710): the reckoning "is contained in a circle or revolution of nineteen years, which began long since, that is, in the very times of the apostles, especially at Rome and in Egypt"; Theophilus, Cyril and Dionysius Exiguus extended it (BEDE 14738–14762). After it, "the circles or revolutions of nineteen years were presently, by public command, sent throughout all the provinces of the Picts […], the erroneous revolutions of eight-four years being everywhere suppressed" (BEDE 14969–14973). Documented (Bede); the letter does not name Anatolius and gives the cycle an apostolic origin (D7).

I2. **Both sides at Whitby named him (664).** The synod met "in the monastery of Streaneshalch […] where the Abbess Hilda […] then presided"; "the Abbess Hilda and her followers were for the Scots" (BEDE 7958–7967). The year: "in the year of our Lord's incarnation 664" (BEDE 8237). Wilfrid had cited "the Church History" (BEDE 8085). Colman: "Did Anatolius, a holy man, and much commended in church history, act contrary to the law and the Gospel, when he wrote, that Easter was to be celebrated from the fourteenth to the twentieth?" (BEDE 8117–8121). Latin: "Numquid Anatolius uir sanctus, et in praefata historia ecclesiastica multum laudatus, legi uel euangelio contraria sapuit, qui a XIIII usque ad XX pascha celebrandum scripsit?" (BEDE-lat 21561–21571; OCR damaged at 21570–21571, "sc lii.sit"; Giles confirms). Wilfrid: "It is evident […] that Anatolius was a most holy, learned, and commendable man; but what have you to do with him, since you do not observe his decrees? For he, following the rule of truth in his Easter, appointed a revolution of nineteen years, which either you are ignorant of, or if you know it, though it is kept by the whole church of Christ, yet you despise it. He so computed the fourteenth moon in the Easter of our Lord, that according to the custom of the Egyptians, he acknowledged it to be the fifteenth moon in the evening; so in like manner he assigned the twentieth to Easter-Sunday, as believing that to be the twenty-first moon, when the sun had set" (BEDE 8134–8148). Latin: "Constat," inquit, "Anatolium uirum sanctissimum, doctissimum, ac laude esse dignissimum; sed quid uobis cum illo, cum nec eius decreta seruetis? Ille enim in pascha suo regulam utique ueritatis sequens, circulum X et VIIII annorum posuit, quem uos aut ignoratis, aut agnitum et a tota Christi ecclesia custoditum pro nihilo contemnitis" (BEDE-lat 21581–21587; legible). The king ruled for Peter and Rome, and Colman went home with those who would not accept it (BEDE 8200–8218, 8226–8233). Documented as Bede's account, written in 731, about 67 years later.
- Before Whitby, Bede on Aidan: he kept Easter Sunday "from the fourteenth to the twentieth moon; the northern province of the Scots, and all the nation of the Picts, celebrating Easter then after that manner, and believing that they therein followed the writings of the holy and praiseworthy Father Anatolius; the truth of which every skilful person can discern" (BEDE 5749–5757; BEDE-lat 18704–18709, "Quod an uerum sit, peritus quisque facillime cognoscit"). Bede's own doubt, credited to him.
- What the Latin book under his name says: "it is granted that we may extend the beginning of our celebration even to the moon's twentieth" (LATCAN 15009–15012); "we decide that it is better that it should be extended even on to the twentieth day, on account of the Lord's day, than that we should anticipate the Lord's day on account of the fourteenth day" (LATCAN 15150–15156). So Colman's words match the Latin book; Wilfrid's match the Greek extract (D4, D5).

I3. **Remembered as the learned bishop.** Eusebius's praise (S1). Jerome, *Lives* 73: "We can get an idea of the greatness of his genius from the volume which he wrote *On the passover* and his ten books *On the institutes of arithmetic*" (JER 40935–40938). Jerome, Letter 70, defending a Christian's use of pagan learning, lists among writers whose works survive "Anatolius chief priest of the church of Laodicea" and says all of them "so frequently interweave in their books the doctrines and maxims of the philosophers" (JEP 16665–16700). Chronicles copy Jerome's line: Prosper (PROSP 51577–51578, apparatus) and Bede's *Greater Chronicle*, entry 389 (BCHRON 23993–23998). Bar Hebraeus, in the thirteenth century: Eusebius and Anatolius, "Alexandrini ambo atque in disciplinis tam sacris quam profanis vere principes" (BARH 4961–4965). Michael the Syrian, in Chabot's French: "A Laodicée était Eusebius. Il eut pour successeur Anatolius qui était célèbre" (MICH 15951–15952; the sentence breaks off in the held text). Documented.

I4. **Later readers used his extract.** Socrates of Constantinople, against Sabbatius's Easter, cites "as is related by Eusebius, on the testimony of Philo, Josephus, Aristobulus" the rule of sacrifice after the vernal equinox: the names are those of Anatolius's extract, though Socrates does not name him (SOC 41696–41704; the note "Extracts from the canons of Anatolius" is the translator's). Elias of Nisibis, in the eleventh century, used "verba Anatolii, episcopi Laodiceae" to show where the spring equinox fell in his time, 22 Adar, two days earlier than in Christ's time (ELIAS 41058–41065). Bede lists among his own works a letter "of the Reason of the Bissextile, or Leap-Year, and of the Equinox, according to Anatolius" (BEDE 15510–15513). Documented.

## Links

L1. **Polycrates of Ephesus** (live, read; and the batch 6 outline). Polycrates's quarrel with Victor was over the day of the week for Easter, near the end of the second century. The Latin book under Anatolius's name tells that quarrel: "a certain contention broke out between the successors of these, namely, Victor, at that time bishop of the city of Rome, and Polycrates, who then appeared to hold the primacy among the bishops of Asia. And this contention was adjusted most rightfully by Irenæus" (LATCAN 15099–15113). Hefele: Anatolius "says nothing about the old question relating to the fast" (HEF 20653–20654). Link kind: the next stage of the Easter question; the Latin book under his name tells Polycrates's quarrel. The Polycrates outline set the Latin book aside as too uncertain to carry an outcome.
L2. **Bede** (live, read). Wrote the account of Whitby (I2); doubted the Irish claim on Anatolius (I2); copied Jerome's line into his Chronicle (I3); wrote a letter on the equinox "according to Anatolius" (I4). Mommsen: Bede "habuit Anatolium subditicium", had the spurious (Latin) Anatolius (BCHRON-ed 18865–18866; OCR damaged, "linl)uit Anatolium suliditicium"; editor's).
L3. **Hild of Whitby** (live, read). The synod met in her monastery; she and her people held with the Irish side (BEDE 7958–7967).
L4. **Eusebius of Caesarea**: wrote his life and kept his Easter extract (E1–E6).
L5. **Jerome**: wrote of him (I3).
L6. **Wilfrid of Ripon**, **Columba**, **Cedd and Chad**: live stories that tell Whitby; none names Anatolius. Columba's story quotes Colman's "our most reverend Father Columba and his successors" (same speech, the next sentence); not reused here.
L7. **Dionysius of Alexandria**: no held source ties him to Anatolius directly. Bardy (BARDY 10725–10728) and Harnack ("Man darf vielleicht annehmen", HARN 4784–4789) guess Dionysius sent Eusebius and Anatolius to Antioch; editors' surmise.
L8. **Rufinus of Aquileia**: his Latin version of the chapter (D1).
L9. **Paul of Samosata**: not a person entry; his case is in `antiochene-church-third-century`.

## Where sources disagree, or come late

D1. **Who did what in the siege.** Eusebius: Anatolius was among the besieged ("he being present"), sent for the deacon Eusebius, who was outside and asked the Roman general; the deacon received the refugees "like a father and physician" (E2, E4). Rufinus, about 402: a civil war "inter cives"; the city in three parts, a third part "quasi fidem Romanis servabat. in qua parte tunc etiam supra dictus Anatolius erat absque inpugnatione. hic ad magistrum militiae refert" (EUS-gr 12447–12448, 12533–12536): Anatolius himself was in the unbesieged part and went to the commander; Rufinus's speech asks only "ut omnem vestrorum manum inbellem […] exire portas sinatis" (12539–12542); the refugees are "praecipue nostri quibus bellum gerere contra propositum erat" (12544–12545); and "Anatolius suscipiens parentis simul ac medici fovebat affectu" (12654): the receiving is given to Anatolius; Rufinus adds that with "misericordi prudentique consilio" he freed the city from famine and war (12655–12658). Duchesne: "Les insurgés étaient retranchés dans le quartier du Bruchion. Parmi leurs chefs figurait un chrétien appelé Anatole" (DUCH 17775–17777), and "compromis sans doute dans la récente insurrection" (DUCH 17794–17795). Harnack: "kann auch, weil er politisch kompromittiert war, Ägypten verlassen haben" (HARN 4790–4791); he was "Mitglied des dortigen Stadtrats" (HARN-M 1402–1404). Eusebius says only that he was honored "by all those in high office" (EUS 42715). The story follows Eusebius and credits him; Rufinus's version is credited where told; the "rebel leader" reading is the historians', Contested.

D2. **The year of the siege.** McGiffert: "it is uncertain which siege is meant […]. It seems to me most likely that we are to think of the time of the revolt of Æmilian […], in 260 a.d. […] Valesius and others think of a later siege under Claudius" (EUS-ed 42708–42714). McGiffert elsewhere: Eusebius's *Chronicle* puts the siege in 272 (EUS-ed 41937–41938). Jerome's *Chronicle*: destroyed in Claudius's first year, after being besieged "per multos annos" (JCHRON 44299–44301). Salmond: "besieged by the Romans in a.d. 262" (ANF6-ed 14730–14731). Harnack: Aemilian's revolt under Gallienus (HARN 4770–4777). Loofs: probably 270, and the journey to Syria before the fall (LOOFS 13975–13985). Contested.

D3. **His subjects.** Eusebius: arithmetic, geometry, astronomy, dialectics, physics, rhetoric (S1). Jerome: grammar in place of physics (JER 40935). Vallarsi: "grammatica" can be struck on the Verona manuscript's authority, since Eusebius did not say it (PL23 58356–58362). Rufinus: from boyhood "adprime eruditus", so that at Alexandria he was compelled "dialecticam docere" (EUS-gr 12445–12447): a teaching post, not Eusebius's report of a school. Duchesne makes him "chef de l'école aristotélicienne d'Alexandrie" (DUCH 18887–18888); Eusebius gives only a report that he was asked to found one (S1).

D4. **The Latin Easter book.** McGiffert: "still extant in a Latin translation supposed to be the work of Rufinus (though this is uncertain) […]. Ideler […] claims that this supposed translation of Anatolius is a work of the seventh century. But there are the best of reasons for supposing it an early translation of Anatolius' genuine work (see Zahn […])" (EUS-ed 42773–42781). Harnack: "von Hagen […] als unecht erwiesen worden, und Ideler […] und Krusch haben ihm beigestimmt. Zahn […] hat dagegen Bedenken erhoben"; the text cites Isidore and Jerome (LATCAN 14790), which Harnack strikes as interpolations, since Columbanus already cites this Latin Anatolius (HARN 4824–4844); he concludes: "Die Schrift wird im 6. Jahrhundert oder schon früher im Zusammenhang mit der britisch-römischen Osterkontroverse gefälscht worden sein" (HARN 4871–4873). Mommsen: Bede had "Anatolium subditicium" (BCHRON-ed 18865–18866, damaged OCR). Salmond calls the Latin "generally ascribed to Rufinus" (ANF6-ed 14747). Contested. The story says plainly that the book Colman's side relied on may not be his, crediting each side.

D5. **What Anatolius taught about the fourteenth to the twentieth.** Colman: he wrote that Easter should be kept from the fourteenth to the twentieth (I2). Wilfrid: he counted in the Egyptian way, so that his fourteenth was the fifteenth by evening and his twentieth the twenty-first; the Irish ignore this (I2). Bede, earlier: the Irish "believing that they therein followed" him, "the truth of which every skilful person can discern" (I2). Each credited.

D6. **Did he reach the synod?** Eusebius: the synod called him; passing through Laodicea he was detained (E5). McGiffert: "on his way to (possibly on his return from)" (EUS-ed 42673–42674). Bardy: "Plusieurs témoignages s'accordent à dire cependant qu'il était présent au concile qui déposa Paul" (BARDY 11408–11412; the witnesses are in a cut footnote). He is not among the names at the head of the synod's letter (EUS 42006–42015). Inferential-Thin. Tell only what Eusebius says.

D7. **Who first used the nineteen years.** McGiffert: Anatolius, "so far as we know" (I1). Hefele: "He discovered the Easter cycle of nineteen years" (HEF 20641–20642; editor's, and stronger than McGiffert). Ceolfrid's letter in Bede: the nineteen-year circle "began long since, that is, in the very times of the apostles, especially at Rome and in Egypt" (BEDE 14740–14742). Wilfrid credits Anatolius with it (I2). Bucherius, quoted in the PL67 notes, thought Anatolius's own cycle was used only a few years, at Laodicea and nearby (PL67-ed 36039–36045). The nineteen-year cycle itself is older than the church: "the old Metonic nineteen-year cycle (already in use among the Greeks in the fifth century b.c.)" (EUS-ed 40903–40905). The story says "the first Christian known to use it", with McGiffert's qualifier; not "invented".

D8. **Nicaea and the cycle.** McGiffert: "The Nicene Council sanctioned it" (EUS-ed 40906). Hefele, citing Ambrose (not held): Nicaea "adopted the cycle of nineteen years" (HEF 21014–21018). The live `dionysius-exiguus` story credits Duchesne that Nicaea made no such ruling. Contested; Leave out.
