In Carthage, in today's Tunisia, people crowded the doors of a house, waiting for Cyprian. Newly baptized, he had tried to step aside for older men, but they wanted him as bishop, says his deacon Pontius, who wrote his life as an admirer.

Cyprian had taught public speaking, and became bishop about two years after his baptism, by modern reckoning. For ten years he led the church of Roman Africa through persecution, plague and division; some eighty letters show how. He taught that the church is one mother, and died by the sword, the city's first martyr bishop, Pontius says.

In 250 the emperor Decius ordered everyone to sacrifice to the gods. The crowds were calling for Cyprian, he wrote, so he withdrew from the city and hid, leading his church by letter. Clergy at Rome wrote to Carthage of the good shepherd who lays down his life and the hired man who runs. Cyprian replied that his acts had been reported "less than plainly and faithfully", and Pontius later had to argue that fear was not the cause.

When Novatian was set up at Rome as a rival bishop, Cyprian would not receive his envoys as church members. He wrote: "Whoever does not have the Church as mother cannot have God as Father." The church, he meant, bears and feeds Christians like a mother.

When plague struck, about 252, people left their own sick and Cyprian urged care for all, Pontius says. He wrote that it tested everyone: "Do the healthy serve the sick? Do relatives love their kin as they should? Do masters pity their sick slaves? Do doctors stand by the sick who beg for help?"

Cyprian and the African councils held that washing in groups cut off from the church was no baptism, and baptized those who came over. Stephen, bishop of Rome, as Cyprian quotes him, said only to lay hands on them as a sign of repentance. Cyprian called his letter proud and his view an error. At a council of eighty-seven bishops, probably in 256, Cyprian said: "For none of us sets himself up as a bishop of bishops, or drives his fellow bishops by a tyrant's terror to obey." No bishop, he meant, may force another; each answers to Christ. A bishop in Cappadocia, on Cyprian's side, reported that Stephen refused the African envoys a roof and called Cyprian a false Christ. Stephen died in 257, with no settlement recorded.

That year, says a Christian account built on the court record, he told the Roman governor he was "a Christian and a bishop", and refused the Roman rites. He was exiled to a small town on the coast, and in 258 a harsher law ordered bishops killed. When officers came to take him to Utica, he left his gardens on friends' advice, he wrote, to declare his faith before his own people. Pontius says he refused friends' offers of refuge. The account says he stayed at his gardens by a holy command.

On 14 September 258 he again refused the rites: in so just a cause, he said, there was nothing to think over. The sentence named him an enemy of the Roman gods and leader of a wicked conspiracy, whose blood would be an example to his followers. Sentenced to the sword, he answered, "Thanks be to God," and the crowd cried that they would be beheaded with him. The account says two clergymen tied the cloth over his eyes; Pontius says he bound them himself.

Later, the Donatists, a breakaway African church, quoted his letters and council to defend baptizing again. Augustine of Hippo, about 140 years after Cyprian's death, answered that Cyprian, though wrong on baptism, never left the church's unity and cut off no one who differed. He quoted the "bishop of bishops" line again and again, asking, "What could be more kind? What more humble?" So both sides claimed him.

The night he died, the account says, his body was carried with candles and torches, in prayer and great triumph, to a burial ground outside the city.
