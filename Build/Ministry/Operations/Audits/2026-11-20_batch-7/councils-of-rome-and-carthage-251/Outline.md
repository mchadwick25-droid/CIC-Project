# Outline: Councils of Carthage and Rome on the lapsed (251)

Sheet items (S, E, I, D) are in `Source_Sheet.md`, which gives the file and line for each. EP and LAPS are Cyprian's own Latin (Hartel, clean TEI texts); ANF is Cyprian's letters in English; EUS is Eusebius in English (with Cornelius's report), EUSG the Greek; SOC is Socrates in English, SOCG2 his Greek; NIC is the canons of Nicaea; HEF is Hefele, a modern historian of the councils.

- **The essence:** When the persecution under the emperor Decius ended, many Christians who had given way under it asked to come back to the church (E1, E2, E6; Documented). In 251 the bishops of Africa met at Carthage under Cyprian, and then sixty bishops at Rome under Cornelius, and chose a middle way: the fallen would not be denied all hope of return, and none would come straight back; they would do long penance, and each case would be examined (E6, E8; Documented, from Cyprian, who called the first council, and from Eusebius's summary of Cornelius's letters). A stricter party would take none of them back to communion; it set up Novatian as a rival bishop of Rome, and the church he led split from the rest and lasted for centuries (E7, E8, I1; Documented).
- **Also told:**
  - **The pressure to take them back at once.** Confessors, Christians jailed for the faith, were handing out written pardons, "thousands" a day, Cyprian says (E3; Documented as his account). A deacon, Felicissimus, and five elders at Carthage offered the fallen peace without penance; Cyprian called it "another persecution" (E5; Cyprian's charge, the only witness).
  - **Cyprian's wait.** From hiding he refused to decide alone: nothing new until the bishops could meet, and meanwhile peace only for the dying; the clergy at Rome agreed (E4; Documented).
  - **What Carthage decided.** The middle way in the council's own terms, from Cyprian (Quote 1). Those who had bought certificates were admitted, case by case; those who had sacrificed were given peace at death; those who asked only when dying, without having repented before, were refused; the leaders of the lax party were cut off (E6; Documented). The book of decisions is lost (E6).
  - **Rome, and the split.** Novatian wrote to the churches that those who had sacrificed must not be admitted, their pardon left to God (Quote 2; Documented as Socrates's report, about two centuries later). Rome's sixty bishops agreed with Carthage, held Novatian and his followers "strangers" to the church, and told the churches to heal the fallen "with the medicines of repentance" (E8; Documented, Eusebius from Cornelius).
  - **What it did to the churches.** Within a year, with a new persecution expected, the African bishops gave peace to all who had kept repenting (E11, Quote 3; Documented; year Hefele's). Novatian's church kept its strict rule for centuries (I1).

- **Setting:**
  - Time: the emperor Decius's order to sacrifice, early 250 (S1). The council at Carthage after Easter 251, probably May, and at Rome probably October 251, both by Hefele's dating (S4; Dominant Modern Reconstruction). The African bishops' new decision, 252 by Hefele (E11). Plain words: "in 251".
  - Place: Carthage, the chief city of Roman Africa, in today's Tunisia; Rome (S2). Plain words: "Carthage, in what is now Tunisia, and Rome".
  - Tradition: the churches of Africa and of Rome (S3). Do not say the tree's labels aloud.

**Words to explain** (the first time each is heard; plain word or short gloss, worked into the sentence)

- **the lapsed**: "those who had given way", "the fallen". Say it once with its plain sense; the census name uses it (E1).
- **to sacrifice**: "to offer a sacrifice to the gods before the officials", the act the order demanded (E1, E2).
- **forum**: 'the city's main square, where the officials sat' (E1).
- **certificate**: "a paper from an official saying they had sacrificed", which some bought without sacrificing (E2). Never "libellus" aloud.
- **letters of peace**: "written pardons handed out by confessors". Keep apart from the certificates of sacrifice; never call both "certificates" (E3).
- **confessors**: "Christians who had been jailed or tortured for the faith and had not given way" (E3).
- **peace / communion**: "being taken back to share the Lord's Supper with the church" (E6). Say once that "peace" was the church's word for it.
- **penance**: "a time of public sorrow and prayer before being taken back" (E6).
- **cut off / excommunicated**: "shut out of the church's fellowship". Prefer "cut off".
- **elders**: for presbyters. Not "priests".
- **"sacred rites"** (Quote 2): the narrator gives the plain sense, the Lord's Supper (E7).
- **"strangers to the church"**, **"the medicines of repentance"**: the decree's words at Rome; plain sense, treated as outsiders; healed by penance (E8).
- **"the battle line"** (Quote 3): the narrator says plainly that a new persecution was expected, and peace was given so they could stand firm in it (E11).

- **Shape:** Council. What called it: the fallen after Decius and the pressure to take them back at once. The major issue and the spine: on what terms the lapsed come back. Decisions: Carthage's terms; Rome's agreement and the cutting off of Novatian's party; the lax leaders cut off at Carthage. Impact: the terms changed within a year; the split lasted. Voices followed: Cyprian (who waited and then wrote the decision), Cornelius (who held Rome's council, through Eusebius), Novatian (through Socrates), and the fallen themselves as Cyprian describes them. The trap is one dispute swallowing the council: the rival bishop is one beat, not the spine.

Here Cyprian acts as the bishop who waited and called the council, and as its witness; his withdrawal under Decius is one clause, its charge is not retold; none of his quotes is reused ("mother", the plague, "bishop of bishops", "less than plainly and faithfully"). `novatian` (batch 7, outline done; job: his life, book, election and church). Here his election is told only as the split: no oaths, no three bishops, no oath at the Lord's table, no book, no Acesius or ladder. His letter of 250 for the Roman clergy ("knock at the doors") is his opening and quote; here one clause, "the clergy at Rome agreed", without the quote. Allocated here by the batch README and the `novatian` outline: Socrates's statement of the rule (Quote 2), "strangers" and "the medicines of repentance", and Socrates's "Phrygia and Paphlagonia ... to the present day". Dionysius's letter to Novatian stays with `novatian`. `novatian` tells the other terms of Nicaea's canon 8; here only its term that Novatian's clergy, if they came over, share communion with the lapsed who had done their penance (NIC 3769–3777). `pontius-the-deacon`: no shared event. `dionysius-of-alexandria` (batch 7): leaves Serapion and the lapsed at Alexandria to this story; cut for length, for the card.
- **Openings:**
  1. **Chosen. The run to the forum** (E1; LAPS 345–352). In about three sentences: in 250 the emperor Decius ordered everyone to sacrifice to the gods. At Carthage, its bishop Cyprian wrote a year later, many Christians did not wait to be arrested; they ran to the forum of their own accord. As evening came on, the officials put some off, and some begged not to be put off. Then the essence: once the persecution ended, many of them asked to come back. Credit Cyprian in the sentence; he writes as their bishop, against what they did. It is the first event in time, so no beat repeats it.
  2. Not chosen: the confessors' letter, "we have given peace to all" (E3). Strong, but it frames the story on the lax side; it belongs in beat 2.

**Story beats** (the opening, the essence and beat 1 are the first minute, about 130 words; beats 2 to 6 deepen it)

1. **Two kinds of falling** (E2). Some had offered the sacrifice. Others had paid an official for a certificate saying they had. Cyprian held that his hand was clean but his conscience was stained (E2, EP 7882–7884; paraphrase). Documented.
2. **The rush to come back** (E3, E5). Confessors, Christians jailed for the faith who had not given way, were handing out written pardons. Cyprian says thousands were given every day, with no one's case examined (E3; Documented as his account), and the confessors wrote to him that they had given peace to all whose conduct since their fall satisfied him (E3; paraphrase; one clause). At Carthage a deacon, Felicissimus, and five elders offered the fallen peace without penance; Cyprian called it "another persecution" (E5; Cyprian's charge; "another persecution" may stand as a short phrase). Keep the force: the pressure came from people the church honoured.
3. **Cyprian waits** (E4). Cyprian had withdrawn from the city when the persecution began and led by letter (one clause; his own story). He would not decide alone: nothing new would be done until the bishops could meet and fix a sentence "tempered with discipline and mercy" (E4, EP 5964–5969; paraphrase, or the short phrase). Meanwhile the dying who repented were to be given peace; the clergy at Rome, who had no bishop, agreed (E4; one clause; Novatian's letter is not quoted here). Documented.
4. **Carthage, 251** (E6). After Easter 251, a large number of African bishops met at Carthage; Scripture was read on both sides (E6, S4). By then Rome had two bishops: Cornelius, chosen in 251, and Novatian, set up against him by a stricter party (E7; one clause). Then Quote 1, as trimmed, with the narrator's clause between its two parts that none would rush straight back, and penance would be long (EP 7588–7590). The terms: those with certificates were admitted, each case examined; those who had sacrificed were given peace at death; and those who began to ask for it only when sickness struck, without having repented before, were refused (E6, Ep. 55.17, 55.23; Documented). The bishops also cut off Felicissimus and the five elders (E6; Documented). The decisions were written in a little book; it is lost (E6). Keep the deathbed refusal: the terms were not soft.
5. **Rome: two bishops and a split** (E7, E8). Novatian wrote to all the churches: Quote 2 (Socrates, writing almost two centuries later and kind to Novatian's church, reports it from his letters; credit him). Socrates says Novatian broke away because Cornelius took back to communion those who had sacrificed; Cyprian says that report was a false rumour (D1; credit each). That autumn sixty bishops met at Rome (E8). By Eusebius's account of Cornelius's letters, they agreed with Carthage: Novatian and his followers were to be counted "strangers" to the church, and the fallen were to be healed "with the medicines of repentance" (E8; short phrases only; Documented).
6. **The next year** (E11). The next year, 252 by Hefele, a new persecution seemed near, and the African bishops wrote to Cornelius that they would now give peace to all who had never left the church and had kept repenting from the first day of their fall (E11; Documented). Hold back Quote 3, the letter's own words, for the ending.

**What came of them** (about a third of the story; heaviest first)

1. **The split outlived the councils.** Novatian's church kept its rule. Socrates, about 439, says its strict discipline was kept "even to the present day" in the provinces of Phrygia and Paphlagonia (I1, SOC 14059–14061; credit Socrates; "to the present day" may stand as a short phrase, with "in his day"; add no modern place name, which the sheet does not hold). At Nicaea in 325, the bishops required that its clergy, if they came over, share communion with those who had lapsed and done the penance set for them (I1, NIC 3769–3777; one clause; only this term). **The connection**: tell it here.

Defended: that the fallen may come back, but only through long penance and each case examined (E6); that the dying who repented are not to be sent away without peace (E4, E6). Against it, Novatian's church defended that the church may not give back communion to those who sacrificed, and left their pardon to God (E7). No held source says who keeps these councils' rules today: do not say "today".

**Connection to carry into the story:** what passed from these councils was a rule and a split. The rule: the fallen could come back, but only after long penance, with each case examined by the bishops, and the dying who repented were not to be sent away without peace (E6; EP 7584–7610, 7978–7984). The split: those who would give back no communion at all to anyone who had sacrificed formed their own church, which held to that rule for centuries (E7, I1; SOC 14059–14061, 14088–14090). At Nicaea in 325 the bishops required that church's clergy, if they came over, to share communion with the lapsed who had done their penance (I1; NIC 3769–3777). The listener must understand three things. First, that the quarrel was over who may come back to communion, not over what Christians believed of God; both sides left the final judgment to God (Cyprian: "we do not prejudge the Lord who will judge", E6, paraphrase; Novatian: "leave their pardon to God", Quote 2), and differed on whether the church could give back its fellowship. Second, what the strict side refused: the councils would take back to communion, after penance or at death, people who had sacrificed to the gods; Novatian's party held that the church could not give that back, and left their pardon to God alone. Third, that the terms were neither lax nor fixed: Carthage refused those who repented only on their deathbed, and a year later gave peace to all who had kept repenting because a new persecution was coming. Tell it through what the bishops decided and what Novatian's church kept, not as a "this matters because" sentence.

**Ending:** close on the councils' own words. The African bishops' letter of 252, held back from beat 6, as the last lines: Quote 3, with one plain sentence before it that a new persecution was expected (E11, EP 8663–8673, 8721–8724; Documented). The opening's image is seen again: the people who had run to the forum to sacrifice are now armed to stand. Do not end on Socrates, Nicaea or Novatian.

- Socrates: some found Novatian's rule cruel and merciless, others just (E7, SOC 14099–14101).
- Nicaea's "ancient canonical law", that no one at the point of death is to be denied communion (I2, NIC 4503–4506), and its years of penance by the kind of fall (NIC 4124–4131, 4353–4355); Hefele's "first penitential book" (E6, HEF 6374–6378). No held source says Nicaea's rules came from 251.
- Alexandria: Dionysius ordered that the dying who asked for peace should receive it, and told of the old man Serapion (I3, EUS 38856–38883).
