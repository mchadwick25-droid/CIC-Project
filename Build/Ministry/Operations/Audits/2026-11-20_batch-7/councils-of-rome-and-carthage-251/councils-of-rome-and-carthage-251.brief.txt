In 250 the emperor Decius ordered everyone to sacrifice to the gods before the officials. At Carthage, their bishop Cyprian wrote later, many Christians did not wait for arrest but ran to the forum, the main square where the officials sat. As evening came and some were put off, they begged not to be.

When the persecution ended, many of the lapsed, those who had given way, asked to come back. In 251, councils at Carthage, in what is now Tunisia, and then at Rome chose a middle way. The fallen could return after long penance, a time of public sorrow and prayer, with each case examined. A stricter party refused them all and set up Novatian as a rival bishop of Rome, whose church lasted for centuries.

Some had sacrificed, while others had bought a certificate, a paper from an official saying they had. Their hands were clean, Cyprian held, their consciences stained.

Honored voices pressed to take them back at once. Confessors, jailed or tortured for the faith without giving way, were handing out written pardons granting peace, the church's word for sharing the Lord's Supper again. Cyprian says thousands went out a day, none examined. A deacon, Felicissimus, and five elders, he says, offered peace without penance: "another persecution", he called it.

Cyprian, leading from hiding by letter, would not decide alone. The bishops must first meet and fix a sentence "tempered with discipline and mercy". Meanwhile the dying who repented would receive peace, and Rome's clergy agreed.

After Easter the African bishops met at Carthage, while Rome already had two bishops, Cornelius and Novatian. Cyprian, who called the council and defended it, is our only witness. "We weighed out a balance, with healing moderation," he wrote. "Hope of communion and peace was not to be wholly denied to the lapsed, so that despair would not make them fall away further." Yet none would rush straight back, and penance would be long. "And the cases, wishes and needs of each person would be examined."

Certificate holders were taken back case by case, and the sacrificed received peace at death, but those who first asked on their sickbed, never having repented, were refused. Felicissimus and the five elders were cut off from the church's fellowship. The council's own book is lost.

Socrates, writing two centuries later and kind to Novatian's church, reports his rule: "They should not admit those who had sacrificed to the sacred rites. They should urge them to repent, but leave their pardon to God, who is able, and has the power, to forgive sins." The sacred rites meant the Lord's Supper. Socrates says Novatian broke away because Cornelius took back those who had sacrificed, but Cyprian calls that a false rumor.

Later that year sixty bishops at Rome, by Eusebius's account of Cornelius's letters, agreed with Carthage. Novatian's followers were "strangers", outsiders, and the fallen would be healed by penance, "with the medicines of repentance".

Novatian's church kept its rule: in Socrates's day, Phrygia and Paphlagonia held it "to the present day". At Nicaea in 325, its clergy who came over had to share communion with the lapsed who did penance.

Within a year, with a new persecution expected, the African bishops gave peace to all who had kept repenting, so they could stand firm. "In this time we give peace not to those who sleep, but to those who keep watch. We give peace not for luxury, but for arms. We give peace not for rest, but for the battle line."
