## Setting

**S1. Pupil of Origen; head of the school.** EUS 36552–36559 (VI.29.4; Eusebius, later): "And in Alexandria Heraclas, having received the episcopal office after Demetrius, was succeeded in the charge of the catechetical school by Dionysius, who had also been one of Origen's pupils." Documented. Jerome agrees and adds "presbyter": JER 40842–40844 (*Illustrious Men* 69): "bishop of Alexandria, as presbyter had charge of the catechetical school under Heraclas, and was the most distinguished pupil of Origen." The year is the editors' only: McGiffert, "in the year 231 or 231" [sic; a misprint for 231 or 232] (EUS-ed 37528–37530); JER-ed, "Presbyter 232" (JER 40842).

**S2. Bishop from about 247.** EUS 37071–37080 (VI.35): "In the third year of this emperor, Heraclas died, having held his office for sixteen years, and Dionysius received the episcopate of the churches of Alexandria." The emperor is Philip; his third year ran "from the summer of 246 to the summer of 247" (EUS-ed 37073–37074). Documented. Whether he kept the school as bishop: "we do not know" (EUS-ed 37532–37535).

**S3. "The great bishop."** EUS 39132–39137 (VII, introduction): "the great bishop of Alexandria, Dionysius, shall again assist us by his own words; relating the several affairs of his time in the epistles which he has left." Greek ὁ μέγας (EUS-ed 37523–37525). Documented as Eusebius's praise.

**S4. His church's reach.** ATH 29577–29580 (§5): bishops "in Pentapolis, Upper Libya" held with Sabellius, and Dionysius acted "as he had the charge of those churches". Documented (Athanasius). Plain words: "the churches of Libya, west of Egypt, which looked to Alexandria's bishop".

**S5. Language and record.** He wrote in Greek. "The writings of Dionysius were chiefly in the form of epistles ... preserved chiefly by Eusebius" (EUS-ed 37561–37564). Documented (editor).

**S6. The persecution of Decius.** EUS-ed 37408–37412 (McGiffert): "The edicts published by Decius early in the year 250 are no longer extant; but we can gather ... that the effort was first made to induce Christians throughout the empire to deny their faith and return to the religion of the state". Widely Accepted (editor's reconstruction; the year is his).

## Events

**E1. The search, under Decius (his own words).** EUS 37639–37650 (VI.40.1–2): "I speak before God, and he knows that I do not lie. I did not flee on my own impulse nor without divine direction. But even before this, at the very hour when the Decian persecution was commanded, Sabinus sent a frumentarius to search for me, and I remained at home four days awaiting his arrival." EUS 37652–37661 (VI.40.3): the searcher "was smitten with blindness, and did not find the house, for he did not suppose, that being pursued, I would remain at home. And after the fourth day God commanded me to depart, and made a way for me in a wonderful manner; and I and my attendants and many of the brethren went away together." Their own words (he alone tells it). Documented as his account. Sabinus was apparently prefect of Egypt; the frumentarius a military agent used "as detectives or secret spies" (EUS-ed 37645–37649). The wonder ("smitten with blindness", "God commanded me") stays in his voice.

**E2. Why he wrote it: a bishop's charge.** The letter answers Germanus, "a bishop of some see, unknown to us, who had accused Dionysius of cowardice in the face of persecution" (EUS-ed 37606–37609). McGiffert: "It must be remembered that the letter is a defense against accusations actually made" (EUS-ed 37611–37614). Eusebius himself: "his own words shall show, which he wrote in answer to Germanus, a contemporary bishop who was endeavoring to slander him" (EUS 40112–40116, VII.11.1). Dionysius's retort: "Germanus indeed boasts of many confessions ... But is he able to reckon up as many as we can, of sentences, confiscations, proscriptions, plundering of goods, loss of dignities ... such as came upon me under Decius and Sabinus, and such as continue even now under Æmilianus?" (EUS 40340–40352, VII.11.18). Documented: a fellow bishop's charge and Dionysius's own defence. The charge itself survives only through Dionysius.

**E3. Arrest and the wedding guests.** EUS 37683–37701 (VI.40.4): "For about sunset, having been seized with those that were with me, I was taken by the soldiers to Taposiris, but in the providence of God, Timothy was not present and was not captured. But coming later, he found the house deserted and guarded by soldiers, and ourselves reduced to slavery." (McGiffert: "reduced to slavery" means only that they were captured, EUS-ed 37701–37705.) EUS 37710–37721 (VI.40.5–6): "One of the country people met Timothy fleeing and disturbed, and inquired the cause of his haste. And he told him the truth. And when the man heard it (he was on his way to a marriage feast, for it was customary to spend the entire night in such gatherings), he entered and announced it to those at the table. And they, as if on a preconcerted signal, arose with one impulse, and rushed out quickly and came and burst in upon us with a shout. Immediately the soldiers who were guarding us fled, and they came to us lying as we were upon the bare couches." EUS 37723–37736 (VI.40.7–8): "But I, God knows, thought at first that they were robbers who had come for spoil and plunder. So I remained upon the bed on which I was, clothed only in a linen garment, and offered them the rest of my clothing which was lying beside me. But they directed me to rise and come away quickly. Then I understood why they were come, and I cried out, beseeching and entreating them to depart and leave us alone. And I requested them, if they desired to benefit me in any way, to anticipate those who were carrying me off, and cut off my head themselves. And when I had cried out in this manner, as my companions and partners in everything know, they raised me by force. But I threw myself on my back on the ground; and they seized me by the hands and feet and dragged me away." EUS 37738–37749 (VI.40.9): "And the witnesses of all these occurrences followed: Gaius, Faustus, Peter, and Paul. But they who had seized me carried me out of the village hastily, and placing me on an ass without a saddle, bore me away." Their own words. Documented as his account.
- Greek (EUS-GR 5815–5837, VI.40.5–9; damaged scan): the marriage feast, γάμους, "διαπαννυχίζειν γὰρ αὐτοῖς ἐν ταῖς τοιαύταις συνόδοις ἔθος" (5818–5819); the request: "καὶ τότε συνεὶς ἐφ᾿ ὃ παρῆσαν, ἀνέκραγον δεόμενος αὐτῶν καὶ ἱκετεύων ἀπιέναι καὶ ἡμᾶς ἐᾶν, εἰ δὲ βούλονταί τι χρηστὸν ἐργάσασθαι, τοὺς ἀπάγοντάς με φθάσαι καὶ τὴν κεφαλὴν αὐτοὺς τὴν ἐμὴν ἀποτεμεῖν ἠξίουν" (5828–5832, normalised from the OCR's "συνεὶς iq! ©", "ἀνέχραγον", "xci", "χεφαλὴν"). Confirmed by ANF6 10873–10877: "Then I understood the purpose of their coming, and cried, entreated, and implored them to go away and leave us alone; and I begged that, if they wished to do us any good, they might anticipate those who led me captive, and strike off my head."
- The "country people": Greek χωριτῶν (EUS-GR 5816; ANF6 10850–10855 notes the variant χωρικῶν, "thus the Alexandrians named the country people"). A later letter of his says the rescuers were "certain persons from Mareotis", who "dragged us away by force, as we were unwilling to follow them" (EUS 40397–40400, VII.11.22). Whether the guests were Christians, no source says.
- "Attendants" (οἱ παῖδες): "taken by many scholars to mean 'children' ... Dittrich translates it 'pupils' ... Others translate 'servants'" (EUS-ed 37662–37675). Timothy: "either Dionysius' son (as Westcott holds) or scholar (as Dittrich believes)" (EUS-ed 37687–37699). HP calls Timothy "one of my disciples" (HP 12326). Contested (D2).

**E4. Hidden in the Libyan desert.** EUS 40402–40405 (VII.11.23, his letter to Domitius and Didymus): "But now I and Gaius and Peter are alone, deprived of the other brethren, and shut up in a desert and dry place in Libya, three days' journey from Parætonium." McGiffert: the rescuers "carried Dionysius to a desert place in Libya, where he was left with only two companions until the persecution ceased" (EUS-ed 37749–37752). Documented (his words); "until the persecution ceased" is McGiffert's.

**E5. His reading of it: providence.** EUS 37676–37678 (VI.40.3): "And that this occurred through the providence of God was made manifest by what followed, in which perhaps we were useful to some." Their own words.

**E6. To Novatian, 251 (a clause only; the quote is Novatian's).** EUS 38903–38925 (VI.45): Dionysius wrote to Novatian, who said he had been made bishop of Rome against his will, urging him to step down; the letter's words are allocated to `novatian` (README, Quotes barred). JER 40852–40857 also has it. Documented.

**E7. Under Valerian: trial and exile (about 257).** EUS 40128–40174 (VII.11.3–5): he went before "Æmilianus" with "my fellow-presbyter, Maximus, and the deacons Faustus, Eusebius, and Chæremon"; the prefect "commanded me to give this up; supposing if I turned from it, the others also would follow me"; Dionysius answered "'We must obey God rather than men.' And I testified openly that I worshiped the one only God ... Thereupon he commanded us to go to a village near the desert, called Cephro." The court record he copies: "it shall by no means be permitted you or any others, either to hold assemblies, or to enter into the so called cemeteries" (EUS 40219–40222). At Cephro: "a large church gathered with us of the brethren that followed us from the city, and those that joined us from Egypt ... At first we were persecuted and stoned; but afterwards not a few of the heathen forsook the idols and turned to God" (EUS 40251–40258). Documented (his words; the conversions his claim). Year: "exiled ... 257" (JER-ed 40842). "We must obey God rather than men" is barred as a quote (it is Polycrates's line in batch 6, and Acts 5:29); paraphrase only.

**E8. Nepos and the three-day conference at Arsinoe (undated).** EUS 41288–41295 (VII.24.1–2): "Nepos, a bishop in Egypt, who taught that the promises to the holy men in the Divine Scriptures should be understood in a more Jewish manner, and that there would be a certain millennium of bodily luxury upon this earth. As he thought that he could establish his private opinion by the Revelation of John, he wrote a book on this subject, entitled Refutation of Allegorists." Dionysius on Nepos: "in many other respects I approve and love Nepos, for his faith and industry and diligence in the Scriptures, and for his extensive psalmody ... But the truth should be loved and honored most of all" (EUS 41312–41318). The conference: "When I was in the district of Arsinoë, where, as you know, this doctrine has prevailed for a long time, so that schisms and apostasies of entire churches have resulted, I called together the presbyters and teachers of the brethren in the villages" (EUS 41343–41354); "sitting with them from morning till evening for three successive days, I endeavored to correct what was written in it" (EUS 41356–41359); "if the reason given satisfied us, we were not ashamed to change our opinions and agree with others" (EUS 41368–41370); Coracion, "the author and mover of this teaching ... acknowledged ... that he would no longer hold this opinion" (EUS 41374–41378). Their own words. Documented. Date: "we have no authority for fixing the date ... with such exactness" (EUS-ed 41271–41278). The tree's tradition entry already tells the conference in its own text (alexandria-catechetical), with no quote.

**E9. Who wrote the Revelation.** All EUS VII.25, from his second book *On Promises*. Their own words; Documented.
- Others had rejected the book: "Some before us have set aside and rejected the book altogether ... maintaining that the title is fraudulent" (EUS 41393–41396), saying "Cerinthus ... prefixed the name" (41402–41404).
- He would not: "But I could not venture to reject the book, as many brethren hold it in high esteem. But I suppose that it is beyond my comprehension, and that there is a certain concealed and more wonderful meaning in every part" (EUS 41418–41421); "I do not reject what I cannot comprehend, but rather wonder because I do not understand it" (41427–41428).
- His judgment: "Therefore that he was called John, and that this book is the work of one John, I do not deny. And I agree also that it is the work of a holy and inspired man. But I cannot readily admit that he was the apostle, the son of Zebedee, the brother of James, by whom the Gospel of John and the Catholic Epistle were written. For I judge from the character of both, and the forms of expression, and the entire execution of the book, that it is not his" (EUS 41453–41474). Greek, EUS-GR 10978–10985: "καλεῖσθαι μὲν οὖν αὐτὸν Ἰωάννην καὶ εἶναι τὴν γραφὴν Ἰωάννου ταύτην οὐκ ἀντερῶ· ἁγίου μὲν γὰρ εἶναί τινος καὶ θεοπνεύστου συναινῶ· οὐ μὴν ῥᾳδίως ἂν συνθείμην τοῦτον εἶναι τὸν ἀπόστολον, τὸν υἱὸν Ζεβεδαίου, τὸν ἀδελφὸν Ἰακώβου" (normalised from OCR "xci", "ϑεο-/πνεύστου"). Confirmed by ANF6 8119–8125.
- The tests: the Gospel writer never names himself; the Revelation's writer names himself again and again (41474–41513). "But I think that he was some other one of those in Asia; as they say that there are two monuments in Ephesus, each bearing the name of John" (41555–41557). Thought and words: the Gospel and letter share "the life," "the light," "truth," "grace," "joy" (41592–41605); "But the Apocalypse is different from these writings and foreign to them ... almost, so to speak, without even a syllable in common with them" (41607–41610). Greek: the Gospel and letter were written "not only without error as regards the Greek language, but also with elegance"; the other writer's "dialect and language are not accurate Greek, but ... he uses barbarous idioms, and, in some places, solecisms" (41624–41637). "I would not have any one think that I have said these things in a spirit of ridicule" (41639–41643).
- McGiffert's judgment of it: "a discussion of the authorship of the Apocalypse, which is unsurpassed in the early centuries as an example of keen and yet judicious and well-balanced literary criticism" (EUS-ed 37543–37546). Editor's view.

**E10. The quarrel over the Son (about 260).** Told by Athanasius (ATH), about ninety years later.
- The occasion: bishops in Pentapolis held with Sabellius, "so successful with their opinions that the Son of God was scarcely any longer preached in the churches"; Dionysius sent men to counsel them, then wrote "to expound from the Gospels the human nature of the Saviour", since they were "ascribing His human actions to the Father", to show "that it was the Son and not the Father that was made man for us" (ATH 29577–29609). Documented as Athanasius's account; the account of his motive is Athanasius's.
- The words objected to, as the Arians cite them: "that the Son of God is a creature and made, and not His own by nature, but in essence alien from the Father, just as the husbandman is from the vine, or the ship-builder from the boat, for that being a creature He was not before He came to be." Athanasius: "Yes, he wrote it, and we too admit that his letter runs thus" (ATH 29543–29548).
- The appeal to Rome: "some of the brethren belonging to the Church, of right opinions, but without asking him ... went up to Rome; and they spoke against him in the presence of his namesake Dionysius the Bishop of Rome. And he, upon hearing it, wrote simultaneously against the partisans of Sabellius and against those who held the very opinions for uttering which Arius was cast out ... And he wrote also to Dionysius to inform him of what they had said about him. And the latter straightway wrote back, and inscribed his books 'a Refutation and a Defence'" (ATH 29870–29884). Documented (Athanasius). "About 260": ATH-ed 29228. Eusebius lists the four books to Dionysius of Rome but tells nothing of the quarrel (EUS 41670–41672, VII.26.1).
- His answer, as Athanasius quotes it: "For never was there a time when God was not a father" (ATH 29940–29941); the Son as brightness of eternal light: "God is light eternal, never beginning nor ceasing. The brightness then lies before Him eternally, and is with Him without beginning and ever-begotten" (29954–29956); "The Father then being eternal, the Son is eternal, being Light of Light" (29961–29962); "I spoke of the Father, and before referring to the Son I designated Him too in the Father" (30006–30007). On the word ὁμοούσιον (of one being): "even if I argue that I have not found this word nor read it anywhere in the Holy Scriptures, yet my subsequent reasonings, which they have suppressed, do not discord with its meaning" (30054–30058). The vine and boat were "less adequate" examples given "cursorily"; he had added a plant from a seed, and a river from a well: "the well was as it were a father, while the river was water from the well" (30043–30072). His own words, through Athanasius. Documented as quoted.
- Basil also quotes the four books, for three hypostases: "If by the hypostases being three they say that they are divided, there are three, though they like it not. Else let them destroy the divine Trinity altogether" (BAS 14417–14421, *On the Holy Spirit* 72).

**E11. War, then plague (about 263).** EUS 41026–41028 (VII.22.1): "After these events a pestilential disease followed the war, and at the approach of the feast he wrote again to the brethren". Date: "shortly before Easter of the year 263" (EUS-ed 41028–41031). His words (all his own; EUS):
- "Now, indeed, everything is tears and every one is mourning, and wailings resound daily through the city because of the multitude of the dead and dying" (41041–41044).
- "every place of affliction was to us a place of festival: field, desert, ship, inn, prison" (41060–41062).
- The pestilence "did not keep aloof even from us, but the heathen it assailed more severely" (41077–41079). His claim.
- "The most of our brethren were unsparing in their exceeding love and brotherly kindness. They held fast to each other and visited the sick fearlessly ..." (41084–41091). (Already quoted on the tree, in the tradition entry's `voices`; not used.)
- "Truly the best of our brethren departed from life in this manner, including some presbyters and deacons and those of the people who had the highest reputation; so that this form of death, through the great piety and strong faith it exhibited, seemed to lack nothing of martyrdom" (41106–41110). Greek EUS-GR 10375–10380: "οἱ γοῦν ἄριστοι τῶν παρ᾽ ἡμῖν ἀδελφῶν τοῦτον τὸν τρόπον ἐξεχώρησαν τοῦ βίου, πρεσβύτεροί τέ τινες καὶ διάκονοι καὶ τῶν ἀπὸ τοῦ λαοῦ, λίαν ἐπαινούμενοι· ὡς καὶ τοῦ θανάτου τοῦτο τὸ εἶδος, διὰ πολλὴν εὐσέβειαν καὶ πίστιν ἰσχυρὰν γινόμενον, μηδὲν ἀποδεῖν μαρτυρίου δοκεῖν" (normalised from OCR "εὐ-/σέθειαν", "δοχεῖν"). Confirmed by ANF6 11516–11521 ("appeared to come in nothing beneath martyrdom itself").
- "And they took the bodies of the saints in their open hands and in their bosoms, and closed their eyes and their mouths; and they bore them away on their shoulders and laid them out" (41112–41115).
- "But with the heathen everything was quite otherwise. They deserted those who began to be sick, and fled from their dearest friends. And they cast them out into the streets when they were half dead, and left the dead like refuse, unburied" (41120–41124). His claim about his neighbours; no pagan witness is held.

**E12. Old age and death (264 or 265).** EUS 41838–41851 (VII.27.2): when Paul of Samosata's teaching was tried at Antioch, "Dionysius of Alexandria was entreated to come to the synod. But being unable to come on account of age and physical weakness, he gave his opinion on the subject under consideration by letter." EUS 41901–41913 (VII.28.3): "Meanwhile, Dionysius died in the twelfth year of the reign of Gallienus, having held the episcopate of Alexandria for seventeen years, and Maximus succeeded him." Documented. On the date, D4.

## Impact (heaviest first: what later witnesses carried on and used)

**I1. The Arians claimed his words; Athanasius defended him (the quarrel's afterlife).** Defended: the Son's eternity ("never was there a time when God was not a father", E10).
- Athanasius's title: "Letter of Athanasius concerning Dionysius, Bishop of Alexandria, shewing that he too was against the Arian heresy, like the Synod of Nicæa, and that the Arians in vain libel him in claiming him as on their side" (ATH 29428–29431). "they have now also ventured to slander the fathers ... vilifying the blessed Dionysius, Bishop of Alexandria, as a partisan and accomplice of their own" (29444–29450). "For the Bishop Dionysius did not hold with Arius, nor was he ignorant of the truth" (29516–29517). His proof: "neither was he condemned and expelled from the church for impiety by other bishops ... nor did he of his own accord leave the church as the partisan of a heresy, but died honourably within it, and his memory is retained and registered along with the fathers to the present day" (29521–29526). His defence of the words: written "by the occasion and the person concerned" (29556–29559), about Christ's human nature (29603–29609). Documented as Athanasius's case; he writes as a partisan against the Arians, whom he calls "madmen" and "new Jews" (29442, 29518–29520).
- Arius's own formula, for contrast: "The Son was not before He was begotten, but there was once a time when He was not" (ATH 29988–29989, Athanasius quoting Arius).
- Rufinus, a second witness to the Arian use, about 397: "Dionysius quoque Alexandrinus episcopus, eruditissimus assertor ecclesiasticae fidei ... in his tamen libris suis quos adversum Sabellii haeresim scripsit, talia inveniuntur inserta, ut frequenter Ariani auctoritate ipsius se defendere conentur. Propter quod et sanctus episcopus Athanasius compulsus est Apologeticum pro libris [eius scribere]" (RUF 48560–48569, damaged scan; clean English at `npnf203` 45362–45375; "Dionysius too, bishop of Alexandria, a most learned defender of the church's faith ... yet in the books he wrote against the heresy of Sabellius such things are found inserted that the Arians often try to defend themselves by his authority. For this reason the holy bishop Athanasius was driven to write a defence of his books"). Rufinus holds the words were inserted by others. D3.
- Jerome, answering Rufinus about 402: Rufinus says that Dionysius "in the four books in which he controverted the doctrines of Sabellius, lapses into the dogma of Arius" (JER 53615–53618, Jerome reporting); Jerome lists Rufinus's examples: "this is an error of which Dionysius is found guilty; these are the words in which the bishop Athanasius defends the error of Dionysius" (JER 53664–53666). Jerome's general line: "before Arius arose in Alexandria as a demon of the south, things were said incautiously which cannot be defended against a malevolent criticism" (JER 53655–53657). Documented as their views.
- Basil, about a century later (Letter 9, to Maximus the philosopher): "I do not admire everything that is written; indeed of some things I totally disapprove. For it may be, that of the impiety of which we are now hearing so much, I mean the Anomœan, it is he, as far as I know, who first gave men the seeds. I do not trace his so doing to any mental depravity, but only to his earnest desire to resist Sabellius. I often compare him to a woodman trying to straighten some ill-grown sapling, pulling so immoderately in the opposite direction as to exceed the mean, and so dragging the plant awry on the other side" (BAS 23712–23721). Basil adds that he was "at one time to be found disloyal to the homoousion ... and at another admitting it in his Apology to his namesake" (23774–23780), and "uttered very unbecoming words about the Spirit" (23780–23783). Elsewhere Basil calls him "the great Dionysius" (BAS 34882) and quotes him for the faith (14401–14423). Their own words. Documented.
- Robertson (editor, 1892): Dionysius "admits the use of these expressions, withdraws the first ... and admits the propriety of the ὁμοούσιον, although he himself prefers Scriptural language" (ATH-ed 29258–29261); "The defence of Athanasius, that Dionysius referred to the Human Nature of Christ, is scarcely tenable" (29268–29269); "Athanasius, not the Arians, had the right to claim him as his own ... At the same time we cannot but see that the Arians had good prima facie ground for their appeal" (29286–29292); he "stated his belief in a manner which put an end to the controversy" (29244–29245). Robertson also reports that "Gennadius ... calls him 'Fons Arii'" (29346–29347); Gennadius's text was not checked. Editor's view.

**I2. His letters became the record of his years.** Eusebius built much of Books VI and VII on them (S3; EUS 39132–39137): "there is nothing like hearing his own words" (EUS 41155–41156). Jerome lists his letters and books (JER 40842–40880). The tree's own tradition entry quotes his plague letter. Documented.

**I3. The Revelation question passed to Eusebius.** Eusebius set the Revelation among the accepted books "if it really seem proper" and again among the rejected ones, "which some ... reject, but which others class with the accepted books" (EUS 18011–18016, 18154–18156, III.25). On Papias's two Johns, Eusebius writes that "there were two tombs in Ephesus, each of which, even to the present day, is called John's", and that "it was the second, if one is not willing to admit that it was the first that saw the Revelation" (EUS 20530–20533, 20548–20550, III.39.6). That Eusebius took this from Dionysius is McGiffert's: "A similar suggestion had been already made by Dionysius ... and Eusebius was undoubtedly thinking of it when he wrote these words" (EUS-ed 20550–20553). Editor's inference for the link; Eusebius's words Documented. Athanasius's Easter letter of 367 still lists "the Revelation of John" among the New Testament books (ATH 68806), as the live `athanasius-of-alexandria` story already tells.

**I4. Church law.** The Council in Trullo (692), canon 2, sets its seal on "the Canons [i.e. the decretal letters] of Dionysius, formerly Archbishop of the great city of Alexandria" (C7 26742–26744); his canonical letter to Basilides is in that collection (C7 1063; corpus-map row). Documented. The live Athanasius story already says that council made Athanasius's rulings church law; here a clause at most.

**I5. His own church's memory (late).** HP heads his life "Dionysius the Wise, the fourteenth patriarch. A.D. 247–264" (HP 12165), retells the rescue (HP 12319–12332), and gives him a conversion story: a pagan philosopher who bought some of Paul's letters from an old widow, then was baptized by Demetrius (HP 11706–11720, 11869–11892). Late legend, ten centuries on; not used.

## Where sources disagree or come late

- **D1. The flight: cowardice or providence.** Germanus, a bishop, accused him; the accusation survives only in Dionysius's answer (E2). Dionysius says God sent him (E1). McGiffert reads the letter as a defence (EUS-ed 37611–37614). Tell both; the narrator takes no side.
- **D2. Who were his "attendants"?** Children, pupils or servants (EUS-ed 37662–37675); Timothy son or pupil (37687–37699); HP says "disciples" (HP 12326, late). Contested. Do not say he was married.
- **D3. His words on the Son.** Athanasius: he wrote them, for a purpose, in a sense the Arians twist, and he was never against Nicaea's faith (I1). Rufinus: such things were inserted into his books by others (RUF 48565–48568; clean English at `npnf203` 45362–45375); Migne's editor answers that Athanasius says nothing of insertion and admits Dionysius wrote them (RUF 48623–48630). Jerome: an "error", which Athanasius "defends" (JER 53664–53666). Basil: he first sowed the seeds of the Anomoeans (those who later said the Son was unlike the Father), from zeal against Sabellius, though he admitted the homoousion to his namesake (I1). Robertson: Athanasius had the right to claim him; the Arians had good ground at first sight; Athanasius's "human nature" defence scarcely holds. Contested; credit each.
- **D4. Dates.** Head of school: 231 or 232 (McGiffert), "Presbyter 232" (JER-ed). Bishop: third year of Philip, 246–247 (Eusebius, EUS 37071–37080); HP "247–264"; but Robertson's introduction says "Bishop of Alexandria 233–265" (ATH-ed 29188–29189), which conflicts with Eusebius and is not used. Death: Eusebius, twelfth year of Gallienus (264–265); McGiffert: "In both versions of the Chron. the death of Dionysius is put in the eleventh year of Gallienus, i.e. August, 263, to August, 264" (EUS-ed 41902–41906). Chronicle (Jerome's Latin, held): seventeen years from Philip's fifth year (CHRON 43881–43883, manuscripts vary) and Maximus ordained in the eleventh year (CHRON 44268–44269). History: Philip's third year and Gallienus's twelfth. The card credits both. Say "about 264 or 265". Jerome says the letter against Paul was written "a few days before his death" (JER 40878–40879); McGiffert calls that "only an inference" (EUS-ed 41851–41853).
- **D5. Basil's letter number.** Robertson cites "Basil (Ep. 41)" (ATH-ed 29345); the held text is Jackson's Letter IX, to Maximus (BAS 23682–23686). A numbering difference; cite Letter 9 as held.
- **D6. The Revelation's later standing.** Eusebius kept it doubtful (I3); Athanasius's list of 367 counts it (ATH 68806). The tree need not settle authorship. McGiffert's own "the author of the fourth Gospel, whom he assumes to be John the apostle" (EUS-ed 20557–20559) is an editor's aside; do not carry it.
