Soldiers had seized Dionysius, the bishop of Alexandria, and were holding him in a small town near the city. A countryman on his way to a wedding feast told the guests, who ran shouting to the house, and the soldiers fled. "Then I understood why they had come. I cried out, begging and pleading with them to go away and leave us alone. If they wanted to do a kindness, I asked them to act before the men leading me away, and cut off my head themselves."

A pupil of Origen, Dionysius ran the church's school at Alexandria, where people were taught the faith. From about 247 he led its church as bishop, through two persecutions and a plague. His letters, which Eusebius of Caesarea copied into his history, are our eyewitness record of those years.

About 250, the emperor Decius set out to make Christians give up their faith and return to the old gods. Dionysius stayed at home four days while an officer hunted him, and the officer, he says, was smitten with blindness and never searched the house. Then God told him to go, he says, and he left, but was caught at sunset, and after the guests carried him off, he hid in the Libyan desert. He told all this to answer a fellow bishop who accused him of running away, so the story rests on his own word.

In one book, Dionysius turned to the Revelation, the last book of the Bible. He would not reject it, but he set its rough Greek beside the polished Greek of the Gospel and letter of John. "That he was called John, and that this book is the writing of a John, I will not dispute. For I agree it is the work of someone holy and inspired by God. But I would not easily grant that this was the apostle, the son of Zebedee, the brother of James." That is John, one of the twelve, and he guessed it was another John, from around Ephesus.

In the churches of Libya, some bishops taught that the Father and the Son were one and the same. To show the Son was not the Father, Dionysius used blunt images. As his opponents later cited them, the Son was as different from the Father as a vine from its farmer, or a boat from its builder. Some of his own church, Athanasius of Alexandria says, went over his head to the bishop of Rome, who wrote against both errors. Dionysius answered in four books that there was never a time when God was not Father, and that the Son comes from him like light from an eternal light.

When plague came, about 263, many Christians, he wrote, nursed the sick without fear and caught it themselves, and he counted their deaths as dying for the faith. "So even this kind of death, which came from great devotion and strong faith, seemed to fall nothing short of martyrdom." The pagans, he wrote, pushed their sick into the streets half dead. He died, old and still bishop, about 264 or 265.

Nearly a century later came the followers of Arius, who taught that there was once a time when the Son did not exist. They quoted the vine and the boat against Nicaea's creed. Dionysius had written two generations before Arius, against the opposite error, yet his words were now read as support for theirs. Athanasius, bishop of the same city, wrote a book to answer them. Dionysius did write those words, he granted, but his four books showed his real faith, and to claim him was a slander. Basil of Caesarea compared him to a woodman who pulls a crooked sapling so hard the other way that he bends it wrong on the other side. Whether the Arians had any ground, later readers still disagree.

For Athanasius, the proof was how Dionysius ended: no bishops ever condemned him or drove him out, and he never left the church. He died with honor inside it, and his memory was kept among the fathers.
