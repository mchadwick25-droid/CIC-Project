# Outline: Dionysius of Alexandria

Sheet items (S, E, I, L, D) are in `Source_Sheet.md`, which gives the file and line for each. EUS is Eusebius's *Church History* in McGiffert's English; EUS-GR Schwartz's Greek (a damaged scan); ANF6 Salmond's English of Dionysius; ATH Athanasius's *On the Opinion of Dionysius*; BAS Basil's letters; JER Jerome; RUF Rufinus's Latin. "-ed" marks an editor's note.

- **The essence:** Dionysius, a pupil of Origen, ran the church's school at Alexandria and then led its church as bishop from about 247 until his death about 264 or 265, through two persecutions and a plague (S1, S2, E1–E12; Documented). His letters, which Eusebius of Caesarea copied into his history, are our eyewitness record of those years, told in his own words (S3, I2; Documented).
- **Also told:**
  - **Carried off by wedding guests.** When the emperor Decius moved against the Christians, about 250, soldiers caught him; guests from a country wedding feast drove them off and dragged him away, though he begged them to cut off his head instead (E1, E3, E4; Documented as his own account). He wrote the story to answer a fellow bishop who said he had run away (E2; Documented as charge and defence).
  - **The plague letter.** In a later plague, about 263, he wrote to his people that many Christians nursed the sick and buried the dead, and died of it, a death he counted as good as martyrdom; the pagans, he says, threw out their own sick (E11; Documented as his words and his claim).
  - **The Revelation.** By comparing its thought, words and Greek with the Gospel and letter of John, he judged that the Revelation was written by a holy man named John, but not the apostle; and still he would not reject the book (E9; Documented, his own words).
  - **The quarrel over the Son.** Writing against teachers who made the Father and the Son one and the same, he used images that set the Son apart from the Father; some of his own people complained to Rome, and he answered in four books that the Son had always been with the Father (E10; Documented through Athanasius). Nearly a century later the followers of Arius claimed those first words for their side, and Athanasius of Alexandria wrote a book to take him back (I1; Documented as Athanasius reports it, and Rufinus confirms the Arian use). Whether the Arians had any ground, his later readers disagree (D3; Contested).
- **Length:** about 4 minutes (about 600 words). Counted first: the three quotes carry about 120 words; the plain sense of the words to explain about 65; the witnesses credited (Eusebius, the accusing bishop, Athanasius, Basil, Rufinus) about 45. That leaves about 370 words for the story. Why it runs to four minutes: the job holds four turning points, each a scene in his own words (the rescue, the Revelation, the quarrel over the Son, the plague), and the quarrel cannot be followed without its two sides and its afterlife. Cut first, if needed: the Valerian exile (beat 4, keep one clause); the Arsinoe conference (beat 3, keep one clause); Basil and Rufinus in What came 1 (keep Athanasius); What came 3 and 4.
- **Setting:**
  - Time: the middle of the third century, from about 231 (head of the school, an editor's date) to about 264 or 265 (death) (S1, S2, D4). Persecution under the emperor Decius from early 250 (S6, editor's date); under the emperor Valerian about 257 (E7, editor's date); plague about 263 (E11, editor's date). Plain words: "about 250", "a few years later", "about 263".
  - Place: Alexandria, in Egypt, with the churches of Libya to the west that looked to its bishop (S4). Taposiris, "a small town ... in the neighborhood of Alexandria" (EUS-ed 37685–37686); give by role, "a small town near the city". The Libyan desert (E4). Arsinoe, a district up the Nile (E8); by role, "a district of the Egyptian countryside".
  - Tradition: Alexandrian Christianity (census `informalName` of `alexandria-catechetical`).
  - Language: he wrote in Greek; his letters survive mostly because Eusebius copied them (S5).

**Words to explain** (the first time each is heard; plain word or short gloss, worked into the sentence)

- **catechetical school**: "the church's school at Alexandria, where people were taught the faith" (S1). Do not use the bare word "catechetical".
- **presbyter** (in Quote 2): "presbyters, the elders who served under the bishop". Say it once alongside the quote.
- **martyrdom / martyr**: "dying for the faith", the first time (Quote 2).
- **the persecution of Decius**: "when the emperor Decius set out to make Christians give up their faith and return to the old gods" (S6, McGiffert's account of the edicts).
- **Revelation / Apocalypse**: "the Revelation, the last book of the Bible". Never "Apocalypse" bare.
- **the apostle John, the son of Zebedee** (Quote 3): "John, one of the twelve apostles, the son of Zebedee", said alongside.
- **the thousand years** (Nepos's teaching): "a thousand years of Christ's reign on this earth, full of bodily pleasures" (E8: "a certain millennium of bodily luxury upon this earth"). Never "chiliasm" or "millennialism".
- **Sabellius / Sabellians**: "teachers who said the Father and the Son were one and the same, so that the Father himself had become man" (ATH 29604–29608: the Son "and not the Father ... was made man"; "ascribing His human actions to the Father"). The name Sabellius need not be said.
- **of one being (homoousios)**: "the word the council of Nicaea later used: of one being with the Father". Dionysius said he had not found the word in Scripture, but his meaning agreed with it (E10).
- **the Arians**: "the followers of Arius, who taught that there was once a time when the Son did not exist" (ATH 29988–29989).
- **Anomoeans** (Basil), if used: "those who later said the Son was unlike the Father". Better left out; say "the worst of the later errors" only if Basil is cut to one clause. Prefer Basil's own image (the woodman) told in paraphrase.
- **exile / Cephro** (beat 4): "sent to a village at the edge of the desert, in Libya". Do not name Cephro.

- **Shape:** Founder or leader. What he led: the church of Alexandria and the churches of Libya under it, for seventeen years. How it ran: by letters, by a bishop's visits and councils (the three days at Arsinoe), and by a bishop who kept the church together from hiding and from exile. Who it served: Egypt's Christians through persecution and plague. The impact is in what outlasted him: his letters, which became the record of his age, his reading of the Revelation, and his words on the Son, which both sides of a later fight claimed. Trap avoided: titles in place of acts; here the acts are his own, in his own words.
- **Sibling:**
  - `origen` (live text, read; job: the scholar's life, from his father's martyrdom to his death). It does not name Dionysius. Here Origen is one clause: Dionysius was his pupil (S1). Origen's own suffering under Decius, his Hexapla and Athanasius's "the hardworking Origen" stay with Origen. Optional, one clause: Dionysius wrote to him "on martyrdom" (L1), if it does not crowd the beat.
  - `novatian` (batch 7, outline not yet done; live text and README read). His letter to Novatian goes to Novatian, quote included. Here one clause in beat 2, no quote: in 251 he wrote to Novatian, newly set up as a rival bishop of Rome, urging him to step down for the church's unity (E6).
  - `athanasius-of-alexandria` (live text, read; job: the bishop exiled five times who held to Nicaea). It already tells the creed of Nicaea, "of one being", the party of Arius, and his Easter list of 367. Here Athanasius appears only as the writer who defended Dionysius (What came 1); none of his live quotes is used.
  - `alexandria-catechetical` (tradition entry, read): its text tells the Arsinoe conference and its `voices` quote the plague letter's "held fast to each other..."; that line is not used here.
- **Openings:**
  1. **Chosen.** The rescue, in his own words (Quote 1; E3). In about three sentences: soldiers had seized the bishop of Alexandria and held him in a small town near the city; a countryman on his way to an all-night wedding feast heard of it and told the guests, who rose as one, ran shouting to the house, and the soldiers fled. Then Quote 1. Then the turn, with the name: the man who begged for his own beheading was Dionysius, bishop of Alexandria, and the story is his own. Then the essence.
  2. Not chosen: the plague letter's "everything is tears and every one is mourning" (E11). Strong, but it belongs in time order at beat 6, near the end.

**Story beats** (the opening, the essence and beat 1 are the first minute, about 150 words)

1. **Pupil, teacher, bishop.** He was one of Origen's pupils, and when Heraclas, Origen's successor, became bishop, Dionysius took charge of the church's school at Alexandria (S1; Documented, Eusebius; the year 231 or 232 is the editors'). About 247 he became bishop himself (S2; Documented). Heraclas by role ("the head of the school before him"), not by name.
2. **Under Decius.** When the persecution was ordered, early in 250, an officer was sent to hunt for him; Dionysius stayed at home four days, and the officer, he says, "was smitten with blindness" and never thought to search the house; then, he says, God told him to go, and he left with his household and many others (E1; Documented as his account; the wonder stays in his voice). They were caught at sunset and held in the small town; from there the opening picks up: the wedding guests carried him off on a donkey without a saddle (E3; do not retell the rescue scene). He hid in a dry place in the Libyan desert, with two companions, until the persecution ended (E4; "until the persecution ceased" is the editor's). He wrote all this years later to answer a fellow bishop who accused him of running away (E2): say it plainly, as a charge and his defence; the narrator takes neither side (D1). He saw God's hand in it: "perhaps we were useful to some" may stand as a short phrase in the narrator's sentence (E5). One clause, no quote: in 251 he wrote to Novatian, newly set up as a rival bishop of Rome, urging him to step down rather than divide the church (E6).
3. **The countryside, and the Revelation (undated).** A bishop out in the Egyptian countryside had taught, from the Revelation, that Christ would reign on earth for a thousand years of bodily pleasure, and had written a book against those who read Scripture for a deeper meaning (E8). That bishop had died; Dionysius praised his faith and his hymns, but "the truth should be loved and honored most of all" may stand as a short phrase (E8). He sat with the village presbyters and teachers from morning to evening for three days, and the teaching's leader gave it up (E8; Documented, his own account). Then, in a book *On Promises*, he turned to the Revelation itself (E9). Others before him, he says, had rejected it altogether (E9). He would not reject it; but from its thought, its words and their order, and its rough Greek beside the polished Greek of the Gospel and letter of John, he judged it was not the apostle's. Then Quote 3. He guessed it was another John of Asia, since, he says, two tombs at Ephesus each bore the name John (E9; Documented as his reasoning; "they say"). Give no year (EUS-ed 41271–41278).
4. **Under Valerian (optional; cut first).** A few years later, about 257, under the emperor Valerian, he stood before the governor of Egypt, refused to give up worshipping the one God, and was sent away to a village at the edge of the desert, in Libya; there, he says, he and those who followed him were stoned at first, and later some pagans turned to God (E7; Documented as his words; the conversions are his claim). His answer, "We must obey God rather than men", is not quoted (barred); paraphrase or omit.
5. **The quarrel over the Son (about 260).** In the churches of Libya under his care, some bishops taught that the Father and the Son were one and the same (S4, E10). He wrote against them, and to show the Son was not the Father he used blunt images: the Son was as different from the Father as a vine from the farmer who tends it, or a boat from its builder, and the Son was not before he came to be (E10; these are the words as his opponents cite them, and Athanasius says, "Yes, he wrote it"). Some of his own church, without asking him, went to Rome and complained to its bishop, who wrote against both errors and wrote to tell Dionysius what had been said against him (E10; Documented, Athanasius). He answered in four books, *A Refutation and a Defence*: there was never a time when God was not Father; the Son shines from the Father like light from an eternal light; and the Son comes from the Father like a river from a spring (E10; his words, through Athanasius; paraphrase only). The word "of one being", he wrote, he had not found in Scripture, but his meaning did not disagree with it (E10). The date "about 260" is the editor's (ATH-ed 29228). Robertson's view that this "put an end to the controversy" is an editor's; if told, credit it.
6. **War and plague (about 263).** A plague followed a war; as the feast drew near (Easter, by McGiffert's note) he wrote to his people that "wailings resound daily through the city" may stand as a short phrase, or tell it plainly (E11). Many Christians, he wrote, nursed the sick without fear and caught the sickness themselves; presbyters, deacons and well-respected people died this way. Then Quote 2. They closed the eyes of the dead, carried them on their shoulders and washed them for burial (E11). The pagans, he says, pushed their sick into the streets half dead and left the dead unburied (E11). Credit both to him: he is a bishop praising his own people and judging his neighbours; no pagan account is held.
7. **Old age and death.** When bishops met at Antioch to judge its bishop's teaching, Dionysius was asked to come, but was too old and weak; he sent his judgment by letter (E12; Documented). He died in the twelfth year of the emperor Gallienus, about 264 or 265, after seventeen years as bishop (E12, D4; Documented; the exact year is not settled).

**What came of him** (about a third of the story; heaviest first)

1. **The Arians claimed him; Athanasius took him back.** (Defended: that the Son had always been with the Father.) Nearly a century later, when the followers of Arius fought the creed of Nicaea, they cited his letter against the Libyan teachers: the vine and the farmer, the boat and the builder, the Son who was not before he came to be (I1; Documented, Athanasius). Athanasius of Alexandria, bishop of the same city, wrote a whole book to answer them. He granted that Dionysius wrote those words, but said he wrote them for one purpose, about Christ as a man, and that his four books to Rome showed his real faith; the Arians, he said, slandered him by claiming him (I1; Documented as Athanasius's case; he writes as their opponent). The listener must hear the link plainly: Dionysius wrote two generations before Arius, against the opposite error, and words aimed at one mistake were later read as support for another. Two later readers, credited one clause each (optional, cut first): Rufinus, near 400, said the Arians often defended themselves by his authority, but held that the troubling lines had been slipped into his books by others (I1, D3); Basil of Caesarea, about a century after him, compared him to a woodman who pulls a crooked sapling so hard the other way that he bends it wrong on the other side (I1; paraphrase only). Do not settle who was right (D3).
2. **His letters became the record.** Eusebius built much of the sixth and seventh books of his history on them, saying "there is nothing like hearing his own words" (I2; may stand as a short phrase). Without him we would not have the rescue, the plague, or the Revelation argument. Optional.
3. **The Revelation stayed in question.** Eusebius set the book among the accepted books "if it really seem proper", and listed it again among those some rejected; on the two tombs at Ephesus he suggested that the second John saw the Revelation, an idea his editor says came from Dionysius (I3; Eusebius's words Documented, the link the editor's). One clause, optional: a century later Athanasius's Easter list still counted the Revelation among the twenty-seven books (I3, already told on the tree).
4. **His rulings became church law.** In 692 a council in Constantinople set its seal on his letters of church rules (I4; Documented). One clause, optional.

**Quotes** (3)

Only these are quoted aloud. All three are rendered from Eusebius's Greek (EUS-GR, Schwartz), which is held only as a damaged OCR scan; each reading is legible word by word and is confirmed by two held English prints, McGiffert (EUS) and Salmond (ANF6). No clean Greek print is held, so the card prints the English alone.

1. EUS-GR 5828–5832 (*Church History* VI.40.8; English EUS 37729–37734, ANF6 10873–10877). Greek: "καὶ τότε συνεὶς ἐφ᾿ ὃ παρῆσαν, ἀνέκραγον δεόμενος αὐτῶν καὶ ἱκετεύων ἀπιέναι καὶ ἡμᾶς ἐᾶν, εἰ δὲ βούλονταί τι χρηστὸν ἐργάσασθαι, τοὺς ἀπάγοντάς με φθάσαι καὶ τὴν κεφαλὴν αὐτοὺς τὴν ἐμὴν ἀποτεμεῖν ἠξίουν." Rendering: "Then I understood why they had come. I cried out, begging and pleading with them to go away and leave us alone. If they wanted to do a kindness, I asked them to act before the men leading me away, and cut off my head themselves." Job: the opening. It shows, in his own voice, a bishop who expected to die and asked for it, and it carries the scene's surprise; the narrator alone cannot.
2. EUS-GR 10378–10380 (*Church History* VII.22.8; English EUS 41106–41110, ANF6 11516–11521). Greek: "ὡς καὶ τοῦ θανάτου τοῦτο τὸ εἶδος, διὰ πολλὴν εὐσέβειαν καὶ πίστιν ἰσχυρὰν γινόμενον, μηδὲν ἀποδεῖν μαρτυρίου δοκεῖν." Rendering: "So even this kind of death, which came from great devotion and strong faith, seemed to fall nothing short of martyrdom." Job: beat 6. It gives his own judgment, that dying while nursing the sick was dying for the faith. The narrator first says who died this way (presbyters, deacons and well-respected people) in paraphrase.
3. EUS-GR 10978–10981 (*Church History* VII.25.7; English EUS 41453–41458, ANF6 8119–8125). Greek: "καλεῖσθαι μὲν οὖν αὐτὸν Ἰωάννην καὶ εἶναι τὴν γραφὴν Ἰωάννου ταύτην οὐκ ἀντερῶ· ἁγίου μὲν γὰρ εἶναί τινος καὶ θεοπνεύστου συναινῶ· οὐ μὴν ῥᾳδίως ἂν συνθείμην τοῦτον εἶναι τὸν ἀπόστολον, τὸν υἱὸν Ζεβεδαίου, τὸν ἀδελφὸν Ἰακώβου." Rendering: "That he was called John, and that this book is the writing of a John, I will not dispute. For I agree it is the work of someone holy and inspired by God. But I would not easily grant that this was the apostle, the son of Zebedee, the brother of James." Job: beat 3. It carries the whole judgment, the concession and the doubt together, in his measured voice. The narrator gives "the son of Zebedee" its plain sense: John, one of the twelve.

**Connection to carry into the story:** what passed from Dionysius to Athanasius of Alexandria was his own books on the Son, and the fight over what they meant. Supporting passages: ATH 29428–29431 (the purpose of Athanasius's book), 29543–29548 (the words the Arians cite, and "Yes, he wrote it"), 29870–29884 (the appeal to Rome and the four books), 29940–29962 (the four books' "never was there a time when God was not a father", and light from light), 29521–29526 (he "died honourably within" the church). What the listener needs to understand: Dionysius wrote about 260, two generations before the fight over Arius, against teachers who made the Father and the Son the same; to separate them he used images (a vine and its farmer, a boat and its builder) that sounded, later, like Arius's claim that the Son had a beginning. When the followers of Arius quoted him, Athanasius answered with Dionysius's own later books, which said the Son had always been with the Father, as light always shines from a light. So a bishop of Alexandria's words became evidence on both sides of the fourth century's great quarrel, and his successor wrote to keep him on Nicaea's side. Carry this through what the people did (he wrote; his people complained to Rome; he answered; the Arians quoted; Athanasius replied), never through a "this matters because" sentence.

**Ending:** close on the person.
1. **Chosen.** Athanasius's answer about his death, after What came 1: Dionysius was never condemned or driven out by other bishops, and never left the church; he died with honour inside it, and his memory was kept among the fathers (ATH 29521–29526; paraphrase, credited to Athanasius). It ends on Dionysius, his death and his memory, through the witness who defended him.
2. Alternative: if What came is placed before beat 7, end on his last act and death: too old and weak to travel to Antioch, he sent his judgment by letter, and died about 264 or 265 after seventeen years as bishop (E12).

**Who tells it**

- **Dionysius himself**, in letters Eusebius copied, about forty to sixty years after his death: the flight and rescue, the hiding, the exile, the Arsinoe conference, the Revelation argument, the plague (E1–E9, E11). The rescue rests on his word alone, written to defend himself against a bishop's charge: say so once, inside the sentence about why he wrote it (beat 2). The plague letter is a bishop praising his own church: credit the pagans' desertion to him.
- **Eusebius of Caesarea**, a generation or two later: the frame, his dates as bishop, his death (S1–S3, E12). Credit Eusebius once, where the essence says the letters survive through him.
- **Athanasius of Alexandria**, about ninety years later, a partisan in the fight over Arius: the quarrel over the Son, the Arians' claim, the four books (E10, I1). Credit him where the quarrel is told; his view of the Arians as slanderers is his.
- **Rufinus and Jerome**, around 400: the Arian use (Rufinus), and the "error" (Jerome). **Basil of Caesarea**, a century on: the woodman. Each credited by name or role if used.
- **The editors**: McGiffert (Eusebius) and Robertson (Athanasius) give the dates and judgments marked "editor's" on the sheet. If an editor's date or verdict is told, credit "his editor" or "a modern editor".
- The doubt that is itself the story: what Dionysius meant about the Son. The scene that tells it is the appeal to Rome and his four-book answer (beat 5), then the Arians and Athanasius (What came 1).

**Do not say**

- "He fled out of fear" or "he bravely refused to flee" → his own account: he says God sent him and he did not flee on his own impulse; a fellow bishop accused him of running away. Tell both as claims (D1).
- "Christians rescued him" → "guests at a country wedding feast"; he later calls them "people from Mareotis". The record does not say they were Christians.
- "his children", "his wife", "his servants" → "his household" or "those with him". The Greek word may mean children, pupils or servants (D2).
- "The officer was struck blind" as fact → "the officer, he says, 'was smitten with blindness'", meaning he never thought to search the house (McGiffert, EUS-ed 37655–37659). Keep it in his voice.
- "he was tortured" or "imprisoned for years" → seized and held one night under Decius; exiled under Valerian.
- "He rejected the Revelation" or "he said it was not Scripture" → he would not reject it; he judged it the work of a holy and inspired John, not the apostle.
- "He was the first to doubt it" → others before him, he says, had rejected it altogether.
- "He proved who wrote it" or "it was John the Elder" → he guessed another John of Asia; "they say" there were two tombs at Ephesus.
- "He was an Arian" or "he taught what Arius taught" → Arius came later; the Arians quoted his early words; Athanasius denied they could claim him.
- "Rome condemned him" or "the pope corrected him" → the bishop of Rome wrote against both errors and told him what had been said against him; he answered in four books. Never "pope".
- "He used the word 'of one being'" → he said he had not found the word in Scripture, but his meaning did not disagree with it.
- "the vine and the vinedresser" given as his settled teaching → these are the words as his opponents cite them; he later called them "less adequate" examples.
- "Christians did not catch the plague" → it reached them too, he says, though it struck the pagans harder.
- "The pagans abandoned their sick" as fact → "he wrote that the pagans ...".
- "He died a martyr" → he died old, as bishop, about 264 or 265.
- "Bishop from 233" (Robertson's slip) → from about 247 (Eusebius).
- "Dionysius the Great" as his name → Eusebius calls him "the great bishop of Alexandria"; the card has the title.
- "Athanasius proved him orthodox" → Athanasius argued it; later readers disagreed (D3).

**Leave out**

- The letter to Novatian's words (allocated to `novatian`); here a clause only.
- "We must obey God rather than men" (barred: batch 6's Polycrates line, and Acts 5:29).
- The plague letter's "held fast to each other and visited the sick fearlessly ..." (already on the tree in the tradition entry's `voices`).
- His accounts of the Alexandrian martyrs under Decius and of the dying Serapion (EUS VI.41–44): side stories, and the lapsed are the councils of 251's job.
- The baptism dispute with Stephen of Rome (Cyprian's job).
- His festal letters and Easter reckoning, the work *On Nature*, the canonical letter to Basilides (beyond the one-clause church-law item).
- Paul of Samosata's case, beyond the one clause in beat 7.
- The *History of the Patriarchs*' conversion story (a pagan philosopher who bought Paul's letters from a widow): late legend, ten centuries on (I5).
- Athanasius's comparison of the Arians to "new Jews" with the devil as their father (ATH 29518–29520): his polemic against Jews is not this story's conflict. His hostility to the Arians is told (he calls their claim a slander).
- Gennadius's "Fons Arii" (known only through Robertson's note; not checked).
- Basil's charge about the Holy Spirit (BAS 23780–23783): a second accusation the story has no room to explain.
