## Setting

S1. **Name, birth and teacher.** "Our author was commonly known among the ancients as Eusebius of Cæsarea or Eusebius Pamphili. The former designation arose from the fact that he was bishop of the church in Cæsarea for many years; the latter from the fact that he was the intimate friend and devoted admirer of Pamphilus, a presbyter of Cæsarea and a martyr" (EUS-ed 1086–1091). Birth: "we shall consequently be safe in concluding that Eusebius was born not far from the year 260 a.d. His birthplace cannot be determined with certainty" (EUS-ed 1120–1123). Jerome: "His surname Pamphilus arose from his friendship for Pamphilus the martyr" (JER 41076–41078). Eusebius: "In his time we became acquainted with Pamphilus" (EUS 43009–43010). Witness: editor (birth, and that he took the name after Pamphilus's death, EUS-ed 43010–43012); Jerome, later (the name). Confidence: name, Documented; birth about 260, Dominant Modern Reconstruction (McGiffert's inference); birthplace, not known.

S2. **Caesarea and the tradition.** CEN `palestinian-church-pre-constantinian`, name "The Church of Roman Palestine before Constantine", region "Caesarea Maritima, Jerusalem, Emmaus", dates "c. 185-312"; CEN `eusebius-of-caesarea`, `placeLabel` "Caesarea Maritima", modern country Israel. Plain words: "Caesarea, a port on the coast of Roman Palestine, in today's Israel". The tradition is named in its census entry; the spoken name is "the Church of Roman Palestine before Constantine".

S2a. **Tyre.** "the capital city of Phœnicia" (VC IV.41; EUS 67227). Plain words: "Tyre, the chief city of Phoenicia".

S3. **Bishop after the persecution.** "Not long after the close of the persecution, Eusebius became bishop of Cæsarea in Palestine, his own home, and held the position until his death. The exact date of his accession cannot be ascertained … it must be admitted that Eusebius may have become bishop at any time between about 311 and 318" (EUS-ed 1600–1604, 1657–1658). Witness: editor. Confidence: Documented that he was bishop; the year, Inferential-Thin (not "in 313").

S4. **Death.** Socrates: "At this time Eusebius, who was bishop of Cæsarea in Palestine, and had the surname of Pamphilus, having died, Acacius, his disciple, succeeded him in the bishopric. This individual published several books, and among others a biographical sketch of his master" (SOC 5802–5806). McGiffert: "We are thus led to place Eusebius' death late in the year 339, or early in the year 340" (EUS-ed 2785–2786); "at the advanced age of nearly, if not quite, eighty years" (EUS-ed 2773–2774). McGiffert: Acacius "wrote a life of the latter (Socr. H. E. II. 4) which is unfortunately lost" (EUS-ed 1017–1019). Witness: Socrates, later (the death and Acacius); editor (the year, and that the life is lost). Confidence: Documented (that he died and Acacius followed); the year, Dominant Modern Reconstruction. Plain words: "about 339".

## Events

### The history and how he made it

E1. **His plan, and the first to try.** HE I.1.1: "It is my purpose to write an account of the successions of the holy apostles, as well as of the times which have elapsed from the days of our Saviour to our own; and to relate the many important events which are said to have occurred in the history of the Church; and to mention those who have governed and presided over the Church in the most prominent parishes, and those who in each generation have proclaimed the divine word either orally or in writing" (EUS 6878–6885). I.1.2: also "the names and number and times of those who through love of innovation have run into the greatest errors" (EUS 6886–6889). I.1.3: also the persecutions and "the confessions which have been made in our own days" (EUS 6895–6900). Witness: own words. Documented.

E2. **The lonely and untrodden path** (Quote 1). HE I.1.4: "But at the outset I must crave for my work the indulgence of the wise, […] for I confess that it is beyond my power to produce a perfect and complete history, and since I am the first to enter upon the subject, I am attempting to traverse as it were a lonely and untrodden path. […] I pray that I may have God as my guide and the power of the Lord as my aid, since I am unable to find even the bare footsteps of those who have traveled the way before me, except in brief fragments, in which some in one way, others in another, have transmitted to us particular accounts of the times in which they lived. From afar they raise their voices like torches, and they cry out, as from some lofty and conspicuous watch-tower, admonishing us where to walk and how to direct the course of our work steadily and safely" (EUS 6926–6927, 6934–6937, 6952–6960). Witness: own words (English only; Greek not held). Documented as his claim. McGiffert: "Eusebius is rightly called the 'Father of Church History.' He had no predecessors who wrote, as he did, with a comprehensive historical plan in view"; Hegesippus and Julius Africanus are "the only two who can in any sense be said to have preceded Eusebius in his province" (EUS-ed 6937–6940, 6950–6952). Confidence that he was first: Widely Accepted (his claim, endorsed by the editor, who names two partial forerunners).

E3. **Plucked from the old writers.** HE I.1.5: "Having gathered therefore from the matters mentioned here and there by them whatever we consider important for the present work, and having plucked like flowers from a meadow the appropriate passages from ancient writers, […] we shall endeavor to embody the whole in an historical narrative, content if we preserve the memory of the successions of the apostles of our Saviour; if not indeed of all, yet of the most renowned of them in those churches which are the most noted" (EUS 6962–6965, 6971–6975). I.1.6: "I know of no ecclesiastical writer who has devoted himself to this subject" (EUS 6977–6979). Witness: own words. Documented. (The "flowers from a meadow" image echoes the live `john-moschus` story's own image; see the outline.)

E4. **The libraries and archives he used.** Jerusalem: letters of learned men "have been kept until our time in the library at Ælia, […] which was established by Alexander, who at that time presided over that church. We have been able to gather from that library material for our present work" (HE VI.20.1; EUS 35433–35443). Edessa: "You have written evidence of these things taken from the archives of Edessa […] But there is no better way than to hear the epistles themselves which we have taken from the archives and have literally translated from the Syriac language" (HE I.13.5; EUS 9499–9500, 9520–9524). McGiffert on the Edessa letters (Jesus and King Abgar): "The epistles were forged probably long before his day, and were supposed by him to be genuine. His critical insight, but not his honesty, was at fault" (EUS-ed 9511–9513). Witness: own words; the forgery verdict is the editor's. Confidence: Documented (the sources he used); forgery of the Abgar letters, Widely Accepted (editor: "no longer a matter of dispute", EUS-ed 9514).

E5. **Pamphilus and the Defense of Origen** (sibling: a clause). "The Apology in his behalf written by us and Pamphilus, the holy martyr of our day. We prepared this carefully and did the work jointly on account of faultfinders" (HE VI.33.4; EUS 36974–36976). Witness: own words. Documented as his claim; who wrote it is the Pamphilus story's job.

### The Great Persecution

E6. **What he saw** (Quote 2). HE VIII.2.1 (English): "All these things were fulfilled in us, when we saw with our own eyes the houses of prayer thrown down to the very foundations, and the Divine and Sacred Scriptures committed to the flames in the midst of the market-places, and the shepherds of the churches basely hidden here and there, and some of them captured ignominiously, and mocked by their enemies" (EUS 43431–43437). Greek (GRK 13615–13620, damaged OCR): "συντετέλεσται δῆτα καθ' ἡμᾶς ἅπαντα, ὁπηνίκα τῶν μὲν προσευκτηρίων τοὺς οἴκους ἐξ ὕψους εἰς ἔδαφος αὐτοῖς θεμελίοις καταρριπτουμένους, τὰς δ' ἐνθέους καὶ ἱερὰς γραφὰς κατὰ μέσας ἀγορὰς πυρὶ παραδιδομένας αὐτοῖς ἐπείδομεν ὀφθαλμοῖς τούς τε τῶν ἐκκλησιῶν ποιμένας αἰσχρῶς ὧδε κἀκεῖσε κρυπταζομένους, τοὺς δὲ ἀσχημόνως ἁλισκομένους καὶ πρὸς τῶν ἐχθρῶν καταπαιζομένους" (normalised from the OCR). Rufinus's Latin, the same file, confirms the core: "divinae vero scripturae in medio platearum igni crematae. pro nefas! ipsi haec nostris vidimus oculis" (GRK 13674–13676). Witness: own words, eyewitness. Documented.

E7. **The edict and its date.** "It was in the nineteenth year of the reign of Diocletian, in the month Dystrus, called March by the Romans, when the feast of the Saviour's passion was near at hand, that royal edicts were published everywhere, commanding that the churches be leveled to the ground and the Scriptures be destroyed by fire, and ordering that those who held places of honor be degraded, and that the household servants, if they persisted in the profession of Christianity, be deprived of freedom" (HE VIII.2.4; EUS 43460–43462, 43487–43490 with notes cut; GRK 13716–13722). McGiffert: the nineteenth year ran to Sept. 16, 303; Lactantius, at Nicomedia, gives Feb. 23 for the opening (EUS-ed 43462–43472). Witness: own words; the exact day, editor and Lactantius. Confidence: 303, Documented; "March", Contested (his own *Martyrs of Palestine* says April, EUS-ed 43472–43474). Plain words: "in 303".

E8. **What he chose not to tell.** HE VIII.2.2–3: "But it is not our place to describe the sad misfortunes which finally came upon them, as we do not think it proper, moreover, to record their divisions and unnatural conduct to each other before the persecution. […] Hence we shall not mention those who were shaken by the persecution, nor those who in everything pertaining to salvation were shipwrecked, and by their own will were sunk in the depths of the flood. But we shall introduce into this history in general only those events which may be usefull first to ourselves and afterwards to posterity" (EUS 43441–43453). Greek GRK 13709–13714 ("οὐκοῦν οὐδὲ τῶν πρὸς τοῦ διωγμοῦ πεπειραμένων … μνήμην ποιήσασθαι προήχθημεν, μόνα δ' ἐκεῖνα τῇ καθόλου προσθήσομεν ἱστορίᾳ, ἃ πρώτοις μὲν ἡμῖν αὐτοῖς, ἔπειτα δὲ καὶ τοῖς μεθ' ἡμᾶς γένοιτ' ἂν πρὸς ὠφελείας"). McGiffert: "Gibbon uses this passage as the basis for his severe attack upon the honesty of Eusebius (Decline and Fall, chap. 16), but he has certainly done our author injustice" (EUS-ed 43453–43456). Witness: own words; Gibbon's attack only as McGiffert reports it (Gibbon not held). Documented (the rule); its fairness, Contested.

E9. **Eyewitness of martyrdoms at Tyre and in Egypt.** At Tyre: "We were present ourselves when these things occurred, and have put on record the divine power of our martyred Saviour Jesus Christ" (HE VIII.7.2; EUS 44161–44163); beasts would not touch the martyrs (EUS 44163–44172): a wonder, his voice only. In the Thebais: "We, also being on the spot ourselves, have observed large crowds in one day; some suffering decapitation, others torture by fire; so that the murderous sword was blunted, and becoming weak, was broken, and the very executioners grew weary and relieved each other" (HE VIII.9.4; EUS 44268–44272). McGiffert: "an eyewitness of numerous martyrdoms not only in Palestine, but also in Tyre and in Egypt"; the dates of the journeys "cannot be determined with exactness" (EUS-ed 1569–1574). Witness: own words. Documented (that he says he saw); the wonder, in his voice only.

E10. **Pamphilus in prison and his death** (sibling: a clause). "Among these was my dearest friend Pamphilus, who was by reason of every virtue the most illustrious of the martyrs in our time" (*Martyrs of Palestine* 7; EUS 46694–46699). "It is time to describe the great and celebrated spectacle of Pamphilus, a man thrice dear to me" (*Mart. Pal.* 11; EUS 47068–47069). Witness: own words. Documented. The dates and the prison are the Pamphilus story's.

E11. **His own escape: not stated.** Eusebius nowhere says he was imprisoned. ATH-ed: "It appears that Eusebius was prisoner at Cæsarea with S. Pamphilus; yet he never mentions the fact himself" (ATH-ed 22313–22315). McGiffert offers guesses (friends in office, the end of the persecution) and calls the charge of cowardice "unfounded" (EUS-ed 1497–1510, 1556–1566). Witness: editors only. Confidence: whether and where he was in prison, Inferential-Thin; how he came out, unknown.

### Constantine

E12. **First sight of Constantine.** "An instance of this we have ourselves seen, when he passed through Palestine with the senior emperor, at whose right hand he stood" (VC I.19; EUS 60955–60957). EUS-ed: Diocletian, "on his way to Egypt in the famous campaign against Achilleus in 296–297" (60957–60958). Witness: own words; year, editor. Documented.

E13. **The vision** (sibling `constantine-the-great` tells it; a clause here, its words barred). "Since the victorious emperor himself long afterwards declared it to the writer of this history, when he was honored with his acquaintance and society, and confirmed his statement by an oath, who could hesitate to accredit the relation" (VC I.28; EUS 61198–61203). EUS-ed (Richardson): "This very circumstantial account has met with doubters from the very beginning, commencing with Eusebius himself" (61209–61211). Documented as Eusebius's report of Constantine's word; the vision, Contested.

E14. **The Life's own rule.** "It is my intention, therefore, to pass over the greater part of the royal deeds of this thrice-blessed prince; as, for example, his conflicts and engagements in the field, his personal valor, his victories and successes against the enemy, and the many triumphs he obtained: likewise his provisions for the interests of individuals, his legislative enactments for the social advantage of his subjects, and a multitude of other imperial labors which are fresh in the memory of all; the design of my present undertaking being to speak and write of those circumstances only which have reference to his religious character" (VC I.11; EUS 60653–60663). Written after the emperor's death: "Henceforward, indeed, there is a full and free opportunity for celebrating in every way the praises of this truly blessed prince, which hitherto we have been unable to do, on the ground that we are forbidden to judge any one blessed before his death" (EUS 60668–60671). Richardson: "it is not stretching his meaning too far to say, expressly limited to his virtuous actions" (EUS-ed 60099–60101). Witness: own words; the gloss, editor. Documented.

E15. **The emperor stood to listen.** "On one occasion … I had begged permission to pronounce a discourse on the subject of our Saviour's sepulchre in his hearing. With this request he most readily complied, and in the midst of a large number of auditors, in the interior of the palace itself, he stood and listened with the rest. I entreated him, but in vain, to seat himself on the imperial throne which stood near … he in his turn was displeased and said that it was not right to listen in a careless manner to the discussion of doctrines relating to God" (VC IV.33; EUS 66986–67003). Witness: own words, his own scene. Documented as his account. Optional.

### Nicaea, 325

E16. **His creed and the added word** (sibling `council-of-nicaea-325` tells the word; a clause here). His letter: "we have been obliged to transmit to you, first, the formula of faith presented by ourselves; and next, the second, which the Fathers put forth with some additions to our words. Our own paper, then, which was read in the presence of our most pious Emperor, and declared to be good and unexceptionable, ran thus" (EUS-ed 2049–2053: the letter as McGiffert prints it in full in his Prolegomena, EUS 2041–2170; also ATH 19554 ff.). The EUS line numbers for the letter below are that Prolegomena print; the words are Eusebius's own. The emperor "advised all present to agree to it … with the insertion of the single word, 'One in substance'" (EUS 2091–2095). Witness: own words, written to his own church after the council. Documented as his account.

E17. **"Not without inquiry."** "On their dictating this formula, we did not let it pass without inquiry in what sense they introduced 'of the substance of the Father,' and 'one in substance with the Father.' Accordingly questions and explanations took place, and the meaning of the words underwent the scrutiny of reason" (EUS 2121–2125). "So much, then, be said concerning the faith which was published; to which all of us assented, not without inquiry, but according to the specified senses, mentioned before the most religious Emperor himself, and justified by the fore-mentioned considerations" (EUS 2158–2162; ATH 19866–19870). "We assented to the sense ourselves, without declining even the term 'One in substance,' peace being the object which we set before us, and steadfastness in the orthodox view" (EUS 2129–2133). Greek, as Socrates quotes it (SOCG 7519–7522, damaged OCR; Hussey's apparatus notes that the clause is missing from the Stephanus and Geneva printings, SOCG 7538–7541, but it stands in his text and in Athanasius's copy): "ᾗ συνεφωνήσαμεν οἱ πάντες οὐκ ἀνεξετάστως, ἀλλὰ κατὰ τὰς ἀποδοθείσας διανοίας, ἐπ' αὐτοῦ τοῦ θεοφιλεστάτου βασιλέως ἐξετασθείσας"; Hussey's Latin: "cui quidem omnes consensimus, non leviter et inconsulto, sed juxta sensus allatos" (SOCG 7558–7560). Also "οὐκ ἀνεξέταστον αὐτοῖς καταλιμπάνομεν" for "we did not let it pass without inquiry" (SOCG 7310). Witness: own words. Documented.

E18. **How others told his signing.** Socrates: "Eusebius, surnamed Pamphilus, bishop of Cæsarea in Palestine, who had held aloof for a short time, after mature consideration whether he ought to receive this definition of the faith, at length acquiesced in it, and subscribed it with all the rest: he also sent to the people under his charge a copy of the Creed, with an explanation of the word homoousios, that no one might impugn his motives on account of his previous hesitation" (SOC 2971–2978). Athanasius (*De Decretis* 3): "Eusebius of Cæsarea in Palestine, who had denied the day before, but afterwards subscribed, sent to his Church a letter, saying that this was the Church's faith … For though he was ashamed at that time to adopt these phrases, and excused himself to the Church in his own way, yet he certainly means to imply all this in his Epistle" (ATH 26780–26786). Witness: Socrates, a century later, friendly; Athanasius, who was at Nicaea, hostile. Documented as each one's account. Where they differ: see D2.

E19. **After Nicaea: Eustathius, Antioch.** Socrates: "Eustathius, bishop of Antioch, accuses Eusebius Pamphilus of perverting the Nicene Creed; Eusebius again denies that he violates that exposition of the faith, and recriminates, saying that Eustathius was a defender of the opinion of Sabellius" (SOC 4708–4711). After Eustathius was deposed, one faction at Antioch "vehemently contended for the translation of Eusebius Pamphilus from Cæsarea in Palestine to Antioch"; violence was checked by the emperor's letters "and Eusebius by refusing to accept the bishopric" (SOC 4752–4760). Witness: Socrates, later. Documented as his account. Optional; probably Leave out (see outline).

### Tyre, 335: the charges

E20. **Why Tyre met; Eusebius's place.** Sozomen: "the emperor convened a council at Cæsarea in Palestine. Athanasius was summoned thither; but fearing the artifices of Eusebius, bishop of the city, of Eusebius, bishop of Nicomedia, and of their party, he refused to attend … At the end of that period, however, he was forced more urgently and repaired to Tyre" (SOC 29941–29947). Epiphanius: Constantine "ordered a synod to be gathered in Phoenicia, in the city of Tyre. He ordered Eusebius of Caesarea to judge, and some others" ("Ἐκέλευσε δὲ δικάζειν Εὐσέβιον τὸν Καισαρείας, καὶ ἄλλους τινάς", EPI 44478–44479; Latin "eique judicio praeesse cum aliis nonnullis Caesariensem Eusebium", EPI 44539–44540). McGiffert: "Whether Eusebius of Cæsarea, as is commonly supposed, or Eusebius of Nicomedia, or some other bishop, presided at this Council we are not able to determine. The account of Epiphanius seems to imply that the former was presiding at the time that Potamo made his untimely accusation" (EUS-ed 2556–2560). Hefele: "the presidency was held by the Church historian Eusebius" (HEF2 1790–1791). Witness: Sozomen (fifth century), Epiphanius (about 40 years later, Athanasian), editors. Confidence: that he sat as a judge, Documented in Epiphanius; that he presided, Contested.

E21. **Potamo's charge** (Quote 3). Epiphanius, *Panarion* 68.8. Greek (EPI 44590–44604, damaged OCR), normalised: "Σὺ καθέζῃ, Εὐσέβιε, καὶ Ἀθανάσιος ἀθῷος ὢν παρὰ σοῦ κρίνεται; τίς ἐνέγκοι τὰ τοιαῦτα; λέγε δέ μοι σύ, οὐ σὺν ἐμοὶ δ' ἦσθα ἐν τῇ φυλακῇ ἐπὶ τοῦ διωγμοῦ; κἀγὼ μὲν ὀφθαλμὸν ἀπεβαλόμην ὑπὲρ ἀληθείας, σὺ δὲ οὔτε φαίνῃ λελωβημένον τι ἔχων ἐν τῷ σώματι, οὔτε ἐμαρτύρησας, ἀλλὰ ἕστηκας ζῶν μηδὲν ἠκρωτηριασμένος. Πῶς ἀνεχώρησας ἀπὸ τῆς φυλακῆς, εἰ μὴ ὅτι ὑπέσχου τοῖς τὴν ἀνάγκην τοῦ διωγμοῦ ἡμῖν ἐπενέγκασι τὸ ἀθέμιτον πρᾶξαι, ἢ ἔπραξας;" Petavius's Latin (EPI 44669–44675): "Tene, inquit, Eusebi, sedere et innocentem Athanasium a te iudicari? Quis ista sustinere possit? Dic sodes, nonne mecum persecutionis tempore in custodia fuisti? Atque ego quidem oculum pro veritate perdidi, tu nulla corporis parte mutilatus es, neque martyrium subiisti, sed vivus integerque consistis. Quanam ratione e custodia evolasti, nisi quod persecutoribus nostris nefarium facinus admissurum te esse recepisti, vel etiam admisisti?" McGiffert's English of the same (EUS-ed 1515–1524): "Dost thou sit as judge, O Eusebius; and is Athanasius, innocent as he is, judged by thee? Who can bear such things? Pray tell me, wast thou not with me in prison during the persecution? And I lost an eye in behalf of the truth, but thou appearest to have received no bodily injury, neither hast thou suffered martyrdom, but thou hast remained alive with no mutilation. How wast thou released from prison unless thou didst promise those that put upon us the pressure of persecution to do that which is unlawful, or didst actually do it?" Hefele's English (HEF2 1793–1801) agrees. Who Potamo was: "the blessed Potamon, the great, bishop of Heraclea and confessor" (EPI 44489–44490, 44577; Latin "magnus ille Potamo fuit Heracleae episcopus … et confessor", 44545, 44662); he "was blinded in an eye in the persecution for the truth" (44583–44584). Witness: Epiphanius, about 40 years later, Athanasian, telling it with sympathy for Potamo ("the blessed", weeping "as happens to the sincere", EPI 44588, 44667–44668). Confidence: Documented as Epiphanius's account; that Potamo said it, Contested (see D3); the charge itself, unproven (D4).

E22. **Eusebius's answer, in Epiphanius.** "Hearing this, Eusebius rose in anger, and standing up dissolved the court, saying: If you have come here and bring such charges against us, then your accusers speak the truth. For if you play the tyrant here, much more in your own country" ("ταῦτα ἀκούσας Εὐσέβιος αἴρεται μὲν εἰς ἀγανάκτησιν, καὶ ἀναστὰς διέλυσε τὸ δικαστήριον, λέγων ὅτι, Εἰ ἐνταῦθα ἤλθετε καὶ πρὸς ἡμᾶς τὰ τοιαῦτα ἀντιλέγετε, ἄρα οὖν ἀληθεύουσιν οἱ κατήγοροι ὑμῶν. Εἰ γὰρ ὧδε τυραννεῖτε, πολλῷ μᾶλλον ἐν τῇ πατρίδι", EPI 44604–44612; Latin 44676–44680; McGiffert's English EUS-ed 1524–1528). My English from the Greek, checked against the Latin. Witness: Epiphanius. Documented as his account; paraphrase only in the story.

E23. **The Egyptian bishops' charge.** The council of Egyptian bishops at Alexandria (ATH 21991–21996; 338, ATH-ed) asked of Tyre: "Was not almost every one among them our enemy? … Was not Eusebius of Cæsarea in Palestine accused by our confessors of sacrificing to idols?" (ATH 22302–22310). Greek (ATHG 61818–61820, damaged OCR): "Οὐχ Εὐσέβιος ὁ ἐν Καισαρείᾳ τῆς Παλαιστίνης ἐπὶ θυσίᾳ κατηγορεῖτο ὑπὸ τῶν σὺν ἡμῖν ὁμολογητῶν;" ATH-ed adds: "[The insinuation of Potammon was groundless: see Dic. C. Biog. ii. 311.]" (ATH-ed 22315–22316). McGiffert: the letter "only says that Eusebius was 'accused of sacrificing,' he does not venture to affirm that he did sacrifice" (EUS-ed 1537–1540; he credits it to "Athanasius himself"; the words are the Egyptian council's, in Athanasius's book). Witness: contemporary, hostile (the bishops who stood with Athanasius at Tyre). Documented as their charge. **Card note:** CARD `uncertain` says "Athanasius says only that Eusebius was 'accused of sacrificing.'" The line is the Egyptian council's letter, preserved by Athanasius.

E24. **The verdict and after.** Socrates: the synod "condemned him [Athanasius] in his absence; and when the result of the enquiry which had been instituted at Mareotis was presented, they voted to depose him" (SOC 5208–5211). Athanasius: those who went on to the emperor were "Eusebius, Theognius, Patrophilus, the other Eusebius, Ursacius, and Valens", who charged that he "had threatened that he would cause the corn to be withheld which was sent from Alexandria"; Constantine "sent me away into Gaul" (ATH 26115–26128). McGiffert names "our Eusebius" among those who went to Constantinople (EUS-ed 2600–2604): that "the other Eusebius" is the Caesarean is the editor's reading. Witness: Socrates; Athanasius, the accused. Documented as their accounts; Eusebius of Caesarea's part in the grain charge, Inferential-Thin (editor's identification). Optional, one clause at most.

E25. **His own telling of Tyre.** VC IV.41: "Meanwhile that spirit of envy which is the enemy of all good, like a dark cloud intercepting the sun's brightest rays, endeavored to mar the joy of this festivity, by again raising contentions to disturb the tranquillity of the Egyptian churches. Our divinely favored emperor, however, once more convened a synod … in order, first, to decide the questions in dispute, and afterwards to perform the dedication" (EUS 67217–67226). VC IV.45: at the dedication in Jerusalem, "I myself too, unworthy as I was of such a privilege, pronounced various public orations in honor of this solemnity" (EUS 67365–67367). He names neither Athanasius nor Potamo. Hefele: "Eusebius also plainly shows (Vita Const. iv. 41) his own injustice towards the orthodox of Egypt" (HEF2 1815–1816, editor). Witness: own words. Documented (what he tells and does not tell).

## What came of him (impact, heaviest first)

I1. **His pages kept what is otherwise lost.** McGiffert: "One of the greatest values of Eusebius' History lies in the quotations which it contains from earlier ecclesiastical writers. The works of many of them are lost, and are known to us only through the extracts made by Eusebius" (EUS-ed 6965–6969). Cases on the tree: Polycrates of Ephesus's letter to Victor survives only as he quotes it (EUS 31324–31413; batch 6 sheet; live `polycrates-of-ephesus`); he is the first writer known to quote Josephus's passage on Jesus (EUS 9229 ff.; live `josephus`, from McGiffert); he took Pliny's ruling from Tertullian (EUS 19448 ff.; live `pliny-the-younger`). Witness: editor (the general claim); own text (the cases). Confidence: Widely Accepted.

I2. **Others carried his story on.** Socrates: "Eusebius, surnamed Pamphilus, writing the History of the Church in ten books, closed it with that period of the emperor Constantine, when the persecution which Diocletian had begun against the Christians came to an end … Now, as we propose to write the details of what has taken place in the churches since his time to our own day, we begin with the narration of the particulars which he has left out" (SOC 1842–1849, 1861–1868, notes cut). Rufinus, prologue: Chromatius, bishop of Aquileia, at the time "quo diruptis Italiae claustris Alarico duce Gothorum se pestifer morbus infudit" (when Italy's barriers were broken and a deadly plague poured in under Alaric, leader of the Goths), asked him to put into Latin "ecclesiasticam historiam, quam vir eruditissimus Eusebius Caesariensis Graeco sermone conscripserat" (the church history that the most learned Eusebius of Caesarea had written in Greek), so that readers' minds might be drawn from the evils of the day; Rufinus joined the history of Book X to Book IX and wrote Books X–XI himself (GRK 26043–26056, 26095–26101). (The live `rufinus-of-aquileia` story already tells this, with the "two fishes" quote: a clause only here.) Jerome translated his *Chronicle* into Latin and carried it on to 378 (JCHRON lines 1–2); his *Lives* 81 lists the Church History, Chronicle, Preparation and Demonstration of the Gospel, Against Porphyry, the Apology for Origen, the Life of Pamphilus and more (JER 41061–41076). Witness: the later historians themselves. Documented.

I3. **The Life of Constantine, judged.** Socrates: in the Life, Eusebius "has but slightly treated of matters regarding Arius, being more intent on the rhetorical finish of his composition and the praises of the emperor, than on an accurate statement of facts" (SOC 1863–1866). Elsewhere: he says Egypt was troubled after Nicaea "not assigning, however, the reason for this, so that hence he has won the reputation of disingenuousness" (SOC 4674–4682). Witness: Socrates, a century later. Documented as his judgment.

I4. **Was he of Arius's party? Judged both ways.** Athanasius (*De Synodis* 17): "Eusebius of Cæsarea in Palestine, in a letter to Euphration the Bishop, did not scruple to say plainly that Christ was not true God" (ATH 57219–57221; ATH-ed adds "[But see Prolegg. ubi supr. note 5]"). Socrates, *Church History* II.21, "Defense of Eusebius Pamphilus": "some have attempted to stigmatize even Eusebius Pamphilus himself as having favored the Arian views in his works … he was both present at the council of Nicæa … and gave his assent to what was there determined" (SOC 6916–6921). Sabinus, who scorned Nicaea, still "commends Eusebius Pamphilus as a trustworthy witness" (SOC 2890–2891). Hefele: "Eusebius was held to be orthodox by Socrates, Theodoret, Gelasius of Cyzicus, … and Valesius … He was declared, on the contrary, to be an Arian by Petavius, Baronius, Montfaucon, Clericus, the Ballerini, and others. Even Athanasius, Epiphanius, and Jerome had not a good opinion of him" (HEF2 1124–1129); "It is certain that Eusebius did not wish to be an Arian … he sought for a middle way" (HEF2 1104–1108). Witness: hostile contemporary, friendly later, modern. Contested.

I5. **"Father of Church History."** McGiffert: "Eusebius is rightly called the 'Father of Church History'" (EUS-ed 6937–6938). Witness: editor. Widely Accepted as a title (CARD legacy).

## Where sources disagree or come late

D1. **Was he first?** His claim (E2) and McGiffert's endorsement, with Hegesippus and Julius Africanus as partial forerunners (EUS-ed 6939–6952). Say "he believed he was the first", or "the first, he wrote"; the forerunners are for the card.

D2. **How he signed at Nicaea.** Eusebius: the emperor approved his church's creed; he assented after questions, "not without inquiry", for peace (E16–E17). Socrates: he "held aloof for a short time" and then subscribed, and wrote "that no one might impugn his motives on account of his previous hesitation" (E18). Athanasius: he "had denied the day before", then signed, and "excused himself to the Church in his own way" (E18). Eustathius, in Socrates: he twisted the creed afterward (E19). All four are told as each one's account; the narrator does not settle his motive.

D3. **Who spoke at Tyre, and to whom.** Epiphanius (about 375): Potamo to Eusebius of Caesarea (E21). Rufinus (about 402), *Church History* X.18, tells a like scene with other men: Paphnutius, a confessor, saw Maximus, bishop of Jerusalem, "who with him had been a confessor with an eye gouged out and a knee cut", sitting with the rest, and said: "you, O Maximus, … I will not allow to sit in the council of the wicked" (GRK 28009–28017: "is eum vidisset Maximum Hierusolymorum episcopum, qui una secum effosso oculo et poplite succiso confessor extiterat … te, inquam, non patiar sedere in concilio malignantium"). Hefele: "it is very possible that Epiphanius' whole account is only another and a false version of what Rufinus relates … This statement of Rufinus is plainly more probable than that of Epiphanius" (HEF2 1826–1835, editor). Hefele also: "Thus relates Epiphanius, while Athanasius and others are silent on the point" (HEF2 1801–1802), though the Egyptian bishops' letter does name a charge of sacrifice (E23). Tell it: Epiphanius tells it this way; Rufinus tells a like scene with Paphnutius and Maximus of Jerusalem, and does not name Eusebius. A modern historian (Hefele) thinks Rufinus's version the likelier.

D4. **The charge itself.** Potamo asks how Eusebius got out of prison "unless" by a forbidden promise or act (E21): a question, not a claim of knowledge. The Egyptian bishops say he "was accused by our confessors of sacrificing" (E23): an accusation, not a finding. McGiffert: Potamo "does not directly charge Eusebius with dishonorable conduct, he simply conjectures" (EUS-ed 1528–1530); Robertson: "groundless" (ATH-ed 22314). Eusebius's own works never say he was imprisoned (E11). No held source says he sacrificed. Contested; told as charges only.

D5. **Who presided at Tyre.** Epiphanius: ordered by the emperor to judge, with others (E20). McGiffert: not determinable. Hefele: Eusebius presided. Say "sat among the judges, Epiphanius says"; not "presided".

D6. **The Life of Constantine: history or praise?** Eusebius's own rule (E14), Socrates's judgment (I3), Richardson's gloss (E14). Told as Eusebius's rule and Socrates's judgment.

D7. **The date of the edict.** March (HE VIII.2.4) against April (*Mart. Pal.*) and February 23 (Lactantius), per McGiffert (E7). Say "in 303".

D8. **The Abgar letters.** He took them as genuine; McGiffert holds them forged before his time (E4). If told, credit the forgery verdict to modern editors.
