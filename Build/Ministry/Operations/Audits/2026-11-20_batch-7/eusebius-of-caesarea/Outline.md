# Outline: Eusebius of Caesarea

Sheet items (S, E, I, L, D) are in `Source_Sheet.md`, which gives the file and line for each. EUS is McGiffert's English of the *Church History* (HE) and the English *Life of Constantine* (VC); GRK the Greek of HE VI–X with Rufinus's Latin; EPI Epiphanius's *Panarion* in Greek and Latin; ATH Athanasius in English; SOC Socrates and Sozomen in English. HE I–V in Greek is not held.

- **The essence:** Early in the fourth century Eusebius, bishop of Caesarea in Roman Palestine, set out to write the first history of the whole church, and built it from the church's own letters, lists of bishops and old books, copying their words into his pages (E1–E4; Documented, his own words; that he was the first, Widely Accepted, his claim and his editor's). Much of what the early Christians wrote survives only because he copied it (I1; Widely Accepted, his editor's judgment, with cases on the tree).
- **Also told:**
  - **Witness to the Great Persecution.** In 303 he saw churches torn down and the Scriptures burned in the market squares, and martyrs die at Tyre and in Egypt; his teacher Pamphilus died a martyr. He wrote that he would leave out those who gave way (E6–E10; Documented, his own words).
  - **Constantine's writer.** He saw Constantine as a young man and later knew him as emperor; after the emperor's death he wrote his Life, telling by his own rule only the deeds that showed his faith. A century later Socrates judged it more praise than accurate history (E12–E15, I3; Documented as each one's words).
  - **Nicaea, 325.** He signed the council's creed, and wrote home that he had agreed "not without inquiry", after asking what its words meant, for the sake of peace (E16–E17; Documented, his letter). Socrates says he held back a short time first; Athanasius says he refused the day before (E18; Documented as their accounts; his motive Contested).
  - **The charges at Tyre, 335.** He sat among the bishops who judged Athanasius of Alexandria, Epiphanius says, and a bishop who had lost an eye in the persecution asked how Eusebius had got out of prison unharmed, unless by doing something forbidden. The Egyptian bishops wrote that he had been "accused" of sacrificing to idols (E20–E23; Documented as charges; that Potamo said it, Contested, D3; the charge itself unproven, D4).
  - **What came of him.** His pages kept letters otherwise lost; Socrates took up the story where he ended, and Rufinus put it into Latin; he has been judged ever since, accused of siding with Arius's party and defended as loyal to Nicaea (I1–I4; Documented as each one's judgment; his views Contested).
- **Length:** about 3 minutes 50 seconds (about 575 words at 150 a minute). Counted first: the three quotes, about 145 words (Q1 about 35, Q2 about 55, Q3 about 55); the plain sense of the words to explain, about 60 words (the persecution, succession lists, shepherds and houses of prayer, creed, confessor, martyr, Arius's party); the witnesses credited, about 70 words (Socrates twice, Athanasius twice, Epiphanius, the Egyptian bishops, Rufinus, the editors once). That is about 275 words before any event is told. Why longer than three minutes: the job names five turns (the history, the persecution, Constantine, Nicaea, Tyre), the last told with three witnesses who differ, and a third goes to what came of him. Cut first, if needed: the emperor standing to listen (beat 4), the Edessa archives (beat 3), the Rufinus variant down to one clause (beat 6), What came 4 down to one sentence.
- **Setting:**
  - Time: born about 260 (S1, editor's inference; say "born around 260", never a year as fact); the Great Persecution from 303 (E7); bishop "after the persecution", between about 311 and 318 (S3; no single year); Nicaea 325; Tyre 335; died about 339 (S4).
  - Place: Caesarea, a port on the coast of Roman Palestine, in today's Israel (S2; CEN). Tyre, the chief city of Phoenicia (S2a). Jerusalem's church library (E4).
  - Tradition: the tree's name is "the Church of Roman Palestine before Constantine" (S2). He outlived it; the story need not name it more than once.
  - Language: he wrote in Greek (sheet, Language).

**Words to explain** (the first time each is heard; plain word or short gloss, worked into the sentence)

- **the Great Persecution**: "the last and greatest persecution of Christians by Rome's emperors, which began in 303". The census tradition name is not needed.
- **successions / succession lists** (E1, E3): "the lists of bishops in each great church, one after another, back to the apostles". Do not say "apostolic succession" bare.
- **edict** (E7), if used: "the emperors' orders". Better: "imperial orders".
- **houses of prayer** (Q2): the narrator gives the plain sense, "churches".
- **shepherds of the churches** (Q2): the narrator gives the plain sense, "the bishops and clergy".
- **martyr**: "someone who died for the faith". In Q3 Potamo's word: "you did not suffer as a martyr", that is, he did not suffer for the faith as Potamo did.
- **confessor** (E21, E23): "one who had suffered for the faith and lived". Potamo was one. Say it in the sentence; do not use the word bare.
- **creed**: "a statement of faith". "Of one being with the Father", the word the council added, is told in one clause; the Nicaea story explains it.
- **Arius's party / Arian**: "those who sided with Arius, a priest of Alexandria, who taught that the Son had a beginning". Never "Arian" bare.
- **council / synod**: "a meeting of bishops". At Tyre: "a council of bishops the emperor called to judge Athanasius".
- **sacrificing to idols** (E23): "offering sacrifice to the Roman gods", the act the persecutors demanded.
- **Life of Constantine**: say "his Life of the emperor Constantine", "his book on Constantine's life".
- **Sabellius / Sabellian** (E19): do not use; leave out (Leave out).

- **Shape:** Teacher or thinker. The question he tried to answer: how could the church's story be told, from its beginning to his own day, when no one had told it whole? What he taught: a history made of the church's own documents, quoted in their own words. Who learned it from him: the historians who carried it on (Socrates, Rufinus, Jerome) and every later reader of the early church. The trap is a list of books; show the idea through his first page (Quote 1) and through one moment in which he is himself the witness (Quote 2).
- **Sibling:**
  - `council-of-nicaea-325` (live, read; job: the council itself, the creed and its business). It already tells, through Eusebius, that the emperor asked for one word, homoousios, to be added, and quotes the emperor's "civil strife" line (barred). Here: one clause for the added word; the story's own part is his letter home and the phrase "not without inquiry", which the council story does not use.
  - `constantine-the-great` (live, read; job: the emperor). It already tells the vision of the cross "Constantine told Eusebius … under oath", with "Conquer by this" (barred), and sets Lactantius's dream beside it. Here: the vision is one clause at most, unquoted; this story's own Constantine material is VC I.11 (the Life's rule), VC I.19 (first sight) and, optional, VC IV.33 (the emperor standing).
  - `pamphilus-of-caesarea` (researched after this; live text read). Its job: the library, the copying of Origen, the Defense of Origen and its disputed authorship, his martyrdom. Here: one clause: his teacher and friend, whose name he took, who died a martyr in the persecution. The Defense is not told here.
- **Openings:**
  1. **Chosen.** His own first page (Quote 1; E2). In about three sentences: the quote, then "So wrote Eusebius, bishop of Caesarea in Roman Palestine, at the start of his Church History", then straight into the essence. Optional, with the torches image (Quote 1, second part) told in the narrator's words: the earlier writers left only fragments, which he heard "like torches" calling from a watchtower.
  2. Not chosen: Quote 2, the churches thrown down "with our own eyes" (E6). Strong, but it belongs in time order at beat 2.

**Story beats** (the opening, the essence and beat 1 make the first minute, about 150 words; beats 2 to 6a follow the life once, in order. The opening and the essence reach ahead to the History; the History itself is told once, at beat 3, after the persecution, when he wrote it)

1. **Caesarea and Pamphilus.** Born around 260 (S1, editor; birthplace unknown), he learned at Caesarea from Pamphilus, a presbyter there, his teacher and friend, whose name he took: he was called Eusebius of Pamphilus (S1; one clause; the library and the Defense of Origen are the Pamphilus story's). Keep this beat short; it sets up the persecution.
2. **The Great Persecution: what he saw.** In 303 the emperors ordered the churches torn down and the Scriptures burned (E7; Documented; say "in 303", D7). Quote 2: what he saw with his own eyes (E6). He saw martyrs die at Tyre and in Egypt; in Egypt, he wrote, so many died in one day that the executioners grew weary and took turns (E9; paraphrase). Any wonder (beasts that would not touch the martyrs) stays in his voice or is left out. Pamphilus was imprisoned and died a martyr: one clause (E10).
3. **Bishop, and the history he wrote.** After the persecution he became bishop of Caesarea, his own home (S3; no year). Then the History, finished about 324, most of it after the persecution, his editor reckons (S5; say "in the years after the persecution"). Its plan, in his own words: to record the successions of the apostles, the bishops of the great churches, the teachers, the heretics and the martyrs (E1). Earlier writers had left only fragments, and he gathered passages from them (E2, E3; do not use the "flowers from a meadow" image: see Do not say). He drew letters from the church library in Jerusalem (E4). Optional (cut first): from the archives of Edessa he took letters said to be between Jesus and a king of Edessa, and translated them from Syriac; modern editors hold them forged long before him (E4, D8). Reason for the act: no one had written the church's history whole (E2, E3; his claim). Then his rule for the persecution: he would not record those who were shaken by it and gave way, but only what would profit his own time and those after (E8; Documented, his own words). The listener must hear the rule plainly, as his own choice about what history should hold. The opening's quote (Quote 1) is from this book; do not repeat it here.
4. **Constantine.** He had seen Constantine years before, as a young man standing at the old emperor's right hand when he passed through Palestine (E12). Later he knew him: Constantine told him of the vision, under oath (E13; one clause, unquoted; the Constantine story tells it). Optional (cut first): when Eusebius spoke on Christ's tomb in the palace, the emperor would not sit, and stood to listen to the end, Eusebius writes (E15). The Life itself is told at beat 6a, in time order, since he wrote it after 337.
5. **Nicaea, 325.** By his own account, his church's creed was read before the emperor, and the council added words to it, among them "of one being" (E16; one clause; the Nicaea story tells the word). He wrote home to his church that they had questioned what the new words meant, and that all had agreed "not without inquiry", for the sake of peace (E17; Documented, his letter; the phrase is his, rendered from the Greek). Then the other accounts, each credited: Socrates says he held back a short time before signing, and wrote home so that no one would doubt his motives (E18); Athanasius, who was there and was his opponent, says he "refused the day before", then signed and excused himself to his church in his own way (E18). The narrator does not settle his motive (D2).
6. **Tyre, 335: the charges.** The emperor called bishops to Tyre to judge Athanasius, now bishop of Alexandria (E20). Eusebius sat among the judges, Epiphanius says, writing some forty years later on Athanasius's side (E20; credit Epiphanius; not "presided", D5). Then Quote 3: Potamo, a bishop from Egypt who had lost an eye in the persecution, called out to him (E21). In Epiphanius's account Eusebius rose in anger, dissolved the court, and said that if they came to accuse him, their own accusers must be telling the truth (E22; paraphrase). The council deposed Athanasius, and the emperor exiled him (E24; one clause; the Athanasius story tells it). Three years later the bishops of Egypt wrote that Eusebius had been "accused by our confessors of sacrificing to idols" (E23; credit them; "accused"). Rufinus, in a later Latin history, tells a like scene at Tyre with other men: a confessor named Paphnutius rebuking Maximus, the bishop of Jerusalem, and no word of Eusebius (D3; one clause). The doubt is part of the story: say plainly that these are charges, that no source shows he sacrificed, and that his own account of Tyre names neither Athanasius nor Potamo, and speaks only of envy troubling the churches of Egypt (E25, D4).
   - 6a. **The Life of Constantine.** After Constantine died in 337, Eusebius wrote his Life. He said he would pass over the emperor's wars, victories and laws, and tell only what showed his faith (E14; Documented, his own rule). A century later Socrates judged the book more intent on the emperor's praise than on an accurate account (I3; credit Socrates). Beat 4 carries only the first sight and the friendship (the optional standing scene is undated and comes before 337); his death is told once, in the Ending.

**What came of him** (about a third of the story; heaviest first)

1. **The voices he kept.** Many early Christian writings are known only from his quotations, his English editor wrote (I1; credit the editor once). The listener must hear the cases: the letter of Polycrates of Ephesus on Easter survives only because he copied it; he is the first writer known to quote Josephus's passage on Jesus; and he retold Pliny the Younger's ruling on the Christians, taken from Tertullian (I1; links; the Pliny story tells how he read it). Say this as what he did: he copied, he quoted.
2. **Others carried his story on.** Socrates of Constantinople began his own history where Eusebius stopped, with the end of the persecution, and set out to tell what he had left out (I2). Rufinus of Aquileia put the Church History into Latin for the churches of the West (I2; one clause; the Rufinus story tells the Goths and Chromatius). Jerome put his Chronicle into Latin and carried it on (I2; optional).
3. **His history kept his own choices.** What he chose to leave out of the persecution (E8) and the Life's rule (E14) have shaped how he is read: a modern editor reports that the historian Edward Gibbon attacked his honesty over the first, and himself defends him (E8; optional; credit "his editor reports").
4. **Judged both ways.** Athanasius wrote that in a letter Eusebius had said plainly that Christ was not true God (I4). Socrates wrote a defense of him: he had been at Nicaea and agreed to what it decided (I4). Historians since have counted him on both sides (I4, Hefele). Tell each credited; the narrator does not decide.

2. **HE VIII.2.1** (Greek GRK 13615–13620, damaged OCR; Rufinus's Latin in the same file confirms the core, "divinae vero scripturae in medio platearum igni crematae. pro nefas! ipsi haec nostris vidimus oculis", GRK 13675–13676; McGiffert's English EUS 43432–43437 agrees). We saw the inspired and sacred Scriptures handed over to the fire in the middle of the market squares. We saw the shepherds of the churches hiding shamefully here and there, and some of them caught in disgrace and mocked by their enemies." ("We saw" is the one verb of the Greek, said once for each object; nothing is added.) The narrator gives the plain sense of "houses of prayer" (churches) and "shepherds" (bishops and clergy). Job: beat 2. The historian as witness, and the record's force: the Scriptures burned, the clergy hiding and mocked.
3. **Epiphanius, Panarion 68.8** (Greek EPI 44590–44604, damaged OCR; Petavius's Latin in the same file confirms it clause by clause, EPI 44669–44675; McGiffert's English, EUS-ed 1515–1524, and Hefele's, HEF2 1793–1801, agree). Greek: "Σὺ καθέζῃ, Εὐσέβιε, καὶ Ἀθανάσιος ἀθῷος ὢν παρὰ σοῦ κρίνεται; τίς ἐνέγκοι τὰ τοιαῦτα; λέγε δέ μοι σύ, οὐ σὺν ἐμοὶ δ' ἦσθα ἐν τῇ φυλακῇ ἐπὶ τοῦ διωγμοῦ; κἀγὼ μὲν ὀφθαλμὸν ἀπεβαλόμην ὑπὲρ ἀληθείας, σὺ δὲ οὔτε φαίνῃ λελωβημένον τι ἔχων ἐν τῷ σώματι, οὔτε ἐμαρτύρησας, ἀλλὰ ἕστηκας ζῶν μηδὲν ἠκρωτηριασμένος. Πῶς ἀνεχώρησας ἀπὸ τῆς φυλακῆς, εἰ μὴ ὅτι ὑπέσχου τοῖς τὴν ἀνάγκην τοῦ διωγμοῦ ἡμῖν ἐπενέγκασι τὸ ἀθέμιτον πρᾶξαι, ἢ ἔπραξας;" Rendering: "You sit there, Eusebius, and Athanasius, an innocent man, is judged by you? **Connection to carry into the story:** what passed from Eusebius was the church's memory of its own first centuries, kept in the words of its own documents. The listener must be told two things. First, how it passed: he did not only tell the story, he copied the letters, lists and books into it, so that writings later lost, such as Polycrates's letter on Easter, survive inside his pages (E3, I1); later historians began where he ended, in Greek (Socrates) and in Latin (Rufinus), so his history became the starting point for theirs (I2). Second, what the listener must know to weigh it: his history was also his own choice of what to keep, and he said so, leaving out those who gave way in the persecution and, in the Life, all but the emperor's faith (E8, E14); and he was a party in the quarrels he lived through, accused at Tyre and judged both ways since (E21–E23, I4). Tell it through these acts: the copying, the rules he set himself, the continuers, the charges. Not as a "this matters because" sentence.

**Ending:** close on the person. Either (a) his death, about 339, and his pupil, who followed him as bishop and wrote his life; that book is lost, his editor notes (S4); or (b) the opening image seen again: he began by admitting he could not write a perfect and complete history (Quote 1, in paraphrase). Recommended: (a), placed after What came, ending on "that book is lost". Give the pupil by role; Acacius need not be named. Add no moral to it (no "the man who kept so many voices…").

**Who tells it**

- **Eusebius himself**, for the plan, the libraries, the persecution, the rule of what he left out, Constantine, and the Nicaea letter. Credit "he wrote" where he speaks. Most of his own life comes from his own pages; say so once, inside the beat 2 sentence about what he saw.
- **Socrates**, about a century later: his signing at Nicaea (beat 5), his judgment of the Life (beat 6a), his continuation and his defense (What came 2, 4). Friendly to Eusebius; say "a historian a century later" at first mention.
- **Athanasius**, his opponent, who was at Nicaea: the refusal "the day before" (beat 5); the letter to Euphration (What came 4).
- **Epiphanius**, about forty years after Tyre, on Athanasius's side: the judges and Potamo's charge (beat 6). The listener must know he was not there and was on the other side.
- **The bishops of Egypt**, Athanasius's party, three years after Tyre: the charge of sacrifice (beat 6).
- **Rufinus**, in Latin, about two generations later: the other Tyre scene (beat 6).
- **His English editor** (Arthur McGiffert, 1890), by role, at most twice: that many writings survive only in his quotations (What came 1); optionally, Gibbon's attack (What came 3). By role, not name.
- **The doubt is itself the story at Tyre:** the scene is Potamo's question, then the Egyptian bishops' "accused", then Rufinus's different telling, then Eusebius's own silence.

**Do not say**

- Any word in "Words to explain" used without its plain sense the first time it is heard.
- Not "Eusebius sacrificed", "Eusebius denied the faith", "Eusebius bought his freedom", or "he was a coward". Say he was asked, and accused; no source shows he did it (D4).
- Not "Eusebius escaped from prison" or "he was in prison with Potamo" in the narrator's voice. It is Potamo's claim; Eusebius never says he was imprisoned (E11).
- Not "Eusebius presided at Tyre" or "led the council". Say "sat among the judges, Epiphanius says" (D5).
- Not "Eusebius condemned Athanasius" or "Eusebius exiled Athanasius". The council deposed him and the emperor exiled him (E24).
- Not "Eusebius had no answer" or "he did not deny it" as fact. McGiffert's "Eusebius, it seems, did not deny the charge" is the editor's reading. Say what Epiphanius tells: he rose in anger and broke up the court (E22).
- Not "Athanasius said he was accused of sacrificing". The words are the letter of the bishops of Egypt, kept in Athanasius's book (E23).
- Not "Eusebius was an Arian" or "Eusebius was orthodox" in the narrator's voice (I4).
- Not "he refused to sign the creed" or "he signed under pressure" as fact. Give each account credited: his own letter, Socrates, Athanasius (D2).
- Not "Eusebius wrote the creed of Nicaea" or "Nicaea adopted his creed". His church's creed was read and approved, and the council added words to it, by his own account (E16).
- Do not quote "Conquer by this" or the emperor's "civil strife" line (barred). Do not retell the vision beyond one clause.
- Do not use the "flowers from a meadow" image: the `john-moschus` story holds a like image. Use "plucked passages" or "gathered".
- Do not use "the two fishes to the loaves", or tell the Goths and Chromatius: they are the Rufinus story's.
- Not "the first church historian" as fact in the narrator's voice. Say "the first, he wrote" or "the first to try, he believed"; his editor names two who partly went before (D1).
- Not "everything we know of the early church comes from Eusebius". Say "many early writings are known only from his quotations, his editor wrote" (I1).
- Not "before the persecution he wrote his History" or the History told before beat 2. He finished it about 324, most of it after the persecution, his editor reckons (S5). The Chronicle and other early works are not told.
- Not "born in Caesarea" or "born in 260". Say "born around 260"; his birthplace is not known (S1).
- Not "he became bishop in 313" or "in 314". Say "after the persecution" (S3).
- Not "he died in 339" or "340" as fact. Say "about 339" (S4).
- Not "he saw Constantine's vision". Constantine told him of it (E13).
- Not "the Life of Constantine is a eulogy" or "propaganda" in the narrator's voice. Give his own rule and Socrates's judgment (E14, I3, D6).
- Not "Eusebius left out the lapsed to protect the church's image" or any motive. His own reason: only what would profit his own time and those after (E8).
- Not "Eusebius forged the Abgar letters". If told, he took them from the archives as genuine; editors hold them forged long before him (E4, D8).
- Not "Pamphilus was killed in 310" or any date for him: the Pamphilus story's.
- Not "the beasts would not touch the martyrs" in the narrator's voice; in his voice only, or left out (E9).
- Not "Potamo was blinded" without "in one eye" (E21).
