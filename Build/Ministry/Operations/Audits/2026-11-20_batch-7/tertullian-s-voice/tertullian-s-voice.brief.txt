In about 197, Tertullian wrote to the governors who sat in judgment for Rome. If they dared not look into the charges openly, he said, "Then let the truth reach your ears at least by the hidden road of silent writing." He pleaded: "One thing only she asks for now: not to be condemned unknown."

Tertullian of Carthage, in Roman Africa, was a convert trained in Roman law, as Eusebius says, who became the foremost early writer of the faith in Latin. He defended the Christians before Rome's courts, claimed the Scriptures for the apostles' churches, and put to work Latin words for God as one substance in three persons. Then he turned to a strict movement of prophets, the New Prophecy, and two centuries later Augustine counted his followers, the Tertullianists, among the heretics.

That book was his Apology, a defense of the Christians. The governors, he argued, hated and judged the name of Christian without learning what Christians did, while crowds blamed them for every flood, drought, famine or plague. He closed defiant: the more you cut us down, the more we grow; "the blood of Christians is seed."

Against teachers who built new doctrines on Scripture, he borrowed a lawyer's plea that throws a case out before it is argued. Their arguments should not be heard until it was settled whose the Scriptures were. The faith, he said, passed from Christ to the apostles and on to their churches, such as Ephesus and Rome, which anyone could visit. "By what right, Marcion, do you cut my timber? By whose leave, Valentinus, do you turn my springs aside?" He went on, "This property is mine. I have held it long; I held it first." And he ended, "I am the heir of the apostles."

The New Prophecy came from Phrygia, in today's Turkey, and its prophets said the Paraclete, the Helper Jesus promised, was speaking anew through them. They asked for stricter fasts, no marrying again after a spouse had died, and no flight from persecution. His defense of the Paraclete, he writes, set him apart from those he called the unspiritual. Why he turned, two later writers disagree. Jerome blames the envy and insults of the clergy at Rome. Augustine says he joined prophets he had once argued against, then broke from them too and gathered his own followers.

After his turn, he wrote against Praxeas, who, by Tertullian's account, taught at Rome that the Father himself was born and suffered. At creation, he said, God commands the light and the Word, the Son, makes it, so the two are distinct. "I have already said how you should take the word 'another'. It names a person, not a substance. It marks a distinction, not a division. I hold everywhere one substance in three who are joined together." Substance is what God is, one and undivided; person is who is distinct: Father, Son and Spirit. His text holds the Latin words trinitas, persona and substantia, which, later writers say, became the Latin church's words for God.

A bishop offered to forgive adultery after repentance. In what is probably his last surviving book, Tertullian replied that only the church of the Spirit could forgive it, "not the church which consists of a number of bishops."

Novatian wrote a book on the Trinity that Jerome took for a summary of Tertullian's, though a modern historian doubts it. Most of Tertullian's life rests on his own books and on Jerome. Jerome heard, at two removes, that Cyprian, the next great bishop of Carthage, read him daily and would ask, "Give me the master." A century on, Lactantius said his Apology had already pleaded the Christians' cause in full.

His followers at Carthage bore his name down to Augustine's day, when the last few joined the Catholic church, the main body, and gave up their building. Augustine named Tertullian a heretic for going over to the New Prophecy.

Tertullian, Jerome says, is said to have lived to a great old age. No one records when or how he died.
