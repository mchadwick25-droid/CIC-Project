# Source sheet: Tertullian

Researcher's sheet for batch 7 (2026-11-20). The job: the Carthage advocate who first wrote the faith in Latin: the Apology to Rome's governors, the churches' claim to the Scriptures in the Prescription, the words for God as one substance in three persons against Praxeas, and his late turn to the strict New Prophecy, which kept him from being counted a saint. Shape: Teacher or thinker. New card. The writer uses the outline, the shapes and the standard only; this sheet is the evidence behind the outline.

**Conventions.** Line numbers are the file's own lines. In the XML files, quotes give the text with tags removed; inline translators' notes are cut and marked […]. Latin is given as the OCR prints it, errors included.

**Language and text.** Tertullian wrote in Latin, with a few works in Greek, now lost (EUS-ed 10260–10262). **Apology and Prescription:** the only held Latin is Oehler's 1853 edition in a damaged Google OCR (OEH1, OEH2); the CSEL editions of these works (vols. 69–70) are in copyright and not held. The ANF English confirms the readings used below. **Against Praxeas:** held in two Latin prints, Kroymann (KRO) and Oehler (OEH2), which agree on every line quoted. **On Modesty:** clean Latin in CSEL20.

**Witness kinds and gaps.**
- OWN = his own works, written at Carthage about 197 to after 217 (Apology, Prescription, Against Praxeas, On Modesty and the rest). Nearly all we know of his life comes from them and from Jerome (EUS-ed 10267–10268: "Our main sources for a knowledge of his life are his own writings, and Jerome's de vir. ill. chap. 53").
- LATER, about a century: **Eusebius** (EUS, early 4th c.), **Lactantius** (LACT, about 305–310).
- LATER, about 170 years: **Jerome**, *Lives* 53, written in the fourteenth year of Theodosius, 392/3 (JER 38785), from an informant two steps from Cyprian.
- LATER, about two centuries: **Augustine**, *On Heresies*, about 428 (AUG 1098, the Maurists' note); **Vincent of Lérins**, 434 (VIN-ed 11809).
- LATE AND DISPUTED: the "Gelasian" decree, put "about 496" by Mansi, its author disputed in Mansi's own note (MANSI8 17236–17252).

## 1. Setting

S1. **Carthage, Roman Africa.** Jerome: "was from the city of Carthage in the province of Africa" (JER 40496). Latin: "provinciae Africae, civitatis Carthaginiensis" (JERL 55614). LATER. Documented. Census: Carthage, today in Tunisia (CEN `modernCountries`). Plain words: "Carthage, the great city of Roman Africa, in today's Tunisia".

S2. **His time.** Jerome: "he flourished chiefly in the reign of the emperor Severus and Antoninus Caracalla" (JER 40498–40499; JERL 55616–55617 "sub Severo principe et Antonino Caracalla maxime floruit"). Census `dates` "c. 197-220", `start` 197 (CEN). Documented for the reigns (193–217); Widely Accepted for 197–220. Plain words: "around the year 200".

S3. **Tradition.** Census `toldWithin`: `latin-apologists`, informal name "The Case Made in Latin" (CEN). Plain words: "the Christians who first made the faith's case in Latin". Do not say "apologists" bare.

S4. **His father.** Jerome's Latin: "patre centurione proconsulari" (JERL 55614–55615), a centurion on the governor's staff. Richardson's English, "son of a proconsul or Centurion" (JER 40497), mistranslates; Heurtley's quotation of Kaye has "son of a proconsular centurion" (VIN-ed 13540). LATER, one witness. Documented as Jerome's report. Leave out of the story unless needed.

S5. **Trained in the law: Eusebius's word, not settled.** Eusebius: "Tertullian, a man well versed in the laws of the Romans, and in other respects of high repute, and one of those especially distinguished in Rome" (EUS 10241–10242, 10272–10280; notes cut). LATER. McGiffert: "he himself became a lawyer and rhetorician in Rome" and "very likely practiced law and rhetoric in Rome until his conversion" (EUS-ed 10248–10249, 10291–10292): editor. Monceaux: his deep study of law is "incontestable", but that he is the jurist Tertullian quoted in Roman law books "is only a hypothesis"; the name was common, he never alludes to such work, and he lived at Carthage; the jurist may be a relative (MON 15444–15497): modern. Sanday: "He was primarily a lawyer" (NPNF9-ed 430): editor. **Contested** that he practised as an advocate; Documented that Eusebius calls him versed in Roman law; Widely Accepted that his writing uses the language of the law courts (S6, MON 15494–15497). Plain words: "trained in Roman law, Eusebius says".

S6. **A word from the courts.** The Prescription's title: "The term præscriptio is a legal one, meaning a demurrer, or formal objection" (ANF3-ed 20037–20038). Holmes glosses another of its terms, *constitutio*, as "a term of the law courts" (ANF3-ed 20779–20781). Editors. Widely Accepted. Plain words: "a lawyer's plea that throws a case out before it is argued".

S7. **A convert.** "Once these things were with us, too, the theme of ridicule. We are of your stock and nature: men are made, not born, Christians" (ANF3 2710–2712, Apology 18). Latin: "Haec et nos risimus aliquando. De vestris sumus. Fiunt, non nascuntur Christiani" (OEH1 18939–18940, damaged OCR). OWN. Documented that he was a convert from paganism. When and how, he does not say; "converted ... probably between 180 and 190" is McGiffert's (EUS-ed 10250).

## 2. Events (in time order)

E1. **The Apology, 197.** Addressed to the presiding judges of the provinces: "Rulers of the Roman Empire, if, seated for the administration of justice on your lofty tribunal, under the gaze of every eye, and occupying there all but the highest position in the state, you may not openly inquire into and sift before the world the real truth in regard to the charges made against the Christians; … if, finally, the extreme severities inflicted on our people in recently private judgments, stand in the way of our being permitted to defend ourselves before you, you cannot surely forbid the Truth to reach your ears by the secret pathway of a noiseless book. She has no appeals to make to you in regard of her condition, for that does not excite her wonder. She knows that she is but a sojourner on the earth, and that among strangers she naturally finds foes; and more than this, that her origin, her dwelling-place, her hope, her recompense, her honours, are above. One thing, meanwhile, she anxiously desires of earthly rulers—not to be condemned unknown" (ANF3 1448–1465). Latin: OEH1 11034–11035, 11253–11261 (T1 below). OWN. Documented. **Date:** McGiffert: "almost all scholars are agreed in assigning it to the years 197–204", and after Bonwetsch, Harnack and Nöldechen "the latter part … of the year 197 … may be accepted as practically established" (EUS-ed 10294–10303); Coxe: "Great diversity exists … Mosheim says, a.d. 198, Kaye a.d. 204" (ANF3-ed 1443–1446); Monceaux: "Dans le courant de 197" (MON 17149–17150). Widely Accepted for 197. **Who the "rulers" were** (antistites): the Latin is general, "presiding at almost the summit of the city to judge" (OEH1 11034–11035); the Pliny story renders them "Rome's governors". Plain words: "Rome's governors, who sat as judges".

E2. **The crowd's cry, and the seed.** "If the Tiber rises as high as the city walls, if the Nile does not send its waters up over the fields, if the heavens give no rain, if there is an earthquake, if there is famine or pestilence, straightway the cry is, 'Away with the Christians to the lion!'" (ANF3 3997–4003, Apology 40). And at the end: "kill us, torture us, condemn us, grind us to dust; your injustice is the proof that we are innocent. … The oftener we are mown down by you, the more in number we grow; the blood of Christians is seed" (ANF3 4653–4661, Apology 50). Latin: "Plures efficimur quotiens metimur a vobis; semen est sanguis Christianorum" (OEH1 30954–30955, OCR "^Flures ^'effidmur … semen est sanguis / Christianorum"; damaged, but this line legible). OWN. Documented. The popular form "the blood of the martyrs is the seed of the church" is not his wording.

E3. **The Apology and Pliny.** "For the younger Pliny, when …" (ANF3 1566 ff., Apology 2): the `pliny-the-younger` story tells this in full, with its quotes. Here a clause at most.

E4. **The Prescription, about 200.** Monceaux puts it in his "second period (from about 200 to 206)", after the first books Against Marcion (MON 17156–17162). Dominant Modern Reconstruction. The argument: heretics may not argue from Scripture at all, because "it ought to be clearly seen to whom belongs the possession of the Scriptures, that none may be admitted to the use thereof who has no title at all to the privilege" (ANF3 20765–20768, ch. 15); the first question is "With whom lies that very faith to which the Scriptures belong. From what and through whom, and when, and to whom, has been handed down that rule by which men become Christians?" (ANF3 20860–20866, ch. 19); what the apostles preached "can … properly be proved in no other way than by those very churches which the apostles founded in person" (ANF3 20937–20942, ch. 21). Run over the apostolic churches, he says: Corinth, Philippi, Ephesus, and Rome, "from which there comes even into our own hands the very authority (of apostles themselves)" (ANF3 21657–21677, ch. 36). Then T2 (ch. 37). OWN. Documented as his argument.

E5. **The turn to the New Prophecy, about 207–213.** His own words: "We indeed, on our part, subsequently withdrew from the carnally-minded on our acknowledgment and maintenance of the Paraclete" (ANF3 51074–51076, Praxeas 1). Latin: "et nos quidem postea agnitio paracleti atque defensio disiunxit a psychicis" (KRO 11745–11746). OWN. Documented that he says his side was separated from the others by the Paraclete. Coxe: this "may only denote a withdrawal from the communion of the Bishop of Rome … it does not, necessarily … define his ultimate separation from his own bishop and the North-African church" (ANF3-ed 51076–51081): editor. Monceaux: a semi-Montanist period 207–212, and the final break "au commencement de 213", between *To Scapula* and *Flight in Persecution* (MON 17249; 17071–17076): modern, Dominant Modern Reconstruction for the dates. **What the New Prophecy asked of him**, in his late books: no second marriage after a spouse's death ("Heretics do away with marriages; Psychics accumulate them", ANF4 5731–5732, On Monogamy 1), stricter fasts (On Fasting), and no flight from persecution (Monceaux on *De fuga*, MON 17058–17070). "Psychics", the unspiritual, is his scornful word for the church members who did not accept the new prophets (ANF3 51070–51072 and note). OWN. Documented.

E6. **Why he turned: two later witnesses differ.** Jerome: "He was presbyter of the church until middle life, afterwards driven by the envy and abuse of the clergy of the Roman church, he lapsed to the doctrine of Montanus, and mentions the new prophecy in many of his books" (JER 40506–40509). Latin: "invidia postea et contumeliis clericorum Romanae Ecclesiae, ad Montani dogma delapsus" (JERL 55626–55629). Augustine: Tertullian became a heretic "quia transiens ad Cataphrygas, quos ante destruxerat, coepit etiam secundas nuptias contra apostolicam doctrinam tanquam stupra damnare, et postmodum etiam ab ipsis divisus, sua conventicula propagavit" (AUG 2868–2873, OCR as printed: "ccpit", "conventieula"): because, going over to the Phrygians (the New Prophecy) whom he had earlier refuted, he began to condemn second marriages as fornication, against the apostle's teaching, and later broke from them too and spread his own gatherings. LATER, each about two centuries on. **Contested**: Jerome gives a motive (Roman clergy's envy and insults); Augustine gives a cause in teaching and a second split. Credit each; do not settle. That he was a presbyter rests on Jerome alone (EUS-ed 10257–10258); Monceaux accepts it (MON 15570–15589). Leave out.

E7. **Against Praxeas, after 213.** Monceaux: "Après 213 … le livre Contre Praxéas, où l'auteur parle lui-même de sa rupture avec les catholiques" (MON 17462–17466). ANF heading: "[Probable date not earlier than a.d. 208]" (ANF3-ed 51001). Dominant Modern Reconstruction. **Written after his turn**: he says "we … (and more especially since we have been better instructed by the Paraclete, who leads men indeed into all truth), believe that there is one only God" (ANF3 51097–51100, ch. 2). OWN. Documented. **Praxeas, by Tertullian's account:** "the first to import into Rome from Asia this kind of heretical pravity, a man in other respects of restless disposition, and above all inflated with the pride of confessorship simply and solely because he had to bear for a short time the annoyance of a prison" (ANF3 51037–51041). He taught "that the Father Himself came down into the Virgin, was Himself born of her, Himself suffered, indeed was Himself Jesus Christ" (ANF3 51014–51016). After the bishop of Rome had acknowledged the prophetic gifts of Montanus, Prisca and Maximilla, Praxeas, "by importunately urging false accusations against the prophets … compelled him to recall the pacific letter" (ANF3 51044–51054). "By this Praxeas did a twofold service for the devil at Rome: he drove away prophecy, and he brought in heresy; he put to flight the Paraclete, and he crucified the Father" (ANF3 51054–51057). Latin: "ita duo negotia diaboli Praxeas Romae procurauit: prophetiam expulit et haeresim intulit, paracletum fugauit et patrem crucifixit" (KRO 11738–11739; OEH2 43100). OWN, a hostile witness. Documented as his account. Which bishop of Rome: "Probably Victor" (ANF3-ed 51044): editor; leave unnamed. Almost nothing is known of Praxeas beyond this book (CEN `modalist-monarchianism` voices). Credit "Tertullian says".

E8. **On Modesty, his last surviving book, 217–222.** Monceaux: "le traité de la Pudicité, qui fut publié entre 217 et 222, en réponse à un édit du pape Calliste, et qui est très probablement le dernier des ouvrages conservés" (MON 17466–17469). Dominant Modern Reconstruction for the date; which bishop issued the edict is disputed (Callistus is Monceaux's; leave unnamed). The `hermas` story tells the edict and "the Shepherd of adulterers"; here a clause. He answers "the Church has the power of forgiving sins" with the Paraclete's saying through "the new prophets": "The Church has the power to forgive sins; but I will not do it, lest they commit others withal" (ANF4 9468–9473). And: "'the Church,' it is true, will forgive sins: but (it will be) the Church of the Spirit, by means of a spiritual man; not the Church which consists of a number of bishops" (ANF4 9542–9545). Latin: "potest ecclesia donare delictum, sed non faciam, ne et alia delinquant" (CSEL20 15213–15214); "et ideo ecclesia quidem delicta donabit, sed ecclesia spiritus per spiritalem hominem, non ecclesia numerus episcoporum" (CSEL20 15312–15314). OWN. Documented.

E9. **Old age; no death recorded.** Jerome: "He is said to have lived to a decrepit old age, and to have composed many small works, which are not extant" (JER 40514–40516). Latin: "Ferturque vixisse usque ad decrepitam aetatem" (JERL 55634–55635). LATER, hearsay ("is said"). No held source gives his death. "died about 245" (JER-ed 40493–40494) and "lived until about 230" (Nöldechen, EUS-ed 10247–10248) are editors'. Inferential-Thin for any date.

## 3. The teaching (his own words)

T1. **Apology 1 (the opening).** Latin (OEH1 11253–11261, damaged OCR as printed): "liceat yeritati yel oc- / cnlta yia tacitarum litterarum' ad aures vestras pervenire. *Nihii / de caosa sua deprecaturt quia nec de condicione miratur. Sdt se / peregrinam in terris agere, inter extraneos facile ^^inimicos inve / Dire, ceterum genus^ sedem, spem, gratiam, dignitatem in caelis / habere. Unum gestit interdum, ne ignorata damneUir." Read: *liceat veritati vel occulta via tacitarum litterarum ad aures vestras pervenire. Nihil de causa sua deprecatur, quia nec de condicione miratur. Scit se peregrinam in terris agere, inter extraneos facile inimicos invenire, ceterum genus, sedem, spem, gratiam, dignitatem in caelis habere. Unum gestit interdum, ne ignorata damnetur.* Confirmed by ANF3 1457–1465 (E1). Documented.

T2. **Prescription 37.** Latin (OEH2 2280–2290, damaged OCR as printed): "ad quos merito dicendum est: Qui estist / lo, el mide ▼enistis? quid in meo agitis, non mei? qno de- / ■qae, Mardon, iure silvam meam caedis? qua licentia, Valentine, / iMtes meoB Iransyertb? qua potestate, Apelles, Kmites meos com- / ■evesT ^Mea esl possessio. … Mea est possessio, olim possideo, / *prisr poBBideo, habeo origines firmas ab ipsis auctoribus quorum / ittt res. Ego sum haeres apostolorum." The OCR has lost "quando et unde" (only "lo, el mide" survives) and garbles "fontes", "limites", "prior", "fuit". ANF3 21742–21751 confirms every clause: "Who are you? When and whence did you come? As you are none of mine, what have you to do with that which is mine? Indeed, Marcion, by what right do you hew my wood? By whose permission, Valentinus, are you diverting the streams of my fountain? By what power, Apelles, are you removing my landmarks? This is my property. … I have long possessed it; I possessed it before you. I hold sure title-deeds from the original owners themselves, to whom the estate belonged. I am the heir of the apostles." Documented.

T3. **Against Praxeas 2: the formula.** "the mystery of the dispensation is still guarded, which distributes the Unity into a Trinity, placing in their order the three Persons—the Father, the Son, and the Holy Ghost: three, however, not in condition, but in degree; not in substance, but in form; not in power, but in aspect; yet of one substance, and of one condition, and of one power, inasmuch as He is one God" (ANF3 51142–51149; notes cut). Latin: "quae unitatem in trinitatem disponit, tres dirigens patrem et filium et spiritum, — tres autem non statu, sed gradu, nec substantia, sed forma, nec potestate, sed specie, — unius autem substantiae et unius status et unius potestatis, quia unus deus" (KRO 11801–11802, 11815–11818, OCR "graclu", "secl", "filiiim", "spiiitum"). Documented.

T4. **Against Praxeas 12: person, not substance.** Context: at creation God says "Let there be light", and the Word makes it; so "you have two Beings—One that commands that the thing be made, and the Other that executes the order and creates" (ANF3 51892–51894). Then: "In what sense, however, you ought to understand Him to be another, I have already explained, on the ground of Personality, not of Substance—in the way of distinction, not of division. But although I must everywhere hold one only substance in three coherent and inseparable (Persons), yet I am bound to acknowledge, from the necessity of the case, that He who issues a command is different from Him who executes it" (ANF3 51894–51902). Latin (KRO 12558–12562): "alium autem quomodo accipere debeas, iam professus sum, personae, non substantiae nomine, ad distinccionem [OCR 'distmccionem'], non ad diuisionem. ceterum, (etsi) ubique teneo unam substantiam in tribus cohaerentibus, tamen alium [OCR 'aiium'] dicam oportet ex necessitate sensus eum, qui iubet, et eum, qui facit." Oehler agrees (OEH2 43865–43868: "personae, non substantiae, nomine, ad distinctionem, non ad divisionem. Ceterum ubique teneam unam substantiam in tribus cohaerentibus"). Holmes's "(Persons)" and "inseparable" are his supplements; the Latin has only "in three who cohere". Documented.

T5. **The words themselves.** *Trinitas* ("unitatem in trinitatem disponit", KRO 11801–11802; and "trinitas unius diuinitatis", CSEL20 15308), *persona* and *substantia* (KRO 12559, 12561) are all in his own text. That he coined or first put them to work in Latin is the census's claim ("where the language had no words for what he wanted to say, he invented them - trinitas, persona, substantia, sacramentum among them", CEN `why`) and Sanday's ("the particular influence which Tertullian exerted in coining the terms and marking out the main lines of Latin theology", NPNF9-ed 427–429). Widely Accepted, as the census and editors' view. **The Greek word "triad" is older**: Theophilus of Antioch uses τριάς, "The earliest use of this word 'Trinity'" (ANF2-ed 8993–8997). Do not say he was the first to call God a Trinity in any language.

## 4. Impact (heaviest first)

I1. **The Latin words for God.** T5. Against Praxeas is where "a great deal of Latin trinitarian vocabulary was first put to work" (CEN `modalist-monarchianism` legacy); "He gave Latin its words for the Trinity" (CEN `legacy`). Kaye thought the Athanasian Creed "was composed by one who had this treatise always in mind" (ANF3-ed 51897–51899): editor, leave out. Widely Accepted (census and editors). **No held source says the Council of Nicaea used his words**; Nicaea's term is Greek.

I2. **Novatian's On the Trinity.** Jerome: Novatian wrote "a great volume On the Trinity, a sort of epitome of the work of Tertullian, which many mistakenly ascribe to Cyprian" (JER 40895–40897). LATER. Documented as Jerome's judgment. Sibling: `novatian` (batch 7).

I3. **Cyprian, "Give me the master".** Jerome's Latin: "Vidi ego quemdam Paulum Concordiae, quod oppidum Italiae est, senem qui se beati Cypriani, jam grandis aetatis, notarium, cum ipse admodum esset adolescens, Romae vidisse diceret, referreque sibi solitum nunquam Cyprianum absque Tertulliani lectione unum diem praeterisse, ac sibi crebro dicere, Da magistrum: Tertullianum videlicet significans" (JERL 55618–55626, OCR "Concordie", "Italie", "eíalis", "Rome", "preterisse"). That is: an old man, Paul of Concordia in Italy, said that as a very young man he had seen at Rome Cyprian's secretary, then aged, who told him Cyprian never let a day pass without reading Tertullian, and often said to him, "Give me the master," meaning Tertullian. LATER, hearsay at three removes. Documented as Jerome's report. **Richardson's English (JER 40500–40505) wrongly makes Paul himself Cyprian's secretary**; Heurtley's quotation of Kaye reads it as the Latin does (VIN-ed 13543–13548). Benson: Cyprian was "his devoted yet discriminating disciple" (BEN 2385–2389), and his book on the Lord's Prayer owes verbal debts to Tertullian's (BEN 40590–40592): modern; card. Sibling: `cyprian-of-carthage`.

I4. **Read, and judged.** Lactantius, about a century later: "Septimius quoque Tertullianus fuit omni genere litterarum peritus, sed in eloquendo parum facilis et minus comptus et multum obscurus fuit. ergo ne hic quidem satis celebritatis inuenit" (LACT 22707–22710); "Septimius Tertullianus also was skilled in literature of every kind; but in eloquence he had little readiness, and was not sufficiently polished, and very obscure. Not even therefore did he find sufficient renown" (LACT-E 4472). And: "Tertullian fully pleaded the same cause in that treatise which is entitled the Apology" (LACT-E 4569, V.4). LATER. Documented. Augustine: "a Tertulliano, cujus multa leguntur opuscula eloquentissime scripta" (AUG 2827–2828), "from Tertullian, many of whose little works, most eloquently written, are read". LATER. Documented.

I5. **His followers at Carthage.** Augustine: the Tertullianists, "usque ad nostrum tempus paulatim deficientes, in extremis reliquiis durare potuerunt in urbe Carthaginensi: me autem ibi posito ante aliquot annos … omni ex parte consumpti sunt. Paucissimi enim qui remanserant, in Catholicam transierunt, suamque basilicam, quae nunc etiam notissima est, Catholicae tradiderunt" (AUG 2829–2835, raw OCR). Dwindling to his day, they lasted in a last remnant at Carthage; when Augustine was there some years before, they came wholly to an end; the very few left crossed to the Catholic church and handed over their basilica, still well known. LATER. Documented as Augustine's report. Census: "There is some evidence of a small group of followers in Carthage after his death" (CEN `legacy`).

I6. **Later writers held the turn against him.** Vincent of Lérins (434) first praises him: "as Origen holds by far the first place among the Greeks, so does Tertullian among the Latins. For who more learned than he …? … Every word almost was a sentence; every sentence a victory" (VIN 13555–13568). Then: "Yet this man also … this Tertullian, I say, too little tenacious of Catholic doctrine, that is, of the universal and ancient faith, more eloquent by far than faithful, changed his belief, and justified what the blessed Confessor, Hilary, writes of him, namely, that 'by his subsequent error he detracted from the authority of his approved writings.' He also was a great trial in the Church" (VIN 13571–13580). The reading "faithful" is disputed (VIN-ed 13575–13576: "Fidelior, Baluz, Felicior, others"). VIN's Latin and Hilary's own commentary on Matthew are **not held**; Hilary's words reach us here only through Vincent in English. LATER. Documented as Vincent's verdict. The "Gelasian" decree lists "Opuscula Tertulliani, apocrypha" among books not received, beside Eusebius's History and the works of Lactantius (MANSI8 17218–17222, damaged OCR); Mansi's own note says its author is long disputed, Gelasius, Damasus or Hormisdas (MANSI8 17236–17252). Late, Contested in date and author; card only. **"Never made a saint"** is the census's claim only (CEN `legacy`); no held source says it, and by Mark's ruling (2026-11-20) the story does not say it. The held verdict is Augustine's: he lists the Tertullianists among the heresies and says Tertullian became a heretic by going over to the New Prophecy (AUG 2827–2835, 2868–2873; I5, E6). Documented as Augustine's.

## 6. Where sources disagree or come late

D1. **Why he turned.** Jerome (envy and insults of the Roman clergy) against Augustine (going over to the Phrygians, condemning second marriage, then splitting from them too) (E6). Credit each.
D2. **How the Cyprian story reached Jerome.** Jerome's Latin: Paul had seen Cyprian's secretary; Richardson's English: Paul was the secretary (I3). Follow the Latin.
D3. **A lawyer?** Eusebius "well versed in the laws"; McGiffert "a lawyer … in Rome"; Monceaux: legal training certain, the jurist of the law books perhaps a relative, not him (S5). The job's "advocate" is not settled by the record.
D4. **"The first to write the faith in Latin."** Jerome places him first among Latin writers "after Victor and Apollonius" (JER 40495; JERL 55610–55613 "primus post Victorem et Apollonium Latinorum ponitur"), whose Latin works are lost. The census calls him "the first major Christian author to write in Latin" (CEN `why`). Widely Accepted as "the first great Latin Christian writer whose books survive"; do not say "the first ever".
D5. **Dates.** Apology 197 (Widely Accepted; ANF3-ed gives 198 and 204 as older views). Prescription about 200, the break about 213, Praxeas after 213, On Modesty 217–222 (Monceaux; Dominant Modern Reconstruction). His death: no source.
D6. **Withdrawal from whom.** "We … withdrew from the carnally-minded" (E5): Coxe reads it as perhaps only a break with Rome's bishop (editor); Monceaux as a break with the church at Carthage (modern). Augustine says he later split from the New Prophecy too. Contested.
D7. **The "Gelasian" decree**: date and author disputed (I6).
