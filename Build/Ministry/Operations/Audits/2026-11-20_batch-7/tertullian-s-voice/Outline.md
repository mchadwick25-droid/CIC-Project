# Outline: Tertullian

Sheet items (S, E, T, I, D) are in `Source_Sheet.md`, which gives the file and line for each. ANF3 and ANF4 are the Ante-Nicene Fathers English; OEH1, OEH2, KRO and CSEL20 the held Latin; JER and JERL Jerome in English and Latin; EUS Eusebius; LACT Lactantius; AUG Augustine; VIN Vincent of Lérins; MON Monceaux (modern).

- **The essence:** Tertullian of Carthage, a convert who knew Roman law well, as Eusebius says, was the first great writer of the faith in Latin whose books survive, around the year 200 (S1, S2, S5, S7, D4; Carthage and the reigns Documented; "trained in Roman law" Eusebius's word, Documented as his, Contested that he practised as an advocate; "first great writer whose books survive" Widely Accepted). He defended the Christians before Rome's governors, claimed the Scriptures for the churches the apostles founded, and gave Latin its words for God as one substance in three persons; then he turned to the strict New Prophecy, and the church that kept reading him never counted him a saint (E1, E4, T4, T5, E5, I6; the books Documented; "gave Latin its words" Widely Accepted, census and editors; "never counted a saint" Widely Accepted, census).
- **Also told:**
  - **The Apology, 197.** A defense of the Christians addressed to Rome's governors as judges: the charge is only the name; the crowd blames the Christians for every flood and famine and cries "the Christians to the lion"; the more they are cut down, the more they grow (E1, E2; Documented; the year Widely Accepted).
  - **The Prescription, about 200.** A lawyer's plea that throws a case out before it is argued: those who teach against the apostles may not argue from Scripture at all, because the Scriptures belong to the churches that received them from the apostles, and those churches can be counted, Corinth, Ephesus, Rome (E4, T2, S6; Documented as his argument; the date Dominant Modern Reconstruction).
  - **Against Praxeas, after 213.** Against a teacher at Rome who, he says, made the Father and the Son one and the same, so that the Father was born and suffered, he answered that God is one substance and three persons, Father, Son and Spirit, distinct but not divided; the Latin words trinitas, persona and substantia are in his text (E7, T3, T4, T5; Documented as his book and words; that later Latin theology took its words from here Widely Accepted). He wrote it after his turn, and says the Paraclete had taught his side better (E7; Documented).
  - **The turn.** He took up the New Prophecy from Phrygia, which held that the Paraclete, the Helper Jesus promised, was speaking anew through its prophets; it asked for stricter fasts, no second marriage and no flight in persecution, and he broke with those he called the unspiritual (E5, E8; Documented). Why, the later witnesses differ (D1; Contested). In his last book, the church of the Spirit may forgive sins, "not the church which consists of a number of bishops" (E8; Documented).
  - **What came of him.** Cyprian read him every day and called him "the master" (I3; Jerome's report, Documented as his). Novatian's book On the Trinity was, Jerome says, a kind of summary of his (I2). Later writers read him and judged him: eloquent and much read (Augustine), learned but obscure (Lactantius), "a great trial in the Church" for his turn (Vincent, quoting Hilary) (I4, I6; Documented as each one's view). His followers at Carthage lasted until Augustine's day and then gave their church building to the Catholics (I5; Augustine's report, Documented as his).
- **Length:** about 3 minutes 40 seconds (about 550 words, at 150 a minute). The words the story must carry come first: three quotes (about 210 words), four short phrases inside the narrator's sentences (about 40), the plain sense of seven words (Apology, prescription, heretics, Paraclete, New Prophecy, substance and person, second marriage: about 60), and five later witnesses credited by name, with Jerome's chain of hearsay and the two accounts of why he turned (about 90). That is about 400 words before the life is told. The job has four parts, each a different book, and each needs its own words; the record is rich (his own books survive), so the length is not thin. A third goes to what came of him. Standing cuts, if it runs long: the Apelles clause in Quote 2; the third sentence of Quote 3; the "Christians to the lion" clause in beat 2; Lactantius in What came 3.
- **Setting:**
  - Time: around the year 200: the Apology in 197, his last surviving book between 217 and 222; Jerome puts his best years under the emperors Severus and Caracalla (S2, E1, E8). Plain words: "around the year 200". Give a year only for the Apology (197).
  - Place: Carthage, the great city of Roman Africa, today in Tunisia (S1; CEN `modernCountries`). Plain words: "Carthage, in Roman Africa". He knew Rome too (EUS 10279–10280: "one of those especially distinguished in Rome"; Eusebius's word).
  - Tradition: the Christians who first made the faith's case in Latin; the tree's name is "The Case Made in Latin" (S3). Do not say "the Latin apologists".
  - Language: Latin, which until then had little Christian writing (D4); a few works in Greek are lost (EUS-ed 10260–10262).

**Words to explain** (the first time each is heard; plain word or short gloss, worked into the sentence)

- **Apology**: "a defense of the Christians" (the Pliny story calls it "Defense of the Christians"). Say the title once with its plain sense: "his Apology, a defense of the Christians".
- **Rome's governors / "rulers of the Roman Empire"**: "the governors who sat in judgment for Rome" (E1). Not "emperors".
- **prescription**: "a lawyer's plea that throws out a case before it is argued" (S6; ANF3-ed 20037–20038 "a demurrer, or formal objection"). Work it into the sentence; do not use the word bare.
- **heretics**: "teachers he held false", or credit it: "those he called heretics" (T2 context).
- **Paraclete**: "the Paraclete, the Helper whom Jesus promised, the Holy Spirit" (E5, E7). Say "the Paraclete" only with this sense the first time.
- **New Prophecy**: "a movement from Phrygia, in today's Turkey, whose prophets said the Spirit was speaking anew through them" (`montanism-the-new-prophecy`; E5). "Montanists" was their opponents' name; the narrator says "the New Prophecy".
- **psychics / the carnally-minded**: his scornful name for the church members who did not accept the new prophets: "those he called the unspiritual" (E5). Never "psychics" bare.
- **substance and person** (Quote 3): give the plain sense beside the quote: substance is what God is, one and undivided; person is who is distinct, Father, Son and Spirit. Do not explain "persona" as "mask": no held source says so.
- **Trinity**: a grown listener knows it; say once that the Latin word, trinitas, is in his text (T5).
- **second marriage**: "marrying again after a husband or wife had died" (E5, E6).
- **Phrygia**: "inland Asia Minor, in today's Turkey".
- **Catholic** (Augustine, What came 4): "the main church", or "the Catholic church, the main body"; the word is Augustine's.

- **Shape:** Teacher or thinker. The question he answered: in a language that had hardly been used for the faith, how could a Christian say what the church is, who owns its Scriptures, and how God is one and yet Father, Son and Spirit, before Rome's courts and against rival teachers? What he taught: the Christians' innocence before the law; the apostles' churches as the owners of Scripture; one substance in three persons. Who learned it: Cyprian, Novatian, Lactantius, and the Latin church after them. The trap is a list of books: show each through one line of his own (Quotes 1–3), and keep Against Marcion, Against the Valentinians and the rest out (Leave out).

- **Openings:**
  1. **Chosen.** His own first words in the Apology (Quote 1; E1, T1). In about three sentences: the governors who judge Christians will not look into the charges openly. So, wrote a man in Carthage in 197, let the truth reach them by the hidden road of silent writing. She asks one thing only: not to be condemned unknown. Then the name and the essence.
  2. Not chosen: Cyprian, half a century later, asking his secretary every day, "Give me the master" (I3). Strong, but it reaches ahead to the end, and its witness chain (Jerome, Paul, the secretary) needs room the opening does not have.

**Story beats** (the opening, the essence and beat 1 are the first minute, about 150 words; beats 2 to 6 deepen it)

1. **A convert at Carthage.** He was from Carthage, Jerome says, and the son of a centurion, an officer of the Roman army (S1, S4; JERL 55614–55615, "centurione proconsulari"; Documented as Jerome's; the father is optional, and "proconsular" need not be explained). Eusebius, writing a century later, calls him "well versed in the laws of the Romans" and well known at Rome (S5; EUS 10272–10280; Documented as Eusebius's). He had been a pagan: "We are of your stock and nature," he told the pagans; "men are made, not born, Christians" (S7; ANF3 2711–2712; a short phrase, may stand). When or why he converted, no source says.
2. **The Apology, 197.** The governors hated and judged the very name of Christian, he argued, without learning what Christians did (E1; ANF3 1472–1478; Documented as his argument). Their crowds blamed the Christians for every disaster: if the Tiber rose to the walls or the Nile did not, if there was drought, earthquake, famine or plague, the cry went up, "the Christians to the lion!" (E2; ANF3 3997–4003; Documented). He even turned an emperor's ruling against the courts (E3; one clause; the Pliny story tells it). He ends defiant: kill us, torture us, grind us to dust; the more you cut us down, the more we grow; "the blood of Christians is seed" (E2; ANF3 4653–4661; OEH1 30954–30955; Documented; short phrase).
3. **The Prescription, about 200.** He wrote against teachers who built new teachings on Scripture, and he used a plea from the law courts, one that throws a case out before it is argued (S6, E4). Their arguments from Scripture should not even be heard, he said, until it was settled whose the Scriptures were (E4; ANF3 20765–20768, 20860–20866). The faith came from Christ to the apostles, and from the apostles to the churches they founded, and anyone can visit those churches, Corinth, Philippi, Ephesus, Rome, and hear the apostles' teaching still read there (E4; ANF3 20937–20942, 21657–21677; Documented as his argument). Then Quote 2. Do not say who first made the argument; the Irenaeus story tells his.
4. **The turn, from about 207.** He came to believe the New Prophecy from Phrygia: that the Paraclete, the Helper Jesus promised, now spoke through its prophets, and asked more of Christians than the church did: stricter fasts, no second marriage after a husband or wife died, and no flight from persecution (E5; Documented; the years Dominant Modern Reconstruction, MON). In the end, he writes, the Paraclete separated his side from the rest, whom he called the unspiritual (E5; ANF3 51074–51076; KRO 11745–11746). Why he turned, two later writers disagree: Jerome says the envy and insults of the clergy at Rome drove him to it; Augustine says that he took up the prophets he had once argued against, condemned second marriage, and later broke from them too and gathered his own followers (E6, D1; Contested; credit each).
5. **Against Praxeas, after 213.** Praxeas, by Tertullian's account, had come to Rome from Asia, persuaded the bishop of Rome to withdraw his welcome to the new prophets, and taught that the Father himself was born and suffered. So, Tertullian wrote, Praxeas "put to flight the Paraclete, and crucified the Father" (E7; ANF3 51054–51057; KRO 11738–11739; his hostile account; short phrase). Against him he wrote that God is one, yet the one God has a Son, his Word, and the Spirit; three not in substance but in form, "yet of one substance" (T3; paraphrase, or the phrase). Then Quote 3, introduced by the creation scene: God says "Let there be light", and the Word makes it, so the one who commands and the one who makes are two (T4; ANF3 51892–51894). He wrote this, he says, as one taught better by the Paraclete (E7; ANF3 51097–51100). Say it plainly: the book the Latin church drew its words for the Trinity from was written after his turn.
6. **The last book.** When a bishop announced he would forgive Christians who had committed adultery and done penance, Tertullian answered that only the church of the Spirit could forgive such sins, through a spiritual man, "not the church which consists of a number of bishops" (E8; ANF4 9542–9545; CSEL20 15312–15314; Documented; short phrase). The Paraclete itself, he quotes, had said through the new prophets that the church could forgive sins, but would not, lest people sin again (E8; ANF4 9471–9473; paraphrase). Do not name the bishop; the Hermas story tells the ruling.

**What came of him** (about a third of the story; heaviest first)

1. **The words.** The words he set to work in Against Praxeas, trinitas, persona, substantia, became the Latin church's words for God, the census and later editors say (T5, I1; Widely Accepted; credit "historians" or "later writers" if a source is named). The Greek word for a triad was older (T5): do not say he was the first anywhere. In Rome, a generation later, Novatian wrote On the Trinity, which Jerome called "a sort of epitome" of Tertullian's work (I2; JER 40895–40897; Documented as Jerome's; one clause).
2. **Cyprian.** Jerome, about 170 years later, tells how he heard it. An old man in Italy, Paul of Concordia, had seen at Rome, as a very young man, the aged secretary of Cyprian, bishop of Carthage. The secretary said Cyprian never let a day pass without reading Tertullian, and would say, "Give me the master," meaning him (I3; JERL 55618–55626; Documented as Jerome's report; a short phrase). Tell the chain as Jerome's Latin gives it, not as the English translation does (D2).
3. **Read, and judged.** Lactantius, a century on, found him learned in every kind of writing but rough and "very obscure", and said his Apology had already pleaded the Christians' cause in full (I4; LACT 22707–22710; LACT-E 4569; Documented as his view; cut first if long). Augustine, about two centuries on, said many of his works were still read, and most eloquent (I4; AUG 2827–2828). Vincent of Lérins, in 434, set him first among the Latin writers, as Origen was among the Greeks, but called him "a great trial in the Church": he had changed his belief, and, as Hilary had written, his later error had taken away the authority of his approved writings (I6; VIN 13555–13557, 13571–13580; Documented as Vincent's; Vincent by role, "a monk in Gaul two centuries later", or by name). He was never counted among the saints (I6; census; Widely Accepted). Do not say anyone decided it.
4. **His followers.** A small group at Carthage bore his name for two centuries. In Augustine's time the last few joined the Catholic church, the main body, and handed over their church building, which was, Augustine says, still well known (I5; AUG 2829–2835; Documented as Augustine's report). Augustine wrote this in his list of heresies, and he named Tertullian a heretic (E6; AUG 2868–2873): say so, so that the force of the judgment is not lost.

**Quotes** (3)

Only Quotes 1–3 are quoted aloud. These short phrases may stand inside the narrator's sentences: "men are made, not born, Christians" (beat 1), "the Christians to the lion!" and "the blood of Christians is seed" (beat 2), "put to flight the Paraclete, and crucified the Father" (beat 5), "not the church which consists of a number of bishops" (beat 6), "Give me the master" (What came 2), "a great trial in the Church" (What came 3). Use no more than four of them. "Very obscure" may stand as two words.

1. **Apology 1**, rendered from the Latin (OEH1 11253–11261). **The Latin is held only in Oehler's damaged 1853 scan**; no other held Latin print has it. Thelwall's English (ANF3 1457–1465) confirms every clause. Latin, read through the OCR: "liceat veritati vel occulta via tacitarum litterarum ad aures vestras pervenire. Nihil de causa sua deprecatur, quia nec de condicione miratur. Scit se peregrinam in terris agere, inter extraneos facile inimicos invenire, ceterum genus, sedem, spem, gratiam, dignitatem in caelis habere. Unum gestit interdum, ne ignorata damnetur." Rendering: "Then let the truth reach your ears at least by the hidden road of silent writing. She makes no plea in her own cause, for her lot does not surprise her. She knows she lives as a foreigner on earth, and easily finds enemies among strangers. But her family, her home, her hope, her favor and her honor are in heaven. One thing only she asks for now: not to be condemned unknown." ("Silent writing" is *tacitarum litterarum*; Thelwall has "a noiseless book". "Favor" is *gratiam*; Thelwall has "recompense".) The writer may stop after the second sentence and go straight to the last. Job: the opening. The Apology's whole case in its first lines.
2. **Prescription 37**, rendered from the Latin (OEH2 2280–2290). **The Latin is held only in Oehler's damaged 1853 scan**, which has lost "quando et unde" and garbled several words; Holmes's English (ANF3 21742–21751) confirms every clause, and the rendering follows it where the scan fails. Latin, read through the OCR and ANF: "Qui estis? quando et unde venistis? quid in meo agitis, non mei? quo denique, Marcion, iure silvam meam caedis? qua licentia, Valentine, fontes meos transvertis? qua potestate, Apelles, limites meos commoves? Mea est possessio. … Mea est possessio, olim possideo, prior possideo, habeo origines firmas ab ipsis auctoribus quorum fuit res. Ego sum heres apostolorum." Rendering: "Who are you? When did you come, and from where? You are not mine. What are you doing with what is mine? By what right, Marcion, do you cut my timber? By whose leave, Valentinus, do you turn my springs aside? By what power, Apelles, do you move my boundary stones? This property is mine. I have held it long; I held it first. I hold firm title from the very founders whose property it was. I am the heir of the apostles." Cut the Apelles sentence if long. Job: beat 3. The churches' claim to the Scriptures, in a property lawyer's words.
3. **Against Praxeas 12**, rendered from the Latin (KRO 12558–12562), checked against Oehler (OEH2 43865–43868), which agrees, and Holmes's English (ANF3 51894–51902). Latin: "alium autem quomodo accipere debeas, iam professus sum, personae, non substantiae nomine, ad distinctionem, non ad divisionem. ceterum, etsi ubique teneo unam substantiam in tribus cohaerentibus, tamen alium dicam oportet ex necessitate sensus eum, qui iubet, et eum, qui facit." Rendering: "I have already said how you should take the word 'another'. It names a person, not a substance. It marks a distinction, not a division. I hold everywhere one substance in three who are joined together. Yet the sense forces me to call the one who commands another than the one who makes." The writer may stop after the fourth sentence. Job: beat 5. His words for God, person and substance, in his own sentence; the narrator gives the plain sense beside it.

**Connection to carry into the story:** what passed from Tertullian was a Latin way of saying the faith: above all the words for God, one substance and three persons, and with them the claim that the Scriptures belong to the churches that received them from the apostles (T2, T4, T5). The listener must understand three things. First, why it was new: until then the faith had been argued mostly in Greek, and he had to make Latin carry it, in the words of the law courts he knew (a plea that throws out a case, a title to an estate) and in words for God that later Latin writers kept (D4, S5, S6, I1). Second, how it passed: Cyprian, the next great bishop of Carthage, read him every day and called him "the master"; Novatian at Rome summed up his book on the Trinity; Lactantius said he had pleaded the cause already (I2, I3, I4). Third, why it passed with a mark against it: he wrote the book on the Trinity after he had joined the New Prophecy, so the same church that took his words would not take him; later writers praised him and called him "a great trial", his followers at Carthage died out by Augustine's day, and he was never counted a saint (E7, I5, I6). Tell it through what people did: Cyprian asking for the book, Novatian writing his own, Vincent weighing him, the last followers handing over their church. Not as a "this matters because" sentence.

**Ending:** close on the person. Either (a) Jerome's last line: he is said to have lived to a great old age; no one records when or how he died (E9; JER 40514–40516; JERL 55634–55635; credit "Jerome says", and "is said" stays); or (b) the opening seen again: the man who asked only not to be condemned unknown is known best, in the end, from his own books (E1, T1; EUS-ed 10267–10268: his own writings and Jerome are the main sources). Recommended: (a), placed after What came, so the story ends on him, not on Augustine or Vincent.

**Who tells it**

Most of his life rests on his own books and on Jerome; say so once, inside a sentence about Jerome, in beat 1 or What came 2 (EUS-ed 10267–10268). His accounts of Praxeas and of the bishops are a hostile witness's: credit "he says", "by his account" (E7, E8).

- **Augustine**, about two centuries later, beside his account of the turn and his followers' end (E6, I5).
- **Lactantius**, about a century later, beside "very obscure" and the Apology (I4).

**Do not say**

- "He was a lawyer" or "an advocate in Rome" as fact. Say "trained in Roman law, Eusebius says", or "who knew the language of the courts". Whether he practised law is not settled (S5, D3).
- "The first Christian to write in Latin." Say "the first great writer of the faith in Latin whose books survive" (D4).
- "He invented the word Trinity" or "he was the first to call God a Trinity." The Greek word is older; say he put the Latin words trinitas, persona, substantia to work, and later Latin writers kept them (T5).
- "The Council of Nicaea used his words." No held source says so; Nicaea's word is Greek.
- "The blood of the martyrs is the seed of the church." His words are "the blood of Christians is seed" (E2).
- "He wrote Against Praxeas before his turn" or "as a loyal son of the church." He wrote it after, and says the Paraclete had taught him better (E7).
- "Praxeas taught that the Father died on the cross" as the narrator's fact. It is Tertullian's charge: "by Tertullian's account" (E7). Do not call Praxeas a bishop or name the bishop of Rome.
- "The church refused to make him a saint," "he was condemned" or "excommunicated." No held source records a decision; say "he was never counted among the saints" and let the later writers' judgments stand (I6).
- "He founded his own church" as fact. Augustine says he broke from the New Prophecy too and gathered his own followers; credit Augustine (E6, I5).
- Why he turned, as fact (envy at Rome, or second marriage). Credit Jerome and Augustine, side by side (D1).
- "Paul of Concordia was Cyprian's secretary." Paul had seen Cyprian's secretary (D2).
- "Montanists" in the narrator's voice for his own side. Say "the New Prophecy"; "Montanists" was the opponents' name.
- "Priest." If his church office comes up at all, it is Jerome's word, "presbyter", an elder; leave it out (E6).
- A year for his death, or "he died a Montanist" (E9).
- "Vincent condemned him." Vincent praised him first and called him a trial; give both (I6).
- "Persona means mask." No held source says it.
- Do not add how he felt at any point, or that he was bitter, proud or lonely; Jerome's "a man of keen and vigorous character" (JER 40497) is the only word on his temper, and it is optional.

**Leave out**

- Against Marcion and Against the Valentinians beyond the two names in Quote 2: the siblings tell them.
- Pliny's ruling in detail, Suetonius's "Chrestianus", Tacitus and the ass's head: siblings.
- "What indeed has Athens to do with Jerusalem?" (Prescription 7): famous, but about philosophy; it would pull beat 3 off its job.
- The soldier's crown (De corona) and the letter to Scapula: the census's documented stories, outside this job.
- The Passion of Perpetua and Felicitas: Coxe's guess that he edited it is an editor's, and her story is her own.
- His church office (presbyter): rests on Jerome alone (E6).
- The jurist Tertullian of the Roman law books (S5): for the card.
- The "Gelasian" decree listing his books as not received: it lists Eusebius and Lactantius too, and its date and author are disputed; heard once it would mislead (I6). For the card.
- Which bishop issued the edict on adultery (Callistus is Monceaux's; disputed): the Hermas story's ground.
- Benson on Cyprian's verbal debts, Kaye on the Athanasian Creed, Sanday on "primarily a lawyer": editors; for the card.
- The Apology's Greek translation and Eusebius's use of it (I7): the Pliny story tells it.
- His views on the soul and God as body (Augustine, AUG 2836–2868): outside the job.
