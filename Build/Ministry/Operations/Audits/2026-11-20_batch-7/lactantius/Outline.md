# Outline: Lactantius

Sheet items (S, E, I, L, D, Q-src) are in `Source_Sheet.md`, which gives the file and line for each.

- **The essence:** Lactantius, a teacher of Latin speech from Africa, was summoned to Nicomedia, Diocletian's capital, and was there when the Great Persecution began in 303 (S2, S3, E2; Documented, his own words and Jerome's). He answered it in the *Divine Institutes*, seven books that argue the faith to educated pagans in polished Latin and from their own poets and philosophers, and hold that religion cannot be forced (E7–E9; Documented, his words). When the persecution was over, *On the Deaths of the Persecutors* told how the emperors who had hunted the Christians died (E13; the book's content Documented; that he wrote it Widely Accepted, D2). Late in life, Jerome says, he taught Constantine's son (E14; Documented as Jerome's account).
- **Also told:**
  - Why he wrote, and how (E6, E7). Two men in the city mocked the faith in print as the church lay in ruins: a philosopher who praised poverty and dined like a lord, and a judge who had pressed for the persecution. He resolved to answer all such writers at once (Q1). His method was deliberate: Tertullian had defended the Christians but was obscure, and Cyprian had answered a pagan with Scripture the pagan thought false. A newcomer, he says, must be fed milk before solid food, and refuted "by his own authorities", the philosophers and historians. Documented, his words.
  - What the *Institutes* say (E8, E9). The gods were once men; the philosophers contradict each other; wisdom and religion cannot be had apart; true worship is a pure mind, not blood or incense; and the world will end after six thousand years and a thousand-year reign. Religion cannot be forced, and must be defended by dying, not killing (Q2). Documented, his words.
  - What *the Persecutors* holds (E1–E4, E12, E13). The inside story of the persecution at Nicomedia, the texts of the edicts of 311 and 313 copied into it, and the deaths of the emperors, each told as God's judgment, down to a persecutor's children aged eight and seven killed by the victor (Documented as the book's text). A modern historian finds it the most exact account of the period, and a partisan one (I2; Monceaux's judgment, credit him by role).
  The sources will not hold: "Diocletian invited him" (Jerome says "summoned … during the reign of Diocletian", naming no one); "he saw the church fall with his own eyes" (he says he was teaching in Bithynia at the time; the book on the persecutors describes the scene); "born in Africa" as fact (Monceaux's reconstruction; Jerome holds that he wrote in Africa as a young man and travelled from Africa); "he wrote the *Institutes* for Constantine" (the address is in some copies only, D4).
- **Length:** about 4 minutes (about 615 words at 150 a minute; 675 is the ceiling). Counted part by part: opening, about 60; essence, about 70; beat 1, about 40; beat 2 (the edict, the killings and the fire's three causes, one clause each), about 65; beat 3 (the two men, Q1 trimmed to one sentence, the method), about 85; beat 4 (what the book says, Q2, leaving Bithynia), about 60; beat 5, about 35; beat 6 (the book, the children, its authorship once), about 60; beat 7, about 30; what came of him, about 80; the ending with Q3, about 30. Inside those: quotes about 70 words, plain sense of the words to explain about 40, credited witnesses (Jerome three times, Eusebius, Constantine, Augustine, a modern historian, a modern editor) about 45. Held at four minutes by the cuts listed under "For the card, not the story". Never cut: the three causes of the fire; Q2; the children; the essence and also told.
- **Setting:**
  - Time: summoned to Nicomedia in Diocletian's reign, about 290 by a modern historian's estimate (S3); the persecution from 23 February 303 (S5); the edicts posted at Nicomedia on 30 April 311 and in June 313 (E12); *the Persecutors* about 314 (E13); Crispus made Caesar in 317 (E14). The writer uses years only where the story needs them.
  - Place: from Africa, a pupil of Arnobius, who taught at Sicca (S2). Nicomedia, in Bithynia: "Diocletian's eastern capital", a Greek-speaking city, where Diocletian built to rival Rome (S3). Later Gaul (E14). Say "Gaul", never Trier.
  - Tradition: told within the Latin Apologists, "the case made in Latin": the Christians who argued for the faith in Latin (S5). He is the last of that line in the census.
- **Words to explain** (the writer works the gloss in where the word is first heard, or uses the plain word):
  - **rhetoric**: "the art of public speaking"; here taught in Latin, in a Greek city (S3).  - **the Great Persecution**: "the persecution of the Christians that Diocletian and Galerius began at Nicomedia in 303" (S5, E2).
  - **edict**: "an emperor's public order".
  - **Institutes**: his own pun: lawyers wrote "Institutes", handbooks of the civil law; his are "divine Institutes", a handbook of true religion (AN 569). Say "the *Divine Institutes*, a handbook of the true religion".
  - **Sibyls**: "pagan prophetesses whose verses the Romans revered" (E8; AN 692).
  - **Caesar** (of Crispus): "junior emperor".
  - **Cicero**: "Rome's great orator" (the census `legacy` glosses Jerome's 'Tully' as Cicero).  - **the Holy Spirit** (in Jerome's charge): known; no gloss.
  - **Bithynia**: "the province around Nicomedia".
- **Shape:** Teacher or thinker. Trap: a list of books. Show the one idea, that the faith must be argued to the learned in their own terms and can never be forced, through the morning the church fell, the two men who mocked it, and his own words. The second book is shown through what it tells, not named in a list.
- **Sibling:**
  - `constantine-the-great` (live, read): it tells the march on Rome, Eusebius's vision, and that "Lactantius, who wrote in Constantine's own day, knows only a dream that told him to mark the sign of Christ on his soldiers' shields"; it also tells the meeting at Milan in paraphrase. Here the dream is not told (cut for length, for the card), and Milan is told only as an edict posted at Nicomedia and copied into his book. None of its quotes is reused.
  - `tertullian-s-voice`, `cyprian-of-carthage` (batch 7, outlines not yet written when this was researched; their old census texts do not count): here each is a clause, as a writer Lactantius read and judged (E7). Nothing from Tertullian or Cyprian is quoted here.
  - `jerome` (live, read): it tells Jerome's own life and does not mention Lactantius. Here Jerome is the witness for the call to Nicomedia, the pupil Crispus and the later verdict.
  - `latin-apologists` (tradition, live, read): it ends its line with Lactantius in two sentences and says Constantine "brought him to Trier". Here: Gaul, as Jerome says.
  - `hilary-of-poitiers` (live): it already tells Augustine's "out of Egypt … loaded with gold" list, which names Lactantius. Not used here.
- **Openings:**
  1. **Chosen.** The church at dawn (E2, AN 10161). At first light on 23 February 303, officials forced the doors of the church at Nicomedia, burned the Scriptures they found and looted the rest. From the palace, in full view of the church, Diocletian and his junior emperor Galerius watched and argued over whether to set it on fire; Diocletian feared the city would burn too, so guards with axes levelled it in a few hours. Lactantius, a teacher of Latin speech from Africa, was teaching in Bithynia at the time. Narrator's paraphrase, not a quote; credit the scene to "his own account" in a clause. Leads straight into the essence.
  2. The governor's triumph (E5, AN 4899). "I saw in Bithynia," Lactantius wrote, a governor as overjoyed as if he had conquered a barbarian nation, because one Christian who had held out for two years seemed at last to give way. Not chosen: it falls two years into the persecution, and the dawn scene sets both the place and the start.

**Story beats** (the opening and the essence are the first minute, about 150 words)

1. **From Africa to a Greek city** (S2, S3, S4, S6). Jerome says he was a pupil of a rhetorician in Africa and was summoned in Diocletian's reign to teach rhetoric in Latin; but Nicomedia was a Greek city, pupils were few, and he turned to writing. When he became a Christian is not known; he was one by 303 (Widely Accepted; D1). Then the essence.
2. **The persecution begins** (E1, E3, E4, D3, E5). Not the church again (it is the opening). The next day an edict stripped Christians of rank and laid them open to torture. Then fire broke out in the palace: Lactantius blames agents of Galerius; Eusebius, a contemporary, says he does not know how it began; Constantine, who was in Nicomedia, later said lightning (one clause each; D3). Priests and household servants were killed, some burned in crowds, some drowned with millstones round their necks. Documented as each witness's account.
3. **Two men who mocked, and his answer** (E6, E7). While the church lay in ruins, a philosopher who preached poverty but dined richly and a judge who had urged the persecution read out books against the Christians in his presence, to his grief (narrate the first sentence of Q-src 1). Then Q1. His method: Tertullian had been obscure; Cyprian answered a pagan with Scripture the pagan thought false. A newcomer, Lactantius says, must have milk before solid food, and be refuted "by his own authorities". Documented, his words. The judge's name: leave him by role (the identification is the editors', E6).
4. **The *Divine Institutes*** (E8, E9, E10, E11). Seven books, written during the persecution (the dates, about 303 to before 311, are a modern reconstruction). Told as substance, in one sentence: the gods were men once honored, the philosophers disagree, wisdom and religion belong together, true worship offers "the mind itself" as the sacrifice, and the world will end after six thousand years. He argues from Cicero, Virgil and the Sibyls (Virgil: AN 1015), by design (beat 3). Then the heart: religion "cannot be imposed by force", and Q2. Meanwhile he left Bithynia, about 305 by modern reckoning (Dominant Modern Reconstruction).
5. **The persecutors fall** (E12). Galerius, dying, as Lactantius tells it, of a rotting disease, ordered in 311 that Christians might "again be Christians", and the order was posted at Nicomedia. Two years later Constantine's fellow emperor had the edict of Milan posted there, giving the Christians back their churches; Lactantius copied its text into his book (the wording of Milan's freedom is the sibling's: do not paraphrase it here). Documented as his account.
6. ***On the Deaths of the Persecutors*** (E13, D2). Written to a friend tortured nine times, about 314, so that "all who shall arise hereafter" might learn how God had dealt with the emperors who persecuted. It runs from Nero to Galerius's worm-eaten body and Diocletian dying in grief, and ends with the victor killing the persecutors' families, a boy of eight and a girl of seven among them: "all the impious received according to the deeds that they had done" (narrate; tell the children plainly, as his verdict, not the narrator's). It survives in a single manuscript, and most scholars now hold that he wrote it, though some have doubted it (Widely Accepted; say once).
7. **Gaul and Crispus** (E14, D5). In extreme old age, Jerome says, he taught Crispus, Constantine's son, in Gaul; Crispus was made Caesar in 317, and was later put to death by his own father (Jerome; Documented as his account). How and when Lactantius died is not known.

**What came of him** (about a third of the story; heaviest first)

1. **The *Institutes* read, praised and faulted** (I1). Jerome admired his eloquence like Cicero's but wished he had been as ready to teach Christian doctrine as to pull down pagan; he charged that Lactantius denied the Holy Spirit was a person, yet asked who could stop him reading the *Institutes* (narrate; credit Jerome). Augustine took the Sibyl's verses that Lactantius had quoted bit by bit through his argument, and set them out in one series in the *City of God*. Documented as each witness's.
2. **The record of the persecution** (I2). His book on the persecutors keeps the Latin of the edict of Milan, a modern editor notes (McGiffert). A modern historian calls it the most exact and complete account of those years, and also a partisan one: every persecutor a bad emperor, every one punished. Credit the judgment to "a modern historian".
3. **What he defended.** DEFENDED: religion freely chosen, argued with words and defended by dying, not by killing (Q2, E9). One clause, carried by Q2 already; do not add a moral.

- **Quotes, 2 or 3 at most** (each rendered by me from Brandt's Latin or Fotheringham's, and checked against the held English where there is one):
  - **Q1.** "Spurred on by their proud ungodliness, by my awareness of the truth itself, and, as I believe, by God, I took up this task." Trimmed for length from the full rendering ("These men set out their godless writings in my presence, and to my grief. … It was to answer the accusers of righteousness with all the strength of my mind."); the first sentence is narrated in beat 3, the last left out.   - **Q2.** "Religion must be defended not by killing but by dying, not by cruelty but by patient endurance, not by crime but by faithfulness." Source: *Divine Institutes* V.19.22 (Brandt; ANF ch. - **Connection to carry into the story:** Two things passed on. First, the *Institutes* gave later Latin readers a full, reasoned case for the faith written for educated outsiders, which starts from their own poets, philosophers and Sibyls rather than from Scripture, and which says belief can only be won, never forced (E7, E9). Jerome read it and argued with it; Augustine took over its Sibyl texts into the *City of God*; the church later doubted its teaching and still read it (I1; JL 14112–14114, 19203–19211; AU 35552–35555; TE 11465–11476). The listener must be told why quoting pagan authors was a choice, not a weakness: Lactantius says Cyprian failed because he quoted Scripture to a man who held it false (AN 4573–4581). Second, *On the Deaths of the Persecutors* passed on the inside record of the persecution at Nicomedia, with the edicts' texts copied into it; the Latin of the edict of Milan survives in his book (I2; AN 10684–10686; EU-ed 50239–50242). The listener must be told that this record is close and exact but written to prove a thesis, that God destroys persecutors, so its verdicts are his. Carry both through what he and his readers did, never as "this matters because".
- **Ending:**
  1. **Chosen.** Jerome's *Chronicle* (Q3, JC 44982–44986): after what came of him, close on Jerome's verdict on the man, the most eloquent of his day and too poor for necessities, without telling the teaching of Crispus again. No comment after it.
  2. The church rebuilt (E13, AN 9983): his book opens with "the temple of God, overthrown by the hands of the wicked, is built with more glory than before", the opening image seen again. Narrated, not quoted. Not chosen: it is from about 314 and would end before the Gaul beat; use only if beat 7 moves into the impact section.
