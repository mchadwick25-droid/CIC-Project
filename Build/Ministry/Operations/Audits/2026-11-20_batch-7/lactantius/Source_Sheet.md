## 1. Setting

S1. **Name.** Jerome: "Firmianus, … known also as Lactantius" (JE 41033–41034); Latin "Firmianus qui et Lactantius" (TE 11177–11179). Census name "Lactantius" (CEN). Monceaux reads "qui et" as a personal nickname beside the family name, and gives the full name as L. Caecilius Firmianus "qui et Lactantius" from two African inscriptions (MO 24733–24790; his reconstruction, Dominant Modern Reconstruction). The name matters only for D2.

S2. **From Africa.** Jerome: "a disciple of Arnobius"; "We have a Banquet of his which he wrote as a young man in Africa and an Itinerary of a journey from Africa to Nicomedia written in hexameters" (JE 41034–41041). LATER. Documented as Jerome's account. Arnobius taught "rhetoric at Sicca in Africa" (JE ch. 79, TE 11258–11262 for the pupil). Born in Africa: Monceaux, "il y est né presque sûrement" (MO 24813), against an older view that he came from Firmum in Italy, which Fletcher reports (AN-ed 470) and Monceaux rejects (MO 24639–24647). Born in Africa: Dominant Modern Reconstruction. Say "from Africa", which Jerome holds.

S3. **Called to Nicomedia.** Lactantius: "When I was teaching rhetorical learning in Bithynia, having been called thither" (AN 4487); Latin "ego cum in Bithynia oratorias litteras accitus docerem" (DI-L 22774; DI-L3 31632). OWN. Jerome: "during the reign of Diocletian summoned to Nicomedia with Flavius the Grammarian … taught rhetoric there and on account of his lack of pupils (since it was a Greek city) he betook himself to writing" (JE 41035–41038). Documented. Who called him is not said: "accitus" names no one. Nicomedia as Diocletian's capital: "Nicomedia, the capital city of Bithynia, became Diocletian's chief place of residence, and was made by him the Eastern capital of the empire" (EU-ed 43826); Lactantius says Diocletian strove "to equal Nicomedia with the city Rome in magnificence" (AN 10083). Plain words: "rhetoric": "the art of public speaking", here in Latin, in a Greek-speaking city. Monceaux dates his arrival about 290 (MO 24921–24932; Dominant Modern Reconstruction).

S4. **His trade, in his own words.** "in which being long engaged, we trained young men not to virtue, but altogether to cunning wickedness" (AN 556, of oratory). "yet I have never been eloquent, inasmuch as I never even engaged in public speaking" (AN 2487); Latin "eloquens numquam fui, quippe qui forum ne adtigerim quidem" (DI-L 12295–12296): literally "I never so much as set foot in the forum", the law courts. OWN. Documented.

S5. **Time and tradition.** The Great Persecution began at Nicomedia on the festival of Terminus, "in the eighth consulship of Diocletian and seventh of Maximian" (AN 10152–10161); the ANF footnote converts it to "23d of February" (AN-ed 10154); the year 303 is the editors' (EU-ed 43826–43834; MO 28768). Documented. Tradition: told within "The Latin Apologists", informal name "The Case Made in Latin" (CEN `latin-apologists`). Era 1. Plain: "the Christians who made the case for the faith in Latin".

S6. **When he became a Christian: not known.** Schanz: he "trat erst in reiferen Jahren zum Christentum über. Ob noch in Afrika oder schon in Nikomedien, wissen wir nicht" ("turned Christian only in his riper years; whether still in Africa or already in Nicomedia, we do not know", SH 38939–38941). Monceaux: almost surely at Nicomedia, between 290 and 303 (MO 25017–25034). That he was a Christian by 303 rests on his grief at the pagan books (Q-src 1; MO 25031–25034). Christian by 303: Widely Accepted. Place and date: Contested (D1).

## 2. Events (time order)

E1. **The persecution prepared** (*Persecutors* 10–11, AN 10126–10141). Christians in Diocletian's household made "the immortal sign on their foreheads" at a sacrifice and the soothsayers could read nothing; Diocletian ordered his household and soldiers to sacrifice or be scourged or dismissed (AN 10126). That winter Galerius pressed him; Lactantius says Diocletian "long opposed the fury of Galerius", consulted Apollo at Miletus, and "attempted still to observe such moderation as to command the business to be carried through without bloodshed; whereas Galerius would have had all persons burnt alive who refused to sacrifice" (AN 10139–10141). OWN (if his, D2). Documented as his account. The interrupted sacrifice: "Nothing easier than for these to pretend such a difficulty" is Coxe's note (AN-ed 10128), not Lactantius.

E2. **The church torn down, 23 February 303** (*Persecutors* 12, AN 10161). "suddenly, while it was yet hardly light, the prefect, together with chief commanders, tribunes, and officers of the treasury, came to the church in Nicomedia, and the gates having been forced open, they searched everywhere for an image of the Divinity. The books of the Holy Scriptures were found, and they were committed to the flames … That church, situated on rising ground, was within view of the palace; and Diocletian and Galerius stood, as if on a watch-tower, disputing long whether it ought to be set on fire. The sentiment of Diocletian prevailed, who dreaded lest, so great a fire being once kindled, some part of the city might he [be] burnt … Then the Pretorian Guards came in battle array, with axes and other iron instruments, and … they in a few hours levelled that very lofty edifice with the ground." Documented as his account. In the Institutes he says only that he was teaching in Bithynia "at the same time the temple of God was overthrown" (AN 4487; DI-L 22774–22775). OWN. Documented: he was in Bithynia when the church fell. "Saw it with his own eyes" is Monceaux's reading (MO 28768, "il y vit détruire l'église"), Widely Accepted; the Institutes line places him there, not at the window.

E3. **The edict, and the man who tore it down** (*Persecutors* 13, AN 10174). "Next day an edict was published, depriving the Christians of all honours and dignities; ordaining also that, without any distinction of rank or degree, they should be subjected to tortures … A certain person tore down this edict, and cut it in pieces, improperly indeed, but with high spirit, saying in scorn, 'These are the triumphs of Goths and Sarmatians.' Having been instantly seized … he was not only tortured, but burnt alive." Documented. Eusebius, a second witness: "a certain man, not obscure but very highly honored … seized the edict as it was posted openly and publicly, and tore it to pieces" (EU 43834–43837). The two agree. McGiffert's view that the man "deserved death" is the editor's (EU-ed 43844–43846); do not use.

E4. **The palace fires** (*Persecutors* 14–15, AN 10185–10198). Lactantius: Galerius "employed private emissaries to set the palace on fire … the blame was laid on the Christians"; a second fire fifteen days later; Diocletian "sat on his tribunal, and saw innocent men tormented by fire"; then "Presbyters and other officers of the Church were seized … and together with their families led to execution … servants, having millstones tied about their necks, were cast into the sea"; altars set by the judges' seats "that every litigant might offer incense before his cause could be heard" (AN 10198). Documented as his account. The fire's cause is disputed (D3).

E5. **What he saw in Bithynia** (*Institutes* V.11). "I saw in Bithynia the præfect wonderfully elated with joy, as though he had subdued some nation of barbarians, because one who had resisted for two years with great spirit appeared at length to yield" (AN 4899); Latin "uidi ego in Bithynia praesidem gaudio mirabiliter elatum tamquam barbarorum gentem aliquam subegisset, quod unus qui per biennium magna uirtute restiterat, postremo cedere uisus esset" (DI-L 24576–24579). OWN, eyewitness. Documented. Also: "for I myself have heard some boasting that their administration has been in this respect without bloodshed" (AN 4899); and a man "in Phrygia, who burnt a whole assembly of people, together with their place of meeting" (AN 4897; not said to be seen). Schanz dates the two years to 305/306 (SH 39050–39054).

E6. **The two writers who mocked** (*Institutes* V.2). After the church fell "there were living at the same place two men who insulted the truth as it lay prostrate and overthrown" (AN 4487). One "professed himself the high priest of philosophy", praised poverty yet "dined less sumptuously in a palace than at his own house", and "vomited forth three books against the Christian religion and name" (AN 4487–4497). Brandt: name unknown; Baronius's guess of Porphyry refuted (DI-L 22781–22783). The other "was then of the number of the judges, and who was especially the adviser of enacting persecution", and wrote two books "to the Christians", attacking Peter and Paul as liars (AN 4503–4513). *Persecutors* 16 names "Hierocles, who from a deputy became president of Bithynia, the author and adviser of the persecution" (AN 10223). That V.2's judge is Hierocles is the editors' identification (AN-ed 4505; MO 25703–25704), Widely Accepted.

E7. **Why he wrote: answering in their own terms** (*Institutes* V.4, I.1). Q-src 1 (V.4.1). Then: "Although Tertullian fully pleaded the same cause in that treatise which is entitled the Apology, yet, inasmuch as it is one thing to answer accusers … and another thing to instruct, which we do … I have not shrunk from this labour" (AN 4569). Cyprian answered Demetrianus with Scripture, "which he plainly considered vain, fictitious, and false"; he should have been answered "by arguments and reason", as an infant is fed "liquid and soft milk" before solid food, "with human testimonies—that is, of philosophers and historians—in order that he might especially be refuted by his own authorities" (AN 4573–4581). In V.1: Tertullian "skilled in literature of every kind; but in eloquence he had little readiness, and was not sufficiently polished, and very obscure"; Cyprian "above all others distinguished", yet "accustomed to be derided by the learned men of this age", one of whom called him "Coprianus" (AN 4472–4476). The learned despise Scripture "because the prophets spoke in common and simple language", so truth needs eloquent defenders (AN 4460–4468). In I.1: "that both the learned may be directed to true wisdom, and the unlearned to true religion" (AN 556); "neither is any religion to be undertaken without wisdom, nor any wisdom to be approved of without religion" (AN 584). OWN. Documented.

E8. **What the Institutes say** (seven books, AN 538–8478). Book I: the false worship of the gods; ch. 15 "how they who were men obtained the name of gods" (AN 1007); ch. 6, "the sibyls and their predictions" (AN 692). Books II–III: the origin of error, "the false wisdom of philosophers", who disagree among themselves (AN 575, 2227). Book IV: true wisdom and religion, Christ. Book V: justice; persecution; Q-src 2; true religion "has the mind itself for a sacrifice" (Book V, ANF ch. XX, AN 5162). Book VI: true worship (AN 5254). Book VII: the happy life and the end of the world, six thousand years and then a thousand-year reign (AN 6648–6652). OWN. Documented. Jerome counts him with those who held the thousand-year reign of Christ on earth (JE 39880–39885). Monceaux: his Bible quotations come almost all from Cyprian's *Testimonies* (MO 24808–24810; his finding, Widely Accepted).

E9. **Religion cannot be forced** (*Institutes* V.19–20). Q-src 2. Also "There is no occasion for violence and injury, for religion cannot be imposed by force; the matter must be carried on by words rather than by blows, that the will may be affected" (AN 5140); Latin "non est opus ui et iniuria, quia religio cogi non potest, uerbis potius quam uerberibus res agenda est, ut sit uoluntas" (DI-L 26133–26135; DI-L2 19033). And "for the religion of God is increased the more it is oppressed" (AN 5136). Brandt's apparatus sets beside it Tertullian, *To Scapula* 2, "sed nec religionis est cogere religionem" (DI-L 26140–26141): the editor's parallel, not a citation by Lactantius. OWN. Documented.

E10. **Leaving Bithynia, and need.** The Institutes speak of Bithynia in the past tense (S3, E5); Schanz: "305/6 scheint er Bithynien verlassen zu haben" (SH 38944–38945); Monceaux: probably late 305 or 306, place unknown (MO 25164–25169). Dominant Modern Reconstruction. Galerius's rule: "eloquence was extinguished, pleaders cut off, and the learned in the laws either exiled or slain. Useful letters came to be viewed in the same light as magical and forbidden arts" (AN 10313). Documented as his account. *On the Workmanship of God* opens: "How disturbed I am, and in the greatest necessities, you will be able to judge from this little book" (AN 9310). OWN. Monceaux links his poverty to the closing of the schools (MO 25160–25163; his inference).

E11. **The address to Constantine** (*Institutes* I.1). "And we now commence this work under the auspices of your name, O mighty Emperor Constantine, who were the first of the Roman princes to repudiate errors, and to acknowledge and honour the majesty of the one and only true God" (AN 571). Present only in some manuscripts: Brandt prints it in the apparatus, "in RSg" (DI-L 285–297); the ANF note says "These words are omitted in some editions" (AN-ed 4432, of the address in V.1). Contested (D4).

E12. **The persecutors' end, 311–313** (*Persecutors* 33–35, 42, 48). Galerius's disease, "the stench was so foul as to pervade not only the palace, but even the whole city" (AN 10462); near his end "he cried aloud … that he would re-edify the Church which he had demolished" (AN 10471), and published an edict permitting Christians "again to be Christians" (AN 10486), posted "at Nicomedia on the day preceding the kalends of May" (AN 10501; 30 April, editor's note). Donatus "set at liberty from a jail, which had been your residence for six years" (AN 10505). Diocletian, his statues thrown down by Constantine's order, "worn out with anguish of mind, expired" (AN 10590). Licinius had the edict of Milan posted at Nicomedia "on the ides of June" (AN 10680–10682): "that it was proper that the Christians and all others should have liberty to follow that mode of religion which to each of them appeared best" (AN 10684). "And thus, from the overthrow of the Church until its restoration, there was a space of ten years and about four months" (AN 10690). The dream before the Milvian Bridge (AN 10620): the sibling's (Outline, Sibling).

E13. **On the Deaths of the Persecutors** (AN 9967–10745). Addressed to Donatus, tortured "nine times" under the governors of Bithynia (AN 10223). Opens: "the temple of God, overthrown by the hands of the wicked, is built with more glory than before … For God delayed to punish them, that, by great and marvellous examples, He might teach posterity that He alone is God" (AN 9983); "Of the end of those men I have thought good to publish a narrative, that all who are afar off, and all who shall arise hereafter, may learn" (AN 9987). It runs from Nero (AN 10004) to Licinius's killing of the persecutors' families: Valeria, Candidianus, Severianus, "Maximus, the son of Daia, a boy eight years old, and a daughter of Daia, who was seven years old … So, by the unerring and just judgment of God, all the impious received according to the deeds that they had done" (AN 10712); Valeria and Prisca beheaded at Thessalonica, "a fall from grandeur which moved the pity of the multitude" (AN 10723). Its close: "I relate all those things on the authority of well-informed persons; and I thought it proper to commit them to writing exactly as they happened, lest the memory of events so important should perish" (AN 10736). Documented as the book's text. Date c. 314–315 (MO 26035–26050; Dominant Modern Reconstruction). One manuscript survives, Paris lat. 2627 (MO 24675–24678), and it names the author "L. Cecilius" (MO 28684).

E14. **Teacher of Crispus.** Jerome: "In his extreme old age he was tutor to Crispus Cæsar a son of Constantine in Gaul, the same one who was afterwards put to death by his father" (JE 41051–41054); Latin "hic extrema senectute magister Caesaris Crispi filii Constantini in Gallia fuit, qui postea a patre interfectus est" (TE 11224–11226). Chronicle, under the year Crispus was made Caesar: Q-src 3. Crispus "crudelissime interficitur" ("is most cruelly put to death", JC 45054–45056). LATER. Documented as Jerome's account. Crispus made Caesar 1 March 317 (MO 25192–25193); the teaching from about 316–317 (MO 25229–25232; SH 38952, 39040; Dominant Modern Reconstruction); Crispus killed 326 (MO 25233–25235). Fletcher: "He is believed to have died at Trèves about a.d. 325" (AN-ed 476): an editor's "believed". Monceaux: "On ne sait absolument rien sur les dernières années de Lactance, ni sur la date de sa mort" (MO 25248–25249). His death: not held.

## 3. Impact (ranked, heaviest first: what later witnesses carried on and used)

I1. **The Institutes read as the Latin case for the faith, praised and faulted.** Jerome: "Lactantius has a flow of eloquence worthy of Tully: would that he had been as ready to teach our doctrines as he was to pull down those of others!" (JL 14112–14114; already in the census `legacy` and `floorNote` fields, so narrate, do not quote). "If you read any of these you will find in them an epitome of Cicero's dialogues" (JL 16719–16721). Jerome's charge: "Lactantius in his books and particularly in his letters to Demetrian altogether denies the subsistence of the Holy Spirit … But who can forbid me to read his Institutes—in which he has written against the Gentiles with much ability" (JL 19203–19211). Augustine took over his Sibyl quotations into the *City of God*: "Lactantius made use of these sibylline testimonies, introducing them bit by bit … and we have set them down in one connected series" (AU 35552–35555). Augustine also names him among those who came out of Egypt "loaded" with its gold (AU 51717–51722): already told in `hilary-of-poitiers`, so a clause at most. The list later credited to Gelasius sets "Opuscula Lactantii apocrypha", his little works among the books not received (TE 11465–11476; its ascription to Gelasius disputed, TE 11489–11492). Monceaux: rarely named before the late 300s, but "beaucoup lu au Moyen-Age et à la Renaissance" (MO 29870–29878); his direct influence on his own time "presque nulle" (MO 29893). Calvin, a thousand years on: "to conclude with Lactantius … 'No religion is genuine that is not in accordance with truth'" (CA 7254–7256); "let Lactantius and Eusebius be read on this subject" (CA 10123). The name "the Christian Cicero" is the editors' (AN-ed 448, 512). Documented as each witness's.

I2. **On the Deaths of the Persecutors as the eyewitness record of the persecution.** It keeps the texts of Galerius's edict (AN 10482–10490) and of Milan (AN 10684–10686). McGiffert: "The greater part of the edict … is extant in its original Latin form in Lactantius' De mort. pers. chap. 48. The Greek translation is still less accurate" (EU-ed 50239–50242; Eusebius gives the Greek, EU 50226–50230). Monceaux: "le pamphlet a pour base le récit le plus exact et le plus complet que nous possédions sur cette période" (MO 29275–29276), confirmed again and again by coins (MO 29258–29266); "Mais l'ouvrage, comme tout pamphlet, n'en est pas moins très partial": every persecutor a bad prince, and every one punished (MO 29277–29282), and his portraits "de vraies caricatures" (MO 29320–29324). The census sibling uses its dream (CEN `constantine-the-great`). Documented as the book's text; the judgments Monceaux's and McGiffert's. Latin of ch. 48: Mirbt 4790–4830.

I3. **DEFENDED: religion freely chosen, defended by dying, not by killing** (Q-src 2, E9). Documented, his words.

## 5. Where the sources differ, or come late

D1. **His conversion.** Schanz: not known whether in Africa or Nicomedia (SH 38939–38941). Monceaux: almost surely at Nicomedia, 290–303 (MO 25017–25034). Fletcher guessed a link between his poverty and conversion; Coxe's note: "I see no force in this suggestion" (AN-ed 474–476). Say: "when he became a Christian is not known."

D2. **Who wrote *On the Deaths of the Persecutors*.** The single manuscript names "L. Cecilius" (MO 28684); Jerome lists a book "On persecution" (JE 41047; TE 11193). Monceaux, after weighing the dispute, judges it more likely his ("Tout bien pesé, nous jugeons plus vraisemblable la thèse de l'authenticité", MO 28624–28625), and says scholars incline more and more that way (MO 28738–28744); Schanz lists it as Jerome's "De persecutione", "deren Echtheit allerdings bestritten ist" (SH 39060–39062), and argues for his authorship (SH 40249–40250, damaged OCR: two columns run together; Monceaux confirms the same position). Widely Accepted; say once that it is "almost certainly his".

D3. **The palace fire.** Lactantius: set by Galerius's agents (AN 10185). Eusebius: "a conflagration having broken out in those very days in the palace at Nicomedia, I know not how, which through a false suspicion was laid to our people" (EU 43982–43984). Constantine, years later: "Nicomedia attests the fact; eyewitnesses, of whom I myself am one, declare it. The palace, and the emperor's private chamber were destroyed, consumed by lightning" (EU 70323–70325). McGiffert, an editor, thinks the case against the Christians "pretty strong" (EU-ed 43999–44001). Three held witnesses, three causes. If told, credit each; the narrator settles none. That the *Oration*'s lightning is the 303 fire is McGiffert's link (EU-ed 43992–43994).

D4. **The address to Constantine.** Brandt (1890) set the addresses aside as later additions (DI-L 285–297); Monceaux (1905) calls them a forger's work, "apocryphes" (MO 25825–25852); Schanz (1922) reports that Brandt later gave up that view (1903) and holds them Lactantius's own, from a second edition (SH 39678–39696). Contested. Say: "some copies open with an address to Constantine; whether Lactantius wrote it is disputed."

D5. **When he taught Crispus, and Crispus's age.** Monceaux: about 316, Crispus born about 300 (MO 25187–25190, 25229–25232); Schanz: called to Gaul about 317, Crispus born about 307 (SH 39040, 39054). Both: late, after 315. Say "late in life, in Gaul, Jerome says", no year.

D6. **Late or editorial claims not to carry.** "Trier" or "Trèves" (Fletcher's "believed", AN-ed 476; the census tradition text says Trier, but Jerome says only "in Gaul"). "Died 325" (NPNF gloss, JE 41033). Born at Firmum (AN-ed 470). "Christian Cicero" as an ancient title (editors' name). Monceaux's character sketch, a "timid Christian" without vocation for martyrdom (MO 25075, 25253): his judgment.
