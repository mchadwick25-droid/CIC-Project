## worlds-tree/donatism/docstory-don.story.bagai-reconciliation
### Revised text
In 393 a deacon named Maximian of Carthage offended his bishop, Primian, the Donatist primate there. Primian cut him off from communion. Maximian went to the nearby bishops and accused Primian before them. They came to Carthage and called Primian to appear. He refused, and they condemned him in his absence. At Cebarsussi they then made Maximian rival primate. Augustine saw in it the act the Donatist founders had once taken against Caecilian: the same call to appear, the same refusal, the same sentence in absence.

The answer came the next year at Bagai. Three hundred and ten Donatist bishops met there and condemned the followers of Maximian in the harshest terms they had. Augustine quotes the decree word for word in his books against the Donatists. It calls them shipwrecked men, dashed by the waves of truth onto the sharp rocks. Their bodies cover the shore, it says, and they cannot even find a grave.

Two or three years later, after the Maximianists had suffered under Optatus Gildonianus, the Donatist party took back two of those condemned bishops, Felicianus of Musti and Praetextatus of Assuris. Augustine says they had first tried to drive the two out of their churches on a proconsul's order, and had failed. Neither man was baptized again or ordained again.

The Donatists taught that baptism given outside their church counted for nothing. Yet these two had baptized while cut off from that church, and the Donatists did not repeat those baptisms. Augustine made this the center of his case. If those baptisms stood, he argued, then the Donatist rule failed even for the Donatists themselves.

## worlds-tree/post-apostolic-house-church/legacy
### Revised text
The question of who held authority in the churches did not stay open forever. By around 200 CE, one bishop had become the common pattern almost everywhere, including in Rome. Rome had once been led by a council of elders. No text says how or when that change was actually felt. All that can be seen is that the older way quietly dropped out of the argument.

The texts of these churches did not survive evenly. Ignatius argued for the single bishop, and Polycarp sent the letters of Ignatius he had to the church at Philippi when it asked for them. First Clement was copied into a great Bible manuscript. The Shepherd of Hermas was once read as scripture by three major later teachers. In 367 Athanasius, bishop of Alexandria, wrote that it stood outside the Bible, among the books the Fathers had appointed for new converts to read. The Didache, a teaching manual from one of these communities, disappeared from use for roughly eighteen centuries. What survived best was not always what mattered most at the time.

- "Some scholars still doubt them.": cut | Modern opinion removed; nothing from the old sources replaces it. | source: none: cut

## worlds-tree/donatism/docstory-don.story.acta-purgationis-felicis
### Revised text
At the center of the case lay a letter to Felix, the bishop of Aptungi who had made Caecilian bishop of Carthage. The Donatists said Felix had handed over the scriptures in the persecution. If that was true, they held, he had no right to make anyone a bishop, and Caecilian's office was void from its first day. The letter's closing lines seemed to prove it. They said Felix had told the magistrate to take his books and codices, and that all were then carried off and burned.

The emperor Constantine ordered the charge looked into, and the proconsul Aelianus heard named witnesses on the record. Their words survive in the dossier of documents kept by Optatus, an opponent of the Donatists. The letter came from a magistrate, Caecilianus. He told the court he had never dictated its closing lines.

Then a town official named Ingentius was questioned. Under the threat of torture, he broke. He admitted that he had added to the letter himself, out of grief over Maurus, his guest. A counsel named Apronianus charged that Ingentius had traveled through Numidia and Mauritania as the Donatist party's agent. Ingentius denied that he had been to Numidia.

The proconsul ruled that nothing had been proved against Felix, and he cleared him.
