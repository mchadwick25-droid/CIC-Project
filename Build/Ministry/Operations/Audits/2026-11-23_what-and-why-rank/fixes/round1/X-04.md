## tree/free-spirit-heresy
### Revised text
In the early fourteenth century the church declared that a sect existed. The decree Ad nostrum, issued at the Council of Vienne in 1312, listed eight errors said to be held by beguines and beghards in Germany. Their core claim was that a soul made perfect in this life can no longer sin. One error spoke of people living 'in the spirit of liberty'. At Erfurt in 1367, an inquisitor put the decree's articles to a beghard one by one, and his answers repeated its words.

A later papal letter shows the other side. Inquisitors had told the pope that heresy hid under the beghards' habit, and that obstinate offenders were burned almost every year. Then the pope heard of poor men and women in Mainz and Magdeburg. They lived in houses the faithful had given them, men apart from women, devoted to prayer. They were troubled, the letter says, because of a name the common people had put on them. The pope let them live together, beg, and wear their long gray habit, as long as they did nothing against the faith.

## worlds-tree/the-reformed-cities-zurich-and-geneva/docstory-rzg.story.calvins-journey-to-zurich
### Revised text
People who respected both churches, Zurich's and Geneva's, had begun to worry. By 1549 they feared that Calvin's teaching on the Supper differed from what Zurich taught. Calvin wrote that it seemed to differ "in some respect, I scarcely know what." Zurich's pastors named what many thought divided them: Christ's bodily presence, the meaning of the words of the Supper, the eating of his body, and what the Supper does.

Calvin thought a friendly conference was the best way to clear up the worry. He went to Zurich himself, with his colleague William Farel.

The two men met with Zurich's pastors, and they set their agreement down in twenty-six short articles. This was the Consensus Tigurinus.

The articles place Christ's body in heaven and reject any idea of him enclosed in the bread. They take "This is my body" figuratively. Christ feeds the soul through faith, by the Holy Spirit.

Calvin's letter afterward calls it a faithful record of the conference. It adds that every colleague serving Christ under Geneva's jurisdiction had signed it to show their approval.

## worlds-tree/syriac-edessa-nisibis/docstory-syr.story.qyama-morning
### Revised text
Aphrahat wrote in Syriac inside the Persian empire in the 330s and 340s. He speaks of people who "choose to become solitaries, sons of a covenant." Before baptism, he says, preachers warned them. Anyone who "sets his heart upon" marriage should marry first. Whoever took up the struggle was derided if he turned back.

Sozomen, a Greek church historian, says Ephrem came from Nisibis or its neighborhood. Harmonius, the son of Bardaisan, had set some of his father's errors to music for the choirs. The Syrians were charmed by the melodies. Ephrem feared they would take in the same opinions, so he wrote poems on the same measures that taught the church's doctrine. From then on, Sozomen says, the Syrians sang Ephrem's odes.

The Doctrine of Addai is Edessa's own telling of its first days. It says the people gathered day by day for "the reading of the Old and New Testament, of the Diatessaron," and were "constant in the vigils of the Church." Eusebius says Tatian made the Diatessaron, "a certain combination and collection of the Gospels."
