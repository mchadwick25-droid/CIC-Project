## tree/catholic-church-ancien-regime
### Revised text
This was the Catholic Church at the height of its establishment and on the edge of its worst crisis: enormously wealthy, woven into the monarchies of Europe, and increasingly unable to control them. Rome's condemnation of Jansenism in the bull Unigenitus (1713) turned into a fifty-year political crisis in France, with parlements and bishops fighting over it. Then the kings of Portugal, France, Spain and Sicily drove the Jesuits from their lands. Pope Clement XIV's bull of 1773 says why. It names quarrels and riots, charges that Jesuit teaching went against the faith and sound morals, and a charge of greed for property. The kings, it says, acted to stop Christians from rising against one another. They pressed the pope until he abolished the Society. Then came the Revolution: church property nationalized, clergy required to swear to a Civil Constitution that split them into jurors and refractories, thousands killed or driven abroad, and Pope Pius VI carried off to die a French prisoner in 1799. His successor signed a concordat with Napoleon in 1801. In 1809 Napoleon annexed the Papal States. His decree said that joining spiritual power to rule over land had caused quarrels. It said his proposals to square the popes' claims with the safety of his armies had failed. The pope was held captive until 1814. He outlived Napoleon, and in 1814 he restored the Jesuits.

Rome ran its missions through Propaganda, the congregation for spreading the faith. Its collected decrees show three acts in these years.

In 1656 the Holy Office ruled on a set of questions. Missionaries in China had sent them to Propaganda in 1645. Christian scholars could join the ceremony in the hall of Confucius when they took a degree. That cult, it said, seemed merely civil and political. Converts could also keep the usual rites for their dead, once anything superstitious was removed.

In 1659 Propaganda sent orders to its vicars apostolic. They were not to urge any people to change its rites, customs and ways. What is more absurd, it asked, than to carry France, Spain or Italy into China (in a plain English rendering)?

In 1726 Propaganda took up a doubt. Did native priests of China, Tonkin and Cochinchina have to swear the oath set by the constitution Ex illa die? The text seemed to name only European missionaries. The answer was yes, all of them. The oath bound a priest to obey the pope's order on the Chinese rites exactly, and without evasion.

## tree/japans-hidden-christians
### Revised text
In 1614 Japan banned Christianity, and the entry before this one tells how it began. That year Valentin Carvalho, a Jesuit who governed the Japanese church after its bishop died, wrote to the pope to say why. Enemies of the faith, he writes, told the ruler that Christians honored men who defied their lords. The ruler called the Christian law diabolical and ordered every missionary out of Japan. Carvalho adds that the ruler and many nobles also believed the missionaries preached in order to seize the kingdom.

The government also had people tread on the cross. This was the e-fumi, the treading of the image. In September 1865 village chiefs told Father Petitjean that in many villages the cross had not been trampled for ages. They said Christian officers had often put images of the Buddha under foot in place of Christ and the Virgin.

On Easter Tuesday Domingo, the baptizer of Urakami, told Petitjean how the faith was kept. Each day the families prayed together. The father led the prayer for a week, then the mother, then each child in turn. When someone lay dying, the family gathered round to pray. Domingo said his son, a young man of twenty-five, would take his place as baptizer.

In 1865 the missionaries studied the papers the villagers had kept.

They included prayer books, written baptism formulas and Christian calendars.

One treatise, Contrition, was dated 1603 and written wholly in Japanese. The baptizer Domingo brought it to the missionaries. It had escaped a search at Urakami eight years before. Then twenty-eight Christians were jailed and most of their books were burned. On 17 March 1865, at about half past twelve, twelve to fifteen people stood at the door of the church of the Twenty-Six Martyrs in Nagasaki.

Three women knelt beside Father Petitjean. One said, with her hand on her heart, that the hearts of all of them were the same as his. Then she asked, "Sancta Maria no gozowa doko?", "Where is the image of Holy Mary?" (our rendering). That spring Petitjean wrote to the Paris seminary that 3,800 Christians had been found around Nagasaki.

Two fathers from the mountains had told him, "In our mountains we are about fifteen hundred Christians" (our rendering).

## tree/moravian-church-after-1815
### Revised text
The Moravian Church did not stop in 1815. Its mission fields went on, and new ones opened.

In Jamaica the church ran twenty-six schools by March 1834. On 1 August 1834 a law of Parliament ended slavery throughout the British colonies. An apprenticeship came first. The missionary James Phillippo, who lived on the island, wrote that the day was 'welcomed and celebrated throughout the island with high and holy joy'. By the end of 1835 the church had 8,521 members on the island. That was 1,339 more than a year before.

At the Cape the Moravian mission began again in 1792. It had 2,386 members when slavery was ended there on 1 December 1834. The slaves of the colony were fully free on 1 December 1838. In the year after that, Genadendal alone took in four hundred and fifty people. The church weighed each person's case on its own merits before taking them in. The sources do not say what the test was.

In Suriname slavery ended by royal proclamation on 1 July 1863. By 1869 the church counted 24,330 members in the colony.

In Labrador the winter of 1836 to 1837 brought a famine. The church at Hebron was dedicated in October 1837.

In the early 1890s four missionaries went to the country north of Lake Nyasa, and they founded Rungwe among the Konde people. On 7 February 1897 a woman named Fiabarema stood up after a farewell sermon at Rungwe. She said, 'I have risen to say that I belong to God.' She was baptized that evening. Within nine years the church manned four stations there and counted more than one hundred converts.
