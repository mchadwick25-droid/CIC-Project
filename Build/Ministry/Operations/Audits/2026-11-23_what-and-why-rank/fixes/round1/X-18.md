## tree/tacitus
### Revised text
When daylight ended in Nero's gardens, people were burned to light the dark. Others, covered in animal skins, were torn apart by dogs or nailed to crosses, while Nero, dressed as a charioteer, mixed with the crowd. The Roman who tells it, the historian Tacitus, thought they were criminals.

Writing about fifty years after the great fire of Rome in 64, Tacitus gives the fullest account outside the church of what Nero did to the Christians. In it he names their founder, Christus, put to death under Pilate. He despised them and said so.

After the fire, no prayers or offerings to the gods could stop the belief that it had been set on an order. To kill the rumor, Nero put the guilt on people the crowd called Christians, hated, he says, for their shameful acts, and he had them tortured.

Tacitus stops to explain the name, and gives Christ in its Latin form. "Christus, from whom the name came, was put to death in the reign of Tiberius by one of our governors, Pontius Pilatus." A superstition, he calls it, and a deadly one. It began in Judaea, the Roman province around Jerusalem, and broke out even in Rome, where, he says, everything shameful in the world gathers.

First those who admitted it were seized, and then, on their word, a huge number more. They were convicted, he says, less of setting the city on fire than of hatred of the human race. Their deaths were made a show, as Nero held games at the chariot-racing ground.

Almost everything known of these deaths rests on this one chapter, and Tacitus never says where he learned of Christus.

About the year 200, Tertullian, defending the Christians, answered him by name over another of his histories. Tacitus had written there that the Jews honored an ass's head, a charge Tertullian thought had passed to the Christians. Yet, he wrote, "that same Cornelius Tacitus, who truly talks more lies than anyone," also says a Roman general found no image in the temple at Jerusalem.

About 400, Sulpicius Severus, a Christian writing a history of the world, told the same deaths in the same order. But where Tacitus called them criminals, he called them innocent.

Back in the gardens at night, Tacitus ends with his own judgment. "So even for criminals who deserved to be punished harshly, as an example to all, a feeling of pity arose. For it seemed they were being destroyed not for the public good, but to feed one man's cruelty."

## tree/josephus
### Revised text
In 67 the Romans took Jotapata, a walled town in Galilee in the north, and found its commander, Josephus, hiding in a cave with forty men. The Roman general Vespasian, later emperor, promised him his life. Josephus prayed in secret to God: "I declare it openly: I do not go over to the Romans as a deserter from the Jews, but as a servant sent by you."

He was a priest of the Temple in Jerusalem by birth, who had led the Jewish forces in Galilee when the Jews rose against Rome. He spent the rest of his life at Rome, under the emperors' favor, writing his people's history, which holds a few lines each on John the Baptist, Jesus and James. Christians quoted those lines for centuries as the word of a Jewish historian outside the church, and argued over the passage on Jesus.

Back in the cave at Jotapata, his companions would rather die than surrender, and when they turned their swords on him, he proposed that they kill each other by lot. One by one they bared their necks. He and one other man were left, by chance, he writes, or by God's guiding hand, and he persuaded that man to live. His own books, written partly to answer the charge of treachery, are the only account.

Brought before Vespasian, he foretold that the general would be emperor, as the Roman writer Suetonius also records, and in 69 the new emperor freed him. At the siege of Jerusalem in 70, he called on the defenders to surrender. The Jews wanted him punished, he writes, while the Romans, whenever they lost, blamed his "treachery".

At Rome, about 93, he finished his history of the Jewish people. In it, the ruler Herod kills John the Baptist, "a good man", fearing his hold on the crowds. And of Jesus: "About this time there lived Jesus, a wise man, if it is right to call him a man. For he did astonishing deeds, and taught people who gladly accept the truth." The passage goes on, "He was the Christ", the Messiah the Jews awaited, crucified under the Roman governor Pilate and seen alive on the third day.

Elsewhere he tells of Ananus, a chief priest of the Temple and a Sadducee, a sect he calls harsh in judging offenders. One Roman governor had died and the next had not yet arrived. Ananus called the judges together and charged James, "the brother of Jesus, who was called Christ", and some others, with breaking the law. He had them stoned. Each passage is only a few sentences.

A century and a half later, the scholar Origen cited Josephus on John and James: "And this is the remarkable thing. He did not accept that our Jesus was the Christ. Yet he still bore witness that James was so upright." Origen never cites the passage on Jesus.

Early in the fourth century, Eusebius of Caesarea was the first writer known to quote the passage on Jesus. He used it against forged records of Jesus's trial, because here was a historian who was himself a Hebrew. Eusebius also told the fall of Jerusalem, from Josephus, as God's judgment on the Jews for what they did to Christ. Jerome's Latin, later in the fourth century, reads that Jesus was believed to be the Christ.

Josephus ended the story of his own life on the emperors' favor and the charges he had outlived, and left the verdict to his readers: "let others judge of my character."

## tree/barsauma-of-nisibis
### Revised text
The Church of the East gathered its synods, its councils of bishops, into one book. Its compiler, about three centuries later, left one out on purpose. Barsauma had held it at Beth Lapat, and he himself had said it was held "out of ill will and human passion."

Barsauma was bishop of Nisibis, a city on the border between the Roman and Persian empires, in the late 400s. His church, the church of the Persian empire, had governed itself for sixty years; its synods now fixed its teaching and rules. Barsauma and his side stood for two things: ministers free to marry, and the Greek Bible teacher Theodore of Mopsuestia as the church's sure guide. In 484 he led a synod against the catholicos, the head of his church, and later took it back in writing. Yet his church made both causes its law, while neighboring churches remembered him as the man who set it apart.

At Nisibis he welcomed the teacher Narsai and asked him to found a school, which counts them both as its founders. By his own account, "Satan stirred up a quarrel" between him and the head of the church, Babowai. Babowai meant to judge a group of bishops who had done wrong. They rose against him and came to Barsauma, and Barsauma gave in to their urging. At Beth Lapat, in April 484, he and the bishops on his side wrote out charges against Babowai and made rules on ministers' marriage. The old sources do not say what the charges were. That year the Persian king put Babowai to death. A later historian of Barsauma's own church says Barsauma passed Babowai's letter to the king and then regretted it. Writers of the rival Syriac church say he had him killed. His own letters speak only of the quarrel.

His marriage is told two ways. The same historian says the king later ordered him to take a wife, and he married a nun, a woman vowed to a single life. A rival writer, later still, says instead that he kept a woman and called her his wife. Another charges he made the marriage rule so he would not be blamed for her.

The year after the synod, with Acacius as the new head of the church, Barsauma took back everything done at Beth Lapat, its rules included. In his letter he says he had yielded to those bishops and done things against Christian conduct and the church's order. His letters to Acacius, the nearest witness to his life, show a frontier pressed by drought, raiding tribes and a Roman army. He asked Acacius to cut his city's rebels off from the church, "like the doctor who amputates," and to threaten to report them to the king.

A synod early the next year, which he stayed away from but accepted in advance, confessed Christ fully God and fully human, the two never mixed. It sent wandering men in monks' black, whom it called frauds, back to the monasteries. It barred bishops from stopping their ministers marrying, and expected new assistant ministers to take a wife.

From 491, he and Acacius were cutting each other off from the church. The old sources name no cause. In 497 a synod cancelled the curses on both sides, calling them disputes over church affairs, thrown for human motives.

That synod also let every minister, from the head of the church down, marry one wife openly. It accepted Beth Lapat "but only on this point."

The Armenian bishops refused the rules that Acacius and Barsauma had set up. Writers of the rival Syriac church drew on a letter written from old men's memories about a century and a half later. They said he told the Persian king his Christians would never be loyal until their faith differed from the Romans'. With the king's soldiers, they said, he killed thousands who refused.

More than a century after Beth Lapat, a synod made Theodore's writings required study for every teacher in the church. It quoted Beth Lapat's ruling on Theodore, and called the synod left out of the book "the divine assembly." It named him with the title Mar, which means my lord. To those bishops he was "the blessed Mar Barsauma."
