## docstories/indonesian-christianity-0
### Revised text
Ludwig Nommensen had come to the Silindung valley of the Toba Batak in 1864, and by 1877 the Rhenish mission there had several thousand baptized Bataks and a Christian village, Huta Dame, built for converts cast out by their clans. Silindung lay outside Dutch territory. The Toba highlands were still independent under Sisingamangaraja XII, the priest-king at Bakkara.

In the autumn of 1877 word spread that about forty Acehnese fighters had come to Lake Toba at his invitation. A Dutch mission history of 1901 says hostile plans against the Dutch government were put down to him. In January 1878 his messengers told the markets of Silindung that he was coming with the Acehnese to kill the Europeans and the native Christians. The missionaries decided to call on the Dutch Resident for help. He already knew of the danger and had sent troops from Sibolga.

In February 1878 a Dutch column reached Silindung, and Nommensen and a colleague, Simoneit, went with it as interpreters. The king's villages were burned, Bakkara was destroyed in May, and Sisingamangaraja fled north to fight a guerrilla war for thirty years until he was shot in 1907. The mission's reports to Germany presented the campaign as deliverance; the Dutch annexed Silindung and then the rest of Toba, with mission stations following the garrisons. The Batak church that grew inside that arrangement, the HKBP, became the largest Lutheran body in Asia. Nommensen stayed fifty-four years and is buried among the Batak; the king he helped to destroy is a national hero of Indonesia; the church holds both.

## docstories/churches-of-christ-twentieth-century-1
### Revised text
The Churches of Christ had no structure above the congregation, which was their principle, and the question of the mid-century was whether congregations could nonetheless act together. In 1952 the Highland Church in Abilene, Texas began the Herald of Truth, a national radio and later television program paid for by hundreds of other congregations and administered by Highland's elders. Orphan homes and colleges were likewise being supported from church treasuries. A minority, led by preachers such as Roy Cogdill and Fanning Yater Tant and the journal Gospel Guardian, held that the New Testament showed no pattern for one eldership spending other churches' money or for churches funding human institutions, and that to do so was the first step back toward the missionary societies of the nineteenth century. The Cogdill–Woods debate of 1957 in Birmingham ran six nights. In December 1954 B. C. Goodpasture, editor of the Gospel Advocate, the movement's oldest and most influential paper, wrote that the time had come to 'quarantine' those who were sowing discord, and the word became policy: objecting preachers were no longer invited, colleges did not hire them, and congregations divided, sometimes quietly and sometimes with lawsuits over the building. The congregations that stood apart came to be called non-institutional Churches of Christ, alike in every other respect, and the two bodies had almost no fellowship in the decades that followed. Both sides kept the argument in print, and each said the other had left.

## tree/church-of-the-east-three-patriarchates
### Revised text
The Church of the East began these years with two rival patriarchs. The split went back to 1551. For almost a hundred years the office had passed inside one family, almost like an inheritance. Andreas Masius, a scholar of that century, wrote that the people resented this. When Patriarch Shimon bar Mama died, they feared his nephew would take the see by right of blood. They met and chose a new patriarch by the old rules, a monk named Sulaka. But the nephew, Denkha Shimon, took the see anyway.

Two lines followed. One lived in the mountains of Hakkari. The other lived on the plain, at Alqosh and Mosul.

Each patriarch of the plain held an order from the Sultan, called a firman. It confirmed his power over his people in the empire. The mountain patriarchs had no such order.

Rome then added a third line. Capuchin friars had begun preaching in Diyarbakır, Mosul and other towns of Mesopotamia in 1636. In 1657 Elia, the patriarch of the plain, sent Rome a statement of his faith, through their work. In 1681 Pope Innocent XI gave Joseph, the church's metropolitan at Diyarbakır, the pallium and the title of patriarch. A second Joseph followed in 1695 and a third in 1714.

In 1778 the line of the plain began to turn to Rome. Patriarch Elia died of plague at Alqosh on 29 April. His nephew Hormizd, a metropolitan, wrote the next part himself: “So on the 30th of April I embraced all that the holy Church believes.” Another nephew, Yeshua-yau, was to be patriarch. Hormizd says Yeshua-yau had joined Rome and left it six times before, from love of money, and had joined this time only “hypocritically.” In May 1779 he turned back, and the two became enemies. In 1782 Pope Pius VI let Hormizd act as patriarch, but not use the patriarch's seal.

Hormizd's own account breaks off in 1795, in the middle of a quarrel among Rome's missionaries and his own people. The old sources used here do not say when the plain line fully joined Rome.
