## docstories/persian-church-of-the-east-early-1
### Revised text
Sozomen, following the Syriac Acts, says that Tarbula, Symeon’s sister, a consecrated virgin, was arrested with her sister and her maid when the queen fell ill, on a charge of having caused the illness by sorcery in revenge for the bishop. Sozomen writes that ‘the Jews’ brought the accusation, and that the queen, who had embraced their customs and kept Jewish rites, believed it. Tarbula was reportedly offered her life by a Magian judge who desired her, and refused. The three were then executed in a manner meant to work as a counter-charm: their bodies were sawn in half and the halves hung on stakes on either side of a road, and the queen was carried between them to break the spell. The account is short and terrible, and it belongs to a genre — the Persian martyr acts — that shaped the Church of the East’s memory of itself for a thousand years: a minority under a Zoroastrian crown, suspected of loyalty to Rome, its women accused of magic, its clergy killed at Easter. The Syriac Acts of Tarbo tell the same story in the same order: the queen falls ill, the Jews say the sisters of Simeon have bewitched her because their brother was killed, and Tarbo, her sister and her maid are seized.

## docstories/cistercian-monasticism-1
### Revised text
The Cistercian conversi took vows of obedience and celibacy, wore beards, lived in a separate range of the monastery, and did most of the farm labor that made the order rich; they could not become choir monks, could not learn to read, and had no voice in the chapter. The statutes of the annual general chapter, the order’s legislative record, mention them mostly when something went wrong. The earliest recorded revolt is at Schönau in the Rhineland in 1168, where the lay brothers rose against the abbot; the chapter ordered the ringleaders expelled and the rest put on bread and water. The old sources we hold do not say what set it off. Over the following century the statutes record risings at more than twenty houses. At Coggeshall the lay brothers refused the bread ration; at several houses the grievance was beer — the chapter had ordered the lay brothers’ ale weakened. The penalties are recorded too: expulsion, imprisonment in the monastery’s cell, transfer to a distant house, loss of the daily wine. What the conversi thought is not recorded, because no one asked them; what they wanted can be read from the complaints. Both are Cistercian documents, and the tradition that preserved Bernard’s sermons on the Song of Songs also preserved the minutes of the men who kept the granges and rioted over the beer.

## docstories/the-haugean-awakening-1
### Revised text
The case against Hauge was the largest the Danish-Norwegian state had ever brought against a layman. Arrested on 24 October 1804, he was questioned for months, first on the Conventicle Act of 1741, which forbade religious meetings not led by the parish clergy, then on the charge that his trading network was fraudulent, then on his books, which the clergy read for heresy and found chiefly plain. A royal commission was appointed in 1806 and sent questions to every bailiff and parish in Norway; more than six hundred witnesses were examined, most of them peasants who said that Hauge had told them to work hard, obey the authorities and stop drinking. Hauge was held for the first years in close confinement and his health was permanently damaged; he was allowed out in 1809 to make salt. The commission's verdict came in December 1813: two years' hard labor in the fortress, for breaking the Conventicle Act and for having led people astray. He appealed. In December 1814, in the year Norway received its own constitution, the high court reduced the sentence to a fine and costs. The sources we hold do not give the court's reasons. The friends paid. Hauge settled on a farm outside Christiania, married, and in his Testament to his Friends of 1821 told them to keep to the Lutheran church, hold their meetings, and do their work. He died in 1824. The Conventicle Act was repealed in 1842 by a Storting many of whose members were farmers his friends had taught to read.
