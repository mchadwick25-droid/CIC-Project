## tree/hippolytus-of-rome
### Revised text
Hippolytus, one of the elders who led the church at Rome with its bishop, set down what, he said, his own bishop, Callistus, taught about Noah's ark. "He also said that Noah's ark was made as a sign of the church. In it were dogs and wolves and ravens, everything clean and unclean. So, he says, it should be with the church." Hippolytus wrote it to show how far, in his eyes, that bishop had fallen.

In the early two hundreds Hippolytus was a Greek-writing teacher at Rome. He wrote a book against the teachings he held false, the Refutation of All Heresies. In it he charged Callistus with forgiving sinners too easily and with holding that Father and Son were one and the same. In 235 the emperor Alexander, a later list says, sent him to Sardinia with Pontianus, the bishop of Rome. The lists give no reason.

A teacher from Smyrna, Hippolytus says, had taught that Christ was the Father himself, so that the Father was born and suffered. When the teaching reached Rome, Hippolytus answered from Christ's words. "Christ did not say, 'I and the Father am one,' but 'are one.' The word 'are' is not said of one person. It speaks of two persons and one power."

Nearly all we know of Callistus comes from this enemy. He says that Callistus, as adviser to the bishop of the day, let the teaching spread and called Hippolytus's side "worshippers of two gods." He paints him as a slave who made away with Christians' savings and was later sentenced to forced labor in the mines of Sardinia, a sentence for convicts.

Once Callistus became bishop in 217, he forgave every sin, Hippolytus says, to anyone who came over to him and would not remove a bishop even for grave sin. He called Callistus's following a school, not a church, and accused him of teaching adultery and murder. For himself, he claimed to be a successor of the apostles, sharing their "high-priesthood." Eusebius says only that he presided over "another church." Jerome could not find out the name of the city. After Callistus died, his Refutation traced each teaching Hippolytus held false back to Greek philosophy, and ended with Callistus himself.

Then, in 235, a list of Rome's bishops records that Pontianus and "the elder Hippolytus," as it calls him, were sent to Sardinia, "the unhealthy island." He had told of Callistus's sentence there, and now he went himself, with Rome's bishop. The list does not say how he died. Rome's calendar of martyrs kept both men on the same day, 13 August.

His Greek books soon dropped out of sight at Rome, where the clergy now wrote Latin. The Refutation turned up in a manuscript from Mount Athos in 1842 and was first printed under the name of Origen.

About 140 years after the exile, Damasus, bishop of Rome, set a verse on his tomb. "It is said that Hippolytus, an elder, while the tyrant's orders pressed hard, had stayed all along in the split of Novatus." The tyrant is the persecuting emperor. By Novatus, Damasus means Novatian, whose party split Rome over a like question in 251, refusing to take back those who had denied the faith. On his way to death, the verse says, Hippolytus told the people to follow the catholic faith, the faith of the one true church. "Damasus tells what he has heard. Christ tests it all."

## tree/jerome
### Revised text
Jerome was a young man in the desert when fasting failed to quiet his restless mind. So he went to a brother who, before his conversion, had been a Jew, and asked to be taught Hebrew. Years later he wrote of what it cost a scholar fresh from Rome's great Latin writers. "I began to learn my letters all over again, and worked at sounding out words that were harsh and throaty."

Jerome put the Old Testament into Latin from the Hebrew itself, not from the old Greek translation the churches read, from which their Latin version had been made. His Bible was fought over in his lifetime. In time it became the Western church's standard text.

In the 380s Damasus, the bishop of Rome, urged him to correct the Latin Gospels the churches already had against the Greek. Jerome wrote that there were almost as many forms of the text as copies.

Most of what we know of Jerome, and of the women around him, comes from his own letters. At Rome, he wrote, he often found himself among women vowed to God, and to some he explained the Scriptures. Then Rome turned on him. When the young daughter of Paula, a Roman widow of a great family, died, people in the funeral crowd, he wrote, said fasting had killed her. They asked why the monks were not thrown into the Tiber. He was accused over his friendship with Paula, though he says his accuser later took it back. He sailed in August, at a time he chose, he says.

Paula followed him east and built a monastery for men and houses for women at Bethlehem. When he was stuck over the Bible, he wrote, she pressed him with fresh questions, and she learned Hebrew. Palladius, a monk on his opponents' side, charged instead that Jerome held her back by his jealousy. From about 391 he addressed many prefaces to Paula and her daughter.

Augustine, a bishop in Africa, begged him not to translate the Scriptures afresh unless he marked where he differed from the old Greek, which Augustine held in the highest esteem. Rufinus of Aquileia, once Jerome's close friend, had fallen out with him over Origen, a Greek scholar whose ideas were under attack. In 400 Rufinus wrote against him. The new translation, he charged, was made under the influence of the Jews, and it tampered with the Scriptures as the apostles handed them down. "Who but you would have laid hands on God's gift, and on the inheritance of the apostles?"

In 403 Augustine told Jerome how a bishop at Oea had Jerome's Jonah read in church. The old Latin said the plant God grew to shade the prophet was a gourd. Jerome had written ivy. Augustine reported, and by the Greeks he means the Greek-speakers in the town: "The Greeks above all found fault with the reading, and fanned the charge that it was false. Such an uproar broke out in the congregation that the bishop was forced to demand the Jews' testimony. The town was Oea." The Jews, Augustine says, whether from ignorance or spite, said the Hebrew agreed with the old version, and the bishop corrected the reading so as not to lose his congregation.

Jerome answered sharply that the Hebrew names a plant that is neither gourd nor ivy. Augustine still would not have the Hebrew version read in church. Yet in 418 Jerome wrote that Catholics revered Augustine as the restorer of the ancient faith.

Jerome died at Bethlehem in 420. Two centuries later a bishop in Spain wrote that his translation was rightly preferred to the others. In 1546 the Council of Trent declared the "old and Vulgate edition," long used in the church, the authentic Latin text, and forbade anyone to reject it. The decree does not name Jerome.

About 411 he wrote of that first hard lesson in Hebrew: "But I thank the Lord that from this seed of learning, sown in bitterness, I now gather sweet fruit."

## tree/eusebius-of-caesarea
### Revised text
"I confess that it is beyond my power to write a perfect and complete history. Since I am the first to enter on this subject, I am trying to travel, as it were, a lonely and untrodden path." So wrote Eusebius, bishop of Caesarea in Roman Palestine, opening his Church History.

He set out to tell the whole church's story from its own letters, lists and books, copying their words into his pages. He wrote that he had "plucked like flowers from a meadow the appropriate passages from ancient writers."

Born around 260, Eusebius studied under Pamphilus, a friend whose name he took.

In 303 the emperors began a persecution, ordering the churches torn down. Lactantius says Galerius, urged on by his mother, pressed Diocletian to destroy the Christians. Diocletian long refused, then gave way. Eusebius, whose own pages tell most of his life, saw it: "With our own eyes we saw the houses of prayer thrown down from top to ground, to their very foundations. We saw the inspired and sacred Scriptures handed over to the fire in the middle of the market squares. We saw the shepherds of the churches hiding shamefully here and there, and some of them caught in disgrace and mocked by their enemies."

By houses of prayer he meant churches, and by shepherds the clergy. Pamphilus was imprisoned and died a martyr for the faith.

Later Eusebius became bishop of Caesarea. He traced each great church's bishops back to the apostles, drawing letters from Jerusalem's church library. He set a rule: those who gave way in the persecution he would leave out, keeping only what would profit his own time and those after.

He had seen the young Constantine, and later knew him as emperor.

At the Council of Nicaea in 325, by his own account, his church's statement of faith was read. The bishops added words, among them "of one being" with the Father. He signed, and wrote home that all agreed "not without inquiry", for the sake of peace. Socrates, a historian a century later, says he held back briefly first. Athanasius of Alexandria, an opponent who was there, says he refused the day before.

Ten years later the emperor called bishops to Tyre to judge Athanasius. Socrates says his enemies charged that he had murdered a bishop named Arsenius and used the dead man's hand in magic, and that his priest Macarius had broken a sacred cup. Eusebius sat among the judges, says Epiphanius, writing some forty years later on Athanasius's side. In his account Potamo, an Egyptian bishop who had lost an eye in the persecution, cried out: "You sit there, Eusebius, and Athanasius, an innocent man, is judged by you?" And then: "Tell me, were you not with me in prison in the persecution? I lost an eye for the truth. But you seem to have no part of your body maimed. You did not suffer as a martyr; you stand here alive, with nothing cut away. How did you get out of prison, unless you promised our persecutors to do something unlawful, or did it?"

Eusebius rose in anger and broke up the court, Epiphanius says. Socrates says Arsenius turned up alive, which ended that charge. Yet the council deposed Athanasius after men it sent to Egypt reported that his accuser spoke the truth about the cup. About three years later the bishops of Egypt wrote that he had been "accused" of offering sacrifice to the Roman gods. Rufinus, writing later in Latin, tells a like scene at Tyre that does not name Eusebius. These were charges; no source shows he sacrificed.

After Constantine died in 337, Eusebius wrote a Life of the emperor, telling, by his own rule, only what showed his faith. Its account of Tyre names neither Athanasius nor Potamo. Socrates judged it more praise than accurate history.

The letter of Polycrates of Ephesus on Easter survives only because Eusebius copied it. He is the first known to quote Josephus on Jesus, and he retold, from a Latin writer, Pliny the Younger's letter about the Christians. Socrates began his history where Eusebius stopped, and Rufinus put the Church History into Latin.

Athanasius said Eusebius had written that Christ was not true God, while Socrates defended him, saying Eusebius had been at Nicaea and agreed to what it decided.

Eusebius died about 339. His pupil and successor as bishop wrote his life. That book is lost.
