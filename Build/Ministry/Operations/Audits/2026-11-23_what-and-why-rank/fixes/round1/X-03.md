## tree/the-union-of-brest-and-ruthenian-greek-catholic-
### Revised text
In 1596 most of the Orthodox bishops of the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth met at Brest and accepted communion with Rome while keeping their Byzantine liturgy, their Church Slavonic, their calendar and their married clergy. Their own decree gives the reasons. The church, they wrote, should have one head, as Christ built it on Peter. The patriarchs of Constantinople now lived under the Turks. Bribery for church office and new heresies were spreading in their own lands. Their opponents met in the same town at the same time and rejected the union outright. They declared the metropolitan and his bishops cast down, because the bishops had handed a church that had always answered to Constantinople to the throne of old Rome, against the holy canons. So the union produced not one church but two rival hierarchies claiming the same people and arguing in print for a century. The new church had to explain itself constantly, to Rome, to the Orthodox it had left, and to Latin bishops who never quite treated it as an equal. Its most famous figure, Archbishop Josaphat Kuntsevych, was killed by a hostile crowd at Vitebsk in 1623. His biographer, Bishop Jakub Susza, writes that the Orthodox of Vitebsk had threatened him for years. On the day, he says, two men burst in crying "Latin!" and "Papist!" and cut him down.

## tree/the-christian-homeschool-movement
### Revised text
In 1975 Raymond and Dorothy Moore published Better Late Than Early. It argued that formal lessons should wait until a child was older. Around 1982 James Dobson interviewed Raymond Moore about the book on the air. Some families who heard the broadcast began to teach their children at home. In March 1983 two homeschooling fathers, the lawyers Michael Farris and J. Michael Smith, launched the Home School Legal Defense Association with their wives. At the time, homeschooling was legal in only a handful of states.

## tree/the-bosnian-church
### Revised text
Between Catholic Hungary and Orthodox Serbia, medieval Bosnia had a church of its own. Its members called themselves krstjani, Christians. It used a Slavonic liturgy and had its own leaders. Rome condemned it as heretical again and again, and from time to time sent friars against it. In 1247 Pope Innocent IV wrote that the church of Bosnia had wholly fallen into heresy. Popes called its people Patarenes, and in 1461 spoke of Manichaean errors among the Bosnians. In 1203 their leaders promised a papal envoy, with Ban Kulin present, to keep altars and crosses in their churches, to read both Testaments, and to have priests who said Mass and heard confession. Bosnia's own rulers protected it and made use of it. The papal letters name the charge. What its members themselves taught, the old sources do not set out. The one major document written from inside is the will of Gost Radin, made in 1466. It reads like ordinary Christianity, with a cross drawn down its margin. Bosnia fell to the Ottomans in 1463, and the church did not outlast the kingdom.
