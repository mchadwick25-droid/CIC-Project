## docstories/indonesian-christianity-the-independent-century-1
### Revised text
In 1965 the army under Suharto blamed the Communist Party and, over the following months, led and licensed a slaughter of people accused of belonging to it. The killers were soldiers and militias, some organized through Muslim youth organizations and some, in a few places, through Christian ones. The new order then required every citizen to hold one of the recognized religions; not to have one was to be suspected of atheism, which was to be suspected of communism. In the years that followed, the Protestant and Catholic churches of Java received people in numbers they had never seen. The churches knew why some had come. Pastors' accounts from those years speak of villages associated with the party whose survivors turned to a church that had not taken part in the killing and did not ask questions, and of catechism classes that were also, plainly, refuge. They also speak of people who stayed.

## docstories/indonesian-christianity-the-independent-century-0
### Revised text
Soe is a market town in the hills of West Timor, and its congregation belonged to the GMIT, the Protestant Evangelical Church of Timor. On the evening of 26 September 1965 a service there passed into something the congregation had not seen: confession of sins aloud, weeping, people speaking of visions, and a fire some said they saw over the building. Over the following weeks lay teams formed, numbered as they went out, and walked to villages across the island, preaching, praying for the sick, and calling on people to burn the fetishes and charms of the older religion; the GMIT's records list the teams and where they went. The church grew fast. In 1971 Mel Tari, a young Timorese who had been on one of the teams, published in the United States Like a Mighty Wind, an account that included water turned to wine for communion, the dead raised, and teams crossing flooded rivers on foot. The church that grew from that night went on.

## docstories/armenian-christianity-after-avarayr-1
### Revised text
Vasak, prince of Siwnik, was marzpan — the Persian-appointed governor — of Armenia when the nobles rose against the order to convert to Zoroastrianism. He had at first joined the resistance; when it became clear that Byzantium would send no help and that the revolt could not win, he went over to the Persian side, took hostages from the rebel families, and worked to bring the country back under the king’s authority without a general massacre. At Avarayr he was on the Persian side of the field. Elishe, writing the church’s account of the war, made him the villain of the whole story: an apostate who ‘loved the world more than God’, whose treachery caused the defeat, and whose punishment followed — summoned to the Persian court, accused by the very nobles he had betrayed, stripped of his honors before the king, and left to die in prison, his body eaten, Elishe says, by worms while he still lived. Elishe says why he wrote: “that all may profit by the fate of Vasag, and avoid his lawless conduct.” Lazar’s version is drier and lets the political calculation show. The Armenian church has kept him as its type of the traitor for fifteen centuries.
