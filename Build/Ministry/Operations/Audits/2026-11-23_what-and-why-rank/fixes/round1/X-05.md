## tree/indonesian-christianity-the-independent-century
### Revised text
Over the twentieth century Indonesia came to hold one of the largest Christian populations in the Muslim-majority world. By 2010 its churches had been running themselves for most of a century. The Batak church, the HKBP, became independent in 1930 — the first self-governing church in the Dutch East Indies. The archipelago's Christianity is regional rather than national: Sumatra, Java, Timor and the eastern islands each have their own story. Two of the century's turning points fall close together. Between 1965 and 1971 a revival ran through Timor, and Mel Tari, who took part in it, told its story in Like a Mighty Wind (1971). And after 1965, in a state that required every citizen to declare a religion, church membership grew very fast.

## tree/church-of-caucasian-albania
### Revised text
Beside Armenia and Georgia there was a third Christian kingdom in the Caucasus. This was Albania, in what is now Azerbaijan and the south of Dagestan. It had its own royal line, its own bishops and its own alphabet. In the late fifth century it held a council at Aghuen. The council made rules for the clergy and for marriage, and set out what the nobles owed the church. Albania also produced a history, put together from older material by a writer known as Movses Daskhurantsi. That is very nearly all the story it left. It was pressed between Persian, Byzantine and then Arab power, and between the rival loyalties of its Armenian and Georgian neighbors. In the early 700s the Armenian catholicos Elia held a council at Partav, on the order of the Arab ruler Abd al-Malik. He called before it Nerses Bakur, the catholicos of the Albanians. The Armenian writer Stepanos of Taron, about the year 1000, calls Nerses a false catholicos who had tried to bring the Albanians over to the Council of Chalcedon. The Armenian church rejected that council because it taught two natures in Christ. The old sources do not tell us here what became of the Albanian see afterward. Its written language was all but lost for more than a thousand years. Then, in 1996, palimpsests at Saint Catherine's Monastery on Sinai were found to hold Caucasian Albanian scripture. The pages had been scraped and written over with later Georgian text.

## worlds-tree/syriac-edessa-nisibis/docstory-syr.story.edessa-flood-201
### Revised text
In November of the year 513 of the Greeks, around the year 201, the river Daisan rose against Edessa in the night. King Abgar stood on the great tower and watched the water by torchlight. That same hour the waters broke down the western wall and swept through the city, overthrowing the king's palace.

The Chronicle of Edessa sets down this account. It was compiled about 340 years later from the archives where the royal scribes had filed it, and no bishop or believer is named in it.

One line among the losses names the church. "And they destroyed the temple of the church of the Christians."

The entry goes on. "More than two thousand men were killed in that event. The waters came upon many of them suddenly as they slept in the night, and they drowned. The city was filled with the sound of mourning."
