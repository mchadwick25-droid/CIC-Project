## tree/vietnamese-catholicism-1815-1906
### Revised text
Under the emperor Gia Long, Catholics were allowed to keep their religious practices. Most still bore labor and military service, like other Vietnamese. Minh Mạng came to power in 1820. He distrusted Europeans. In 1825 he forbade European teachers to enter his kingdom. He called the missionaries already there to his court, so that they could do no more work.

In 1832 the Paris mission, the MEP, counted 160,000 to 170,000 Christians in Tonkin. Its bishop was Joseph Havard. The count listed about sixty native priests, several hundred catechists, and thirty convents with 400 sisters. They were the Lovers of the Cross, founded in 1670.

On 6 January 1833 Minh Mạng issued the first great edict against the Christians. It told them to give up their faith. It told the mandarins to make them trample the cross. It ordered their churches and the priests' houses razed. On 17 October a French priest, Gagelin, was strangled in public, condemned for spreading the religion of Jesus in several provinces.

Later in the century French pressure grew. In 1873 the French officer Francis Garnier seized Hanoi, with help from Christians. When the French handed the land back, the scholars burned more than three hundred Christian villages in two provinces and killed native priests and catechists. To their countrymen, the Christians would bear the blame.

In July 1885, after an Annamite attack on the French at Hue failed, the regent Tuyêt ordered the mandarins and scholars to rise. In six provinces of the eastern Cochinchina mission they did. About 25,000 Christians died, with eight missionaries. France then held the south and ruled the center and north as protectorates. Catholics gained protection. The taint of the French tie lingered.

## docstories/waldensians-in-the-alps-0
### Revised text
Innocent VIII’s bull of April 1487 called the faithful to take up arms against the Waldensians of the Alpine valleys, granting the indulgences of a crusade and the goods of the heretics to those who took them. The archdeacon Alberto Cattaneo was made commissioner, and in the spring of 1488 an army raised in Dauphiné under the royal lieutenant Hugues de la Palud moved up the valleys on the French side while a Savoyard force attacked the Piedmontese valleys of Angrogna and Pragelato from the east. According to the testimony gathered twenty years later, when the survivors’ heirs sued for the restoration of their confiscated property and the king’s commissioners took evidence from the valley, the inhabitants of Val Louise took their families, their animals and their stores up to a large cave on the mountainside — the Balme Chapelue, ‘the bearded cave’, above the hamlet of Aiguefreide — where they had sheltered from raids before. La Palud’s men reached the ledge and piled brushwood at the entrance. The testimony says that those who came out were killed and those who stayed inside suffocated; later accounts give the dead as a few hundred, including women and children. Perrin, a Waldensian pastor writing in 1618, says the valley was emptied and settled again by newcomers. In 1501 King Louis XII sent commissioners to the neighboring valley of Freyssinières, where an inquisitor had condemned the people for not appearing and seized their goods. Perrin says the commissioners heard twenty-four witnesses and lifted the sentence, and the king ordered the goods returned, but no one gave anything back. The Waldensians of the Piedmontese valleys, who had fought, were still there to meet the Reformers in 1532.

## docstories/the-coptic-monastic-and-sunday-school-revival-1
### Revised text
Shenouda III had been patriarch since 1971, the first from the Sunday School generation, and had made the church a public body as his predecessors had not: preaching to thousands each Wednesday in the cathedral, and protesting to the government, with fasts and canceled Easter celebrations, against the attacks on Copts that had multiplied with the Islamist revival. In June 1981 sectarian fighting in Cairo killed dozens. On 3 September 1981 Anwar Sadat, by presidential decision, revoked the state's recognition of Shenouda as patriarch, appointed a committee of five bishops to administer the church, and confined him to the monastery of St Bishoy in Wadi Natrun, the desert his own generation had repopulated. The old sources we hold do not say why Sadat acted that day. Shenouda went. He said no public word against the decree; the Holy Synod did not accept the committee; and the faithful kept his name in the liturgy. On 6 October Sadat was shot by Islamist officers, and his successor Hosni Mubarak left the patriarch where he was. Shenouda spent forty months in the desert writing; on 2 January 1985 Mubarak restored him, and he returned to a cathedral that could not hold the crowd. The old sources do not say what moved Mubarak. He was still patriarch a quarter-century later; the church came to count the exile as the moment it learned that its patriarch could be removed by a president and could outlast him.
