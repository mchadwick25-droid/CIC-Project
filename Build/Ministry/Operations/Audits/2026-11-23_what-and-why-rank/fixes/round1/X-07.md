## tree/ebionite-nazoraean-current
### Revised text
The first Christians were Jews, and in Palestine and Syria some communities of Jewish believers in Jesus went on keeping Torah, circumcision and the Sabbath long after the wider church had moved away from them. Later heresy-hunting writers — Irenaeus, Origen, Eusebius, Epiphanius, Jerome — describe them under two names, 'Ebionites', from the Hebrew for the poor, and 'Nazoraeans', reporting that they used their own Hebrew or Aramaic gospel, honored James the brother of Jesus, and regarded Paul as an apostate from the Law. But those writers disagree with one another, wrote from outside and often centuries later, and had every reason to sharpen the picture. Eusebius, for one, says there were two kinds of Ebionites. The first held that Jesus was a plain man, born of a man and Mary. The second accepted the virgin birth but denied that he existed before it. Both, he says, kept the Law strictly. Not one text these communities wrote about themselves survives intact — only fragments quoted by the people refuting them.

## worlds-tree/lutheran-wittenberg-and-its-congregations/docstory-witt.story.augsburg-before-cajetan
### Revised text
Years later, at his own table, Luther told his students how he was summoned to Augsburg in the summer of 1518. He had attacked the sale of indulgences, and the Pope's legate, Cardinal Cajetan, was to hear his case. Frederick, the Elector of Saxony, gave him a strong escort and a safe-conduct, and he was warned not to trust the Italians.

He waited three days for the Emperor's own safe-conduct. In that time an Italian led him to Cardinal Cajetan, urging him the whole way to say one word, Revoco, which means I recant. Say it, the man promised, and the Cardinal would commend him to the Pope's favor.

Before the Cardinal, Luther said, he knelt and then went all the way down to the ground. He stayed there until Cajetan told him three times to rise. The Cardinal was pleased, and he hoped, Luther said, that Luther would think better of things.

The next day Luther came again and would not recant anything. Writing to Carlstadt, Luther named the two points the Cardinal pressed. One was his claim that an indulgence is not the treasure of Christ's merits. The other was that whoever comes to the Lord's table must believe. The Cardinal would hear only the words "I recant."

In Luther's memory, the Cardinal asked whether he thought the Pope cared for Germany, or that princes would raise armies to defend him. Where, Cajetan asked, would he be safe then?

"Under Heaven," Luther answered.

## tree/post-aksumite-ethiopian-christianity
### Revised text
Aksum had been one of the great powers of the late-antique world, minting its own coins, its kings Christian since the fourth century. Then the trade that sustained it moved away, the coinage stopped, and Ethiopian Christianity went quiet for something close to five hundred years — one of the least documented stretches in the whole history of any ancient church. What can be said with confidence is small but decisive. The church survived, its center shifted south into the highlands, and it went on receiving its chief bishop from the Coptic patriarch in Alexandria, a link that would last into the twentieth century. Later Ethiopian tradition fills the silence with the story of a queen remembered as Gudit, who is said to have destroyed churches around the year 960, but that account comes from much later and largely hostile sources and cannot be verified. When the record resumes under the Zagwe kings in the twelfth century, there is a functioning church with monasteries, a liturgy in Ge'ez and its own saints.
