## docstories/st-thomas-christians-under-portuguese-pressure-d-1
### Revised text
Archbishop Aleixo de Menezes of Goa arrived in Malabar early in 1599 with soldiers, money and a determination that the Thomas Christians should have no bishop but one Rome sent. Through the spring he toured their churches, confirming and ordaining, on the reasoning that priests who owed their orders to him would owe him obedience. The Archdeacon George, who had governed the community since the death of its last East Syrian bishop, resisted, was threatened with excommunication and with Portuguese pressure on the pepper trade, and submitted. The synod met at Udayamperur, which the Portuguese called Diamper, from 20 to 26 June 1599, with some 150 priests and more than 600 lay representatives. Its decrees, read in Portuguese and Malayalam, declared the patriarch of the East a heretic, a Nestorian and head of what they called a cursed sect, and forbade priests to name him in the Mass. They condemned the prayers for Nestorius and Theodore. They forbade priests to marry after ordination and suspended those already married until they sent their wives away. They named seven sacraments. And they ordered Syriac books to be burned or corrected, among them the Book of the Fathers, the Book of Synods and the Book of Homilies, because the synod found in them errors, such as the claim that Mary should not be called the Mother of God. Menezes then made a second circuit collecting manuscripts, and many were burned at the churches where they were found. The chronicler Gouvea, who wrote the archbishop's official account, presents this as rescue. Much of what is known about the community's worship before 1599 is what the synod condemned.

## docstories/russian-evangelicals-stundists-0
### Revised text
Vasily Pashkov, a Guards colonel and one of the richest landowners in Russia, had been converted in 1874 under the preaching of the English evangelist Lord Radstock, and had opened his house to meetings where princesses and their coachmen sat together to hear the gospel preached in Russian; the Pashkovites, as the capital called them, had spread through his estates and beyond. In the south, Ukrainian peasants who had learned to read the Bible in the Stunden of their German neighbors, Russian Baptists, Mennonite Brethren and Molokans had grown into scattered congregations that the state watched. In April 1884 Pashkov and Count Modest Korff invited representatives of all of them to St Petersburg, some seventy delegates, peasants and nobles, to find a common confession. They met in Pashkov's house from 1 April. On the fourth day the police arrested the provincial delegates, held them in the Peter and Paul fortress overnight, and sent them home under escort. The sources we hold do not say why the police acted. In June the emperor, on the advice of Pobedonostsev, the Holy Synod's chief procurator, required Pashkov and Korff to sign an undertaking to cease all religious activity; they refused, and were ordered to leave Russia. Neither returned. Pashkov died in Paris in 1902. The delegates who went home united, the same year and without the nobles, at a meeting in the Caucasus, as the Union of Russian Baptists, and the Pashkovites and the Baptists remained two streams.

## docstories/african-initiated-churches-harrist-aladura-zioni-1
### Revised text
Christianah Abiodun Akinsowon was the fifteen-year-old daughter of an Anglican family in Lagos when, on 18 June 1925, watching the Corpus Christi procession, she fell into a trance in which, by her account, she was taken to heaven and shown things she was to tell; she lay unconscious for days and her family sent for Moses Orimolade Tunolase, a lame itinerant Yoruba preacher known for prayer and healing. He prayed and she revived, and the two of them began to hold prayer meetings that drew Christians who wanted what the mission churches did not offer: visions, healing, prophecy, and prayer against witchcraft. In September 1925 the society took the name Cherubim and Seraphim, given, Abiodun said, in a vision. It grew fast, on her visions and his prayer, and within four years it had branches across Yorubaland and the white garments and processions that mark the Aladura churches. Then it divided. In 1929 Abiodun, now nineteen and a leader in her own right, and Orimolade, old and ill, fell out over authority; the elders sided with Orimolade, and Abiodun's followers were excluded and formed their own society, the Cherubim and Seraphim Society, under her, while Orimolade's became the Eternal Sacred Order. He died in 1933. She led her branch for sixty-five years, was called Captain Abiodun, and died in 1994.
