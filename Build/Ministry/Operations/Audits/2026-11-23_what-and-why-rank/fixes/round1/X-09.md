## tree/polish-and-lithuanian-catholicism-under-the-partitions
### Revised text
Poland was divided among Russia, Prussia and Austria in three partitions, in 1773, 1793 and 1795. This story starts in 1815.

In 1831 an insurrection against Russia failed. After it, convents were closed, and so were churches.

Harsher measures came after 1863. A cabinet order of 1864 took the church's property and shut the convents still open. In 1867 the clergy were put under a commission at St Petersburg, with no regard for the wishes of the Apostolic See.

The measures were harshest in Lithuania. In the seven years after 1863 no more than ten priests were ordained there. Public prayers, processions and wayside crosses were forbidden.

In Prussia the state acted through new laws. In 1873 Pope Pius IX wrote to King Wilhelm that his government's measures aimed more and more at the ruin of Catholicism. The king answered that part of his Catholic subjects had formed a political party against the state, and that he would enforce the laws. In 1875 the pope told the Prussian bishops that the new laws let lay judges remove bishops. He declared the laws void. The archbishop of Gniezno and Poznan was already in custody and declared deposed.

After the defeat of 1863 and 1864 the clergy held their ground. In Prussia they built social and farm groups and loan offices for craftsmen and workers.

Only after the war with Japan did Russia proclaim an edict of toleration. It let Catholics who had been brought into the Orthodox church be reconciled with Rome.

## docstories/byzantine-imperial-church-macedonian-1
### Revised text
Byzantine church law permitted a second marriage with penance and a third barely; a fourth was forbidden, and Leo VI himself had legislated to say so. His mistress Zoe Karbonopsina bore one in 905, and the patriarch Nicholas Mystikos agreed to baptize the child, the future Constantine VII, on condition that Zoe leave the palace. Instead Leo married her, and had a palace priest crown her empress. On Christmas Day 906 Leo came in procession to Hagia Sophia and Nicholas met him at the imperial door and would not let him in. Nicholas was, on his own account, trying to protect the law and offer the emperor a penitential way back. Leo appealed to Rome and the eastern patriarchs, all of whom allowed a dispensation, and in February 907 he deposed Nicholas, exiled him to a monastery, and installed a patriarch who admitted him. Nicholas’ letters survive, more than a hundred and sixty of them, and they show a man who did not think he had been wrong and did not think the emperor had either — he calls the marriage a sin and Leo a son. He was restored after Leo’s death, presided over a council that condemned fourth marriages while accepting Constantine VII’s legitimacy, and served as regent for the boy whose birth had caused it all.

## docstories/anti-trinitarian-currents-servetus-polish-brethr-0
### Revised text
Michael Servetus had escaped the Inquisition at Vienne, where a court condemned him for his book Christianismi Restitutio. In it he wrote that those who teach the Trinity worship a three-headed monster. He came to Geneva in August 1553 and went to hear Calvin preach. He was recognized and arrested. The trial before the city council ran two months. The council had Calvin copy out articles word for word from Servetus’s book and answer them in Latin. It consulted the other Swiss cities, which all advised severity. On 26 October the sentence was death by burning. The charges included that Christ was Son of God only since his birth, and that infant baptism was a devil’s invention. Calvin asked that it be commuted to beheading and was refused. On 27 October Servetus was taken to the hill of Champel, chained to a stake with a copy of his book tied to him, and burned with green wood, so that he died slowly, crying out — the pastor Farel reported his last words as ‘Jesus, Son of the eternal God, have mercy on me’, and noted that he would not say ‘eternal Son of God’. In March a book appeared at Basel under the name Martinus Bellius: Concerning Heretics, Whether They Are to Be Persecuted, an anthology of Christian writers against coercion with a preface arguing that to kill a man is not to defend a doctrine but to kill a man, and that ‘if Christ himself came to Geneva he would be crucified’. Its author was Sebastian Castellio, a former colleague of Calvin’s. Calvin defended the burning in a book of 1554, whose title says Servetus was justly punished.
