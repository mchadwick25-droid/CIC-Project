## tree/canudos-belo-monte
### Revised text
Antônio Conselheiro was a lay preacher of the Bahia backlands. In 1876 he came to Itapicuru de Cima. His followers carried a rough cedar oratory with an image of Christ. At each stop they knelt to pray. They entered the villages singing litanies. Euclides da Cunha wrote the best-known account of him. He was a hostile witness.

In 1893 the Conselheiro was in Bom Conselho when the new town councils posted tax notices. He had the notices burned in the square and preached open rebellion against the laws. That year he reached Canudos, a hamlet in decay. Euclides says it grew fast from then on. A witness says their great wish was to sell what they had and share the money with «o Santo Conselheiro».

Euclides prints prophecies that he says were written in many small notebooks found at Canudos. One reads: «Em 1896 hade rebanhos mil correr da praia para o certão; então o certão virará praia e a praia virará certão.» In English: “In 1896 a thousand flocks will run from the coast to the backlands; then the backlands will become coast and the coast will become backlands” (a rendering). Euclides compares him to old heretics. In that comparison he says the Conselheiro rose against the Roman Church. He says it had lost its glory and obeyed Satan. He calls him «um gnostico bronco», a crude gnostic (a rendering). So his account is not neutral. An officer he quotes saw it the other way. He says the Conselheiro promoted baptisms, weddings, feasts and novenas.

The church acted against him. In 1882 the archbishop of Bahia told his parish priests to forbid their people to gather to hear him. Only the ministers of religion, he wrote, may teach the people. In May 1895 a Capuchin friar, João Evangelista de Monte-Marciano, came to Canudos in the archbishop's name. He came to open a mission and to tell the people to go home. The friar reports the Conselheiro's answer. Under the monarchy, he said, he had let himself be arrested, because he recognized that government. Now he would not, because he did not recognize the Republic (a rendering).

On 6 September 1897 army guns brought down the towers of the new church. For weeks defenders had fired from them into the soldiers' trenches. The infantry officer Macedo Soares calls them the town's chief stronghold. General Barbosa's order called their fall the sign of coming victory. The last shelling began at dawn on 1 October. Euclides says Canudos fell on the 5th, at dusk. He writes that four defenders were left, an old man, two grown men and a child, with five thousand soldiers raging before them.

## tree/suetonius
### Revised text
In his life of the emperor Claudius, the Roman scholar Suetonius lists what Claudius did. He freed the people of old Troy from tax, and he let German envoys sit with the senators at the theater. Between the two comes one sentence: "He drove from Rome the Jews who were constantly rioting at the urging of Chrestus."

Suetonius wrote his lives of twelve of Rome's rulers around 120, decades after Claudius, and the Christians get this line and one other: under Nero, they were punished.

Suetonius was a close friend of Pliny the Younger, and served for a time as secretary to the emperor Hadrian, until Hadrian dismissed him. Spartianus, who wrote Hadrian's life, gives the reason. Suetonius and his friend Septicius Clarus, he says, had behaved toward the empress Sabina more familiarly than respect for the imperial household allowed.

The Claudius line gives no year and no source. In the New Testament, the Acts of the Apostles tells of a Jew named Aquila and his wife, whom the apostle Paul met at Corinth. They had come from Italy "because Claudius had commanded all the Jews to depart from Rome." Suetonius never says who Chrestus was, a name close in sound to Christus, the Latin for Christ.

The Nero line sits in another list, between rules for food shops and a ban on chariot drivers' pranks. It reads: "Punishments were inflicted on the Christians, a kind of people given to a new and harmful superstition." Superstition was his word of contempt for their religion. He names no charge and no kind of punishment, and he counts this among Nero's acts that were "beyond criticism." Elsewhere he blames Nero for the great fire, but never ties it to the Christians. Another Roman, Tacitus, tells in full of Christians put to death under Nero.

Christian writers said that Romans got their name wrong. Around 200, Tertullian, defending the Christians, told them so. "And even when you wrongly say 'Chrestianus', since you do not even know the name for certain, it is formed from sweetness or kindness." The Greek chrestos means kind, or good. The Christian writer Lactantius says the same of Christ's own name: the ignorant, by changing one letter, call him Chrestus.

Seven Latin words, between old Troy's tax and the Germans' theater seats, from a man not writing about Christians at all, still read word by word for one name.

## tree/philippine-catholicism-gomburza-to-aglipay
### Revised text
Spanish friars had run the Philippine church for three centuries, and the century's great church fight was over whether Filipinos could run their own parishes. The secularization campaign — transferring parishes from Spanish religious orders to diocesan clergy, most of them native-born — turned into a national grievance. In 1872 a mutiny broke out at the Cavite arsenal. Spanish officials held three priests who had championed the campaign as plotters in it, and a Spanish council of war sentenced them to the garrote: Mariano Gomez, Jose Burgos and Jacinto Zamora, remembered ever since by the single name Gomburza. Their execution did more for the independence movement than their campaign ever had; Jose Rizal dedicated his second novel to their memory. When Spanish rule ended, part of the church went with it. In 1902 the labor organizer Isabelo de los Reyes proclaimed an Iglesia Filipina Independiente and named the former revolutionary chaplain Gregorio Aglipay its head, and for a few years a substantial minority of Filipino Catholics followed it.

A lay movement inside this church met refusal and then force. In 1840, by Montero's account, many people began to meet at Lucban, in Tayabas province, as a Confraternity of St Joseph. Its founder was Apolinario de la Cruz, a native lay servant of a Manila hospital. He told his questioners he was a Roman Catholic and meant to honor the saint. He asked the church authorities for leave to make the group lawful. They refused. His own statement, as Montero gives it, names no reason. He appealed to the colony's high court.

In October 1841 the governor of Tayabas ordered the group, camped at Igsaban, to disperse. They turned the order down with scorn, Montero says. Then the governor marched on the camp with about 300 men. He was killed. On 1 November troops overran its next camp. On 4 November Apolinario was shot. Montero counts 240 dead in the battle and about 260 more in the pursuit. Govantes, whom he cites, says more than a thousand. Montero also quotes a letter of 16 November from the colony's finance chief. It praised the officer of Batangas for keeping "the fire of rebellion" out of his province.

The movement speaks here only through its enemies. Montero draws on a parish priest's report, an official's letter and a writer named Mas. Montero says Apolinario began it only to live off the gullible. Apolinario said he meant to honor St Joseph. Asked what they wanted, pardoned members answered, as Mas tells it: to pray. Asked what they would have done in victory, they said they would have tied the Spaniards and friars to trees for the women to shoot with arrows.

Montero says the group let in no Spaniards and no Chinese mestizos. He takes that as proof of a political aim. That is his reading. Apolinario's letters were read aloud at the meetings, and were seized in 1841. The Library holds none of them.
