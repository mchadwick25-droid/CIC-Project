## tree/kongo-christianity-after-1650
### Revised text
In the late 1660s two Capuchin friars, Dionigi Carli and Michelangelo Guattini, served in Bamba, in Kongo. Carli's book came out in 1671. He writes that a headman sent word through the village to bring the children for baptism, because the adults were all baptized. The people called the friar "Ngamga," and Carli adds that they put a word after it to honor him.

Giovanni Antonio Cavazzi was a Capuchin. He printed his history of Kongo, Matamba and Angola in 1687. He writes that priests rarely came to remote places to keep the law of Christ in force. The people there still took pride in it.

Cavazzi tells of the queen Njinga, whom he calls queen of the Jaga. He says ambition drove her to invade the lands the Portuguese held, and that she needed a larger force to do it. So she turned her back on the true law and joined the Jaga, to lead them. He calls them greedy for human flesh. He says that for twenty-eight years she followed their way of life, which he calls the most beastly of all. Her later return to the church is not shown in the text read here.

Almost nothing was read for the years from 1687 to 1879. How the church lived then waits on sources not yet read.

Bentley's list dates the Baptist station at San Salvador to 1879. The Portuguese priests there first came in 1881. Bentley judged from what he heard that the people were about evenly split between the priests and the Baptists.

## docstories/charismatic-renewal-and-the-jesus-movement-0
### Revised text
Dennis Bennett was rector of St Mark's Episcopal Church in Van Nuys, California, a congregation of some 2,500 in a new suburb, well regarded and by his own account spiritually dry. In November 1959 a couple from his parish told him they had received the baptism in the Holy Spirit and spoken in tongues, and after weeks of questioning them he prayed with them and did the same. Word spread, and on Passion Sunday, 3 April 1960, Bennett decided to tell the whole congregation rather than let it be told for him. At the first two services he described what had happened plainly, without asking anyone to seek it. At the third, an assistant priest pulled off his vestments and walked out, saying he could no longer work with him; a vestryman stood and demanded his resignation; and Bennett, to prevent the parish tearing, resigned that morning. The bishop of Los Angeles, Francis Eric Bloy, issued a pastoral letter forbidding the practice of tongues under church auspices in the diocese. Newsweek and Time both carried the story that summer. Episcopalians, Lutherans and Presbyterians across the country who had had the same experience wrote to Bennett, and found each other. The bishop of Olympia offered him St Luke's in Seattle, a failing mission of a few dozen; he took it in July 1960, and within a decade it had two thousand members.

## docstories/ruthenian-uniate-18th-century-1
### Revised text
The Pochaiv Lavra in Volhynia, a monastery of the Uniate Basilian order since 1713 and the greatest shrine of the Ruthenian church, ran one of the most productive presses in the Commonwealth, for a church whose services were in Slavonic and whose people spoke Ukrainian, Belarusian and, among the gentry, Polish. In 1790 and 1791 it issued the Bohohlasnyk, 'the voice of God', a collection of some two hundred and fifty spiritual songs arranged by the church year and by their subjects, the feasts of Christ and the Mother of God, the saints, and death and judgment, with melodies indicated for many, in Church Slavonic, in the vernacular Ruthenian of the villages, in Polish and in Latin. Its editors, Basilian monks whose names are not given, drew on what was already being sung: on manuscript songbooks of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, on the songs of the wandering blind singers and the church cantors, on Polish Catholic hymns and Latin sequences, on the songs sung at Pochaiv itself by the pilgrims to its icon. The Bohohlasnyk was reprinted at Pochaiv and Lviv through the following century. In February 1839 three Uniate bishops of the Russian empire signed an act uniting their church with the Orthodox Church. After that the book passed across the confessional line into Orthodox use, and became the common possession of Ukrainians of both churches, which is more than either church managed on its own. It is the clearest surviving evidence of what the Uniate faithful of the eighteenth century actually sang, as distinct from what the Synod of Zamość had told them to believe.
