## docstories/athonite-and-comnenian-byzantine-monasticism-0
### Revised text
Athanasios was a monk on Mount Athos when the general Nikephoros Phokas begged him to build a lavra, a house of monks, where Nikephoros himself might one day become a monk. When Nikephoros became emperor in 963, he asked only that the church not be left half built. His charter made the house self-governing, under its own abbot. Later, monks of the Mountain were unhappy that the Lavra had upset the old order of Athos. Their complaints reached the emperor John in the capital. He sent Euthymios, a monk of the Stoudios monastery, who settled the matter with the abbots in long hearings. A Life of Athanasios, written by a monk of the Lavra under his successor, says Athanasios was accused of changing the customs of the Mountain. It says the emperor took his side. The house was meant for eighty monks, and the number rose to a hundred and twenty when the emperor John raised its yearly grant. Athanasios died at work on his monastery. The same Life tells the end. It says Athanasios and six of his companions set out to fit the keystone into the vault of a dome. The dome fell in and buried them, and they were pulled from the rubble dead. The man whose rule became the pattern for the other houses of Athos died under the weight of a monastery.

## docstories/the-church-of-the-east-on-the-silk-road-1
### Revised text
The stele set up at Chang’an in 781 is the community’s own history of itself. Its heading says it was ‘Composed by King-Tsing, A Priest of the Syrian Church.’ Lines of Syriac run down its sides. A Syriac line at its foot says the stone was set up in the days of the patriarch Hanan-Yeshu. The stone tells how, in the time of the emperor Taitsung, ‘the Most-virtuous Olopun, from the country of Syria’ came east. ‘Observing the azure clouds, he bore the true sacred books; beholding the direction of the winds, he braved difficulties and dangers.’ He reached Chang’an in 635. The emperor sent his prime minister to the western border to bring him in as a guest. The books were translated in the imperial library, and the emperor studied the teaching in his private rooms. In 638 he issued a decree, which the stone quotes: ‘Right principles have no invariable name, holy men have no invariable station’. The teaching, it says, ‘is beneficial to all creatures; it is advantageous to mankind.’ It was to be published through the empire, with a church in the capital for twenty-one priests. The stele then follows the church for a century and a half. It tells of the emperors’ portraits hung in its churches. It also tells of attacks. In 699, it says, Buddhists, gaining power, raised their voices against the church in the eastern capital. In 713 ‘some low fellows’ mocked it and spread slanders in the western capital. The stone does not say what the quarrel was about. Priests of the church held firm, it says. An emperor sent five princes to visit the church, and restored its timbers and stones. It ends with the alms of I-sz’, a man from a distant city who served at a Tang general’s side: ‘the naked came and were clothed; the sick were attended to and restored; the dead were buried in repose’.

## tree/coptic-christianity-under-early-islam
### Revised text
Egypt's miaphysite Christians had spent the century before the Arab conquest under Byzantine pressure for refusing the Council of Chalcedon. When Arab armies took Alexandria in 641-42 their patriarch Benjamin came out of hiding and was restored to his see. The church's own History has him come out on the conqueror 'Amr's promise of safety. Michael the Syrian, a Syriac patriarch of the twelfth century, found it told that Benjamin had promised the Arabs Alexandria if they would drive out Cyrus, the Chalcedonian patriarch, and give him back the churches. What followed was the long transition that made the Coptic church what it still is. No longer persecuted for its confession but taxed and governed as a protected minority, shifting gradually from Coptic into Arabic, and beginning the long, centuries-long drift toward minority status that conversions would eventually bring. Its own record of those years, the History of the Patriarchs of Alexandria, follows the church bishop by bishop with unusual care, though it says far more about patriarchs and taxes than about ordinary Egyptian Christians. The window ends under the caliph al-Hakim in the early eleventh century. Abu Salih, who wrote about Egypt's churches, says al-Hakim ordered a special costume for Christians for nine years. He closed their churches, and wrecked or burned some and turned others into mosques. The patriarch Zacharias was imprisoned. Abu Salih names the instigator: Jonas, a monk who wanted to be a bishop. The patriarch's nephew had asked him for money and blocked him.
