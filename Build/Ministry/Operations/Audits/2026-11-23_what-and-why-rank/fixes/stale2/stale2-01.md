## tree/bagratid-armenian-christianity
### Revised text
After two centuries under Arab governors, an Armenian kingdom was restored under the Bagratid dynasty in the 880s. With it came a burst of church building and writing. The catholicos, the head of the Armenian church, moved his seat to the royal capital of Ani. Its cathedral, finished around 1001 by the architect Trdat, still stands roofless on the plain. Stepanos of Taron, who wrote about the year 1000, adds that an earthquake split Hagia Sophia in Constantinople. Greek architects struggled to repair it. Trdat, an Armenian who happened to be there, drew up the plan and directed the first work. The period's most honored voice, though, was not an architect but a monk. Gregory of Narek wrote a book of ninety-five prayers, called the Narek after his monastery. Centuries later, Armenians still used it for private prayer. They held it a powerful protection against all kinds of dangers. The kingdom ended in 1045, when Byzantium annexed Ani. The Seljuk Turks took the city in 1064.

## docstories/bagratid-armenian-christianity-1
### Revised text
Gregory was raised from infancy in the convent of Narek, in the land of the Rushtunians, and in time became its abbot. There he composed a book of ninety-five prayers, called the Narek after the house that raised him. The whole Armenian nation held Gregory himself in the highest regard, and his prayers were still used in church services in the 1700s. Two of Gregory's prayers to the Holy Spirit stand in the Armenian Liturgy. In the first, the people assembled before God pray, 'we implore Thee with out-stretched arms and deep groans.' By the early 1900s, Armenians also used the Narek for private prayer. They held it a powerful protection against all kinds of dangers.
