## docstories/antiochene-church-third-century-0
### Revised text
Lucian was a presbyter of Antioch who spent his life on the text of the Greek Bible, producing a revision of the Septuagint and Gospels used across the Greek East for centuries. He was also, on the later testimony of his pupils, a teacher whose circle — Arius and Eusebius of Nicomedia among them — called themselves ‘fellow-Lucianists’ long after he was dead. What the record preserves about the man himself is the manner of his death. Eusebius, writing within a few years, says the emperor Maximinus had again turned his governors against the Christians. Lucian was brought before him at Nicomedia, spoke in defense of his doctrine, and was put in prison and killed. Rufinus, translating Eusebius, gives the speech Lucian is said to have made: Christians worship one God, made known through Christ. Christ died and rose, and the sun went dark at his death. A later notice puts the death on 7 January 312 and adds that he had been held for nine years. Chrysostom, preaching on his feast three generations later, says that under torture Lucian gave every question the same answer: I am a Christian. Later still comes the story that, chained on his back in the cell and forbidden the sacrament, he celebrated the Eucharist on his own chest for the Christians imprisoned with him. The scholar is documented; the confessor is attested; the prisoner-priest is devotion. All three belong to how Antioch remembered him, and the awkward fact that the most notorious heresy of the next century traced itself to his classroom did not stop the church from keeping his feast.

## docstories/syriac-orthodox-west-syriac-christianity-1
### Revised text
The same John of Ephesus who chronicled the persecution of his own church by the Chalcedonian emperors accepted a commission from one of them. In Justinian’s reign he was appointed teacher of the pagans in the four provinces of Asia, Caria, Phrygia and Lydia. John reports the results himself. In the Lives he counts eighty thousand people converted from paganism in those provinces. He also counts ninety-eight churches and twelve monasteries founded, and seven more churches made from Jewish synagogues. His Ecclesiastical History gives ninety-nine new churches and twelve monasteries over the thirty-five years his colleague Deuterius worked with him. His deacons, he writes, uprooted altars, destroyed shrines and cut down trees. At Derira, in the mountains near Tralles, he leveled a famous temple to its foundations and built his chief monastery on the site. Justinian paid for it and for the other monasteries and churches. Elsewhere he records with approval the later trials in Constantinople of people accused of secret pagan worship, some of whom were put to death. Yet his own church suffered persecution in Justinian’s time, and he would later be imprisoned under Justin II, with the other bishops of his church, for refusing the council of Chalcedon. That council, in 451, taught that Christ is one person in two natures, and it adopted Pope Leo’s letter. John’s side held that this divided the one Lord into two natures. His own pages record both: the persecution his church suffered, and the campaign he led for an emperor against the old religion.

## docstories/carolingian-reform-christianity-0
### Revised text
The Royal Frankish Annals, the court’s own record, state it in a sentence. In 782 the Saxons destroyed a Frankish army in the Süntel hills, and Charlemagne came to the place where the Aller flows into the Weser. There the Saxons handed over to him the men behind the revolt, four thousand five hundred of them, to be put to death, and so it was done. Only Widukind, whose counsel had stirred the Saxons to war, escaped, fleeing to the Northmen. The revised version of the annals adds that they were all beheaded in one day, at a place called Verden. The Capitulary for the Saxon Regions, from about these years, set out the terms of the conquest. It gave death to anyone who burned a church, killed a bishop or priest, or burned the dead in the pagan way. It gave death, too, to a Saxon who hid unbaptized, scorned to come to baptism and chose to stay a pagan. The same law made every Saxon, noble, free or half-free, give a tenth of his goods and his labor to the churches. Alcuin of York, the reform’s leading scholar, saw it otherwise. Faith, he held, is a matter of the will, and no one can be forced to it. The Saxons, newly Christian, should not be harassed with tithes and harsh punishments. He urged Charlemagne to ease the tithes, and held that the demand for them had hindered the Saxons’ conversion. Both kinds of text are the reform’s: the law it enforced, and the objection its own scholar raised.
