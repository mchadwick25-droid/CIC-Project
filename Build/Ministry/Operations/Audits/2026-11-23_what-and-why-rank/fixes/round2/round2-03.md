## docstories/african-initiated-churches-harrist-aladura-zioni-1
### Revised text
Christianah Abiodun Akinsowon was the fifteen-year-old daughter of an Anglican family in Lagos when, on 18 June 1925, watching the Corpus Christi procession, she fell into a trance in which, by her account, she was taken to heaven and shown things she was to tell; she lay unconscious for days and her family sent for Moses Orimolade Tunolase, a lame itinerant Yoruba preacher known for prayer and healing. He prayed and she revived, and the two of them began to hold prayer meetings that drew Christians who wanted what the mission churches did not offer: visions, healing, prophecy, and prayer against witchcraft. In September 1925 the society took the name Cherubim and Seraphim, given, Abiodun said, in a vision. It grew fast, on her visions and his prayer, and within four years it had branches across Yorubaland and the white garments and processions that mark the Aladura churches. Then it divided. In 1929 Abiodun, now nineteen and a leader in her own right, and Orimolade, old and ill, fell out over authority; the elders sided with Orimolade, and Abiodun's followers were excluded and formed their own society, the Cherubim and Seraphim Society, under her, while Orimolade's became the Eternal Sacred Order. He died in 1933. She led her branch for sixty-five years, was called Captain Abiodun, and died in 1994.

## docstories/the-coptic-monastic-and-sunday-school-revival-1
### Revised text
Shenouda III had been patriarch since 1971, the first from the Sunday School generation, and had made the church a public body as his predecessors had not: preaching to thousands each Wednesday in the cathedral, and protesting to the government, with fasts and canceled Easter celebrations, against the attacks on Copts that had multiplied with the Islamist revival. In June 1981 sectarian fighting in Cairo killed dozens. On 3 September 1981 Anwar Sadat, by presidential decision, revoked the state's recognition of Shenouda as patriarch, appointed a committee of five bishops to administer the church, and confined him to the monastery of St Bishoy in Wadi Natrun, the desert his own generation had repopulated. The sources do not say why Sadat acted that day. Shenouda went. He said no public word against the decree; the Holy Synod did not accept the committee; and the faithful kept his name in the liturgy. On 6 October Sadat was shot by Islamist officers, and his successor Hosni Mubarak left the patriarch where he was. Shenouda spent forty months in the desert writing; on 2 January 1985 Mubarak restored him, and he returned to a cathedral that could not hold the crowd. The sources do not say what moved Mubarak. He was still patriarch a quarter-century later; the church came to count the exile as the moment it learned that its patriarch could be removed by a president and could outlast him.

## tree/indonesian-christianity-the-independent-century
### Revised text
Over the twentieth century Indonesia came to hold one of the largest Christian populations in the Muslim-majority world. By 2010 its churches had been running themselves for most of a century. The Batak church, the HKBP, became independent in 1930 — the first self-governing church in the Dutch East Indies. The archipelago's Christianity is regional rather than national: Sumatra, Java, Timor and the eastern islands each have their own story. Two of the century's turning points fall close together. Between 1965 and 1971 a revival ran through Timor. Mel Tari, who took part in it, told its story in Like a Mighty Wind (1971). And after 1965, in a state that required every citizen to declare a religion, church membership grew very fast.
