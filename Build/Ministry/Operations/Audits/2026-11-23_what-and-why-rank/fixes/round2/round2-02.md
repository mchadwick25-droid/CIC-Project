## docstories/indonesian-christianity-the-independent-century-0
### Revised text
Soe is a market town in the hills of West Timor, and its congregation belonged to the GMIT, the Protestant Evangelical Church of Timor. On the evening of 26 September 1965 a service there passed into something the congregation had not seen: confession of sins aloud, weeping, people speaking of visions, and a fire some said they saw over the building. Over the following weeks lay teams formed, numbered as they went out, and walked to villages across the island, preaching, praying for the sick, and calling on people to burn the fetishes and charms of the older religion; the GMIT's records list the teams and where they went. The church grew fast. In 1971 Mel Tari, a young Timorese who had been on one of the teams, published in the United States Like a Mighty Wind, an account that included water turned to wine for communion, the dead raised, and teams crossing flooded rivers on foot. The church went on.

## docstories/armenian-christianity-after-avarayr-1
### Revised text
Vasak, prince of Siwnik, was marzpan — the Persian-appointed governor — of Armenia when the nobles rose against the order to convert to Zoroastrianism. He had at first joined the resistance; when it became clear that Byzantium would send no help and that the revolt could not win, he went over to the Persian side, took hostages from the rebel families, and worked to bring the country back under the king’s authority without a general massacre. At Avarayr he was on the Persian side of the field. Elishe, writing the church’s account of the war, made him the villain of the whole story: an apostate who ‘loved the world more than God’, whose treachery caused the defeat, and whose punishment followed — summoned to the Persian court, accused by the very nobles he had betrayed, stripped of his honors before the king, and left to die in prison, his body eaten, Elishe says, by worms while he still lived. Elishe says why he told it: “in order that all who hear this may know that punishment followed him, and that they may keep themselves far from his ways.” Lazar’s version is drier and lets the political calculation show. The Armenian church has kept him as its type of the traitor for fifteen centuries.

## docstories/st-thomas-christians-under-portuguese-pressure-d-1
### Revised text
Archbishop Aleixo de Menezes of Goa arrived in Malabar early in 1599 with soldiers, money and a determination that the Thomas Christians should have no bishop but one Rome sent. Through the spring he toured their churches, confirming and ordaining, on the reasoning that priests who owed their orders to him would owe him obedience. The Archdeacon George, who had governed the community since the death of its last East Syrian bishop, resisted, was threatened with excommunication and with Portuguese pressure on the pepper trade, and submitted. The synod met at Udayamperur, which the Portuguese called Diamper, from 20 to 26 June 1599, with some 150 priests and more than 600 lay representatives. Its decrees were read in Portuguese and Malayalam. They declared the patriarch of the East a heretic. They called the patriarchs Nestorians and heads of a cursed sect, and forbade priests to name him in the Mass. They condemned the prayers for Nestorius and Theodore. They forbade priests to marry after ordination and suspended those already married until they sent their wives away. They named seven sacraments. They forbade anyone to keep or read a list of Syriac books, among them the Book of the Fathers, the Book of Synods and the Book of Homilies. The synod said these books taught errors, such as that Mary should not be called the Mother of God. It ordered every Syriac book handed in, to be corrected or destroyed. Menezes then made a second circuit collecting manuscripts, and many were burned at the churches where they were found. The chronicler Gouvea, who wrote the archbishop's official account, presents this as rescue. Much of what is known about the community's worship before 1599 is what the synod condemned.
