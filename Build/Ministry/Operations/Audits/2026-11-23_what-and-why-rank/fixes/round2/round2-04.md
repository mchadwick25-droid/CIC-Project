## tree/church-of-caucasian-albania
### Revised text
Beside Armenia and Georgia there was a third Christian kingdom in the Caucasus. This was Albania, in what is now Azerbaijan and the south of Dagestan. It had its own royal line, its own bishops and its own alphabet. In the late fifth century it held a council at Aghuen, which made rules for the clergy and for marriage. It was pressed between Persian, Byzantine and then Arab power, and between the rival loyalties of its Armenian and Georgian neighbors. In the early 700s the Armenian catholicos Elia held a council at Partav, on the Arab ruler Abd al-Malik's order. He had Nerses Bakur, the Albanian catholicos, brought there. Stepanos of Taron, an Armenian writer of about the year 1000, calls Nerses a false catholicos who tried to make the Albanians accept the Council of Chalcedon. The Armenian church rejected that council for teaching two natures in Christ. The old sources do not say what followed. Its written language was all but lost for more than a thousand years. Then, in 1996, palimpsests at Saint Catherine's Monastery on Sinai were found to hold Caucasian Albanian scripture. The pages had been scraped and written over with later Georgian text.

## tree/the-liturgical-movement
### Revised text
In September 1909 Dom Lambert Beauduin, a monk of Mont-César in Louvain, spoke at a Catholic congress in Malines, Belgium. The journal Orate Fratres later reported what he said. He pointed to how little ordinary people knew of the liturgy, the Church's public worship. He named the harm this did and a remedy. In all his work he urged a revival of the liturgy for religious and social reasons, and to defend the faith. The movement began at once at his abbey, Mont-César, and spread rapidly. In 1926 Virgil Michel of Saint John's Abbey in Minnesota founded that journal, the movement's voice. He also set up the Liturgical Press. The journal's first number said it aimed at an understanding of worship that would shape the life of the individual Catholic and of the parish.

## worlds-tree/cappadocian-nicene-pastoral-monastic-tradition/legacy
### Revised text
The teachers of these churches did not stay local. Their names and arguments became curriculum, liturgy and law for later traditions. Those traditions trace their roots through the councils these churches lived through and helped write.

Orthodox, Catholic and, more distantly, Protestant teaching all still argue in vocabulary this circle fought to place. It is the vocabulary of one God in three persons. Basil set out two Greek terms in a letter to Count Terentius. Ousia, the essence, is common, like goodness or Godhead. Hypostasis is what is particular to each: Fatherhood, Sonship, the power to sanctify.
