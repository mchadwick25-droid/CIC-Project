## tree/latin-africa-under-byzantium
### Revised text
Belisarius destroyed the Vandal kingdom in 533. The Nicene church of Roman Africa came out of nearly a hundred years of Vandal rule and waves of persecution. Within twenty years it was among the most stubborn foes Justinian faced anywhere in his own empire.

Justinian condemned the Three Chapters: a letter of Ibas of Edessa and the writings of Theodore of Mopsuestia and Theodoret of Cyrus. The chronicler Victor of Tunnuna wrote that the opponents of Chalcedon urged him on, and that he acted in the name of winning back those who had split from the church. Victor also wrote that the Council of Chalcedon had approved the letter and praised the other two. The bishops of Africa said no. Facundus of Hermiane wrote twelve books defending them. Reparatus of Carthage refused, lost his see and was sent into exile. Victor spent years in prison and exile for the same stand.

Maximus the Confessor lived in Africa. In 645 he debated the ousted patriarch Pyrrhus there. Pyrrhus defended one will in Christ. Maximus held that a will belongs to a nature, so Christ has two. In 646 the councils of Africa rejected the one-will teaching, which the emperor Heraclius had issued as a statement of faith.

Then Arab armies reached Carthage in the 690s. The old African church, which had given the Latin church Tertullian, Cyprian and Augustine, was gone within a few generations.

## stories/hugeburc-of-heidenheim
### Revised text
Hugeburc (or Hygeburg) was an Anglo-Saxon nun who came to Germany in the wake of Boniface's mission and lived at Heidenheim, the double house founded by the West Saxon siblings Wynnebald and Walburga. Between about 776 and 786 she wrote Lives of Wynnebald and of his brother Willibald, bishop of Eichstätt. The Life of Willibald, known as the Hodoeporicon, is unlike anything else of its period: the old bishop dictated to her, in the presence of witnesses, the story of his ten-year pilgrimage in the 720s — Rome, Sicily, Cyprus, Emesa and Damascus under the caliphs, imprisonment as a suspected spy, Jerusalem's holy places, Tyre, Constantinople and Monte Cassino — and she wrote it down, calling herself only 'an unworthy Saxon woman' and apologizing for presuming to write. She also inserted a line of letters replaced by numbers. Read back into letters, it says 'Ego una Saxonica nomine Hugeburc ordinando hec scribebam' — 'I, a Saxon nun named Hugeburc, wrote this.' That makes her the earliest English woman known to have written a full-length work.

## tree/church-of-the-east-three-patriarchates
### Revised text
The Church of the East began these years with two rival patriarchs. The split went back to 1551. For almost a hundred years the office had passed inside one family, almost like an inheritance. Andreas Masius, a scholar of that century, wrote that the people resented this. When Patriarch Shimon bar Mama died, they feared his nephew would take the see by right of blood. They met and chose a new patriarch by the old rules, a monk named Sulaka. But the nephew, Denkha Shimon, took the see anyway.

Two lines followed. One lived in the mountains of Hakkari, at Kochanes. The other lived on the plain, at Alqosh and Mosul.

Each patriarch of the plain held an order from the Sultan, called a firman. It confirmed his power over his people in the empire. The mountain patriarchs had no such order. They ruled with their own people's consent, and the Kurdish emirs tolerated and protected them.

Rome then added a third line. Capuchin friars had begun preaching in Diyarbakır, Mosul and other towns of Mesopotamia. In 1657 Elia, the patriarch of the plain, sent Rome a statement of his faith, through their work. In 1681 Pope Innocent XI gave Joseph, the church's metropolitan at Diyarbakır, the pallium and the title of patriarch. Four more Josephs followed, five in all. Neither the old bishops nor the Ottoman government recognized the line at first, and it did not reach beyond Diyarbakır.

In 1778 the line of the plain began to turn to Rome. Patriarch Elia died of plague at Alqosh on 29 April. His nephew Hormizd, a metropolitan, wrote the next part himself: “So on the 30th of April I embraced all that the holy Church believes.” Another nephew, Yeshua-yau, was to be patriarch. Hormizd says Yeshua-yau had joined Rome and left it six times before, from love of money, and had joined this time only “hypocritically.” In May 1779 he turned back, and the two became enemies. In 1782 Pope Pius VI let Hormizd act as patriarch, but not use the patriarch's seal.

The submission of the patriarch of the plain to Rome came as early as 1778. The Diyarbakır see was then declared abolished, though the fifth Joseph kept his hold there until his death, about 1828.
