## tree/the-union-of-brest-and-ruthenian-greek-catholic-
### Revised text
In 1596 most of the Orthodox bishops of the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth met at Brest and accepted communion with Rome while keeping their Byzantine liturgy, Church Slavonic and married clergy. Their decree gives reasons: the church needs one head, built on Peter, and the patriarchs of Constantinople lived under the Turks. Their opponents met in the same town and rejected the union. They declared the bishops cast down for putting a church that answered to Constantinople under old Rome, against the holy canons. So the union produced two rival hierarchies claiming the same people and arguing in print for a century. The new church had to answer to Rome, to the Orthodox it had left, and to Latin bishops who never quite treated it as an equal. Josaphat Kuntsevych was killed at Vitebsk in 1623. His biographer Jakub Susza writes that two men burst in crying that he was a Latin and a papist.

## tree/the-bosnian-church
### Revised text
Between Catholic Hungary and Orthodox Serbia, medieval Bosnia had a church of its own. Its members called themselves krstjani, Christians. It used a Slavonic liturgy and had its own leaders. Rome condemned it as heretical again and again, and sent friars against it. In 1247 Pope Innocent IV wrote that the church of Bosnia had wholly fallen into heresy. In 1203 its leaders promised a papal envoy, with Ban Kulin present, to keep altars and crosses. They also promised to read both Testaments and to have priests for Mass and confession. In 1461 three Bosnians in Rome renounced what they called the errors of the Manichees. Bosnia's own rulers protected it and made use of it. The old sources do not set out what its members themselves taught. The one major document written from inside is the will of Gost Radin, made in 1466. It reads like ordinary Christianity, with a cross drawn down its margin. Bosnia fell to the Ottomans in 1463, and the church did not outlast the kingdom.

## docstories/indonesian-christianity-the-independent-century-1
### Revised text
In 1965 and the months after, people accused of belonging to the Communist Party were slaughtered. The army under Suharto led and licensed the killing. The killers were soldiers and militias, some organized through Muslim youth organizations and some, in a few places, through Christian ones. The new order then required every citizen to hold one of the recognized religions; not to have one was to be suspected of atheism, which was to be suspected of communism. In the years that followed, the Protestant and Catholic churches of Java received people in numbers they had never seen. The churches knew why some had come. Pastors' accounts from those years speak of villages associated with the party whose survivors turned to a church that had not taken part in the killing and did not ask questions, and of catechism classes that were also, plainly, refuge. They also speak of people who stayed.
