## worlds-tree/donatism/docstory-don.story.bagai-reconciliation
### Revised text
In 393 a deacon named Maximian offended Primian, the Donatist primate at Carthage. Primian cut him off from communion. Maximian accused him before the nearby bishops. They came to Carthage and summoned Primian. He refused, and they condemned him in his absence. At Cebarsussi they made Maximian rival primate. Augustine saw in it the act the Donatist founders had once taken against Caecilian.

The answer came the next year at Bagai. Three hundred and ten Donatist bishops met there and condemned the followers of Maximian in the harshest terms they had. Augustine quotes the decree word for word. It calls them shipwrecked men, dashed by the waves of truth onto the sharp rocks. Their bodies cover the shore, it says, and they cannot even find a grave.

Later the Donatist party took back two of those condemned bishops, Felicianus of Musti and Praetextatus of Assuris. Neither was baptized again or ordained again. Optatus Gildonianus, one of the Donatist bishops, used soldiers to enforce their return.

The Donatists taught that baptism outside their church counted for nothing. Yet these two had baptized while in schism, and those baptisms were not repeated. Augustine made this the center of his case. If they stood, he argued, the rule failed even for the Donatists.

## worlds-tree/lutheran-wittenberg-and-its-congregations/docstory-witt.story.augsburg-before-cajetan
### Revised text
Years later, at his own table, Luther told his students how he was summoned to Augsburg in the summer of 1518. He had attacked the sale of indulgences, and the Pope's legate, Cardinal Cajetan, was to hear his case. Frederick, the Elector of Saxony, gave him a strong escort and a safe-conduct, and he was warned not to trust the Italians.

He waited three days for the Emperor's own safe-conduct. Meanwhile an Italian led him to Cardinal Cajetan, urging him the whole way to say one word, Revoco, which means I recant. Say it, the man promised, and the Cardinal would commend him to the Pope's favor.

Before the Cardinal, Luther said, he knelt and then went all the way down to the ground. He stayed there until Cajetan told him three times to rise. The Cardinal was pleased, he said, and hoped Luther would think better of things.

The next day Luther came again and would not recant anything. Writing to Carlstadt, he named two points. An indulgence, he said, is not the treasure of Christ's merits. And whoever comes to the Lord's table must believe.

In Luther's memory, the Cardinal asked whether he thought the Pope cared for Germany, or that princes would raise armies to defend him. Where, Cajetan asked, would he be safe then?

"Under Heaven," Luther answered.

## docstories/persian-church-of-the-east-early-1
### Revised text
Sozomen, following the Syriac Acts, says that Tarbula, Symeon’s sister, a consecrated virgin, was arrested with her sister and her maid when the queen fell ill, on a charge of having caused the illness by sorcery in revenge for the bishop. Sozomen writes that ‘the Jews’ brought the accusation, and that the queen, who had embraced their customs and kept Jewish rites, believed it. Tarbula was reportedly offered her life by a Magian judge who desired her, and refused. The three were then executed in a manner meant to work as a counter-charm: their bodies were sawn in half and the halves hung on stakes on either side of a road, and the queen was carried between them to break the spell. The account is short and terrible, and it belongs to a genre — the Persian martyr acts — that shaped the Church of the East’s memory of itself for a thousand years: a minority under a Zoroastrian crown, suspected of loyalty to Rome, its women accused of magic, its clergy killed at Easter. The Syriac Acts, where she is called Tarbo, tell the same story in the same order. The queen falls ill, and the Jews say the sisters of Symeon have bewitched her because their brother was killed. Tarbula, her sister and her maid are seized.
