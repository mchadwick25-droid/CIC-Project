## tree/catholic-church-ancien-regime
### Revised text
This was the Catholic Church at the height of its establishment and on the edge of its worst crisis: enormously wealthy, woven into the monarchies of Europe, and increasingly unable to control them. Rome's condemnation of Jansenism in the bull Unigenitus (1713) turned into a fifty-year political crisis in France. Then the kings of Portugal, France, Spain and Sicily drove out the Jesuits. In 1773 Clement XIV abolished the Society. His bull names quarrels, riots, charges against Jesuit teaching, and greed for property. The kings, it says, wished to stop Christians rising against one another. Then came the Revolution: church property nationalized, thousands killed or driven abroad, and Pope Pius VI carried off to die a French prisoner in 1799. His successor signed a concordat with Napoleon in 1801. In 1809 Napoleon annexed the Papal States, saying that spiritual power joined to rule over land had bred quarrels. He kept the pope captive until 1814. The pope outlived him and restored the Jesuits that year.

Rome ran its missions through Propaganda, the congregation for spreading the faith. Its collected decrees show three acts in these years.

In 1656 the Holy Office answered questions sent from China in 1645. Christian scholars could join the ceremony in the hall of Confucius when they took a degree. That cult, it said, seemed merely civil and political. Converts could also keep rites for their dead, once anything superstitious was removed.

In 1659 Propaganda sent orders to its vicars apostolic. They were not to urge any people to change its rites, customs and ways. What is more absurd, it asked, than to carry France, Spain or Italy into China (in a plain English rendering)?

In 1726 Propaganda ruled that native priests of China, Tonkin and Cochinchina must swear an oath. It was the one set by the constitution Ex illa die. That text seemed to name only Europeans. The oath bound a priest to obey the pope's order on the Chinese rites, without evasion.

## tree/japans-hidden-christians
### Revised text
In 1614 Japan banned Christianity, and the entry before this one tells how it began. That year Valentin Carvalho, who led the Japanese church after its bishop died, wrote to the pope to say why. Enemies of the faith, he writes, told the ruler that Christians honored men who defied their lords. The ruler called the Christian law diabolical and ordered every missionary out of Japan. He adds that the ruler and many nobles believed the missionaries meant to seize the kingdom.

In 1666 the government at Edo set up an inquiry in every town and village to learn what each family believed. Once a year every Japanese had to tread on a cross or an image of the Virgin and the saints. This was the e-fumi, the treading of the image. In September 1865 village chiefs told Father Petitjean that in many villages the cross had not been trampled for ages.

On Easter Tuesday Domingo, the baptizer of Urakami, told Petitjean how the faith was kept. Each day the family prayed together, the father leading for a week, then the mother, then each child in turn. Domingo said his son, a young man of twenty-five, would take his place as baptizer.

In 1865 the missionaries studied the papers the villagers had kept.

They included prayer books, written baptism formulas and Christian calendars.

One treatise, Contrition, was dated 1603 and written wholly in Japanese. The baptizer Domingo brought it to the missionaries. On 17 March 1865, at about half past twelve, twelve to fifteen people stood at the door of the church of the Twenty-Six Martyrs in Nagasaki.

Three women knelt beside Father Petitjean. One said, with her hand on her heart, that the hearts of all of them were the same as his. Then she asked, "Sancta Maria no gozowa doko?", "Where is the image of Holy Mary?" (our rendering). That spring Petitjean wrote to the Paris seminary that 3,800 Christians had been found around Nagasaki.

Two fathers of families, Pierre and Sébastien, had told him, "In our mountains we are about fifteen hundred Christians" (our rendering).

## tree/moravian-church-after-1815
### Revised text
The Moravian Church did not stop in 1815. Its mission fields went on, and new ones opened.

In Jamaica the church ran twenty-six schools by March 1834. On 1 August 1834 a law of Parliament ended slavery throughout the British colonies. An apprenticeship of four or six years, according to the kind of work, came first. The Baptist missionary James Phillippo, who lived on the island, wrote that the day was 'welcomed and celebrated throughout the island with high and holy joy'. By the end of 1835 the church had 8,521 members on the island. That was 1,339 more than a year before.

At the Cape the Moravian mission began again in 1792. It had 2,386 members when slavery was ended there on 1 December 1834. The slaves of the colony were fully free on 1 December 1838. In the year after that, Genadendal alone took in four hundred and fifty people. The church weighed each person's case on its own merits before taking them in. The sources do not say what the test was.

In Suriname slavery ended by royal proclamation on 1 July 1863. About two-thirds of all the slaves had already been in connection with the Moravian Church. By 1869 the church counted 24,330 members in the colony.

In Labrador the winter of 1836 to 1837 brought a famine, with misery worst at Nain, Okak and Hebron. The church at Hebron was dedicated in October 1837.

In the early 1890s four missionaries went to the country north of Lake Nyasa, and they founded Rungwe among the Konde people. On 7 February 1897 a woman named Fiabarema stood up after a farewell sermon at Rungwe. She said, 'I have risen to say that I belong to God.' She was baptized that evening. Within nine years the church manned four stations there and counted more than one hundred converts.
