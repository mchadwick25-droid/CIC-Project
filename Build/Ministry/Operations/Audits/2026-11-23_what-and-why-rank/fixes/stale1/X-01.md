## docstories/antiochene-church-third-century-0
### Revised text
Lucian was a presbyter of Antioch who spent his life on the text of the Greek Bible, producing a revision of the Septuagint and Gospels used across the Greek East for centuries. He was also, on the later testimony of his pupils, a teacher whose circle — Arius and Eusebius of Nicomedia among them — called themselves ‘fellow-Lucianists’ long after he was dead. What the record preserves about the man himself is the manner of his death. Eusebius, writing within a few years, says the emperor Maximinus had again turned his governors against the Christians. Lucian was brought before him at Nicomedia, spoke in defense of the doctrine he professed, and was put in prison and killed. Jerome adds that he died for confessing Christ. Rufinus, who translated Eusebius into Latin, gives the speech Lucian is said to have made. Christians worship one God, he says, made known through Christ. Christ died and rose again, and the sun went dark at his death, as the court’s own records would show. Lucian was then ordered to prison and killed there, away from any crowd. A later notice puts it on 7 January 312 and adds that he had been held for nine years. Later still comes the story that, chained on his back in the cell and forbidden the sacrament, he celebrated the Eucharist on his own chest for the Christians imprisoned with him. The scholar is documented; the confessor is attested; the prisoner-priest is devotion. All three belong to how Antioch remembered him, and the awkward fact that the most notorious heresy of the next century traced itself to his classroom did not stop the church from keeping his feast.

## docstories/syriac-orthodox-west-syriac-christianity-1
### Revised text
The same John of Ephesus who chronicled the persecution of his own church by the Chalcedonian emperors accepted a commission from one of them. In Justinian’s reign he was appointed teacher of the pagans in the four provinces of Asia, Caria, Phrygia and Lydia. John reports the results himself. In the Lives he counts eighty thousand people converted from paganism in those provinces. He also counts ninety-eight churches and twelve monasteries founded, and seven more churches made from Jewish synagogues. His Ecclesiastical History gives ninety-nine new churches and twelve monasteries over the thirty-five years his colleague Deuterius worked with him. His deacons, he writes, uprooted altars, destroyed shrines and cut down trees. At Derira, in the mountains near Tralles, he leveled a famous temple to its foundations and built his chief monastery on the site. Justinian paid for it and for the other monasteries and churches. Elsewhere he records with approval the later trials in Constantinople of people accused of secret pagan worship, some of whom were put to death. Yet his own church suffered persecution in Justinian’s time, and he would later be imprisoned under Justin II, when his bishops would not accept the council of Chalcedon. That council, in 451, taught that Christ is one person in two natures, and it adopted Pope Leo’s letter. John’s side held that this divided the Lord in two. Its bishops told the patriarch they would curse the council and Leo’s letter until their last breath. His own pages record both: the persecution his church suffered, and the campaign he led for an emperor against the old religion.

## docstories/the-church-of-the-east-under-persia-1
### Revised text
The School of Nisibis had written rules for its students’ day, their examinations and their discipline. For a century it had trained the clergy of the Church of the East in one way of reading Scripture. It was the way of Theodore of Mopsuestia, ‘the Interpreter’, and his commentaries were the school’s course of study. Around 572 Henana of Adiabene became its head. All accounts call him brilliant, and he began to teach differently. He leaned on Chrysostom rather than Theodore, and he allowed allegory. Babai the Great, who fought him, wrote that Henana taught that fate and the stars rule everything, so that no one is free. Babai wrote that he said all people share God’s nature, as Origen taught, and that Christ has one nature. The school split. Barhadbshabba, writing the school’s own history while Henana still taught, praises him. The church’s synods of 585 and 596 forbade anyone to reject Theodore’s writings. The synod of 596 also condemned those who say that people sin against their will, or that Adam was made immortal. The acts never name Henana. The Chronicle of Seert tells it as a fight inside Nisibis. Gregory, metropolitan of the city, condemned Henana’s books, and Henana sought help from the king’s physicians. In the Chronicle the catholicos Sabrisho takes Henana’s side. Yet Sabrisho’s own synod of 596 had upheld Theodore. The Chronicle says the students were moved by Sabrisho’s choice of Henana’s word over Gregory’s. About three hundred of them walked out. Only twenty people were left in the school, and scarcely as many children. The exiles founded or strengthened schools elsewhere: at Balad, at Seleucia, and above all at the monastery of Izla. There Babai the Great wrote the Life of the martyr George, in which George answers Henana’s followers. Christ, he says, is two natures and two hypostases, each keeping its own properties, joined in one person. The synod of 605 formally bound the church to Theodore. Nisibis never fully won back its standing. This is the Church of the East’s own account of how it settled its teaching: not by council alone, but by students voting with their feet.
