## docstories/coptic-christianity-under-early-islam-0
### Revised text
In the Hijra year 216, Makrizi says, the Copts rose in revolt, and the general Afshin led troops against them. He could not subdue them until the caliph al-Ma’mun came to Egypt himself. Eutychius of Alexandria calls the rebels the Bimaidae, of the lower part of Egypt. They refused, he says, to pay their tax and tribute, and al-Ma’mun sent his brother al-Mu’tasim against them before coming himself. Bar Hebraeus, who had the history of the Syriac patriarch Dionysius of Tel Mahre before him, tells what the two church leaders did. Al-Ma’mun ordered Dionysius to come with him to Egypt, to send him as envoy to the Christians of the lower country and turn them from rebellion. Dionysius went with Joseph, patriarch of Egypt, to reconcile them. But Afshin refused peace, and Bar Hebraeus gives no reason. He burned their villages, vineyards and gardens and killed a great many. The rest he sent in chains by ship to Antioch and on to Baghdad, and many died on the way. Dionysius went back and told the caliph that they were being crushed. Makrizi says al-Ma’mun heard the case, condemned many to death and had the women and children sold, so that a great multitude went into slavery. From then on, he adds, the Copts were so broken that they never again dared to rise against the Muslims.

## tree/byzantine-imperial-church-heraclian-iconoclast
### Revised text
This is the imperial church of the empire's worst century and a half. Heraclius recovered the True Cross from Persia in 630 and within a decade had lost Syria, Palestine and Egypt to the Arab armies. His government's attempt to hold the remaining eastern Christians together with a compromise formula — that Christ had two natures but only one will — was pushed as state policy and resisted by a monk, Maximus, who was tried, had his tongue and right hand cut off, and died in exile. In 680 a council at Constantinople condemned the formula and taught that Christ has two wills, though it did not name him. Then came iconoclasm: emperors from Leo III onward removed and destroyed religious images, which they held to be idolatry, as John of Damascus recorded in his answer to them. A council of 338 bishops endorsed the policy in 754, the Council of Nicaea reversed it in 787, a second imperial iconoclasm followed, and only in 843 was the matter closed for good. Through all of it the same institution — palace, patriarchate, councils — kept running, which is why this era looks so different depending on whether you stand with the court or with the monasteries.

## docstories/syriac-orthodox-under-islam-1
### Revised text
Dionysius of Tel Mahre had been a monk of Kenneshre. In 818, by his own account, two monks seized him and held him like a criminal until the bishops came, and he was made patriarch against his tears and protests. He learned to deal with the Abbasid court. In 829 the caliph al-Ma’mun let him come to him alone in his garden, and there ruled that the church could depose a sinful bishop, though it could not shut him out of prayer. Dionysius wrote a history of his own times, which survives only in passages that later writers kept, and Bar Hebraeus tells most of his travels. When al-Ma’mun went to Egypt, Bar Hebraeus says, he ordered the patriarch to go with him, meaning to send him as envoy to the Christians called Byamites, in Lower Egypt, to turn them from rebellion. Dionysius went with Joseph, the patriarch of Egypt, to reconcile them. But Afshin, the general, refused peace, and Bar Hebraeus gives no reason. He burned their villages, vineyards and gardens and killed a great many; the rest he sent in chains by ship to Antioch and on to Baghdad, and many died on the way. Dionysius went back to al-Ma’mun, told him that the Byamites were being crushed, and then took his leave for Damascus. Bar Hebraeus also quotes what Dionysius found in Egypt. The Copts, he wrote, were a chaste and sincere people, who gave him every honor due to their own pope, but the study of Scripture had been taken from them, and no one reached the office of bishop without two or three hundred gold coins. He saw the pyramids and judged them not Joseph’s granaries, as some thought, but the tombs of ancient kings, solid and not hollow. Bar Hebraeus keeps only a few passages in Dionysius’s own words. The rest of the journey is Bar Hebraeus’s telling.
