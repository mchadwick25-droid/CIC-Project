## tree/byzantine-imperial-church-macedonian
### Revised text
With the icons restored in 843, the Byzantine church entered two centuries of confidence. It recovered its learning and sent missions that converted Bulgaria, Moravia and eventually Rus'. Photius is the age's emblem: a layman raised straight to the patriarchate, an enormous reader who summarized 279 books, by his own count, many of which survive nowhere else, and the man who put in its sharpest form the Eastern objection to the Latin West's addition of one clause, the Filioque, to the Creed. That argument, along with disputes over papal authority and over who had jurisdiction among the newly converted Slavs, hardened across the following century and a half, until in 1054 a papal delegation and the patriarch of Constantinople excommunicated one another inside Hagia Sophia.

## tree/bagratid-armenian-christianity
### Revised text
After two centuries under Arab governors, an Armenian kingdom was restored under the Bagratid dynasty in the 880s. With it came a burst of church building and writing. The catholicos, the head of the Armenian church, moved his seat to the royal capital of Ani. Its cathedral, finished around 1001 by the architect Trdat, still stands roofless on the plain. Stepanos of Taron, who wrote about the year 1000, adds that an earthquake split Hagia Sophia in Constantinople. Greek architects struggled to repair it. Trdat, an Armenian who happened to be there, drew up the plan and directed the first work. The period's most honored voice, though, was not an architect but a monk. Gregory of Narek wrote a book of ninety-five prayers, called the Narek after his monastery. Armenians used it for private prayer. They held it a powerful protection against all kinds of dangers. The kingdom ended in 1045, when Byzantium annexed Ani. The Seljuk Turks took the city in 1064.

## docstories/bagratid-armenian-christianity-1
### Revised text
Gregory was raised from infancy in the convent of Narek, in the land of the Rushtunians, and in time became its abbot. Armenian tradition tells how the book came to be. Gregory long prayed to see, with his own eyes, the Virgin holding the Son of God, and at last was granted the sight. Then, filled with the Holy Spirit, he composed the wonderful book of prayers called Narek. It holds ninety-five prayers. The whole Armenian nation held the book in the highest regard, and it was still used in church services in the 1700s. Two of Gregory's prayers to the Holy Spirit stand in the Armenian Liturgy. In the first, the people assembled before God pray, 'we implore Thee with out-stretched arms and deep groans.' Armenians also used the Narek for private prayer. They held it a powerful protection against all kinds of dangers. The book still carries the name of the house that raised him.
