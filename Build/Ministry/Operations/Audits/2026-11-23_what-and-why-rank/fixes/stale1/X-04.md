## docstories/makurian-nubian-christianity-0
### Revised text
The History of the Patriarchs tells it in the life of Michael I, patriarch of Alexandria from 744, and its writer says he shared the patriarch’s prison. A new governor of Egypt, Abd al-Malik, son of Musa, son of Nasir, hated the Christians greatly, the History says, and caused them much trouble. He demanded money from the patriarch. Then he seized Michael and held him. Kyriakos, king of Makuria, was ‘the Great King’, the History says, with thirteen kings under him. He marched north with a great army: a hundred thousand horsemen, with a hundred thousand horses and a hundred thousand camels. A man who saw them told the writer that their horses were small, no higher than asses, and fought with their hooves as their riders fought on their backs. The Nubians plundered the Muslims of Upper Egypt and came on toward Misr, camping at a pool still called the Pool of the Ethiopians. The king had sent his Eparch ahead to demand the patriarch’s release, and the governor jailed the envoy with Michael. Then, afraid and unable to resist, he freed the Eparch to talk the king into going home. The Eparch told Kyriakos that the patriarch had been released and kindly treated. Michael wrote letters of peace to the king, blessing him and his men, and the king went home without a battle. The chronicle presents it as the Nubian church’s loyalty to its mother church in action. What the Coptic church recorded was that when its head was in chains, the only Christian power that could do anything about it was an African kingdom on the upper Nile, and that it did.

## docstories/athonite-and-comnenian-byzantine-monasticism-0
### Revised text
Athanasios was a monk on Mount Athos when the general Nikephoros Phokas begged him to build a lavra, a house of monks, where Nikephoros himself might one day become a monk. When Nikephoros became emperor in 963, he asked only that the church not be left half built. His charter made the house self-governing, under its own abbot. Later, two monks of the Mountain went to the emperor John in the capital. The emperor’s own charter says they complained of long quarrels with Athanasios, and that some men were being cut off and wronged by him. John sent Euthymios, a monk of the Stoudios monastery. After a week of hearings, the charter says, both sides were found blameless. The house was meant for eighty monks, and the number rose to a hundred and twenty when the emperor John raised its yearly grant. Athanasios died at work on his monastery. One Life tells the end. A monk of the Lavra wrote it under his successor. It says Athanasios and six of his companions set out to fit the keystone into the vault of a dome. The dome fell in and buried them, and they were pulled from the rubble dead. The man whose rule became the pattern for the other houses of Athos died under the weight of a monastery.

## docstories/the-quakers-religious-society-of-friends-0
### Revised text
James Nayler, a Yorkshire farmer who had served in the parliamentary army, was by 1656 the best-known Quaker preacher in London. On 24 October 1656 he rode into Bristol in pouring rain on a horse led by a man with his head bare, while a small group of women, Martha Simmonds and Hannah Stranger among them, walked before him through the mud singing ‘Holy, holy, holy, Lord God of Sabaoth’ and spreading garments in the way. He was arrested at once. A Baptist church’s record that year says he was ‘professing himself to be Christ’, with many doing him homage and crying ‘hosanna’, and that he was proclaimed a blasphemer. Parliament took the case itself and debated it for ten days in December; the motion to put him to death failed by 96 votes to 82. The sentence was that he be pilloried and whipped through the streets of London, 310 lashes, his tongue bored through with a hot iron and his forehead branded B for blasphemer, and then carried to Bristol to ride into the city again facing his horse's tail and be whipped there, and be kept in solitary confinement at Bridewell at hard labor. He was released in 1659, broken in health. Fox had refused Nayler’s kiss at Exeter, he wrote, because Nayler ‘had turned against the power of God’. Now he received him coldly; Nayler knelt to him. In October 1660, robbed and beaten on the road home to Yorkshire, he died at a Friend's house near King's Ripton, and his last words were written down: ‘There is a spirit which I feel, that delights to do no evil, nor to revenge any wrong.’

- "he rode into Bristol in pouring rain on a horse led by a man with his head bare" (what the ride was meant to be, why blasphemy): told from an old witness | Added the Baptist church record of 1656, which says Nayler was "professing himself to be Christ", that many did him homage and cried "hosanna", and that he was proclaimed a blasphemer; the ride's own details are left as they were. | source: cic/texts/fenstanton-church_records-of-fenstanton-warboys-hexham_underhill1854.txt:14451-14456 (the church is the Baptist church of Warboys; the entry stands among the 1656 records and before the 1657 heading at 14469)

The quoted words "had turned against the power of God" are exact. | source: cic/texts/fox_journal-v1_ellwood-text-pickard1901.txt:14426-14431
