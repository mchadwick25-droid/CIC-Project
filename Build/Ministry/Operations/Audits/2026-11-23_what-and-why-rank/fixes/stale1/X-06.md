## docstories/anglo-saxon-christianity-2
### Revised text
Willibald, who wrote Boniface’s Life, tells how the missionary worked among the Hessians. Some of them still sacrificed to trees and springs, he says, some in secret and some openly. Bishop Daniel of Winchester wrote to Boniface with advice. Let the pagans tell how their gods were born, Daniel urged. Then show that gods who were born had a beginning. Ask them calmly who made the world, and let them blush for their own opinions. But Willibald tells of a bolder act. With the advice of Hessians who had given up pagan practice, Boniface set out to fell a huge oak at a place called Geismar. Its old pagan name was the Oak of Jupiter. A great crowd of pagans gathered, cursing him in their hearts. After only a little cutting, Willibald says, the tree crashed down and split into four equal trunks. The crowd, he writes, then believed and blessed the Lord. From the timber Boniface built a wooden oratory and dedicated it to Saint Peter. Willibald also names monasteries he founded, at Ohrdruf, and beside his churches at Fritzlar and Amöneburg. Boniface was killed in Frisia in 754. The Frankish annals say he was preaching the word of the Lord there and became a martyr of Christ. Willibald says a mob of pagans struck him down. The English church that had received the Gospel from Rome and Iona sent it back across the sea.

## docstories/carolingian-reform-christianity-0
### Revised text
The Royal Frankish Annals, the court’s own record, state it in a sentence. In 782 the Saxons destroyed a Frankish army in the Süntel hills, and Charlemagne came to the place where the Aller flows into the Weser. There the Saxons handed over to him the men behind the revolt, four thousand five hundred of them, to be put to death, and so it was done. Only Widukind, whose counsel had stirred the Saxons to war, escaped, fleeing to the Northmen. The revised version of the annals adds that they were all beheaded in one day, at a place called Verden. The Capitulary for the Saxon Regions, from about these years, set out the terms of the conquest. It ordered that the new churches in Saxony have no less honor, but more, than the idols’ shrines had had. It gave death to anyone who burned a church, killed a bishop or priest, or burned the dead in the pagan way. It gave death, too, to a Saxon who hid unbaptized, scorned to come to baptism and chose to stay a pagan. The same law made every Saxon, noble, free or half-free, give a tenth of his goods and his labor to the churches. Both texts are the court’s own: the record of the killing, and the law for the conquered.

## docstories/carolingian-reform-christianity-1
### Revised text
Dhuoda was married to Bernard of Septimania, one of the great lords in the civil war among Charlemagne’s grandsons. In 841, Nithard says, Bernard sent their son William, then fifteen, to Charles the Bald. He told the boy to commend himself to the king, if Charles would grant him Bernard’s holdings in Burgundy. Their second son, born that year, was taken from her before he was baptized and carried off to his father. She did not know his name. Left alone at Uzès, she wrote a book for William and had it copied and sent to him. She calls it a handbook — Liber manualis. She writes, she says, ‘though I am unworthy and frail’, because she cannot be with him, and a mother’s words may do what her presence cannot. She tells William to love God and to honor his father in all things. He is to be loyal to the king, to read, and to pray for his father’s dead kin, whom she lists by name. She quotes Scripture again and again, with lines of the poet Prudentius and the grammarian Donatus, and she includes acrostic verses spelling out her name and his. She asks him to teach his little brother when he is old enough, and to have her epitaph, which she composes, carved on her grave. Bernard, the Annals of St-Bertin say, was judged guilty of treason by the Franks and put to death in Aquitaine in 844, on Charles’s order. The same annals tell of William’s end. In 850, in the Spanish March, he seized two counts by trickery, but was himself caught by a deeper trick and killed at Barcelona. The book shows, from inside a Frankish household, what the reform’s program of Christian formation looked like when a mother had to do it alone by post.
