## docstories/syriac-orthodox-under-islam-0
### Revised text
Jacob came from ‘Ayndaba, a village in the country of Antioch. He studied Greek at the monastery of Kenneshre and gathered learning in Alexandria. The patriarch Athanasius of Balad made him bishop of Edessa. After four years he was in trouble with clergy he had barred for their disorders. Before he left his see, Michael the Syrian says, he argued with the patriarch Julian and the bishops over keeping the church’s canons. No one listened, and all told him to bend to the times. So he brought the book of canons and burned it at the door of the patriarch’s monastery. He cried aloud that he was burning, as useless, the canons they trampled underfoot and did not keep. Then he gave up his see and withdrew to the monastery of Mar Jacob at Kaisum. There he wrote two tracts of reproach, one against the church’s shepherds and one against those who break its canons. For eleven years the monks of Eusebona had him teach Greek, whose study had died out there. Brothers who hated the Greeks made trouble for him, so he moved to the great monastery of Tell ‘Ada. There he spent nine years correcting the Old Testament. In about 701 he also revised the Syriac of Severus of Antioch’s sermons against the Greek. When the next bishop of Edessa died, the city called him back. He held the see four months, then went to fetch his books and pupils, and died at Tell ‘Ada. Bar Hebraeus puts his death on 5 June in the year 1019 of the Greeks. One place in Michael’s manuscript reads 1015, four years earlier. His canons live on in part, quoted under his name in Bar Hebraeus’s book of church law. The story is the record of a scholar who expected the church’s law to mean what it said, and of a church that let him go rather than agree.

## docstories/makurian-nubian-christianity-1
### Revised text
Under an old treaty between Muslim Egypt and Christian Makuria, Nubia owed the caliph a yearly tribute of slaves. The Muslim historian al-Baladhuri says ‘Abdallah ibn Sa‘d, who made peace with the Nubians, set it at 400 slaves a year. The caliph al-Mahdi later fixed it at 360. In the time of the patriarch Joseph, Abu Salih says, Ibrahim, brother of the caliph al-Ma’mun, wrote to King Zacharias of Nubia for the tribute owed for fourteen years. The History of the Patriarchs has the governor of Egypt write instead. The king could not pay. He feared, that history says, that raiders would overrun his country if he left it, so he sent his son Georgios, his chosen heir. Georgios came to Egypt, where the patriarch Joseph received him kindly, and went on to the caliph at Baghdad. Abu Salih says Ibrahim rejoiced to see him. The king had sent the son he held dearest of all, and the son had gone into exile at his father’s word. The History of the Patriarchs says the caliph was so pleased by the prince’s respect that he remitted the arrears and sent him back to Egypt loaded with honors, gifts and fine robes. Georgios carried a gold cross in his hand, and everyone kissed it. In Egypt he asked the patriarch to consecrate a wooden altar for him, so that the liturgy could be said wherever the king’s camp moved. Bishops from Nubia traveled with him. When they said the liturgy, he had bells rung on the roof of the fortress where he lodged, to the great joy of the Christians. The episode is the one in which a Nubian Christian ruler is seen from outside as a diplomat rather than a raider, and in which the terms under which a Christian kingdom lived beside the caliphate were settled face to face by the people who had to keep them.

## docstories/athonite-and-comnenian-byzantine-monasticism-1
### Revised text
Symeon, called the New Theologian, was abbot of Saint Mamas in Constantinople, and he taught his monks that God could be seen as light. His disciple Niketas Stethatos wrote his Life. Symeon kept a yearly feast for his own teacher, Symeon the Pious, a monk of Studios, with hymns and an icon. The patriarch Sergius read the hymns, Niketas says, and praised them. Sixteen years on, Stephen, once metropolitan of Nicomedia and now the patriarch’s aide, began to attack the feast. Niketas puts it all down to envy. A synod had the word saint blotted out on the teacher’s icon. When Symeon would not stop, the synod sentenced him to exile, and he was deported to Chrysopolis, across the Bosporus. Later he was recalled and offered honors, even a metropolitan’s see, if he would give way. He refused, and he was left free to honor his teacher as he wished. Sergius told the nobles, in words Niketas reports, that Symeon had not been punished for any error in the church’s teaching. What the record shows is a monk exiled by his own church for refusing to give up the memory of the man who had taught him, and a hierarchy that found the feast easier to attack than the claim that God could be seen.
