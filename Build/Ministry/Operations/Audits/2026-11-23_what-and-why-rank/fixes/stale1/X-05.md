## tree/the-christian-right
### Revised text
Jerry Falwell, Tim LaHaye, Charles Stanley, D. James Kennedy and Greg Dixon founded the Moral Majority on 6 June 1979. It named four principles of its own: it was pro-life, pro-family, pro-morals and pro-American. From its base in Washington, it worked in politics for those aims. In August 1980 Falwell called it entirely political, not a religious group. Its backers, he said, were not all evangelicals. That month Ronald Reagan spoke in Dallas, at a briefing run by another group, the Religious Roundtable. He told the crowd that he endorsed them and what they were doing. In 1989 Pat Robertson founded the Christian Coalition, with Ralph Reed as its executive director. Reed went on to found the Faith and Freedom Coalition in 2009.

## docstories/palestinian-ascetic-monasticism-early-0
### Revised text
Jerome’s Life of Hilarion is full of cures, but one episode is different because it is about a town. Italicus, a Christian of Maiuma, the port of Gaza, kept racehorses for the circus against a rival who was a magistrate of the city and a devotee of Marnas, Gaza’s god. Hilarion’s first reaction, Jerome says, was to tell him to give the horses’ price to the poor and be done with it; Italicus answered that the races were a public duty of his office, not a pleasure, and that as a Christian he could not use magic himself, so he had come to a servant of Christ, ‘especially against the people of Gaza who were enemies of God, and who would exult over the Church of Christ more than over him’. The old man was persuaded. He had an earthenware cup filled with the water he drank from and gave it to Italicus, who sprinkled his stable, his horses, his charioteers and the starting gates with it. On race day the city turned out; Italicus’ team won at every turn; the crowd shouted that Marnas had been beaten by Christ, and — Jerome reports — a good many of the spectators asked for baptism.

## docstories/coptic-christianity-under-early-islam-1
### Revised text
Samuel of Qalamun was a seventh-century abbot who had resisted the Chalcedonian governor Cyrus and been tortured for it; a later writer put into his mouth an apocalypse foretelling what would happen to Egypt’s Christians under the Arabs. Christians, the monk is made to say, will abandon the beautiful language of Coptic in which the Holy Spirit spoke through the fathers; they will teach their children Arabic and boast of it; they will take Arab names, swear by the names of Arab rulers, marry among the Muslims, and in the churches the people will no longer understand the readings. The lament is explicit that this is not persecution: no one forces them to do this, the text says — they choose it, for advancement and ease. Most bitterly, it foresees that the Copts will forget the names of the martyrs and the sayings of the fathers because they can no longer read them. The apocalypse survives in Arabic. By the eleventh century the History of the Patriarchs was being compiled in Arabic, and a bishop, Severus ibn al-Muqaffa‘, was explaining that he wrote theology in Arabic because his flock no longer understood anything else. The tradition survived the change; the text records what the change cost.
