## docstories/the-church-of-the-east-on-the-silk-road-0
### Revised text
Timothy came from Hazza in Adiabene and was bishop of Beth Beghash when the head of the Church of the East died. Elias of Nisibis dates his enthronement to a Sunday in 780. Mari ibn Sulayman says the church was split over whom to choose. Timothy won, Mari says, by a trick: he promised money to the heads of the school and showed them sacks of stones as if they held coins. Eight months later the bishops ordained him. Timothy himself writes that those who stayed away cut themselves off from him for about two years. He lived, Mari adds, under five caliphs: al-Mahdi, al-Hadi, Harun, al-Amin and al-Ma’mun. ‘Abdisho of Nisibis lists among his works a disputation with the caliph al-Mahdi, but its text is not among the sources used here. Amr ibn Matta says the caliph al-Hadi often called Timothy in to talk about religion. Amr also tells that Harun al-Rashid once asked him to say in few words which religion God approves. The one, Timothy answered, whose laws and commands agree with the reason God keeps in dealing with his creatures. The caliph said it was a full answer, and no objection could be raised to it. The same patriarch looked far beyond Baghdad. Thomas of Marga, writing about 840, tells how he made monks metropolitans of Gilan and Dailom, peoples no preacher had reached since the apostles. In their country, Timothy wrote to them, the Book of the Gospels could lie on the throne in place of the third bishop at an ordination. In one letter Timothy names ‘the Indians, the Chinese, the Tibetans, the Turks’ among the lands of his see. There was no king, Mari says, to whom he did not write. The record shows how a church lived on in a caliph’s capital: by answers given at court, by letters, and by monks sent out far beyond it.

## docstories/the-church-of-the-east-on-the-silk-road-1
### Revised text
The stele set up at Chang’an in 781 is the community’s own history of itself. Its heading says it was ‘Composed by King-Tsing, A Priest of the Syrian Church.’ Lines of Syriac run down its sides, naming the patriarch of the day, Hanan-Yeshu. The stone tells how, in the time of the emperor Taitsung, ‘the Most-virtuous Olopun, from the country of Syria’ came east. ‘Observing the azure clouds, he bore the true sacred books; beholding the direction of the winds, he braved difficulties and dangers.’ He reached Chang’an in 635. The emperor sent his prime minister to the western border to bring him in as a guest. The books were translated in the imperial library, and the emperor studied the teaching in his private rooms. In 638 he issued a decree, which the stone quotes: ‘Right principles have no invariable name, holy men have no invariable station’. The teaching, it says, ‘is beneficial to all creatures; it is advantageous to mankind.’ It was to be published through the empire, with a church in the capital for twenty-one priests. The stele then follows the church for a century and a half. It tells of the emperors’ portraits hung in its churches. It also tells of attacks. In 699, it says, Buddhists, gaining power, raised their voices against the church in the eastern capital. In 713 ‘some low fellows’ mocked it and spread slanders in the western capital. The stone gives no reason for either. Priests of the church held firm, it says, and an emperor sent five princes to the church to restore its timbers and stones. It ends with the alms of I-sz’, a man from a distant city who served at a Tang general’s side: ‘the naked came and were clothed; the sick were attended to and restored; the dead were buried in repose’.

## tree/coptic-christianity-under-early-islam
### Revised text
Egypt's miaphysite Christians had spent the century before the Arab conquest under Byzantine pressure for refusing the Council of Chalcedon. When Arab armies took Alexandria in 641-42 their patriarch Benjamin came out of hiding and was restored to his see. The church's own History has him come out on the conqueror 'Amr's promise of safety. Michael the Syrian, a Syriac patriarch of the twelfth century, found it told that Benjamin had promised the Arabs Alexandria if they would drive out Cyrus, the Chalcedonian patriarch, and give him back the churches. What followed was the long transition that made the Coptic church what it still is. No longer persecuted for its confession but taxed and governed as a protected minority, shifting gradually from Coptic into Arabic, and beginning the long, centuries-long drift toward minority status that conversions would eventually bring. Its own record of those years, the History of the Patriarchs of Alexandria, follows the church bishop by bishop with unusual care, though it says far more about patriarchs and taxes than about ordinary Egyptian Christians. The window ends under the caliph al-Hakim in the early eleventh century. Abu Salih, who wrote about Egypt's churches, says al-Hakim ordered a special costume for Christians for nine years, closed their churches, and wrecked or burned some and turned others into mosques. The patriarch Zacharias was imprisoned. Abu Salih names the instigator as a monk, Jonas, who had sought a bishopric from Zacharias and been blocked by the patriarch's nephew after the nephew demanded money from him.
