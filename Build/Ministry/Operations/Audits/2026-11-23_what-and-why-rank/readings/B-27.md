## tree/athanasius-of-alexandria
Heart: For forty-five years Athanasius was bishop, the church's leader, in Alexandria. He held to the creed of Nicaea, the statement of faith bishops agreed in 325: that the Son is "of one being" with the Father, fully God as the Father is. He held it against the party of Arius, who taught that the Son had a beginning. For Athanasius the word mattered because of what God had done: God's Son became human so that human beings might be made divine, joined to God and freed from death. That could happen, he wrote, only if the Son were truly God (T1, T2; Documented, his own words). Five times he had to leave his city, by order or in flight (S1, S2, E4–E13; Documented each time; the count of five is Widely Accepted, D2). In those years he wrote the *Life of Antony*, the story of an Egyptian hermit, which carried the desert monks' way of life to readers abroad. And in 367, in the yearly letter that set the date of Easter, he listed the books to be read as Scripture, with the twenty-seven of the New Testament (E11, E14; the letter Documented; the *Life* as his work Widely Accepted, D1).

## tree/gregory-of-nyssa
Heart: Gregory of Nyssa, a bishop who came home from exile in 378, held to the faith of Nicaea against Eunomius, wrote that the Holy Spirit shares the rank of Father and Son, and taught that all creation will in the end be healed and give thanks.

## tree/nestorius
Heart: Nestorius was bishop of Constantinople, the empire's capital, from 428. He taught that Christ is both God and man, and that the two stay distinct, joined in one Christ whom Christians worship as one. So he would not simply call Mary "Mother of God", the one who gave birth to God, and offered "Mother of Christ" instead (E4, E5: Documented in his own words; Socrates says he rejected the name outright, E3). Cyril, bishop of Alexandria, and a council of bishops at Ephesus in 431 judged that he split Christ in two, and removed him from office; he denied it, and died in exile, his last known years spent in the Egyptian desert (E9, E12: Documented; the place and date of death unknown, D4; what he really taught is Contested, D2).

## tree/william-tyndale
Heart: William Tyndale, an English priest and scholar, put the New Testament, the gospels and letters first written in Greek, into English straight from the Greek (M7: Widely Accepted). The church's Bible was then in Latin, and English Scripture needed a bishop's leave (S3). Barred from the work at home, he had it printed abroad, at Worms on the Rhine in 1526, and smuggled into England (M3, M6: Widely Accepted, a diary's hearsay and the editor). There the bishop of London, the church's chief officer for the city, ordered every copy handed in, and the king ordered them burned (M8: Documented). Hunted by the king's agents, he was betrayed at Antwerp in 1535, and in 1536 he was strangled while tied to a post, and his body burned (M12, M16, M17: Documented to Widely Accepted; the manner is Foxe's account, D3).

## tree/john-wyclif
Heart: John Wyclif held that God's law belongs to everyone in their own tongue, and that Scripture is the test of the church's wealth and power. His circle made the first whole English Bible, and his followers carried the work on in secret until Tyndale.

## tree/jan-hus
Heart: Jan Hus, a priest and master of Prague's university, preached to the city in Czech at the Bethlehem Chapel, read John Wyclif's books and would not condemn him (S4, S5, E1, E4; Documented, his own words and a papal bull; the 1402 appointment Widely Accepted, Lützow). He went to the Council of Constance under King Sigismund's safe-conduct, was arrested within weeks, refused to abjure what he said he had never taught, and was burned there on 6 July 1415 (E7, E9, E16, E18, S7; Documented, eyewitnesses). Within two months the lords and knights of Bohemia and Moravia set 452 seals on a protest vowing to defend the preachers of God's law, and his death turned a university quarrel into a nation's movement (I1, Documented; the turn, I2, Widely Accepted, the tree's entry).

## tree/catherine-of-siena
Heart: Catherine of Siena, a dyer's daughter who never entered a convent, gathered a circle of followers and dictated letters to popes, cities and rulers (S1, E1-E3; Documented). In 1376 she went to Avignon and pressed Gregory XI to take the papacy back to Rome, and that September he left for Rome (E7-E11; Documented). How far she moved him is disputed: her confessor says she led him to it, while her own letters show he had already decided, and later writers gave other causes (D1-D3; Contested).

## tree/gregory-palamas
Heart: Gregory Palamas, a monk of Mount Athos, defended the hesychasts, the monks of stillness, when the scholar Barlaam mocked their silent prayer and the light they said they saw (E2, E7; Documented as Kantakouzenos's account and Palamas's own words). He taught that God cannot be shared in his essence, yet gives himself truly in his uncreated energy, so that one who is joined to God is joined to God himself (E8, E9; Documented, his own words; that this is true, the councils' verdict, contested by his opponents, D2).

## tree/martin-luther
Heart: Martin Luther, a friar (a member of a religious order under vows) who taught theology at Wittenberg, taught that forgiveness is God's free gift, received by trusting God's promise, not earned or bought (G1–G3: Documented, his own words of 1517, 1518 and 1520). So in 1517 he protested against the sale of indulgences, letters of pardon said to cancel punishment for sin, even for the dead (M1, M2, S5: Documented). Condemned by the pope, he would not take back his books before the emperor at Worms in 1521 unless Scripture or clear reason proved him wrong (M5, M7: Documented; "Here I stand" is Contested, D1). Outlawed and hidden, he put the New Testament into German (M9–M11: Documented). The churches that grew from his protest, the Lutheran churches, carry his name (CEN; I4: Documented).

## tree/john-calvin
Heart: John Calvin was a French scholar in exile. He wrote the Institutes, a book of instruction in the Christian faith, first to defend the Protestants being burned in France. Passing through Geneva for one night in 1536, he was held there. He gave the city a written plan for running its church: pastors to preach, teachers, deacons to care for the poor and sick, and elders, laymen who watched over people's lives. Elders and pastors met each week as the consistory, a church court. Protestant churches in France and Scotland built their own churches on that plan, and his name became the label for the whole family.

## tree/huldrych-zwingli
Heart: From 1519 Huldrych Zwingli, people's priest of Zurich's main church, preached the Bible book by book, and in 1523 asked to defend his preaching in a public debate before the city council; the council ruled that every preacher must preach only what Scripture could defend, and over the next two years Zurich removed its images and ended the Mass (E1, E3-E5; Documented, the council's decrees and his own words). The churches called Reformed look to him as a founder; on the tree, their tradition begins at Zurich in 1519 (I2; Widely Accepted, editor and the tree's own entries).
