## tree/thomas-cranmer
Heart: Thomas Cranmer was archbishop of Canterbury from 1533 under Henry VIII, and under the boy king Edward VI he led the making of the Book of Common Prayer of 1549 and 1552, which put the church's services into English and gave the whole realm one form of worship (S3, E3, E7–E9, E11; Documented for the books and the office; that he led their making, Widely Accepted, the census and his editor); under Elizabeth his book of 1552 became the book of 1559, and it went on to shape worship in English (I1; CLAY 431–437, 443; census tradition `legacy`; Documented for 1559 from 1552, Widely Accepted for the wider legacy). Under the Catholic queen Mary he signed a series of recantations, then at Oxford in 1556 publicly took them back and was burned; Foxe, the Protestant writer who collected the stories of those burned under Mary, tells that he put into the fire first the hand that had signed them (E17, E20, E22, E24; the recantations and their withdrawal, Documented, his enemies' own print and register; the hand, Documented as Foxe's account).

## tree/teresa-of-avila
Heart: Teresa of Ávila was a Carmelite nun who, after more than twenty years in a large and easy-going convent in Ávila, founded in 1562 a small, poor house of no more than thirteen nuns, St Joseph's, living by the order's old strict rule, against the city's attempt to close it; then she spent the rest of her life founding more houses like it, seventeen for nuns by her death in 1582, and houses of friars (E10–E12, E16; Documented, her own words; the count Widely Accepted, her editors'). Those houses became the Discalced, or barefoot, Carmelites, still a worldwide order (I1; Documented, the tree's home entry).

## tree/john-wesley
Heart: John Wesley was a priest (an ordained minister) of the Church of England, England's state church. One May evening in 1738 he felt, for the first time, that he trusted Christ alone and that God had forgiven him (his own Journal, "what I now first felt", J1 27078, M4; Documented). From 1739 he preached outdoors, in fields and open ground, to crowds he counted in thousands, though he had thought preaching belonged only inside a church (his own Journal, M5–M6; Documented). And he gathered those who answered into "societies", small voluntary prayer groups within the church, and split each society into weekly "classes" of about twelve under a leader. Those groups held the "Methodists", a nickname from his Oxford days, together and became the frame of the Methodist churches (his own Plain Account and Journal, M9, M11; the American record, I1; Documented).

## tree/polycarp-of-smyrna
Heart: Polycarp, bishop of Smyrna, refused as an old man to curse Christ before the Roman governor and was burned for it, and the church there kept his death as a martyr's birthday. He also stood for peace between churches that kept Easter differently.

## tree/lactantius
Heart: Lactantius, a teacher of Latin speech from Africa, was summoned to Nicomedia, Diocletian's capital, and was there when the Great Persecution began in 303 (S2, S3, E2; Documented, his own words and Jerome's). He answered it in the *Divine Institutes*, seven books that argue the faith to educated pagans in polished Latin and from their own poets and philosophers, and hold that religion cannot be forced (E7–E9; Documented, his words). When the persecution was over, *On the Deaths of the Persecutors* told how the emperors who had hunted the Christians died (E13; the book's content Documented; that he wrote it Widely Accepted, D2). Late in life, Jerome says, he taught Constantine's son (E14; Documented as Jerome's account).

## tree/constantine-the-great
Heart: Constantine marched on Rome in 312 after a vision of Christ's sign, ended the persecution with the edict of Milan, and as emperor stepped into the church's quarrels, calling the bishops to Nicaea over Arius. With him begins the church of the emperors.

## tree/basil-of-caesarea
Heart: Basil of Caesarea gave up teaching rhetoric for a life of prayer and shared work. He wrote the rules that taught monks to live together and serve one another, built a great lodging for the poor and the sick a little way outside Caesarea, and as its bishop from 370 defended the Holy Spirit's place beside the Father and the Son. He died in 378 or 379, before the council of 381 confessed it.

## tree/gregory-of-nazianzus
Heart: Gregory of Nazianzus led the small Nicene church in Constantinople in 380, preaching the Trinity between the merging of Sabellius and the division of Arius, until Theodosius forced the Arian bishop out. He also wrote that whatever Christ did not take on, He did not heal.

## tree/patrick-of-ireland
Heart: Patrick, a Briton from a Roman family, was carried off to Ireland as a slave at about sixteen. He escaped after six years. Years later he went back of his own will as a bishop, a senior church leader who could appoint other ministers, to the people who had enslaved him (E1, E3, E5, E7, E13; Documented, his own words). Almost all we know of him comes from his own two short Latin writings: the Confession, his account of his life and his thanks to God, and a letter against the soldiers of a warlord named Coroticus. They are the only words that survive from the first church in Ireland (I1; Documented that they are his and were kept; "only words" Widely Accepted, the census and White).

## tree/pamphilus-of-caesarea
Heart: Pamphilus, a presbyter of the church at Caesarea in Roman Palestine, gathered a library of sacred books for his church and, Jerome says, copied most of Origen's books with his own hand; Jerome held twenty-five of those volumes about eighty years later (E3, E4; the library Documented by Eusebius and Jerome; the copying Documented as Jerome's report, with Photius's "is said"). Arrested in the Great Persecution, he wrote a Defense of Origen in prison, working with his pupil and friend Eusebius, and was put to death for his faith about 310 (E7, E8, E11; Documented, Eusebius's own account).

## tree/francis-of-assisi
Heart: Francis, a trader's son of Assisi in central Italy, gave up his family's wealth in the first years of the 1200s, went to serve the lepers he had once loathed, and took to the roads to preach (E2–E6; Documented: his own Testament for the lepers, Celano for the rest). The brothers who joined him, the Friars Minor, the "lesser brothers", were to own nothing, work with their hands and beg when no wage came; by day they went among the townspeople, and he refused a cardinal's advice to live as a monk or a hermit (E7, E9–E11; Documented, his own Rule and Testament, Celano for the refusal; a contemporary bishop saw how they lived, E10).
