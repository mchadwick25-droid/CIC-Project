## tree/tacitus
Heart: Tacitus, a Roman historian writing about fifty years after the great fire of Rome in 64, gives the fullest account outside the church of what Nero did to the Christians. Rumoured to have ordered the fire, Nero put the guilt on them and had a huge number killed as a public show. To explain their name, Tacitus adds that their founder, Christus, was executed under the emperor Tiberius by Pontius Pilate (M2–M6: Documented as Tacitus's account; the year 64, S1: Documented; the date he wrote, S3: Dominant Modern Reconstruction). He despised them and said so, and his one chapter became what later historians built on (M3, M4, M7, D4: Documented; I1: Documented).

## tree/hermas
Heart: Hermas was a Christian at Rome in the second century. His book opens, in the text most editions follow, with the words that he was once sold to a woman in Rome (E1; Documented as that text's words; two other old copies, one Latin and one Greek, say instead that the man who raised him sold a young woman; that he was later freed is never said, D2). He wrote down visions in which the Church, and then an angel dressed as a shepherd, told the Christians of Rome to repent while there was still time. They offered a baptized Christian who sinned one more chance to repent, and only one (E4, E9, E10; Documented as the book's teaching; that the visions happened rests on his word alone, D3). His book, the Shepherd, was read for centuries: some quoted it as Scripture, others shut it out (I1, I3, I6, I7; Documented).

## tree/ignatius-of-antioch
Heart: Ignatius, bishop of the church at Antioch in Syria, was taken under armed guard across the Roman world to Rome, condemned to die by wild beasts in the arena (E1, E2, S6; Documented, his own words). On the road he wrote seven letters, most of them to churches that came out to meet him, and they are almost all we know of him (E3, D2; the letters Documented; "almost all we know" is McGiffert's judgement, Widely Accepted; that these seven are his, Widely Accepted, long disputed). Polycarp, bishop of Smyrna, sent on the ones he held, and later writers quoted them (I1, I2–I4; Documented).

## tree/marcion-of-pontus
Heart: Marcion, at Rome in the mid-second century, taught that the God of Moses and the prophets who made the world was not the Father of Jesus, kept his own Scripture, and founded churches that spread far; we know him only through the writers who opposed him.

## tree/justin-martyr
Heart: Justin, a philosopher turned Christian teacher at Rome, defended Christians to the emperor as unjustly hated, called Christ the Word (logos) in whom all peoples share, and was beheaded about 165 for his faith.

## tree/tatian
Heart: Tatian, a man born "in the land of the Assyrians" and trained in Greek learning, became a Christian at Rome and a hearer of Justin. In his *Address to the Greeks* he turned on everything he had studied: their gods, their philosophers and their arts, all borrowed, he said, from the peoples they called barbarians, and all younger than Moses (S2, E3, E5; Documented, his own words). After Justin's death he broke with the church, by the account of his opponent Irenaeus, about 172 by Eusebius's date (E6, S4; Documented as Irenaeus's account and Eusebius's date). He went back east, and his *Diatessaron*, the four Gospels woven into one continuous story, became for about two centuries the gospel most Syriac-speaking churches heard read (E7, E8, I1; the journey east Widely Accepted, resting on Epiphanius two centuries later; the harmony's use Documented; "very widely if not universally read" is Hogg's judgment, Widely Accepted).

## tree/clement-of-alexandria
Heart: Clement, a teacher at Alexandria around 200, answered Christians who feared Greek philosophy by arguing that God gave it to the Greeks as a guide to Christ, as the Law was for the Hebrews, and he kept what was well said in every school.

## tree/polycrates-of-ephesus
Heart: Near the end of the second century, Polycrates, bishop of Ephesus, led the bishops of the Roman province of Asia in keeping Easter on the fourteenth day of the moon, the day of the Jewish Passover, whatever day of the week it fell; the other churches whose letters Eusebius saw kept it only on a Sunday (S1, S2, S4, E1, E3; Documented, Eusebius and the letter). When Victor of Rome pressed for one custom, Polycrates answered in a letter naming the apostles and martyrs buried in Asia who had kept that day, and Victor tried to cut all the churches of Asia off from communion (L1–L5, E5; Documented; whether the break took hold, Contested, D1).

## tree/hippolytus-of-rome
Heart: Hippolytus was a Greek-writing teacher and elder of the church at Rome in the early two hundreds (S1, S2; Documented). In his *Refutation of All Heresies*, written after 222, he attacked Callistus, bishop of Rome from 217, as a man who forgave sinners too easily and who shared the teaching that Father and Son were one and the same (E4, E6, E7, E9; Documented as his charge; what Callistus taught, Contested). In 235 he was sent into exile on the island of Sardinia together with Pontianus, the bishop of Rome (E11; Documented, the list of 354; that the exile is this Hippolytus, Widely Accepted; that he was set to work in the mines is the historians' reading, DUH 11685–11686, EUS 35698–35700). Whether he led a rival church against the bishops of Rome is disputed (D2; Contested).

## tree/dionysius-of-alexandria
Heart: Dionysius, a pupil of Origen, ran the church's school at Alexandria and then led its church as bishop from about 247 until his death about 264 or 265, through two persecutions and a plague (S1, S2, E1–E12; Documented). His letters, which Eusebius of Caesarea copied into his history, are our eyewitness record of those years, told in his own words (S3, I2; Documented).

## tree/novatian
Heart: Novatian was a leading elder of the church at Rome and a writer on the faith; his *On the Trinity* set out what the church believed of Father, Son and Spirit (E2; the book Documented; "leading" Widely Accepted, from Cornelius's sneer and Harnack, E2, E5). In 251 a stricter party made him a rival bishop of Rome against Cornelius, and he led a church that would not take back to communion those who had denied the faith under persecution; it left their pardon to God (E5, E8; Documented). That church lasted for centuries, and almost all we know of him comes from the men who fought him (S3, I1, I2; Witness kinds; Documented).

## tree/pontius-the-deacon
Heart: Pontius, Cyprian of Carthage's deacon, went with him into exile in 257 by choice, and was with him on his last night in 258 (E2, E6; Documented, the book's own claims; his name and his office are Jerome's, E1). Soon after, he wrote *The Life and Passion of Cyprian*, so that Cyprian's example would reach later generations (E8; Documented, his own words; written about 259, Dominant Modern Reconstruction). It tells a bishop's life from his baptism to his death; a leading historian called it the oldest Christian biography that survives, and others call it a speech of praise (I3; Contested). The book is unsigned; Jerome, about 130 years later, gives the deacon's name (E1; Widely Accepted).
