## tree/eusebius-of-caesarea
Heart: Early in the fourth century Eusebius, bishop of Caesarea in Roman Palestine, set out to write the first history of the whole church, and built it from the church's own letters, lists of bishops and old books, copying their words into his pages (E1–E4; Documented, his own words; that he was the first, Widely Accepted, his claim and his editor's). Much of what the early Christians wrote survives only because he copied it (I1; Widely Accepted, his editor's judgment, with cases on the tree).

## tree/peter-of-alexandria
Heart: Peter was bishop of Alexandria, leading the churches of all Egypt, through the Great Persecution that began in 303 (S1–S3; Documented). In 306 he set rules for taking back Christians who had given way under it, measuring each case by what the person had suffered and chosen, and in 311 he was beheaded (E1, E3–E12, D1, D2; Documented). In those same years a bishop of his, Melitius, broke away; the rival church that split left lasted beyond his death and came before the Council of Nicaea (M1–M11, I2; Documented that it happened; why it began, Contested, X1).

## tree/epiphanius-of-salamis
Heart: Epiphanius, bishop of Salamis on Cyprus, wrote the Panarion, listing eighty heresies with a cure beside each, and carried his fight against Origen's teaching to Jerusalem and Constantinople, where it brought him quarrels and an unfinished journey home.

## tree/cyril-of-jerusalem
Heart: Cyril of Jerusalem taught the people preparing for baptism through the forty days of Lent, in the great church beside Golgotha. He went through the creed line by line, and after Easter he explained the rites they had just been through. His lectures survive because some of his hearers took them down as he spoke, an old note in one copy says. As bishop he was driven from his city three times in the quarrels over Arius's teaching, and Jerusalem's way of making new Christians became a pattern that a pilgrim from the West wrote home about.

## tree/eusebius-of-nicomedia
Heart: When Alexander of Alexandria put Arius out, Eusebius, bishop of Nicomedia, the city where the emperor lived, took up Arius's cause (SOC 2123-2126, 2381-2391). After a short exile that followed Nicaea, he led the bishops who drove Arius's opponents from their churches, and he died bishop of Constantinople (SOC 4662-4668, 5885-5890, 6175-6177). Nearly everything we know of him was written by his enemies (Source_Sheet §5.1). Documented.

## tree/evagrius-ponticus
Heart: Evagrius Ponticus, a gifted deacon at Constantinople, fled the city over a love affair and became a monk in the Egyptian desert, where he named the eight tempting thoughts that trouble a monk and taught that prayer is the mind's own work. Long after his death, councils named him with Origen among the condemned, yet monks in the Latin West and the Syriac East went on using what he taught.

## tree/damasus-of-rome
Heart: Damasus was bishop of Rome from 366 to 384. He won the office in an election fought with clubs and killing. He then held his church to the faith of Nicaea, and in 380 the emperors' law named him as that faith's standard. *(Election: AMM 23176–23189, Documented; the clubs: AVE 5107, 5159, the Ursinian account, Contested; the dates: S1, S4, E53, Dominant Modern Reconstruction; the law: CTH 83598–83601, Documented.)*

## tree/cyril-of-alexandria
Heart: Cyril was bishop of Alexandria, the head of the church in Egypt's great city, from 412 to 444. He taught that Christ is one: God's own Son, whom the Bible calls the Word, was himself born of Mary as a man, so she may rightly be called Mother of God, the one who gave birth to God (E4: Documented, his own words). He led the attack on Nestorius, bishop of Constantinople, which ended with a council of bishops at Ephesus in 431 removing Nestorius from office (E6: Documented). His twelve short statements of faith, each cursing those who denied it, and his phrase "one incarnate nature of God the Word", meaning one Christ, God become man and never divided, became the test of true faith for the churches of Egypt, Syria and Armenia that later refused the Council of Chalcedon (I1, I2: Documented for Egypt and Syria; Armenia from the census Chalcedon text, I4). Chalcedon, the council of 451 that said Christ is known in two natures, fully God and fully human, also took his letters as a guide (I4: Documented).
