## tree/leo-of-rome
Heart: Leo, bishop of Rome from 440 to 461, wrote the letter called the Tome in 449 to say how Christ is one person, fully God and fully human; two years later the Council of Chalcedon took it as its rule of faith (E7, E17, E18, I1; Documented). He held that Peter still spoke and ruled through the bishop of Rome, and he would not accept the council's canon that ranked Constantinople next to Rome (E3, E21, E22; Documented, his own words).

## tree/councils-of-rome-and-carthage-251
Heart: When the persecution under the emperor Decius ended, many Christians who had given way under it asked to come back to the church (E1, E2, E6; Documented). In 251 the bishops of Africa met at Carthage under Cyprian, and then sixty bishops at Rome under Cornelius, and chose a middle way: the fallen would not be denied all hope of return, and none would come straight back; they would do long penance, and each case would be examined (E6, E8; Documented, from Cyprian, who called the first council, and from Eusebius's summary of Cornelius's letters). A stricter party would take none of them back to communion; it set up Novatian as a rival bishop of Rome, and the church he led split from the rest and lasted for centuries (E7, E8, I1; Documented).

Heart: In 325 the emperor Constantine called bishops to Nicaea to settle whether the Son is God as the Father is; they condemned Arius and wrote a creed calling the Son "begotten, not made", of one being with the Father, though it divided the church for decades.

## tree/council-of-chalcedon-451
Heart: In 451 more than five hundred bishops at Chalcedon confessed one Christ, one person known in two natures, fully God and fully human, taking Leo's Tome as their guide; the Greek and Latin churches accepted it, while the churches of Egypt, Syria and Armenia refused it and still stand apart.

## tree/barsauma-of-nisibis
Heart: Barsauma was bishop of Nisibis, a city on the border between the Roman and Persian empires, in the late 400s. In 484 he led a synod, a council of bishops, at Beth Lapat against the catholicos, the head of his own church, and later took it back in writing (E5, E8, E10; Documented, his own letters). Yet what his side stood for became the Church of the East's settled law: clergy, the church's ordained ministers, free to marry, allowed in 486 and, in 497, accepted from Beth Lapat "only on this point"; and Theodore of Mopsuestia, a Greek Bible teacher, as its sure guide, confirmed in 605 (E11, I2, I3; Documented, the church's own synods). Writers of the churches that broke with it remembered him as the man who set the Persian church apart (I4, I5; Documented as their hostile claims).

## tree/babai-the-great
Heart: For close to twenty years in the early 600s, the Persian king Khosrau would not let the Church of the East have a catholicos, the head of the whole church (S5; Documented as Thomas of Marga's account; length Contested, 17 to 20 years). In those years Babai, abbot (head) of the Great Monastery on Mount Izla near Nisibis, a monk and not a bishop, went round the church's monasteries for its senior bishops. He tested what the monks believed and drove out those the bishops judged heretics, people teaching what the church had condemned (E5; Documented as Thomas's account, about 840). He wrote the *Book of the Union*, on how Christ is one person who is both fully God and fully human, and his church kept and honoured his books (E6, I1; the books' keeping Documented; that his writing settled his church's teaching on Christ is the judgment of modern historians, Dominant Modern Reconstruction).

## tree/timothy-i-of-baghdad
Heart: For more than forty years, from 780, Timothy was the head of the Church of the East, the church of the Christians of Mesopotamia and Persia. He led it from Baghdad, under five caliphs, the Muslim rulers of the empire (S1, S2, S3: Documented). He argued for his faith at their court: a later list of his writings names a debate with the caliph al-Mahdi, and a later historian says the next caliph, al-Hadi, sent for him on most days to talk about religion (M7: Documented as those witnesses' accounts; the debate's text is not held, N1). And he sent monks out as bishops, church leaders for a region, to peoples south of the Caspian Sea. He let them make more bishops where they were, without the usual three bishops or a journey to him. His letters name a church that reached toward the Turks and China (M11, M12, M14, M17: Documented as Thomas of Marga's and Mingana's accounts; that any bishop he sent reached China is not held, N5).
