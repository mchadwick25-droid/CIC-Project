Around the year 200, in the Egyptian city of Alexandria, a Christian teacher named Clement looked back on his search for a teacher. He had gone from one to another, in Greece, southern Italy and the East, until he reached the last. "I tracked him down where he was hidden in Egypt," he wrote, "and I found rest."

Eusebius, a church historian writing a century later, names that teacher as Pantaenus. Clement wrote down what he had heard so that he would not forget it in old age, and he called the patched-together notes Miscellanies.

Clement was one of the elders who led the church at Alexandria beside its bishop, and he taught there. Many Christians, he wrote, feared Greek philosophy the way children fear masks, and some wanted bare faith alone. Clement answered that this was like wanting grapes without tending the vine.

Perhaps, he suggested, God gave philosophy to the Greeks to lead them, the way a household servant walked a child to his teacher. "For it was a guide to bring the Greek mind to Christ," he wrote, "as the Law brought the Hebrews." So he took no single school of philosophy whole, but kept whatever had been well said.

Clement wrote that every day he saw martyrs, people who died rather than deny Christ, burnt, impaled and beheaded. Alexander, a bishop held in prison, trusted him to carry a letter to Antioch that praised him as a man who had strengthened and built up the church. In a later letter, Alexander counted Clement among the blessed men who had already died.

Eusebius listed his books, quoted them, and trusted him as a witness to the earliest church. By Eusebius's and Jerome's account, Origen had been Clement's pupil as a boy. He followed his teacher's example with ten books of Miscellanies of his own, setting Christian views beside those of the philosophers.

Two centuries on, a critic asked Jerome why he quoted pagan books. For his answer, Jerome pointed to Clement, "in my judgment," he wrote, "the most learned of men", whose books were drawn "from the very heart of philosophy".
