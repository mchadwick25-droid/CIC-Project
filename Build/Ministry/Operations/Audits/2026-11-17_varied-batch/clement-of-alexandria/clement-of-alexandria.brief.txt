Around the year 200, in Alexandria, in Egypt, a Christian teacher named Clement looked back on his search. As a learner, he had gone from teacher to teacher, from Greece to southern Italy and the East, until he found the last and greatest. "I tracked him down where he was hidden in Egypt, and I found rest."

Clement called him the bee of Sicily, because he gathered from the prophets and apostles. Eusebius, a church historian writing a century later, names him Pantaenus.

Clement became a presbyter, an elder who led the church at Alexandria beside the bishop. Eusebius says the boy Origen was among his pupils. As a remedy against forgetting, he wrote down what he had heard, in notes he called the Miscellanies, patched together from one subject to the next.

Could a Christian keep his Greek learning? Many Christians feared philosophy as children fear masks. Some wanted bare faith alone, which Clement said was like wanting grapes without tending the vine. "Perhaps philosophy was also given to the Greeks directly, as God's own gift, until the Lord should call them. For it was a guide to bring the Greek mind to Christ, as the Law brought the Hebrews." Here he echoes the apostle Paul, who called the Law a guide of this kind: the household servant who walked a child to his teacher.

Alexander, a bishop held in prison, trusted Clement to carry his letter to the church at Antioch. He praised him as a good and tested elder who had built up the church. Later, Alexander named him among the blessed men who had gone on ahead in death.

Eusebius counted him a trustworthy witness to the earliest church, who kept its true faith. Jerome, writing two centuries on, called him the most learned of men. Asked why he quoted pagan books, Jerome pointed to Clement's writings. "Is learning missing from these books? Or are they not rather drawn from the very heart of philosophy?"

And Origen, his pupil, followed his example. Jerome says he wrote ten books of Miscellanies of his own. In them he set the views of Christians beside the views of philosophers.
