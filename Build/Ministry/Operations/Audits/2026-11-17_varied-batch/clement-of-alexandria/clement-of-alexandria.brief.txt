Around the year 200, in Alexandria, in Egypt, a teacher of the church named Clement looked back on his search. As a young man he had gone from teacher to teacher, from Greece to southern Italy and the East, until he found the last. "I tracked him down where he was hidden in Egypt, and I found rest."

Clement called him the bee of Sicily, because he gathered from the prophets and apostles. Eusebius, a church historian writing about a hundred years later, names the teacher Pantaenus.

Clement became a presbyter of the church at Alexandria, an elder who led beside the bishop. He taught in that city of libraries and lecture halls, and Eusebius says the boy Origen was among his pupils. As a remedy against forgetting, Clement wrote down what he had heard, in notes he called the Miscellanies.

His books raise a hard question. Some Christians feared Greek philosophy as children fear masks, and wanted bare faith alone. Clement said that was like wanting grapes without tending the vine. "Perhaps philosophy was also given to the Greeks directly, as God's own gift, until the Lord should call them. For it was a guide to bring the Greek mind to Christ, as the Law brought the Hebrews."

Here Clement echoes the apostle Paul, who used that word for the Law: the household servant who walked a child to his teacher.

The last we hear, Clement carried a bishop's letter from prison to the church at Antioch.

Eusebius trusted him as a witness to the earliest church, and as one who kept its true faith. Jerome, writing two centuries on, called him the most learned of men. Asked why he quoted pagan books, Jerome pointed to Clement's writings. "Is learning missing from these books? Or are they not rather drawn from the very heart of philosophy?"

And Origen, his pupil, followed his example. He wrote ten books of Miscellanies of his own, setting Christian teaching beside the views of the philosophers.
