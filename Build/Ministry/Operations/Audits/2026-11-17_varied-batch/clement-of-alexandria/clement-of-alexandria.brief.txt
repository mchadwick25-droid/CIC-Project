Around the year 200, in Alexandria, in Egypt, a Christian teacher named Clement looked back on a long search. He had gone from teacher to teacher, in Greece, southern Italy and the East, until he reached the last. "I tracked him down where he was hidden in Egypt," he wrote, "and I found rest."

Eusebius, a church historian writing a century later, names that teacher as Pantaenus. Clement wrote down what he had heard so that he would not forget it in old age, and because the notes were patched together, he called the book Miscellanies.

As one of the elders who led the church at Alexandria beside its bishop, Clement taught in a city of libraries and lecture halls. Yet some Christians there feared Greek philosophy the way children fear masks, and they wanted bare faith alone. Clement answered that this was like wanting grapes without tending the vine.

Perhaps, he suggested, God gave philosophy to the Greeks as he gave the Law to the Hebrews, to lead them as a household servant walked a child to his teacher. "It was a guide to bring the Greek mind to Christ," he wrote, "as the Law brought the Hebrews." So he took no single school whole, but kept whatever had been well said.

The last we hear of him comes from Alexander, a bishop held in prison, who sent a letter to the church at Antioch by Clement's hand. Later, Alexander counted him among the blessed men who had gone on ahead.

Eusebius kept his books and trusted him as a witness to the earliest church. Two centuries on, a critic asked Jerome why he quoted pagan books. Jerome pointed to Clement, "in my judgment the most learned of men", whose books were drawn "from the very heart of philosophy". And Origen, his pupil as a boy, followed his example with ten books of Miscellanies of his own, setting Christian views beside those of the philosophers.
