# Outline: Clement of Alexandria

Sheet = `Source_Sheet.md` in this folder. Codes (S3, M1, T1 …) are its entries.

- **Length:** 2 minutes (about 300 words). He is a major figure, but the record of his life is thin. No held source gives his birth, his conversion, or his death. His own books and later readers are rich, but one idea told well is enough. Do not stretch to fill.

- **Setting, in words to say aloud:** "Around the year 200, in Alexandria, in Egypt" (S3: his own count reaches the death of the emperor Commodus, 194 years after Jesus' birth; Eusebius and Jerome place him under the emperor Severus). Tradition: "the church at Alexandria", "the Christian teachers of Alexandria", a city of libraries and lecture halls (S6). Do not say "Catechetical School" or "Christian-Platonist" aloud (S6b, D1).

- **Thread:** Clement taught that Greek learning could be a guide that leads to Christ, and he argued that a Christian could keep his learning and his faith together.

- **Shape:** Teacher or thinker.

- **Openings:**
  1. His own memory of his search for teachers: from Greece to southern Italy and the East; then the last one, "concealed in Egypt", and "I found rest" (M1).
  2. A question his own books raise: many Christians were "frightened at the Hellenic philosophy, as children are at masks" (S5a). Could a Christian keep his Greek learning?

- **Story beats:**
  1. **The search.** As a learner he went from teacher to teacher, from Greece to southern Italy and the East (M1). In Egypt he found the one he calls "the Sicilian bee", gathering from the prophets and apostles. He found rest there (M1, Q1). Eusebius names that teacher as Pantaenus; say it as Eusebius's word (M2). Documented (his memory); Dominant Modern Reconstruction (the name).
  2. **Notes against forgetting.** He wrote down what he had heard, as "a remedy against forgetfulness" in old age. He called the book *Miscellanies*, notes "patched together", passing from one thing to another (M3, T6, S1). Documented.
  3. **Teaching at Alexandria.** He was a presbyter, an elder who led the church beside the bishop. He taught in Alexandria, and Eusebius says the boy Origen was among his pupils (S2, M4). Say "taught", not "headed the school": whether a formal school existed is disputed (D1). Widely Accepted (taught, Origen); Contested (school).
  4. **The question and his answer.** Some Christians wanted "bare faith alone" and would not touch philosophy or science. He said that was like wanting grapes without tending the vine (S5b). His answer: philosophy was given to the Greeks as the Law was given to the Hebrews. It was a guide to bring them to Christ (T1, Q2). Paul's word in Galatians, which Clement echoes here, meant the household servant who walked a child to his teacher (T1 note: ANF2 27893 note 1853, 34509–34510, 18697–18699). Clement's own Greek is not held. He did not take any one school whole. He kept "whatever has been well said" and rejected what men had falsified (T3). Documented.
  5. **Faith in ordinary work.** For him the whole of life was a festival kept with God: "we cultivate our fields, praising; we sail the sea, hymning" (T4). Optional, if room. Documented.
  6. **Persecution.** He saw martyrs, people who died rather than deny Christ, "burnt, impaled, beheaded" every day (S4). He taught that no one should hand himself over to the judge (M8). Do not say Clement fled, or why, and do not set any report of teachers driven from Alexandria next to this teaching (D3). Documented (his words); Inferential-Thin (any link to his leaving).
  7. **The last we hear.** Alexander, a bishop held in prison, sent a letter to the church at Antioch by Clement's hand. He called Clement "the blessed presbyter, a man virtuous and approved", who had "strengthened and built up the Church of the Lord" (M7). In a later letter, Alexander counts Clement among the blessed men who had gone on ahead of us, that is, who had died (M9). Do not name the place of Alexander's prison (N3). Documented. The quoted phrases in this beat are McGiffert's English, given as wording from the record, not as further quotes. If one is spoken as a quote, it needs a rendering from the Greek (EUSG 2401–2403, 2490–2494).

- **What came of them** (about a third; heaviest first):
  1. **Origen.** His pupil, by Eusebius's and Jerome's account. Jerome says Origen copied his example and wrote his own ten books of *Miscellanies*, setting Christian and philosophers' views side by side (I1). Widely Accepted. Origen's own surviving words name Pantaenus, not Clement (D4): card only.
  2. **Eusebius kept him and trusted him.** The church historian listed his books and used him as a witness to the earliest church: how the Gospels came to be written (I2). He copied out Clement's whole story of the apostle John and a young robber "for the benefit of my readers". **Defended:** Eusebius named him among the trustworthy who "maintained the orthodoxy of the Church" (I2). Documented.
  3. **Jerome used him to defend Christian learning.** Asked why he quoted pagan books, Jerome pointed to Clement, "in my judgment the most learned of men", whose books were "drawn from the very heart of philosophy" (I4, Q3). **Defended** (his method). Documented.
  4. **Alexander, a bishop who knew him, honoured him and remembered him.** He trusted Clement with his prison letter (M7). Later he named "the holy Clement" among the blessed men through whom he came to know Origen (I3). Clement dedicated a book to him (I3). Documented (the honour). That Alexander was Clement's pupil is a modern editor's inference (McGiffert, EUS 34358–34359): Dominant Modern Reconstruction, card only, not story.
  Suggested end: Jerome's defence (I4, Q3), followed by Origen's copy of the *Miscellanies* (I1).

- **Quotes, 2 or 3 at most:**
  - **Q1** — the opening; his own voice. Source: Stromata I.1, ANF2 27489–27495 (Wilson's English; Clement's Greek is not held, so this is rendered from the printed English). Verbatim: "When I came upon the last (he was the first in power), having tracked him out concealed in Egypt, I found rest. He, the true, the Sicilian bee, gathering the spoil of the flowers of the prophetic and apostolic meadow, engendered in the souls of his hearers a deathless element of knowledge." Rendering: "Then I came upon the last of them, and he was the greatest in power. I tracked him down where he was hidden in Egypt, and I found rest. He was the true bee, the bee of Sicily. He gathered what the flowers held in the meadow of the prophets and apostles. In the souls of those who heard him, he brought into being a knowledge that does not die."
  - **Q2** — the one idea. Source: Stromata I.5, ANF2 27890–27895 (rendered from Wilson's English). Verbatim: "Perchance, too, philosophy was given to the Greeks directly and primarily, till the Lord should call the Greeks. For this was a schoolmaster to bring 'the Hellenic mind,' as the law, the Hebrews, 'to Christ.' Philosophy, therefore, was a preparation, paving the way for him who is perfected in Christ." Rendering: "Perhaps philosophy was also given to the Greeks directly, as God's own gift, until the Lord should call them. For it was a guide to bring the Greek mind to Christ, as the Law brought the Hebrews. So philosophy was a preparation. It cleared the road for the one who is made complete in Christ." ("Directly" is set against what God gives "by consequence", ANF2 27888–27889. "Schoolmaster" stands for Paul's word in Galatians 3:24, which Clement echoes: the servant who led a child to the teacher. "Guide" avoids the modern sense of a teacher who runs a school. The writer may give the servant image in its own words, from beat 4.)
  - **Q3** — what came of him; a later reader's defence. Source: Jerome, Letter 70.4 to Magnus, JER 16626–16636 (Fremantle's English; Jerome's Latin of this letter is not held, so this is rendered from the printed English). Verbatim: "Clement, a presbyter of Alexandria, in my judgment the most learned of men, wrote eight books of Miscellanies and as many of Outline Sketches, a treatise against the Gentiles, and three volumes called the Pedagogue. Is there any want of learning in these, or are they not rather drawn from the very heart of philosophy?" Rendering: "Clement was a presbyter of Alexandria, and in my judgment the most learned of men. He wrote eight books of Miscellanies, and as many of Outline Sketches. He wrote a book against the pagans, and three books called the Instructor. Is learning missing from these books? Or are they not drawn from the very heart of philosophy?" ("Gentiles" is given as "pagans", because in modern English "Gentiles" means non-Jews. The "Pedagogue" is the book this outline calls the *Instructor*.) Job: Jerome's answer to a critic who asked why he quoted pagan books (JER 16477–16479). It shows a later writer using Clement's example to defend the very idea Clement argued for.

- **Links:**
  - Pantaenus — heard him teach (M1, M2).
  - Origen — taught him (M4, I1).
  - Alexander, a bishop — met him; Clement carried his letter to Antioch; Clement dedicated a book to him (M7, M9, I3; EUS 34056–34112, NP3 40259–40261).
  - Eusebius — kept and quoted his books (I2).
  - Jerome — wrote his life; quoted his example (I4).

- **Who tells it:**
  - Clement's own books (*Exhortation*, *Instructor*, *Miscellanies*, *Who is the Rich Man*): his own time, in English translation only.
  - Alexander's two letters: his own time, by a bishop who knew him, kept only because Eusebius copied them.
  - Eusebius, *Church History*: about a century later. Line to say aloud: "Most of what we know of his life comes from Eusebius, a church historian writing about a hundred years later."
  - Jerome, *Lives* and Letter 70: about two centuries later. Line: "Jerome, writing two centuries on, called him the most learned of men."

- **Leave out:**
  - "Head of the Catechetical School" as fact, and the tidy line of school heads (D1).
  - Why or when he left Alexandria; McGiffert's date of 202 (D3).
  - Birth in Athens, conversion as an adult, death in Palestine: editors' notes, not held witnesses (N1).
  - The names guessed for his six teachers (D2).
  - The details of the John and robber story, and the traditions from his lost *Outlines*: they are other people's stories (I2).
  - Rufinus's "presbyter of Antioch" (D5), and Photius's attack on the *Outlines* about 650 years later (I6): card only.
  - Clement of Rome: same name, different man (S1). Only if a listener might confuse them.
