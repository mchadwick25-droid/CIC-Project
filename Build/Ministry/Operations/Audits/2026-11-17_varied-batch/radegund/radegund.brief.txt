Her friend the poet Fortunatus tells the scene. At Noyon, the king's nobles dragged the bishop back from the altar, while a queen put on a nun's dress with her own hands and walked out to face him.

This was the sixth century, in the kingdom of the Franks, in what is now France. Radegund was a Thuringian princess, taken as spoil when the Franks destroyed her people's kingdom, and King Lothar married her.

She tended poor women's sores herself, and it was said the king had married a nun. When Lothar had her brother killed, she left the court and begged Medard, bishop of Noyon, to consecrate her, though he hesitated because she was married. At the altar she warned him: "If you put off consecrating me, and fear a man more than God, then, shepherd, may the soul of your sheep be demanded from your hand." At that, he laid his hand on her and made her a deaconess.

With the wealth the king had given her, she built a monastery for women at Poitiers and gave it everything she owned. Its written Rule, from Caesarius of Arles, kept each nun inside its walls for life, and she sent to the emperor in the East for wood of the Cross.

To guard the house after her death, she asked the bishops to stop anyone, even the city's own bishop, who tried to break the Rule or replace the abbess. "Because I could not come in person," she wrote, "I bow down through this letter in my place, as if fallen at your feet."

She died in 587, among about two hundred nuns. Soon afterwards more than forty of them walked out in revolt, so the abbess read Radegund's letter aloud and sent copies to the bishops near by. The Rule, the abbess and the property all held, and Gregory of Tours copied the whole letter into his History.

Before the Cross she had brought, Gregory saw a lamp overflow with oil. From then on, he wrote, he preached the power of the holy Cross.
