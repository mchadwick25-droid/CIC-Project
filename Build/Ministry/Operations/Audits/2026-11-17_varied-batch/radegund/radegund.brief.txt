In the sixth century, in the kingdom of the Franks, in what is now France, a king's nobles dragged a bishop back from his altar. A queen named Radegund put on a nun's dress herself and came out to face the bishop. Her friend the poet Fortunatus tells the story.

She had come to the Franks as spoil of war, a princess of conquered Thuringia. King Lothar married her, and as queen she bathed poor women and tended their sores herself. Then Lothar had her brother killed.

She left the court and begged Medard, bishop of Noyon, to consecrate her to God. He hesitated, because she was a married woman, and the king's nobles dragged him from the altar. In her nun's dress, she challenged him. "If you put off consecrating me, and fear a man more than God, then, shepherd, may the soul of your sheep be demanded from your hand." He made her a deaconess, a woman set apart for the church's service.

With the wealth Lothar had given her, she built a house for women at Poitiers and gave it a Rule, a written law of life. She sent to the East for wood of the Cross of Christ, and the emperor sent it, set in gold.

To guard the house after her death, she wrote to the bishops. "Because I could not come in person, I bow down through this letter in my place, as if fallen at your feet." If anyone, even the city's own bishop, tried to break the Rule or replace the abbess, they must stop him.

The bishops wrote that young women flocked from their districts to live under her Rule. When she died in 587, about two hundred nuns stood round her body, and Gregory of Tours, a bishop who knew her, buried her.

Soon after her death, forty or more nuns left the house in revolt, and the abbess read Radegund's letter aloud. At the bishops' hearing, the abbess defended the house's ways as "the custom of the lady Radegund", and Gregory copied the whole letter into his History.

The house came to be called Holy Cross. Gregory saw a lamp before the Cross overflow with oil, and he wrote: "from then on I preached the power of the holy Cross."
