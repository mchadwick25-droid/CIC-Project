In the sixth century, in the kingdom of the Franks, in what is now France, a king's nobles dragged a bishop back from his altar. A queen named Radegund went into the sacristy, put on a nun's dress herself, and walked out to face him. Her friend the poet Fortunatus tells the story.

She had come to the Franks as spoil of war, a princess of Thuringia, a kingdom they had destroyed. King Lothar had her taught to read, then married her. As queen she bathed poor women and tended their sores herself. Then Lothar had her brother killed.

She left the court and begged Medard, bishop of Noyon, to consecrate her to God. He hesitated, because she was a married woman. So in her nun's dress she charged him. "If you put off consecrating me, and fear a man more than God, then, shepherd, may the soul of your sheep be demanded from your hand." He laid his hand on her and made her a deaconess, a woman set apart for the church's service.

With the wealth the king had given her, she built a house for women at Poitiers and gave it all she owned. She made Agnes, a girl she had raised, its abbess, and obeyed her. She gave the house a Rule, a written law of life. And she sent to the East for wood of the Cross.

To guard the house after her death, she wrote to the bishops. If anyone, even the city's own bishop, tried to break the Rule or replace the abbess, they must stop him. "Because I could not come in person, I bow down through this letter in my place, as if fallen at your feet."

She died in 587, and Gregory of Tours buried her. About two hundred nuns stood round her body.

Soon after, forty or more nuns walked out in revolt. The abbess read Radegund's letter aloud and sent copies to the bishops. She defended the house's ways as "the custom of the lady Radegund." The Rule and the abbess held, and Gregory copied the whole letter into his History.

The house came to be called Holy Cross. Gregory saw a lamp before the Cross overflow with oil, and he wrote: "from then on I preached the power of the holy Cross."
