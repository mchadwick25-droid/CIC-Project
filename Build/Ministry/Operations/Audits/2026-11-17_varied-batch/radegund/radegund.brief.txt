At Noyon, the king's nobles dragged a bishop back from his altar. A queen then put on a nun's dress herself and walked out to face the bishop.

This was the sixth-century kingdom of the Franks, in what is now France. Radegund was a Thuringian princess, taken as spoil when the Franks destroyed her people's kingdom. King Lothar carried her off as his captive and made her his wife.

As queen she tended poor women's sores herself, but when Lothar had her brother killed, she left the court and begged Medard, bishop of Noyon, to consecrate her. When he hesitated because she was married, and the nobles pulled him away, she charged him in words her friend the poet Fortunatus gives her. "If you put off consecrating me, and fear a man more than God, then, shepherd, may the soul of your sheep be demanded from your hand." He laid his hand on her and made her a deaconess.

Her own letter says King Lothar founded and enriched her monastery for women at Poitiers. She gave it all the property the king had given her. Its written Rule, from Caesarius of Arles, kept each nun inside its walls for life.

She also sent to the emperor in the East for wood of the Cross, which came set in gold. Her writers compared her to Helena, Constantine's mother, who was said to have found the Cross. Gregory of Tours, a bishop who knew her, wrote that he saw a lamp before her Cross overflow with oil. From then on, he says, he preached the power of the holy Cross.

To guard the house after her death, she asked the bishops to stop anyone, even the city's own bishop, who tried to break the Rule or replace the abbess. "Because I could not come in person," she wrote, "I bow down through this letter in my place, as if fallen at your feet."

She died in 587, with about two hundred women in her house, and Gregory buried her. Soon afterwards forty or more of her nuns walked out in revolt, led by Clotild, who called herself a king's daughter and said they were insulted and humiliated there. The abbess read Radegund's letter aloud and sent copies to nearby bishops, and Gregory copied the whole letter into his History. At the bishops' hearing the abbess defended the house's ways as "the custom of the lady Radegund".
