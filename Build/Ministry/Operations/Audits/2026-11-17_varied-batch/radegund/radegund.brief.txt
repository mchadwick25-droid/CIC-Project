In the sixth century, in the kingdom of the Franks, in what is now France, a king's nobles dragged a bishop back from his altar. A queen named Radegund put on a nun's dress herself and walked out to face him. Her friend the poet Fortunatus tells the story.

She had come to the Franks as spoil of war, a princess from the conquered kingdom of Thuringia. King Lothar married her, and as queen she bathed poor women and tended their sores herself. Then Lothar had her brother killed.

She left the court and begged Medard, bishop of Noyon, to consecrate her to God. He hesitated, because she was a married woman, so she challenged him. "If you put off consecrating me, and fear a man more than God, then, shepherd, may the soul of your sheep be demanded from your hand." He made her a deaconess, a woman set apart for the church's service.

With the wealth King Lothar had given her, she built a house for women at Poitiers and gave it all she owned. She made Agnes, a girl she had raised, its abbess, and obeyed her. She gave it a Rule, a written law of life, and sent to the East for wood of the Cross, which the emperor sent, set in gold.

To guard the house after her death, she wrote to the bishops. If anyone, even the city's own bishop, tried to break the Rule or replace the abbess, the bishops must stop him. "Because I could not come in person, I bow down through this letter in my place, as if fallen at your feet."

She died in 587, and Gregory of Tours, a bishop who knew her, buried her. About two hundred nuns stood round her body.

Soon after, forty or more nuns walked out in revolt. The abbess read Radegund's letter aloud, and the Rule and the abbess held. Gregory copied the whole letter into his History.

The house came to be called Holy Cross. Gregory saw a lamp before the Cross overflow with oil, and he wrote: "from then on I preached the power of the holy Cross."
