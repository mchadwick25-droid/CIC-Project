At Noyon, the king's nobles dragged a bishop back from his altar. A queen then put on a nun's dress herself and walked out to face the bishop.

This was the sixth century, in the kingdom of the Franks, in what is now France, and the church of Frankish Gaul. Radegund was a Thuringian princess, taken as spoil when the Franks destroyed her people's kingdom, and King Lothar married her.

As queen she tended poor women's sores herself, but when Lothar had her brother killed, she left the court and begged Medard, bishop of Noyon, to consecrate her. When he hesitated because she was married, and the nobles pulled him away, she charged him in words her friend the poet Fortunatus gives her. "If you put off consecrating me, and fear a man more than God, then, shepherd, may the soul of your sheep be demanded from your hand." He laid his hand on her and made her a deaconess.

With her royal wealth she built a monastery for women at Poitiers and gave it everything she owned. Its written Rule, from Caesarius of Arles, kept each nun inside its walls for life.

She also won wood of the Cross from the emperor in the East, set in gold, and her writers compared her to Helena, Constantine's mother, said to have found the Cross.

To guard the house after her death, she asked the bishops to stop anyone, even the city's own bishop, who tried to break the Rule or replace the abbess. "Because I could not come in person," she wrote, "I bow down through this letter in my place, as if fallen at your feet."

She died in 587, and Gregory of Tours, a bishop who knew her, buried her. Soon afterwards forty or more of her nuns walked out in revolt, so the abbess read Radegund's letter aloud and sent copies to nearby bishops. At their hearing she defended the house's ways as "the custom of the lady Radegund", and Gregory copied the whole letter into his History.

Gregory wrote that he saw a lamp before her Cross overflow with oil, and that from then on he preached the power of the holy Cross.
