# Review: Radegund brief

Reviewer's report on `radegund.brief.txt`, 2026-11-17. Checked against `Outline.md` (as rechecked to PASS in `Outline_Check.md`), `Source_Sheet.md`, the kit's `Standard.md` and `Shapes.md`, and the held files in `cic/texts/`.

## Verdict: REVISE

Two BLOCKING findings, both small to fix. The body is well built and the quotes are sound. The hook is never tied back into the story, and the payoff of the impact section says more than the outline holds. The impact is also short of a third and goes flat just where it should carry most weight.

## 1. Listener read (one pass, written before anything else)

- **Opening.** Strong and clear on when and where: sixth century, the Franks, now France. Then I had a question: who is "him"? The bishop, or the king? And why are nobles dragging a bishop? I took it to be the bishop and waited for the reason.
- **Her past.** Captive princess, married the king, washed poor women, brother killed. Easy to follow, and the brother line lands hard.
- **Medard.** I did not link "Medard, bishop of Noyon" to the bishop in the opening until the challenge. The nobles never come back, so I never learned why they dragged him. The challenge quote is the best moment in the brief. I remembered "shepherd" and "your sheep".
- **The house.** A lot arrives at once: house, all she owned, Agnes, obeyed her, the Rule, the East, the Cross, the emperor, gold. I lost "obeyed her" (why does that matter?) and was not sure what "wood of the Cross" is or which emperor.
- **The letter.** "To guard the house after her death" is a clear set-up, and I waited for the payoff. The first-person quote surprised me for a second. Then I worked out it was her letter.
- **Death and after.** Two hundred nuns: vivid. The revolt went by in three quick sentences. I heard "the Rule and the abbess held" and took it that her letter won. The lamp ending is clear and quiet.
- **What I'd remember.** A captive queen who left the king, shamed a bishop into making her a deaconess, founded a women's house, and wrote a letter that protected it after she died. A lamp overflowed at her Cross.
- **What I would not come away with.** Why the opening scene happened. How many women followed her, or why that mattered.

## 5. Findings

1. **BLOCKING (lost the listener). The hook is never tied back in.** The opening shows the nobles dragging "a bishop" and Radegund walking out "to face him". When the story comes back to it in paragraph 3, the nobles are gone. Medard is not marked as that bishop, and the listener never hears why they dragged him. The outline gives the reason in beat 3: he hesitated because she was a married woman, and the king's nobles dragged him from the altar (L8: so he would not veil the king's wife). "Him" in the opening could also be heard as the king. And "walked out" is used again later for the revolt, which blurs both.
   *Change:* In paragraph 3, bring the scene back explicitly: Medard hesitated because she was married, and the king's nobles dragged him from the altar. Then she put on the nun's dress and challenged him. In the opening, make "him" unambiguous ("to face the bishop") and use a different verb from the revolt's ("came out", per the outline's "came to the altar").

2. **BLOCKING (unsupported). "The Rule and the abbess held" says more than the outline.** The outline's I1 gives the revolt, the abbess reading the letter aloud and sending copies, the abbess defending the house's ways as "the custom of the lady Radegund", and Gregory copying the letter. It does not give the outcome. ("DEFENDED: the Rule, the abbess, the property" names what the letter defended, not that it won.) The record does have the outcome: the bishops found "no cause shown for the degradation of the abbess" (DAL 21462–21465). But it is not in the outline, and the brief tells only what the outline holds. As written, the sentence also implies that reading the letter is what settled the revolt.
   *Change:* Either (a) tell what the outline holds: "At the bishops' hearing, the abbess defended the house's ways as 'the custom of the lady Radegund'." That also shows her pattern carrying on in her own house. Or (b) have the researcher add the hearing's outcome (DAL 21462–21465) to the outline and sheet, with a targeted recheck, before the brief uses it.

3. **MINOR (craft: impact short and flat).** The impact is about a fifth of the brief, not a third, and it runs as a chronicle of short, unjoined sentences (see §2). It carries the heaviest item, the letter, and the Cross. It drops what the outline gives as the women who followed her (I2): the bishops wrote that young women flocked from their dioceses to take up her Rule. The two hundred nuns sit in the death paragraph as a picture, not as the following she drew. So the listener hears what she built, but only faintly what it became.
   *Change:* Give the impact the room the body is over-using (Finding 4). Join the revolt sentences so they lead into each other, with time words the record supports ("soon after her death... when...") rather than causes it does not give. Add I2 in a sentence: women came from other dioceses to live under her Rule. Keep the lamp ending; it works.

4. **MINOR (length). 357 words against the outline's 300, and all the extra sits in the body.** Paragraphs 2–5 take 223 words (62%). Paragraph 4 alone carries six facts in three sentences.
   *Change:* Trim paragraph 4. "Sent to the East for wood of the Cross, which the emperor sent, set in gold" also repeats "sent". Move what is saved into the impact. Aim for about 300 to 320 words.

5. **MINOR (accessibility). "Wood of the Cross" and "the emperor" are not explained.** A newcomer may not know that this means a piece of the cross Jesus died on, kept as a relic, or which emperor. Without that, the later lamp wonder and the name Holy Cross have less to stand on.
   *Change:* Explain it in the same sentence, from the outline's beat 5. For example: "wood of the Cross, believed to be from the cross of Christ", or use the Helena comparison the outline offers. Place the emperor as the outline and sheet allow (L16).

6. **MINOR (accessibility). The letter quote arrives without a speaker cue.** After the indirect request ("the bishops must stop him"), a first-person line begins with no cue. A listener has to work out that it is her letter. In the letter itself, this line comes before the requests.
   *Change:* Put the quote straight after "she wrote to the bishops", or cue it: "In the letter she wrote:".

## Not findings (checked)

- Rendering "adorandae crucis" as "the holy Cross" is the outline's rendering and does not mislead.
- The opening scene followed by a flashback is the outline's own Opening 1, not Polycarp's arc.
