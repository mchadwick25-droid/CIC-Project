# Outline: Columba (Colum Cille)

Sheet items (S, E, I, D) are in `Source_Sheet.md`, which gives the file and line for each.

- **Length:** about 2½ minutes (about 375 words). Why: a major figure, the founder of Iona, with two independent later witnesses (Adomnán and Bede) and the annals. The extra room is for one plain line on how late the record is, and for the Whitby defence. Not 3: there is no word of his own and no witness from his lifetime, so nothing more should be built.
- **Setting, in words to say aloud:** the sixth century; Ireland, then Iona, a small island off northern Britain, in what is now Scotland; the Irish monastic church, which was led by abbots rather than city bishops. (S3, S4, S5, S7)
- **Thread:** Columba left Ireland as a pilgrim for Christ and built on Iona a monastery whose heirs led a family of houses, took the faith to the English, and held to his ways long after him.
- **Shape:** Founder or leader. Trap: titles in place of acts. Keep to what he built on Iona, how it lived, and what outlasted him.
- **Openings:**
  1. An old abbot copies the Psalms on his last day. He stops at "they that seek the Lord shall not want any good thing" and leaves the rest of the page to his disciple Baithene. (E12; AD 10783–10789)
  2. A question the record raises: Bede, an English monk writing more than a century later, says he is not sure what Columba himself was, but he is sure of the men Columba left behind. (I6; BEDE 5866–5869)

**Story beats**

1. Born to a noble family in Ireland: his father Fedilmith, his mother Aethne. Adomnán gives no year; say "early in the sixth century" (a Welsh annal, as its 1860 editor dates it, puts his birth in 521). (S2, D5 — Documented as Adomnán's statement; year Inferential-Thin)
2. In his forty-second year, two years after a great battle in Ireland, he sailed to Britain "to make a pilgrimage for Christ", with twelve disciples. Pilgrimage here means leaving home for good for God's sake. The Irish and Welsh annals say 562 and Bede 565; about 563 is the usual reckoning (Huyshe's). (E4, S6, D1 — Documented; year Contested)
3. He founded a monastery on Iona. Bede says the Picts gave him the island; the Irish annals say a king named Conall did. Before this he had already founded Durrow, "the Field of Oaks", in Ireland. (E5, E2, D4 — Documented; giver Contested)
4. The life there: Adomnán says he never let an hour pass without prayer, reading, writing or manual work, and that he slept on bare rock with a stone for a pillow. His monks copied the Psalms. (E6, E7 — Documented as Adomnán's portrait)
5. He went to Brude, king of the Picts, in his fortress. Adomnán says the king first kept the gates shut, then honoured him for the rest of his life. He preached through an interpreter; Adomnán tells of a countryman baptized "with all his household", and of an old chief. Bede, later and in England, says he turned the northern Picts to the faith; Adomnán never says the king was baptized. (E8, E9, D6 — Documented as Adomnán's account; scope of conversion Contested.) If a wonder is wanted, use only the gates opening "of their own accord", in Adomnán's voice (AD 7885–7888).
6. His last Saturday, in June, in 597 by the usual reckoning (the Irish annals give 9 June): he blesses the barn and says his monks will have enough grain for the year. The white horse that carried the milk pails lays its head on his chest. He copies the Psalms until the verse above, then stops for Baithene. (E12, D2 — Documented as Adomnán's account; year Widely Accepted, annals differ)
7. That night he gave his last words for the monks, to love one another and keep peace; only Diormit heard them. At the midnight bell he ran ahead into the church and knelt in prayer at the altar. Diormit found him lying there and held his head. Diormit raised his right hand, and Columba moved it as much as he could to bless the monks, then died. (E13, E14 — Documented as Adomnán's account)

**What came of them** (about a third of the story; heaviest first)

1. **A family of monasteries, led from Iona.** Bede: many houses in Britain and Ireland grew from his, and Iona was "the principal of them all". In Bede's day its abbot was a priest, and even bishops were under him, "according to the example of their first teacher". Baithene succeeded him. (I1, S7)
2. **The English.** Almost forty years after his death, in 635, the Northumbrian king asked Iona for a bishop and was sent Aidan, "a monk of the island called Hii" (Iona). (I2)
3. **Defended at Whitby, 664.** His way of dating Easter was the custom of his country, in Bede's words, and it clashed with Rome's. At the synod of Whitby, Colman, the bishop from Iona's line, defended "our most reverend Father Columba and his successors". The king ruled for Rome. Iona itself kept his custom until about 716. DEFENDED. (I3, I4)
4. **His memory.** About a century after him, Iona's ninth abbot, Adomnán, wrote his life, and claimed his name had reached Spain, Gaul, Italy and Rome itself. End here, or on Bede's verdict (Quote 3). (I5, I6)

**Quotes** (3)

1. Verbatim (AD 10787–10789, Huyshe's English, 1905): ""Here", he says, " I must stop at the foot of this page, and what follows let Baithene write."" Rendering (from Huyshe's printed English; the Latin is not held): "Here I must stop, at the bottom of this page. Let Baithene write what comes next." Job: the opening image, and the handing on of his work.
2. Verbatim (AD 10815–10817, Huyshe's English): "These my last words I commend to you, O my sons, that ye have mutual and unfeigned charity among yourselves, with peace" (the sentence goes on with a promise; this is the first clause). Rendering (from Huyshe's English): "My sons, I entrust these last words to you. Love one another without pretence, and live in peace." ("Charity" is rendered "love": its modern sense misleads.) Job: his own voice at the end, and the one thing he asked them to keep.
3. Verbatim (BEDL 18882–18885, Bede's Latin, Plummer 1896): "Uerum qualiscumque fuerit ipse, nos hoc de illo certum tenemus, quia reliquit successores magna continentia ac diuino amore regularique institutione insignes" (Giles's English, BEDE 5866–5869: "But whatsoever he was himself, this we know for certain, that he left successors renowned for their continency, their love of God, and observance of monastic rules."). Rendering (from the Latin): "But whatever kind of man he was, this much about him we hold as certain. He left successors known for great self-control, for the love of God, and for life under a rule." Job: an outsider's verdict that points the story to what outlasted him; a possible last line.

**Links**

- Baithene: his disciple; succeeded him as abbot; finished his page.
- Diormit: his attendant; heard his last words.
- Brude (Bridius), king of the Picts: met him; honoured him.
- Finnian: his teacher.
- Aidan of Lindisfarne: monk of his monastery; carried its faith to the English.
- Colman: defended his custom at Whitby.
- Adomnán: wrote his life.
- Bede: wrote of him.

**Who tells it**

- Adomnán, ninth abbot of Iona, wrote about a hundred years after Columba died, from older writings and the memories of old monks. He was Columba's successor and writes to honour him; his book is arranged by kinds of wonder. Say aloud: "Our fullest account comes from Adomnán, abbot of Iona about a hundred years later."
- Bede, an English monk, finished his history in 731, more than a century later, and wrote as a critic of Iona's Easter. Say aloud: "Bede, writing in England more than a century later, ..."
- The Irish and Welsh annals: year-by-year records kept and copied over centuries; their dates differ by a few years.
- No writing by Columba and no witness from his own lifetime is held.

**Leave out**

- L1. Any reason for his leaving Ireland beyond "a pilgrimage for Christ". Adomnán names the battle of Cooldrevny only to date the voyage. The one held link between the battle and his life is a guess in Huyshe's 1905 note, and it is about the excommunication, not the voyage (E3, E4). The annal that the battle was won "through the prayers of Colum-Cille" (AU 4539–4540) is a side story and doubtful.
- The excommunication at Teltown (E3): real in Adomnán, but its cause is unknown, so it needs more room than it earns here.
- The Book of Lismore's numbers (45 years, twenty bishops and fifty students): fifteenth-century legend (D7).
- The books later credited to him (the Cathach, the Book of Durrow, "three hundred books") and the hymns: later tradition, in Huyshe's introduction only (E7).
- The other wonders: the floating stone, the raised boy, the thunder voice, the angel's book of glass, the visions at his death. Keep at most one, in Adomnán's voice.
- Aedhan's ordination and the Convention of Drumceatt (E10, E11): side stories.
- Do not join him to Columbanus, a different Irish monk.
- Do not say "the Celtic Church", "Insular", or "from Scotland".

- **Do not say** (added from the round-two review, 2026-11-17): The gates opening "of their own accord" is Adomnán's wonder: say "Adomnán says the king kept his gates shut against him until they opened", never as the narrator's fact.
