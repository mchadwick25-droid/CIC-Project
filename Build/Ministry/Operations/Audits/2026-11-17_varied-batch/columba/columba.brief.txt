On his last Saturday, an old abbot sat copying the Psalms. At the words "they that seek the Lord shall not want any good thing", he stopped. "Here I must stop, at the bottom of this page. Let Baithene write what comes next."

The abbot was Columba, also called Colum Cille. He lived in the sixth century, in Ireland and then on Iona, a small island off northern Britain, in what is now Scotland. His was the Irish monastic church, led by abbots rather than city bishops. Nothing he wrote survives, and our fullest account comes from Adomnán, abbot of Iona about a hundred years later.

Born to a noble Irish family, Columba was in his forty-second year when he sailed with twelve disciples "to make a pilgrimage for Christ". That meant leaving home for good, for God's sake.

On Iona he built a monastery where, his biographer says, he never let an hour pass without prayer, reading, writing or work. He slept on bare rock with a stone for a pillow. He went to Brude, king of the Picts, whose gates opened "of their own accord", Adomnán says. The king honoured him for the rest of his life.

On that last day, in 597 by the usual reckoning, the white horse that carried the milk pails laid its head on his chest. That night he left words for his monks, and only his attendant Diormit heard them: "My sons, I entrust these last words to you. Love one another without pretence, and live in peace." At the midnight bell he knelt at the altar. Diormit raised his right hand, and with it Columba blessed his monks and died.

Baithene became abbot, and many houses in Britain and Ireland grew from Iona, which led them all. In 635 the English king of Northumbria asked Iona for a bishop, and it sent Aidan, who took the faith to the English.

Iona kept its own country's way of dating Easter, which clashed with Rome's. At the synod of Whitby in 664, Colman, a bishop from Iona's line, defended "our most reverend Father Columba and his successors". The king ruled for Rome, but Iona kept the custom until about 716.

Bede, writing in England more than a century later, opposed that Easter. Still, he wrote: "But whatever kind of man he was, this much about him we hold as certain. He left successors known for great self-control, for the love of God, and for life under a rule."
