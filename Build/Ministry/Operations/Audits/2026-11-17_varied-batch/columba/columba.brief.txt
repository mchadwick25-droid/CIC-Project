On his last day, an old abbot sat copying the Psalms. When he reached the line "those who seek the Lord will lack no good thing," he said, "Here I must stop, at the bottom of this page. Let Baithene write what comes next."

That abbot was Columba of Iona, a small island off northern Britain, in what is now Scotland. He came from sixth-century Ireland.

Born to a noble Irish family, he had already founded a monastery at Durrow. Then, in about 563, he left Ireland for Britain with twelve disciples. Adomnán, abbot of Iona a century later and our fullest witness, calls this a pilgrimage for Christ. On Iona, Columba founded a monastery where, Adomnán says, he never let an hour pass without prayer, reading, writing or work.

Adomnán tells how Brude, the proud king of the Picts, kept his gates shut against Columba until they opened "of their own accord", and then honored him for life. Bede, writing in England over a century later, says Columba turned the northern Picts to the faith, although Adomnán never says the king was baptized.

On his last night, in June 597 by the usual reckoning, only his attendant Diormit heard his charge to the monks. "My sons, I entrust these last words to you. Love one another without pretense, and live in peace." At the midnight bell he ran into the church and knelt at the altar. There Diormit found him and raised his hand, so that Columba could bless his monks before he died.

Baithene succeeded him, and many houses in Britain and Ireland grew from his monasteries, with Iona, Bede says, the chief of them all. In 635 the king of Northumbria asked Iona for a bishop and was sent Aidan, who carried its faith to the English.

Iona's way of reckoning Easter differed from Rome's. At the synod of Whitby in 664, a bishop of Iona's line defended it in the name of "our most reverend Father Columba and his successors". The king ruled for Rome, but Iona kept the old custom until about 716.

Bede, a critic of that custom, still gave this verdict: "But whatever kind of man he was, this much about him we hold as certain. He left successors known for great self-control, for the love of God, and for life under a rule."
