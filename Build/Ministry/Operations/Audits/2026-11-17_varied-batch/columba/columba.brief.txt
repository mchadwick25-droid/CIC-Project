On a Saturday in June, an old abbot sat copying the Psalms. He came to the words "they that seek the Lord shall not want any good thing", and there he stopped. "Here I must stop, at the bottom of this page," he said. "Let Baithene write what comes next."

The abbot was Columba, also called Colum Cille. He lived in the sixth century, first in Ireland and then on Iona, a small island off northern Britain, in what is now Scotland. He belonged to the Irish monastic church, which was led by abbots rather than city bishops. Nothing he wrote has come down to us, and no one who knew him left an account. Our fullest account comes from Adomnán, abbot of Iona about a hundred years later.

Columba was born early in the sixth century to a noble family in Ireland. His father was Fedilmith and his mother was Aethne. He had already founded a monastery at Durrow, "the Field of Oaks", when he sailed to Britain in his forty-second year, with twelve disciples. He went "to make a pilgrimage for Christ", which meant leaving home for good, for God's sake. The records differ on the year, but about 563 is the usual reckoning.

On Iona he built a new monastery. Who gave him the island is not certain: some records name the Picts, and others a king called Conall. Adomnán says Columba never let an hour pass without prayer, reading, writing or work with his hands. He slept on bare rock, with a stone for a pillow, and his monks copied the Psalms.

He also went to Brude, king of the Picts, at his fortress. At first the king kept his gates shut. Adomnán says they then opened "of their own accord", and Brude honoured him for the rest of his life. Columba preached through an interpreter, and Adomnán tells of a countryman baptized "with all his household". A later writer said he turned the northern Picts to the faith, but Adomnán never says the king was baptized.

His last Saturday came in 597, by the usual reckoning. He blessed the barn and said his monks would have enough grain for the year. The white horse that carried the milk pails laid its head on his chest. Then he copied the Psalms until that verse, and left the rest for Baithene.

That night he gave his last words for the monks, and only his attendant Diormit heard them. "My sons, I entrust these last words to you. Love one another without pretence, and live in peace." At the midnight bell he ran ahead into the church and knelt at the altar to pray. Diormit found him lying there and held his head. He raised Columba's right hand, and Columba moved it as much as he could to bless his monks. Then he died.

Baithene took his place as abbot. Many houses in Britain and Ireland grew from Columba's, and Iona was the head of them all. Its abbot was a priest, yet even bishops were under him, "according to the example of their first teacher".

In 635 an English king, of Northumbria, asked Iona for a bishop. The island sent him Aidan, one of its monks, and through him the faith reached the English.

Iona dated Easter by the custom of its own country, and that clashed with Rome's. In 664, at a church meeting called the synod of Whitby, Colman, a bishop from Iona's line, defended "our most reverend Father Columba and his successors". The king ruled for Rome. Iona itself kept Columba's custom until about 716.

About a century after Columba died, Adomnán wrote his life. He claimed his name had reached Spain, Gaul, Italy and Rome itself. Bede, writing in England more than a century later, was a critic of Iona's Easter. Still, he wrote this: "But whatever kind of man he was, this much about him we hold as certain. He left successors known for great self-control, for the love of God, and for life under a rule."
