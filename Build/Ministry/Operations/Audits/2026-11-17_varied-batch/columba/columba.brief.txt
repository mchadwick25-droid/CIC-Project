On his last day, an old abbot sat copying the Psalms. When he reached the line "they that seek the Lord shall not want any good thing," he laid down his pen. "Here I must stop, at the bottom of this page," he said. "Let Baithene write what comes next."

The abbot was Columba, and his home was Iona, a small island off northern Britain, in what is now Scotland. He came from sixth-century Ireland, where the monastic church was led by abbots rather than city bishops.

Born to a noble Irish family, he had already founded a monastery at Durrow before he sailed for Britain in his forty-second year. With twelve disciples he went "to make a pilgrimage for Christ", leaving home for good, in about 563, though the old records differ.

Our fullest account comes from Adomnán, abbot of Iona about a hundred years later, who says Columba never let an hour pass without prayer, reading, writing or work. When he went to Brude, king of the Picts, the gates stayed shut until they opened "of their own accord", and the king honoured him for life. Bede, writing in England more than a century later, says he turned the northern Picts to the faith, although Adomnán never says the king was baptized.

On his last night, in June 597, only his attendant Diormit heard his words for the monks: "My sons, I entrust these last words to you. Love one another without pretence, and live in peace." At the midnight bell he ran into the church and knelt at the altar. There Diormit found him and raised his hand, so that Columba could bless his monks before he died.

Baithene succeeded him, and many houses in Britain and Ireland grew from Iona, which Bede called "the principal of them all". In 635 the king of Northumbria asked Iona for a bishop and was sent Aidan, who carried its faith to the English.

Columba's way of dating Easter clashed with Rome's, so at the synod of Whitby in 664 a bishop from Iona's line defended "our most reverend Father Columba and his successors". The king ruled for Rome, but Iona kept the old custom until about 716.

Bede was a critic of that custom, yet he gave this verdict: "But whatever kind of man he was, this much about him we hold as certain. He left successors known for great self-control, for the love of God, and for life under a rule."
