# Outline: Macrina the Younger

"Sheet" = `Source_Sheet.md` in this folder, by line number.

- **Length:** 2 minutes (about 300 words). Thin in kind: one witness (her brother) for the whole life, and none of her own writing. But that witness is close and detailed, and her afterlife is real. So stay at the target; do not stretch.

- **Setting, in words to say aloud:** "In the fourth century, in Pontus, in what is now Turkey, by a river that runs to the Black Sea" (sheet 38-43). Tradition: "the churches of Cappadocia and Pontus", or "the family of Basil of Caesarea and Gregory of Nyssa" (sheet 45). She died in 379, as scholars date it, some nine months after her brother Basil (sheet 43).

- **Thread:** She turned her family's house into a community of prayer and shared work, and her teaching on death and the hope of rising again was kept, in her voice, by the brother who sat with her as she died.

- **Shape:** Monk or ascetic. The turn (refusing marriage), the life she chose (equal with the slave women, prayer and work), the others drawn after her (mother, brothers, foundlings, widows). Keep wonders out (see Leave out).

- **Openings:**
  1. Her brother Gregory arrives after eight years apart. She is lying on the floor, on a sack spread over a board, with another board for a pillow. She lifts herself on one elbow but cannot rise to meet him (sheet 101-105).
  2. After her death, Gregory asks the deaconess Lampadia for burial clothes from the store-cupboard. Her answer (Quote 2) (sheet 129-131).

- **Story beats:**
  1. Eldest child of a Christian family in Pontus. Her grandparents had lost their goods, and one grandfather his life, for confessing Christ. She was named after her father's mother, Macrina, who had stood firm in the persecutions (sheet 33-35, 47-48). Documented (family memory, as Gregory tells it).
  2. At twelve her father chose her a husband; the young man died before the marriage. She refused every other offer. There is one marriage, she said, as there is one birth and one death, and her promised husband was not dead but only away, because of the hope of rising again (sheet 66-70). Documented.
  3. She would not leave her mother. After her father died she helped run an estate spread over three districts, and baked bread for her mother with her own hands (sheet 72-76). Documented.
  4. Gregory says that when their brother Basil came home from his studies, proud of his speaking, she drew him to a simple life of work with his hands. Basil's friend Gregory of Nazianzus says Basil had already chosen that life. Tell it as Gregory's account (sheet 78-79, 213). Contested.
  5. She led her mother to live as an equal with the slave women of the house: the same food, the same kind of bed, prayer and hymns day and night. Gregory calls this "philosophy", meaning a life given wholly to God (sheet 50-53, 81-85). She raised her baby brother Peter, who later led the men's side of the community (sheet 89-92, 153-157). In a famine she picked up babies left by the roadside to die, and raised them (sheet 93-97). Documented.
  6. In 379, after Basil's death, Gregory came. Fevered and dying, she talked with him about the soul, death and rising again. When he told her of his troubles and exile, she told him to see the gift in his fame (Quote 3) (sheet 101-117). Documented as Gregory's report.
  7. At evening, her couch turned to the east, she prayed (Quote 1). A lamp was brought; she tried to sing the evening thanksgiving for the lighting of the lamps, could not, and died with the prayer (sheet 118-128). She owned only a cloak, a head-covering and worn shoes (sheet 129-131). Crowds kept an all-night vigil "as in the case of martyrs' festivals", and she was buried beside her mother (sheet 142-146). Documented.

- **What came of them** (about a third of the story; heaviest first):
  1. Her teaching, in her voice. Gregory wrote a dialogue, *On the Soul and the Resurrection*, in which she is "the Teacher" and he the grieving questioner. **She defends** the soul's life after death and the body's rising, and tells him not to grieve like those "without hope". Its Greek title was simply her name: "the Macrinia" (sheet 165-169). Say plainly that the words are Gregory's writing of their talk; how much is hers is not known (sheet 215). Documented / Contested (how much is hers).
  2. More than a thousand years later, in 1438, Latin and Greek church leaders met at Ferrara to argue over what happens to souls after death. The Latin side quoted "Macrina speaking" from Gregory's book. The Greek side answered that Gregory was a man and could err. The Latins replied that he was the brother of Basil the Great and of blessed Macrina (sheet 170-180). Documented. Do not say she taught purgatory; say her words were quoted and argued over.
  3. The house she made: mistress and former slaves living as sisters, women and men in one community, prayer by night and day. Later scholars read Gregory's account as one of the clearest early pictures of such a house (sheet 153-161, 182-185). Documented (the house) / Dominant Modern Reconstruction (its later weight).
  4. Her brothers: Peter, who took her as "the model of all good"; Gregory, who called her "the great Macrina"; Basil, as Gregory tells it (sheet 186-191). Documented, Basil Contested.
  5. Gregory wrote her life so it would not be "unknown to our time" (sheet 192-193). Possible last line: the end of item 2, or Gregory's own purpose.

- **Quotes** (rendered by me from Gregory's Greek in Migne, PG 46, checked against Clarke's 1916 English; Greek printed with the OCR's letter errors corrected, as listed in the sheet):
  1. **Her dying prayer.** Greek, PG46 71687, 71690, 71730-71732: «Σὺ,» φησὶν, «ἔλυσας ἡμῖν, Κύριε, τοῦ θανάτου τὸν φόβον. Σὺ ζωῆς ἀληθινῆς ἀρχὴν ἡμῖν ἐποίησας τὸ τέλος τῆς ἐνταῦθα ζωῆς. Σὺ πρὸς καιρὸν διαναπαύεις ὕπνῳ τὰ σώματα, καὶ πάλιν ἀφυπνίζεις ἐν τῇ ἐσχάτῃ σάλπιγγι. (Clarke: LIFE 230, sheet 118-121.)
     **Rendering:** "You, Lord," she said, "have set us free from the fear of death. You have made the end of this life the beginning of true life for us. For a time you give our bodies rest in sleep. And you wake them again at the last trumpet."
     **Job:** her own faith at the moment it was tested; the one idea she left.
  2. **Lampadia on her wealth.** Greek, PG46 72082-72085: Ποίοις, εἶπεν, ἀποκειμένοις; Ἐν χερσὶν ἔχεις πᾶν τὸ ἀπόθετον· ἰδοὺ τὸ ἱμάτιον, ἰδοὺ τῆς κεφαλῆς ἡ καλύπτρα, τὰ τετριμμένα τῶν ποδῶν ὑποδήματα· οὗτος ὁ πλοῦτος· αὕτη ἡ περιουσία. (Clarke: LIFE 284, sheet 129-131.)
     **Rendering:** "What store?" she said. "Everything she put by is right here. Here is the cloak, here is the head-covering, and the worn-out shoes on her feet. This is her wealth. This is what she owned."
     **Job:** her poverty shown by a witness who lived with her, not told by the narrator.
  3. *(Optional, if room.)* **To Gregory.** Greek, PG46 71505-71506, 71536: «Σὺ δὲ,» φησὶ, «πόλεσι, καὶ δήμοις, καὶ ἔθνεσιν ὀνομαστὸς εἶ· καὶ σὲ πρὸς συμμαχίαν τε καὶ διόρθωσιν Ἐκκλησίαι πέμπουσι, καὶ καλοῦσι· καὶ οὐχ ὁρᾷς τὴν χάριν; (Clarke: LIFE 218, sheet 112-117.)
     **Rendering:** "But you," she said, "are known by name in cities, peoples and nations. Churches send for you and call on you, to help them and set them right. And do you not see the gift?"
     **Job:** the elder sister teaching a bishop, even from her deathbed.

- **Links:**
  - Macrina the Elder: grandmother; named for her (sheet 33-35, 201).
  - Emmelia: mother; she lived with her, led her into the ascetic life, and was buried beside her (sheet 202).
  - Basil of Caesarea: brother; drew him to the ascetic life, as Gregory tells it (contested) (sheet 78, 203).
  - Peter of Sebaste: brother; raised and taught him (sheet 89-92, 205).
  - Gregory of Nyssa: brother; he heard her teach, wrote her life, and gave her the teacher's voice in his dialogue (sheet 165-169, 206).
  - The Latin side at Ferrara, 1438: quoted her words, through Gregory's book (sheet 170-179, 209).

- **Who tells it:**
  - Gregory of Nyssa, her younger brother: an eyewitness of her last two days, writing within a few years of her death, in her honour (sheet 27, 221). Say aloud: "Almost all we know of her comes from her brother Gregory, who sat with her as she died and wrote her life soon after."
  - The Ferrara debate, 1438, more than a thousand years later (sheet 170). Say aloud: "More than a thousand years later, her words were still being quoted."
  - Clarke, Moore and Wilson, Morison, Delehaye: modern scholars; card only.

- **Leave out:**
  - The wonder stories: the healed tumour, the soldier's daughter's eye, the grain that did not run out, Gregory's dream of shining relics (sheet 136-141). They are reports Gregory heard after her death, and Gregory himself held most back. If one is wanted, tell only that he chose to leave many out.
  - The secret name Thecla from her mother's dream (sheet 57-60); the iron cross and the ring with a relic (sheet 134); the opening of the family grave: side stories.
  - "Purgatory" as her teaching (sheet 178); "Phaedo"; "double monastery"; the estate's name Annesi; the exact number of children (sheet 219, 223): modern terms or disputed details.
  - Gregory of Nazianzus as a link to her: no held text of his names her (sheet 207).
  - Any feast day: none found in the held texts (sheet 197).
