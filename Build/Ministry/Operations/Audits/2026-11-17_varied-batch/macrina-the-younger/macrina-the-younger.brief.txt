When Gregory reached his sister after eight years apart, she was lying on the floor, on a sack spread over a board. She lifted herself on one elbow, but she could not rise to meet him.

Her name was Macrina, the eldest child in the family of Basil of Caesarea and Gregory of Nyssa. She lived in the fourth century, in Pontus, a region on the Black Sea, in what is now Turkey. Almost all we know of her comes from her brother Gregory, who sat with her as she died and wrote her life a few years later, as scholars date it.

When she was twelve, the young man her father had chosen for her died before the wedding. She refused every other offer, because she believed her promised husband was not dead but only away, in the hope of rising again. Instead she stayed with her mother, whom she persuaded to live as an equal with the slave women, sharing their food, their beds and their prayer by night and day.

She died in 379, praying at evening with her couch turned to the east. "You, Lord," she said, "have set us free from the fear of death. You have made the end of this life the beginning of true life for us." When Gregory asked for burial clothes, the deaconess Lampadia explained that nothing had been stored away. "Here is the cloak, here is the head-covering, and the worn-out shoes on her feet. This is her wealth."

Gregory later wrote a dialogue on the soul and the resurrection, in which she is the Teacher and he the grieving questioner, although nobody knows how much of it is truly hers. More than a thousand years later, her words were still being quoted. In 1438, when Latin and Greek church leaders gathered at Ferrara to argue over what happens to souls after death, the Latin side quoted Macrina speaking. The Greeks objected that Gregory was a man and could be mistaken, but the Latins answered that he was the brother of Basil the Great and of blessed Macrina.
