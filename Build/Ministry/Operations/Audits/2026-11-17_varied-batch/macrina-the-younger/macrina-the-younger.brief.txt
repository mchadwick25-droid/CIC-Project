Gregory had not seen his sister for eight years. When he came, she lay on the floor on a sack spread over a board, and she could not rise to meet him.

This was Macrina, of the family of Basil of Caesarea and Gregory of Nyssa. She lived in the fourth century, in Pontus, a region on the Black Sea, in what is now Turkey. Almost all we know of her comes from her brother Gregory, who sat with her as she died and wrote her life a few years later.

At twelve she was promised to a young man, but he died before the wedding. She refused every other offer, because she held that her promised husband was only away, in the hope of rising again.

So she stayed with her mother, and led her to live as an equal with the slave women of the house, sharing their food and praying day and night. In a famine she took in babies left by the roadside to die.

She died in 379, as scholars date it. At evening she prayed. "You, Lord," she said, "have set us free from the fear of death." Then her voice failed, and she finished with her hands and lips.

When Gregory asked for burial clothes, a deaconess who lived with her answered, "You have in front of you everything she put by. Here is the cloak, here is the head-covering, and the worn-out shoes on her feet. This is her wealth."

Gregory then wrote a dialogue on the soul and the rising of the dead, with Macrina as the teacher. The words are his, and no one knows how much is hers, but in Greek the book bore her name.

More than a thousand years later, her words were still being quoted. In 1438, at Ferrara, Latin and Greek church leaders argued over what happens to souls after death, and the Latins quoted "Macrina speaking". The Greeks answered that Gregory was a man and could be wrong. The Latins replied that he was the brother of Basil the Great, and of blessed Macrina.
