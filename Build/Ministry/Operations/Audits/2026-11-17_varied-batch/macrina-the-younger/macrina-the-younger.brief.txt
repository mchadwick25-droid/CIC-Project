When Gregory reached his sister after eight years apart, she was lying on the floor, on a sack spread over a board. She lifted herself on one elbow, but she could not rise to meet him.

His sister was Macrina, the eldest of their family. Two of her brothers became bishops: Basil, of the great church of Caesarea, and Gregory himself, of Nyssa. She lived in the fourth century in Pontus, a region on the Black Sea coast of what is now Turkey. Gregory, who sat with her as she died and wrote her life a few years later, is nearly our only witness.

When she was twelve, the young man her father had chosen for her died before the wedding. She refused every other offer, because she believed her promised husband was not dead but only away, in the hope of rising again. After her father died, she led her mother to live as an equal with the slave women of the house. Mistress and slaves ate the same food, slept on the same kind of bed and prayed together by night and day.

So the family house became a community of prayer and shared work. Macrina raised her baby brother Peter, who later led its men's side, and during a famine she picked up babies left by the roadside to die, and raised them.

On the evening she died, in 379 as scholars date it, she prayed. "You, Lord," she said, "have set us free from the fear of death." Afterwards the deaconess Lampadia described all that Macrina had owned. "Here is the cloak, here is the head-covering, and the worn-out shoes on her feet. This is her wealth."

As she lay dying, she had talked with Gregory about the soul, death and rising again. From that talk he wrote a dialogue in which Macrina, as the Teacher, defends the soul's life after death and the body's rising. The words are Gregory's, so how much is hers is not known. More than a thousand years later, in 1438, Latin and Greek church leaders met at Ferrara to try to heal the split between their churches. Arguing there over what happens to souls after death, the Latin side quoted Macrina's part in the dialogue. The Greeks objected that Gregory was a man and could be mistaken, but the Latins answered that he was the brother of Basil the Great and of blessed Macrina.
