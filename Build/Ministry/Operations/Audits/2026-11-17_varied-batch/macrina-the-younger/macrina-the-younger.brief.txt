Gregory had not seen his sister for eight years. When he came, she was lying on the floor, on a sack spread over a board, with another board for a pillow. She lifted herself on one elbow, but she could not rise to meet him.

This was Macrina. She lived in the fourth century, in Pontus, a region on the Black Sea, in what is now Turkey. She came from the family of Basil of Caesarea and Gregory of Nyssa. Almost all we know of her comes from her brother Gregory, who sat with her as she died and wrote her life a few years later, as scholars date it.

She was the eldest child, and her grandparents had lost their goods for confessing Christ. At twelve she was promised to a young man, but he died before the wedding. She refused every other offer. There is one marriage, she said, as there is one birth and one death. Her promised husband was not dead, only away, because of the hope of rising again.

So she stayed with her mother and made bread for her with her own hands. In time she led her mother to live as an equal with the slave women of the house, with the same food, the same kind of bed, and prayer day and night. She raised her baby brother Peter. In a famine she picked up babies left by the roadside to die, and raised them too.

She died in 379. At evening she prayed: "You, Lord, have set us free from the fear of death. You have made the end of this life the beginning of true life for us." Then her voice failed, and she finished her prayer with her hands and lips.

Gregory asked for burial clothes from the store. A deaconess named Lampadia, who had lived beside her, answered: "What store? You have in front of you everything she put by. Here is the cloak, here is the head-covering, and the worn-out shoes on her feet. This is her wealth."

Gregory kept her teaching alive. He wrote a dialogue on the soul and the rising of the dead, with Macrina as the teacher and himself as the grieving brother. The words are his writing of their talk, and no one knows how much is truly hers. Still, in Greek the book was also called by her name.

More than a thousand years later, her words were still being quoted. In 1438, Latin and Greek church leaders met at Ferrara to argue over what happens to souls after death. The Latin side quoted "Macrina speaking". The Greeks answered that Gregory was only a man and could be wrong. The Latins replied that he was the brother of Basil the Great, and of blessed Macrina.
