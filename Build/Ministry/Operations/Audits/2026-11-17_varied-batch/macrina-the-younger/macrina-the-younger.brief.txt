Gregory had not seen his sister for eight years. When he came, she lay on the floor on a sack spread over a board, and she could not rise to meet him.

This was Macrina, of the family of Basil of Caesarea and Gregory of Nyssa. She lived in the fourth century, in Pontus, a region on the Black Sea, in what is now Turkey. Almost all we know of her comes from her brother Gregory, who sat with her as she died and wrote her life a few years later.

At twelve she was promised to a young man, but he died before the wedding. She refused every other offer. Her promised husband, she said, was not dead but only away, because of the hope of rising again.

So she stayed with her mother, and made their house a community of prayer and work. Her mother lived as an equal with the slave women, sharing their food. In a famine she took in babies left by the roadside to die.

On her last evening, in 379 as scholars date it, she prayed. "You, Lord," she said, "have set us free from the fear of death." When a lamp was brought, she wanted to sing the evening thanksgiving. Her voice failed, so she finished it in her heart, moving her hands and lips. Then she drew a deep breath, and her life and her prayer ended together.

When Gregory asked for burial clothes from what she had stored away, the deaconess Lampadia answered, "You have in front of you everything she put by. Here is the cloak, here is the head-covering, and the worn-out shoes on her feet. This is her wealth."

Gregory wrote a dialogue on the soul and the rising of the dead, with Macrina as the teacher, telling her grieving brother that the dead will rise. The words are his, and no one knows how much is hers.

More than a thousand years later, her words were still being quoted. In 1438, at Ferrara, Latin and Greek church leaders argued over what happens to souls after death, and the Latins quoted "Macrina speaking". The Greeks answered that Gregory was a man and could be wrong. The Latins replied that he was the brother of Basil the Great, and of blessed Macrina.
