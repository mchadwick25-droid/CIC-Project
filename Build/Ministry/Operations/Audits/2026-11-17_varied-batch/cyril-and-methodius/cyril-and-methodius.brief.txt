In the ninth century, in the 860s, the emperor in Constantinople asked a tired scholar to go to Moravia, in central Europe, in what is now the Czech Republic. He would go gladly, the scholar said, "if they have letters for their language." Without them, he asked, "who can write words on water, and who wants to be called a heretic?"

The scholar was Constantine, later named Cyril, from Thessalonica, a Greek city where Slavic was spoken too. With his brother Methodius he began the mission of Cyril and Methodius, and the Slavic church that grew from it. Most of what we know comes from two Lives written by their own followers. They survive only in copies made centuries later.

Rastislav of Moravia had asked for teachers in his people's own language. The Slavs had no letters, so Constantine made them, after prayer, the Lives say. Then the brothers put the church's services into Slavic. Latin clergy objected that God had chosen only three languages, the three written over the cross: Hebrew, Greek and Latin.

At Venice, Constantine answered: "Does God's rain not fall on all alike? Does the sun not shine the same on everyone?" How then, he asked, could they "fix on three languages only"? "You would order every other people and tribe to be blind and deaf."

In Rome he fell ill, became a monk named Cyril, and died in 869. He told Methodius they were "partners ploughing a single furrow," and begged him not to give up the teaching to go back to life as a monk.

Methodius went back to the Slavs as a bishop. Frankish bishops held him for two and a half years, and a Salzburg writer complained of this "Greek" and his "newly invented Slavic letters." But in 880 the pope wrote that God, who made Hebrew, Greek and Latin, "also himself created all the others, for his praise and glory."

After Methodius died in 885, his disciples were driven out of Moravia. Clement, Naum and Angelarius crossed the Danube to Bulgaria. A Life of Clement, written in Bulgaria a generation or more later, says Prince Boris thanked God for sending them. Clement became the first bishop of the Bulgarian tongue. Bulgarian priests could barely follow Greek, so he wrote them plain sermons for every feast. In this way, the Life says, he handed down to the Bulgarians all that belongs to the Church.

Writing in Kiev more than two centuries later, the chroniclers said this Slavic writing was first made for the Moravians and the Slavs around them. By then it was found among the Danube Bulgarians, and in Rus as well.
