In the ninth century, in the 860s, the emperor in Constantinople asked a tired scholar to go to Moravia, in central Europe, in what is now the Czech Republic. He would gladly go, he said, "if they have letters for their language." Without them, he asked, "who can write words on water, and who wants to be called a heretic?"

The scholar was Constantine, later named Cyril, from Thessalonica, a Greek city where Slavic was spoken too. His brother Methodius had governed a Slavic district, then became a monk on a mountain of monks called Olympus. Their work is remembered as the mission of Cyril and Methodius, and the Slavic church grew from it. Most of what we know comes from two Lives written by their own followers, which survive only in copies made centuries later.

Rastislav of Moravia had asked for teachers in his people's own language. The Slavs had no letters, so Constantine made them, and the brothers put the church's services into Slavic. Latin clergy objected that God had chosen only three languages: Hebrew, Greek and Latin.

At Venice he answered: "Does God's rain not fall on all alike? Does the sun not shine the same on everyone?" Then, he asked, "how are you not ashamed to fix on three languages only? You would order every other people and tribe to be blind and deaf."

In Rome he fell ill, became a monk named Cyril, and died in 869. He told Methodius they had been "partners ploughing a single furrow," and said: "Do not give up your teaching for the mountain's sake."

Methodius went back as bishop for the Slavs of Pannonia, but Frankish bishops said he was teaching in their land. He said it was St Peter's, so they held him prisoner for two and a half years. A Salzburg writer complained of his "newly invented Slavic letters." But in 880 Pope John VIII wrote that God, who made those three, "also himself created all the others, for his praise and glory."

After Methodius died in 885, his disciples were driven out, and Clement, Naum and Angelarius crossed the Danube to Bulgaria. A Life of Clement, written there a generation or more later, says Prince Boris thanked God for sending them. It calls Clement "the first bishop of the Bulgarian tongue": their priests could barely follow Greek, so he wrote them plain sermons for every feast.

In Kiev, more than two centuries later, chroniclers wrote that these letters were first made for the Moravians. By then they were found among the Danube Bulgarians, and in Rus, the chroniclers' own land.
