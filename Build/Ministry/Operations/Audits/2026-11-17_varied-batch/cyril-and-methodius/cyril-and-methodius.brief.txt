In the ninth century, in the 860s, the emperor in Constantinople asked a tired scholar to go to Moravia, in central Europe, in what is now the Czech Republic. He would go gladly, the scholar said, "if they have letters for their language." Without them, he asked, "who can write words on water, and who wants to be called a heretic?"

The scholar was Constantine, later called Cyril. He and his brother Methodius came from Thessalonica, a Greek city where Slavic was spoken too. Their journey began the mission of Cyril and Methodius, and the Slavic church that grew from it. Most of what we know comes from two Lives written by their own followers. They survive only in copies made centuries later.

Rastislav, the prince of Moravia, had asked for someone to teach his people in their own language. But the Slavs had no letters, so Constantine made them. The Lives say it came after prayer. He began with "In the beginning was the Word." In Moravia the brothers put the church's services into Slavic. Latin clergy objected that God had chosen only three languages, Hebrew, Greek and Latin, the three written on the cross.

At Venice the bishops pressed him, and Constantine answered: "Does God's rain not fall on all alike? Does the sun not shine the same on everyone? Do we not all breathe the air alike? Then how are you not ashamed to fix on three languages only? You would order every other people and tribe to be blind and deaf."

In Rome the disciples sang the liturgy in Slavonic in St Peter's. There Constantine fell ill, became a monk named Cyril, and died in 869, aged 42. Methodius had once been a monk on a mountain of monks called Olympus. Now his brother told him they had been "partners ploughing a single furrow," and begged him: "Do not give up your teaching for the mountain's sake."

Methodius went back as a bishop. Frankish bishops held him for two and a half years. A Salzburg writer of his day complained of this "Greek" and his "newly invented Slavic letters." But in 880 Pope John VIII questioned him, found him sound, and wrote that he who made the three chief languages "also himself created all the others, for his praise and glory." Methodius died in 885.

Then his disciples were driven out of Moravia. Clement, Naum and Angelarius crossed the Danube to Bulgaria. A Life of Clement, written in Bulgaria a generation or more later, says Prince Boris thanked God for sending them. Clement became the first bishop of the Bulgarian tongue. He wrote plain sermons for every feast, because Bulgarian priests could barely follow Greek.

Writing in Kiev more than two centuries later, the chroniclers said this Slavic writing was first made for the Moravians and their neighbours. By then it was found among the Danube Bulgarians, and in Rus as well.
