In the 860s, the emperor in Constantinople asked a tired scholar to travel north. He would go gladly, he said, "if they have letters for their language." Without them, he asked, "who can write words on water, and who wants to be called a heretic?"

The scholar was Constantine, later known as Cyril. With his brother Methodius he came from Thessalonica, a Greek city where Slavic was spoken too. So the emperor sent them to Moravia, in what is now the Czech Republic, whose ruler had asked for teachers in his people's own language.

Because the Slavs had no letters, Constantine made them: most likely the script now called Glagolitic, not the Cyrillic alphabet later named after him. Two Lives written by their followers tell most of their story, and they survive only in copies made centuries later.

In Moravia they turned the church's services into Slavic, but Latin clergy said God had chosen only Hebrew, Greek and Latin. Later, at Venice, Constantine answered such bishops: "Does God's rain not fall on all alike? Does the sun not shine the same on everyone? Do we not all breathe the air alike? Then how are you not ashamed to fix on three languages only? You would order every other people and tribe to be blind and deaf."

In Rome the pope came out to meet them, because they carried relics of Clement, an early bishop of Rome. There Constantine fell ill, took the monk's name Cyril, and died in 869, telling his brother, "Look, brother, we were partners ploughing a single furrow. And I fall in the field, having finished my day."

Methodius went back as a bishop, but Frankish bishops held him for two and a half years. A Salzburg writer of his own day complained that this "Greek", with his "newly invented Slavic letters", had partly cheapened the Latin Masses in the people's eyes. In 879 Pope John the Eighth repeated an earlier ban on singing Mass in Slavic, though Methodius could still preach in it. In 880, John questioned him in Rome, in front of the pope's bishops, and found him sound. He allowed Mass in Slavic, with the Gospel read first in Latin. And he wrote that "he who made the three chief languages, Hebrew, Greek and Latin, also himself created all the others, for his praise and glory."

After Methodius died in 885, a man he had condemned became bishop, and his disciples were driven out. Two of them, Naum and a man also named Clement, carried the letters and the Slavic services across the Danube to Bulgaria. There, a later Life of this Clement says, Prince Boris thanked God for them. Clement became "the first bishop of the Bulgarian tongue" and wrote plain sermons for priests who could barely follow Greek. Writing in Kiev more than two centuries later, the chroniclers said these letters were first made for the Moravians and their neighboring Slavs. They were found, they wrote, among the Rus and the Danube Bulgarians too.
