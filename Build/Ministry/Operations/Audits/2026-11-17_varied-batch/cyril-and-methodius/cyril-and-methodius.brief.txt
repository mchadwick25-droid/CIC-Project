In the ninth century, in the 860s, the emperor in Constantinople asked a tired scholar to travel north. He would go gladly, the scholar said, "if they have letters for their language." Without them, he asked, "who can write words on water, and who wants to be called a heretic?"

He was Constantine, a teacher of philosophy, who with his brother Methodius, a monk, came from Thessalonica, a Greek city where Slavic was spoken too.

The call came from Rastislav of Moravia, in central Europe, in what is now the Czech Republic. His people, he said, had no one to teach them in their own language. Because the Slavs had no letters, Constantine made them, and the brothers' followers say God revealed them after prayer. Most of what we know comes from two Lives written by their followers, which survive only in copies made centuries later.

In Moravia the brothers turned the church's services into Slavic. Latin clergy objected that God had chosen only three languages, the three on the sign Pilate put over the cross. At Venice Constantine answered the bishops: "Does God's rain not fall on all alike? Does the sun not shine the same on everyone? Do we not all breathe the air alike? Then how are you not ashamed to fix on three languages only? You would order every other people and tribe to be blind and deaf."

In Rome Constantine fell ill, took the monk's name Cyril, and died in 869, telling his brother, "Look, brother, we were partners ploughing a single furrow. And I fall in the field, having finished my day."

Methodius went back as a bishop, though Frankish bishops held him for two and a half years. A Salzburg writer of his own day complained that this "Greek", with his "newly invented Slavic letters", had cheapened the Latin Masses in the people's eyes. Yet in 880 Pope John wrote that "he who made the three chief languages, Hebrew, Greek and Latin, also himself created all the others, for his praise and glory."

After Methodius died in 885, a man he had condemned took his place and drove his disciples out. Clement and Naum crossed the Danube to Bulgaria, where Prince Boris thanked God for them, and Clement wrote plain sermons for priests who could barely follow Greek. Writing in Kiev more than two centuries later, the chroniclers said these Slavic letters, first made for the Moravians, were found among the Rus and the Danube Bulgarians too.
