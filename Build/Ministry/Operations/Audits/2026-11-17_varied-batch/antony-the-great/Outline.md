# Outline: Antony the Great

Sheet references (S, E, I, L, D, G) point to `Source_Sheet.md` in this folder, which gives each file and line.

- **Length:** stretch toward 3 minutes, about 420 words. Reason: a major figure (census marks him major, CEN 88288), and the impact needs room: a book about him changed lives far from Egypt (I1, I2). The record of his own words is thin (G1), so the life itself stays tight and the extra room goes to what came of him.

- **Setting, in words to say aloud:** "In Egypt, in the time of the emperor Constantine and his sons" (S4). A wealthy Christian family in an Egyptian village (S1, S2). The tradition: "the desert monks of Egypt", the men and women later called "the Desert Fathers and Mothers" (S6). Do not say "the first monk" (D3), and give no birth year as fact (D5).

- **Thread:** Antony took one Gospel reading as spoken to him, gave everything away and went into the desert to seek God, and the story of his life became the pattern that monks far beyond Egypt copied (E2, I1).

- **Shape:** Monk or ascetic.

- **Openings:**
  1. A young man walks into church thinking about the apostles who left everything, and hears the Gospel read "as though ... the passage had been read on his account" (E2).
  2. Twenty years shut in a ruined fort; friends break the door down, and he walks out looking just the same (E8).
  3. Far away in Italy, years later, a man in a garden remembers hearing about Antony and that same Gospel reading (I2).

- **Story beats:**
  1. **Orphaned young.** His Christian parents died when he was about eighteen or twenty, leaving him the farm and a little sister (E1, S1). Documented as the *Life*'s account.
  2. **The reading.** Less than six months later, on his way to church, he was thinking of the apostles who left all to follow Jesus. Inside, he heard: "go and sell that thou hast and give to the poor." He took it as meant for him. He gave his family's fields to the villagers and sold the rest for the poor, keeping a little for his sister. Soon after he gave that away too, and left his sister with trusted women who had vowed to stay unmarried (E2, E3; say "trusted women", not "convent", E3 note). Documented as the *Life*'s account.
  3. **Learning near home.** At that time there were hermits only near the villages; no monk yet knew the far desert. He learned from an old hermit in the next village and from every good man he heard of, working with his hands, giving to the needy, eating bread and salt once a day (S5, E4, S7). "Discipline" here means a life of training, like an athlete's (S7). Documented as the *Life*'s account.
  4. **Going further out.** He shut himself in a tomb some way from the village, then crossed the river to a deserted fort and walled up the door. Bread was let down to him twice a year. The *Life* says he fought demons there; keep any of this in its voice: "the *Life* tells how..." (E6, E7). About twenty years later, many wanted to live as he did. People who knew him came and forced the door. He came out, says the *Life*, neither fat nor wasted, the same as before. Many followed, and, in the *Life*'s Greek, "the desert became a city of monks" (rendered from VAG 70548–70549; do not say the 1892 "colonised", whose modern sense misleads; E8). Documented as the *Life*'s account; the struggle with demons is a wonder in the witness's voice.
  5. **In the city of the martyrs.** When persecution came under the emperor Maximinus, he went to Alexandria to serve Christians held in prisons and mines for their faith. He wanted to be martyred but would not hand himself in. When monks were banned from the court, he washed his cloak and stood in plain view of the governor (E10). Documented as the *Life*'s account.
  6. **The inner mountain.** Crowds and healings drove him three days into the desert, to a mountain with a spring and a few palms. He grew his own grain so as to burden no one, and wove baskets to give visitors in return for what they brought him. When people came for healing, he told them it was not his but the Lord's (E11, E12). Emperors wrote to him; he told his monks not to be amazed, since an emperor is a man (Q1, E16). Documented as the *Life*'s account.
  7. **Defending the faith; the hidden grave.** DEFENDED: when Arians claimed he agreed with them, he came down to Alexandria and taught that the Son of God was not created (E14; gloss in E14). Near a hundred and five, he asked his two companions to bury him secretly, so no one would keep his body in a house as the Egyptians did their holy dead. He left his sheepskins to two bishops, Athanasius and Serapion (E19, E20, E21). Documented as the *Life*'s account.

- **What came of him** (about a third of the story):
  1. **The book became the pattern.** It was written for monks abroad as "a sufficient pattern" (I1a), and was soon in Latin (I1c). The *Life* itself asks how a man hidden on a mountain came to be heard of in Spain, Gaul, Rome and Africa (I1b).
  2. **Augustine.** At Trier, two officials of the emperor's court found the *Life* in a monks' cottage, read it, and left the court that day to serve God (I2). Pontitianus, a fellow official who was at Trier that day, told the story to Augustine in Milan. In a garden there, in tears, Augustine remembered Antony and that Gospel reading. He opened Paul's letters, read, and his doubt was gone (I2, Q3; AUG 13125–13134). End here, or on the reach in item 1.
  3. **The desert filled.** Disciples spread to Egypt, Libya, Palestine, Syria and Arabia (Sozomen, much later, I3b). Hilarion came from Palestine to learn from him, went home, and began "as Antony had begun" (Jerome, I3c).
  4. DEFENDED: his stand for Nicaea's faith and for his bishop (I4). Already told in beat 7; one clause at most here.

- **Quotes (3 at most):**
  - **Q1** — job: shows his plain, unimpressed voice and what he thought truly amazing. Source: VA §81, Greek VAG 77108–77112; printed English NP4 33193–33195: "Do not be astonished if an emperor writes to us, for he is a man; but rather wonder that God wrote the Law for men and has spoken to us through His own Son." Greek: "Μὴ θαυμάζετε, εἰ γράφει βασιλεὺς πρὸς ἡμᾶς, ἄνθρωπος γάρ ἐστιν· ἀλλὰ μᾶλλον θαυμάζετε, ὅτι ὁ Θεὸς τὸν νόμον ἀνθρώποις ἔγραψε, καὶ διὰ τοῦ ἰδίου Υἱοῦ λελάληκεν ἡμῖν." (OCR corrected as in E16). **Rendering (from the Greek):** "Do not be amazed if an emperor writes to us, for he is a man. Be amazed instead that God wrote the Law for human beings. And he has spoken to us through his own Son."
  - **Q2** — job: why he would not stay among people; the image his tradition is remembered by. Source: VA §85, Greek VAG 77522–77527 and 77605; printed English NP4 33352–33355: "Fishes, if they remain long on dry land, die. And so monks lose their strength if they loiter among you and spend their time with you. Wherefore as fish must hurry to the sea, so must we hasten to the mountain. Lest haply if we delay we forget the things within us." Greek as in E17. **Rendering (from the Greek):** "Fish that linger on dry land die. In the same way, monks who linger with you and spend their days among you lose their strength. So, as the fish must hurry to the sea, we must hurry to the mountain. If we linger, we may forget what is within." (The last clause is read across a column break in the OCR, E17.)
  - **Q3** — job: the impact, in Augustine's own words. Source: Augustine, *Confessions* VIII.12.29, Latin AUGL 13470–13476, 13501; printed English AUG 13117–13124 (Pilkington). Latin: "audieram enim de Antonio, quod ex euangelica lectione, cui forte superuenerat, admonitus fuerit, tamquam sibi diceretur quod legebatur: uade, uende omnia, quae habes, da pauperibus et habebis thesaurum in caelis; et ueni, sequere me, et tali oraculo confestim ad te esse conuersum." **Rendering (from the Latin):** "For I had heard this of Antony. He had come in by chance during the Gospel reading, and was urged on by it. It was as though the words read were spoken to him. 'Go, sell all that you have, give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven. And come, follow me.' By such a divine word he was at once turned to you." ("You" is God; Augustine's book is addressed to God. The writer should make that clear in the sentence that introduces it. "Divine word" renders *oraculum*; "oracle" would mislead.)

- **Links:**
  - Athanasius of Alexandria — wrote his life (traditional ascription, D1); received one of his sheepskins (L1).
  - Serapion, bishop — was with him; received the other sheepskin (L2).
  - Augustine of Hippo — heard of him; read of him; quoted the Gospel reading that turned him (L15, I2).
  - Hilarion — met him; learned from him; kept his feast at his mountain (Jerome's account, written later; L10, I3c).
  - Constantine and his sons — wrote to him (L7).
  - Evagrius of Antioch — translated his life into Latin (L14).
  - Jerome — wrote of him (L16).
  - Didymus the Blind — met him (card only, L9, D6).
  - Paul of Thebes — Jerome's later story of a meeting (card only, L11, D3).

- **Who tells it:**
  - The *Life of Antony*: written within a few years of his death (editor's dating, 356–362), long credited to Athanasius, bishop of Alexandria, who says he saw him many times (D1). It is a book of praise and full of wonders. Line to say aloud: "Most of what we know comes from one book, a Life written soon after his death, which the church has long credited to Athanasius, bishop of Alexandria."
  - Augustine, *Confessions*: about forty years after Antony's death, telling his own experience. He calls Antony's deeds "so recent, and almost in our own" times (AUG 12565–12566). Line to say aloud: "A generation after Antony died, in Milan, Augustine heard his story." If Augustine's sense of nearness is wanted, credit it to him: deeds, he wrote, "so recent, and almost in our own" times.
  - Jerome (about thirty-five years later) and Sozomen (about ninety years later): later writers. If Sozomen's disciples list is used, say "a historian writing nearly a century later".

- **Leave out:**
  - "The first monk" or "father of all monks" as fact: the *Life* shows hermits before him, and Jerome backs Paul of Thebes (D3).
  - Exact birth or death years, and the village name Coma, unless clearly marked as later report (D4, D5).
  - The long run of demon fights, healings and foreknowledge (E5, E6, E12; §§57–66, 82, 86): one at most, and only in the *Life*'s voice. The beast visions and the hyenas are side stories.
  - Balacius and the letter he spat on: the two accounts disagree (D2).
  - "He could not read or write" as flat fact (D7). Say, with the *Life*, that he had not learned letters and needed an interpreter for Greek.
  - The seven Coptic letters as texts we have: none is held (G1, I6).
  - The *Paradise* sayings: compiled centuries later (I5).
  - Paul of Thebes and Antony's visit to him: Jerome's later story (L11).
  - Burial-custom detail beyond one sentence (E20): it explains the hidden grave, nothing more.

- **Do not say** (added from the round-two review, 2026-11-17): Do not drop his stand against the Arians (beat 7): it is what he defended.

- **Do not say** (added from Mark's source question, 2026-11-17): do not attach "in the time of the emperor Constantine and his sons" to his youth or the church visit. Those rulers wrote to him late in life (VA §81). Say instead that he "lived on into the time of the emperor Constantine and his sons".
