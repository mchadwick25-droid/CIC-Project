In Egypt, in the time of the emperor Constantine and his sons, a young man named Antony walked to church thinking about the apostles, who had left everything to follow Jesus. Inside, he heard the Gospel's call to sell what you have and give to the poor, and he took it as spoken to him. That choice led him into the life of Egypt's desert monks.

His wealthy Christian parents had died months before, when he was about eighteen or twenty, leaving him the family farm. He gave the fields to the villagers and sold the rest for the poor, and then he learned from the hermits who lived near the villages, working with his hands and eating bread and salt once a day.

Most of what we know comes from one book, a Life written soon after his death, which the church has long credited to Athanasius, bishop of Alexandria. It tells how he went further, to a deserted fort across the river, where he walled up the door, and bread was let down to him twice a year. After about twenty years, because many wanted to live as he did, people who knew him forced the door. He came out the same as before, and so many followed him that "the desert became a city of monks."

Crowds seeking healing drove him three days into the desert, to a mountain with a spring and palms. Yet when Arians claimed he agreed with them, he came down to Alexandria and taught that the Son of God was not created. Near a hundred and five, he asked to be buried in secret, so that no one could keep his body in a house, as Egyptians kept their holy dead.

Written for monks abroad as a pattern to follow, the Life was soon read in Latin. It asks how a man hidden on a mountain came to be heard of in Spain, Gaul, Rome and Africa. At Trier, two court officials read it in a monks' cottage and left the court that day to serve God.

A generation after Antony died, in Milan, Augustine heard that story from an official who had been at Trier. Soon after, while he wept in a garden, he remembered Antony, and writing to God in his Confessions, he recalled: "For I had heard this of Antony. He had come in by chance during the Gospel reading, and was urged on by it. It was as though the words read were spoken to him. 'Go, sell all that you have, give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven. And come, follow me.' By such a divine word he was at once turned to you."

Augustine opened Paul's letters and read, and his doubt was gone.
