A young man walked into church one day, thinking about the apostles who left everything to follow Jesus. There he heard the Gospel tell him to sell what he had and give to the poor, and he took it as meant for him.

His name was Antony, and he lived in Egypt, in the time of the emperor Constantine and his sons. His wealthy Christian parents had died less than six months before, leaving him the farm and a little sister. Most of what we know comes from one book, a Life written soon after his death, which the church has long credited to Athanasius, bishop of Alexandria.

Antony gave his fields to the villagers, sold the rest for the poor, and left his sister with trusted women who had vowed to stay unmarried. Hermits then lived only near the villages, so he learned from an old hermit nearby, eating bread and salt once a day.

Then he crossed the river to a deserted fort and walled up the door. The Life tells how he fought demons there. About twenty years later, people who knew him forced the door open, and he came out, says the Life, the same as before. Many followed him, and in the Life's words, the desert became a city of monks.

He moved on to a mountain deep in the desert, and grew his own grain so that he would burden no one. Even emperors wrote to him, and he told his monks: "Do not be amazed if an emperor writes to us, for he is a man. Be amazed instead that God wrote the Law for human beings."

When a group called the Arians claimed he agreed with them, he came down to Alexandria and taught that the Son of God was not created. Near a hundred and five, he asked to be buried in secret, so no one could keep his body in a house, as Egyptians did their holy dead.

The Life was written for monks in other lands, as a pattern to follow, and it was soon in Latin. At Trier, two officials of the emperor's court found it in a monks' cottage. They read it and left the court that same day to serve God.

A generation after Antony died, a fellow official told that story in Milan to a man named Augustine. In a garden there, in tears, Augustine remembered Antony. He later wrote: "For I had heard this of Antony. He had come in by chance during the Gospel reading, and was urged on by it. It was as though the words read were spoken to him." That one reading, Augustine wrote, had turned Antony at once to God.

Then Augustine opened Paul's letters and read, and his doubt was gone.
