In Egypt, a young man walked to church thinking about the apostles, who had left everything to follow Jesus. Inside, he heard the Gospel's call to sell what you have and give to the poor, and Antony took it as spoken to him. That choice took him into the desert. He was still there when the emperor Constantine and his sons wrote him letters.

His wealthy Christian parents had died months before, when he was about eighteen or twenty, leaving him the farm. After giving the fields to the villagers and selling the rest for the poor, he learned from nearby hermits, working with his hands and eating bread and salt once a day.

His story is told in one book, a Life written soon after his death, which the church has long credited to Athanasius, bishop of Alexandria. Demons fill much of it. In a tomb outside the village, it says, they beat him until a friend found him lying as though dead. Then he went further, to a deserted fort across the river, where he walled up the door, and bread was let down to him twice a year. After about twenty years, because many wanted to live as he did, people who knew him forced the door. He came out, it says, the same as before, and so many followed him that "the desert became a city of monks."

Crowds seeking healing drove him three days into the desert, to a mountain with a spring. The Life says the Arians, who taught that the Son of God was created, lied that he agreed with them. Then, it says, the bishops summoned him, and he came down to Alexandria to teach that the Son was not created. Near a hundred and five, he asked to be buried in secret, so that no one could keep his body in a house, as Egyptians kept their holy dead.

Written for monks abroad as a pattern to follow, the Life was soon read in Latin. It asks how a man hidden on a mountain came to be heard of in Spain, Gaul, Rome and Africa. At Trier, two court officials read it in a monks' cottage and left the court that day to serve God.

A generation after Antony died, in Milan, an official who had been at Trier told Augustine what happened there. Soon after, weeping in a garden, Augustine remembered Antony and that same Gospel reading. In his Confessions, he recalled: "For I had heard this of Antony. He had come in by chance during the Gospel reading, and was urged on by it. It was as though the words read were spoken to him."

There in the garden, Augustine opened Paul's letters and read, and his doubt was gone.
