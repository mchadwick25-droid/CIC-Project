In Egypt, in the time of the emperor Constantine and his sons, a young man walked to church thinking about the apostles, who had left everything to follow Jesus. Inside, he heard the Gospel's call to sell what you have and give to the poor, and Antony took it as spoken to him.

His wealthy Christian parents had died a few months before, when he was about eighteen or twenty, leaving him the farm and a little sister. He gave the fields to the villagers and sold the rest for the poor, and soon he left his sister with trusted women who had vowed to stay unmarried.

Most of what we know comes from one book, a Life written soon after his death, which the church has long credited to Athanasius, bishop of Alexandria. Because hermits at that time lived only near the villages, Antony learned from them there, working with his hands and eating bread and salt once a day.

Then he went further out, to a deserted fort across the river, where he walled up the door, so that bread had to be let down to him twice a year. When people who knew him forced the door some twenty years later, he came out, the Life says, the same as before. So many followed him that "the desert became a city of monks."

Crowds seeking healing drove him three days deeper into the desert, to a mountain with a spring and a few palms, where he grew grain so as to burden no one. When he was near a hundred and five, he asked to be buried in secret, so that no one could keep his body in a house, as Egyptians kept their holy dead.

The Life was written for monks abroad as a pattern to follow, and it was soon read in Latin. At Trier, two officials of the emperor's court found it in a monks' cottage, read it, and left the court that day to serve God.

A generation after Antony died, in Milan, Augustine heard that story from a fellow official who had been at Trier, and soon after, while he wept in a garden, he remembered Antony. In his Confessions, a book he addressed to God, he wrote: "For I had heard this of Antony. He had come in by chance during the Gospel reading, and was urged on by it. It was as though the words read were spoken to him. 'Go, sell all that you have, give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven. And come, follow me.' By such a divine word he was at once turned to you."

Augustine opened Paul's letters and read, and his doubt was gone.
