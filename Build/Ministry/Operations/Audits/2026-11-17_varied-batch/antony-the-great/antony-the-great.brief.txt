In Egypt, in the time of the emperor Constantine and his sons, a young man from a wealthy Christian family walked to church thinking about the apostles, who had left everything to follow Jesus. Inside, he heard the Gospel's call to sell what you have and give to the poor, and Antony took it as spoken to him.

His parents had died a few months before, when he was about eighteen or twenty, leaving him the farm and a little sister. He gave the fields to the villagers and sold the rest for the poor, and before long he left his sister with trusted women who had vowed to stay unmarried.

Most of what we know comes from one book, a Life written soon after his death, which the church has long credited to Athanasius, bishop of Alexandria. At that time hermits lived only near the villages, and Antony learned from them, working with his hands and eating bread and salt once a day.

Then he went further out, to a deserted fort across the river, where he walled up the door and had bread let down to him twice a year. When people who knew him forced the door some twenty years later, he came out, the Life says, the same as before, and so many followed him that "the desert became a city of monks."

Because crowds came seeking healing, he moved three days deeper into the desert, to a mountain with a spring and a few palms, where he grew his own grain so as to burden no one. Near the age of a hundred and five, he asked his two companions to bury him in secret, so that no one would keep his body in a house, as Egyptians kept their holy dead.

The Life was written for monks abroad as a pattern to follow, and it was soon read in Latin. At Trier, two officials of the emperor's court found it in a monks' cottage, read it, and left the court that same day to serve God.

A generation after Antony died, in Milan, Augustine heard that story from a fellow official who had been at Trier. Soon after, weeping in a garden, he remembered Antony. In his Confessions, a book he addressed to God, he wrote: "For I had heard this of Antony. He had come in by chance during the Gospel reading, and was urged on by it. It was as though the words read were spoken to him. 'Go, sell all that you have, give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven. And come, follow me.' By such a divine word he was at once turned to you."

Augustine opened Paul's letters and read, and his doubt was gone.
