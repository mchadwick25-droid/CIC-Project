A young man walked into church one day, thinking about the apostles who left everything to follow Jesus. There he heard the Gospel tell him to sell what he had and give to the poor, and he took it as meant for him.

His name was Antony, and he lived in Egypt, in the time of the emperor Constantine and his sons. His wealthy Christian parents had died less than six months before, leaving him the farm and a little sister. Most of what we know comes from one book, a Life written soon after his death, which the church has long credited to Athanasius, bishop of Alexandria.

Antony gave his family's fields to the villagers and sold the rest for the poor. He left his sister with trusted women who had vowed to stay unmarried. In those days hermits lived only near the villages, so he learned from an old hermit nearby, working with his hands and eating bread and salt once a day.

Then he crossed the river to a deserted fort and walled up the door, and bread was let down to him twice a year. The Life tells how he fought demons there. About twenty years later, people who knew him forced the door, and he came out, says the Life, the same as before. Many followed him, and in the Life's words, the desert became a city of monks.

He moved on to a mountain three days into the desert, and grew his own grain so that he would burden no one. Even emperors wrote to him, and he told his monks: "Do not be amazed if an emperor writes to us, for he is a man. Be amazed instead that God wrote the Law for human beings."

Near the age of a hundred and five, he asked to be buried in secret, so that no one would keep his body in a house, as Egyptians kept their holy dead.

The Life was written for monks in other lands, as a pattern for their own lives, and it was soon in Latin. At Trier, two officials of the emperor's court found the book in a monks' cottage. They read it and left the court that same day to serve God.

A generation after Antony died, a fellow official told that story in Milan to a man named Augustine. In a garden there, in tears, Augustine remembered Antony. He wrote of it in a book that speaks to God, so the "you" is God: "For I had heard this of Antony. He had come in by chance during the Gospel reading, and was urged on by it. It was as though the words read were spoken to him. 'Go, sell all that you have, give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven. And come, follow me.' By such a divine word he was at once turned to you."

Augustine opened Paul's letters and read, and his doubt was gone.
