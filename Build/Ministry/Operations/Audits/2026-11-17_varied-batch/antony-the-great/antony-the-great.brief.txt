A young man walked into church, thinking about the apostles who left everything to follow Jesus. There the Gospel reading spoke of selling all you have and giving to the poor, and he took it as meant for him.

This was Antony, in Egypt, in the time of the emperor Constantine and his sons. He would become one of the desert monks of Egypt, the men and women later called the Desert Fathers and Mothers. His wealthy Christian parents had died less than six months before, leaving him the farm and a little sister. Most of what we know comes from one book, a Life written soon after his death and long credited to Athanasius, bishop of Alexandria.

Antony gave his fields to the villagers, sold the rest for the poor, and left his sister with trusted women who had vowed to stay unmarried. Hermits then lived only near the villages, and no monk yet knew the far desert. He learned from an old hermit nearby, eating bread and salt once a day.

Then he crossed the river to a deserted fort and walled up the door. The Life tells how he fought demons there. About twenty years later, many wanted to live as he did, so people who knew him forced the door. He came out, says the Life, the same as before. Others followed, and in the Life's words, the desert became a city of monks.

When crowds came for healing, he moved three days into the desert, to a mountain where he grew his own grain so he would burden no one. Even emperors wrote to him. He told his monks: "Do not be amazed if an emperor writes to us, for he is a man. Be amazed instead that God wrote the Law for human beings."

When a group called the Arians claimed he agreed with them, he came down to Alexandria and taught that the Son of God was not created. Near a hundred and five, he asked to be buried in secret, so no one could keep his body in a house, as Egyptians did their holy dead.

The Life was written as a pattern for monks in other lands, and was soon in Latin. At Trier, two officials of the emperor's court found it in a monks' cottage. They read it and left the court that same day to serve God.

A generation after Antony died, a fellow official told the story to Augustine in Milan. In a garden there, in tears, he remembered Antony, and later wrote: "For I had heard this of Antony. He had come in by chance during the Gospel reading, and was urged on by it. It was as though the words read were spoken to him."

Then Augustine opened Paul's letters and read, and his doubt was gone.
