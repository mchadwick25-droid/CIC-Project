## (c) Quotations

Q1 (one sentence, split in the brief by "Suetonius writes")
- file: `suetonius_lives-of-the-caesars-loeb_rolfe1914.txt`
- lines: 25907-25909
- printed: "Since the Jews constantly made disturbances at the instigation of Chrestus, he expelled them from Rome."
- rendering: "The Jews were constantly causing disturbances at the instigation of Chrestus," ... "and he expelled them from Rome."
- original: "Iudaeos impulsore Chresto assidue tumultuantis Roma expulit." (SR 25843-25844)
- notes: The Latin has a participle, "tumultuantis", with no word for "since"; Rolfe adds the cause, and the rendering joins with "and" instead. The Latin can also be read as "he expelled the Jews who were constantly causing disturbances", a smaller group; the rendering, like both printed versions, takes it of the Jews as a whole. "Impulsore" is "instigator"; "at the instigation of" follows both printed versions. Thomson and Forester print "one Chrestus" (ST 10640); the Latin has no word for "one".

Q2
- file: `suetonius_lives-of-the-caesars-loeb_rolfe1914.txt`
- lines: 28523-28525
- printed: "Punishment was inflicted [on] the Christians, a class of men given to a new and mischievous superstition." (the file's scan reads "inflicted a the Christians"; "on" is supplied)
- rendering: "The Christians were punished, a class of people with a new and harmful superstition."
- original: "afflicti suppliciis Christiani, genus hominum superstitionis novae ac maleficae" (SR 28458-28460)
- notes: "Maleficae" is told as "harmful". Rolfe's "mischievous" now sounds light, and Thomson and Forester's "impious" (ST 11613) is a different sense; the Latin word can also carry the sense of sorcery, which neither printed version gives and the rendering does not claim. "Suppliciis" is plural, "punishments"; "were punished" keeps the passive and drops the plural. "Hominum" is "people". "Given to" is Rolfe's addition; the Latin has a bare genitive, told as "with".

## (e) Significance and stances

Suetonius gives two outside notices: that Claudius expelled the Jews of Rome over disturbances "at the instigation of Chrestus" (SR 25843-25844), and that under Nero the Christians were punished as holders of a new and harmful superstition (SR 28458-28460). Acts 18:2 confirms the expulsion; whether Chrestus is Christ cannot be settled from the line, though Tertullian and Lactantius show that Romans did say Chrestus and Chrestian. What came of the notices among Christians the held sources do not show; what they show is Christian writers using Suetonius as an authority and a model (Tertullian; Jerome's preface, J3 38773-38780). The record stops there.

## (f) Craft self-check

Line of the story: a Roman court biographer leaves two short notices of the Christians, one of them unclear, and later Christians valued him as a model for writing lives, not for those notices.

- Good storytelling. Weakest: "The second notice is in the life of Nero, in a list of abuses he put down, between a rule on what taverns could sell and a curb on chariot drivers." Bar: Methodius, "Little else about Methodius is certain." A thin record is weighed in place; mine carries more list than story.
- Flow. Weakest link: from "...among Rome's Jews." to "Tertullian of Carthage did complain that Romans said Chrestian for Christian". Bar: Athanasius, "Sozomen adds a rumor that Athanasius had hidden the man himself. Either way, the council deposed him." The word "did" carries the link to the second reading; it is thinner than the bar's.
- No AI tells. Weakest: "Jerome looked to Suetonius for something else." Bar: John Chrysostom, "He rebuked men in office too." It is a short turn that sets up the next sentence; a reviewer may hear it as a connector.
- No hanging sentences. Weakest: "The first is in the life of Claudius." Bar: Benedict, "The Rule is Benedict's own words." Short, but the quotation follows at once.
- Everything relevant. Weakest: "The book of Acts also says that Claudius ordered all the Jews to leave Rome, so the expulsion has a second witness." Bar: Constantine, "Lactantius, who wrote in Constantine's own day, knows only a dream ...". Kept because it bears on how far the notice can be trusted.
- Sentences cut on the sentence test: 8 (his father at Betriacum; his full name; Pliny's letters and the bar; the dismissal by Hadrian; the Claudius notice's place among dealings with foreign peoples; Tertullian on the games; "Christian writers do complain that Romans got the name wrong"; the old brief's closing line on Paulinus). The old brief's four sentences on Rolfe's and Thomson and Forester's notes moved to the card.

## Notes for the coordinator

- "Around 120" and "secretary to the emperor Hadrian" rest on Rolfe's introduction and the Thomson and Forester preface, both reporting Spartianus. The held file has Spartianus's Latin only for the reason for the dismissal (SR 239-241), not for the office. If that is too thin for a plain statement, the opening could say "In the time of the emperor Hadrian".
- "The sources we hold show no early Christian writer quoting either notice" is a negative from searches, not from reading every file. Orosius, who is known to quote the Claudius notice, is not held. If a negative of this kind should not stand in a brief, cut the sentence and let "Jerome looked to Suetonius for something else" follow the Nero paragraph.
- The second reading of the Chrestus line ("a confused report of quarrels over Christ among Rome's Jews") is the modern editors' reading (SR 25871-25875; ST 20363-20372) told without their names, as the assignment asked. No ancient writer held applies the Chrestus spelling to this line of Suetonius; Tertullian and Lactantius only show that the spelling was common.
- Lactantius is placed only as "later". His date is not checked against a held line here.
- "Near the end of the fourth century" for Jerome's preface is reckoned from "the fourteenth year of the Emperor Theodosius" (J3 38784-38786); the NPNF note prints "a.d. 492", a misprint for 392.
- Old brief: it quoted the Nero notice in Thomson and Forester's wording ("impious"); the Latin "maleficae" does not mean impious, so the new rendering is made from the Latin. Its statement that Rolfe "takes" the missing "one" as a sign that Christ is meant is accurate to Rolfe ("absence of quodam") but belongs on the card. Its biography (father, Pliny, dismissal) is accurate to the held files and was cut only for relevance. I found no claim in it that the held files contradict.
