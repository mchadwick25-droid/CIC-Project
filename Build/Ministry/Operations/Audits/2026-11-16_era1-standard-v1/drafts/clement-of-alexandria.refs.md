## (e) Significance and stances

- He defended the use of Greek philosophy by Christians as a preparation for Christ (Stromata I.5). Consequence shown by Jerome, Letter 70: Origen wrote Miscellanies after his example, and Jerome cites Clement's books to justify quoting pagan writers.
- He held, against the Valentinians, that knowledge is built on faith and no one is saved by nature (Stromata II.3, V.1, VII.10). The record stops there: no held source shows a reply or a result.
- He taught Origen and Alexander of Jerusalem (Eusebius VI.6, VI.14; Jerome, Lives 38), and Alexander's letter says he built up the church (Eusebius VI.11).

## (f) Craft self-check

The line of the story: a man who searched the Greek world for a teacher came to lead the school at Alexandria, argued that Greek philosophy prepared the way for Christ and that knowledge rests on faith, and his pupils and Jerome carried the first of these forward.

1. Good storytelling. Weakest: "The sources we have show no reply from the Valentinians." Bar: Gregory of Nyssa, "The sources we have do not show what came of this teaching in his own day." It reaches the same plain limit; it is a stop in the story, as the bar's is.
2. Flow. Weakest link: from "...the heart of philosophy." to "The followers of Valentinus, as Clement reports them, left faith to simple believers...". The second stance starts cold. Bar: Gregory of Tours, "He held the same ground against the Arians of Spain." The bar links its second stance better; mine relies on the paragraph break.
3. No AI tells. Weakest: "Clement answered that God is the cause of every good thing." Bar: Origen, "He held that the Son had always been with the Father". Plain report of a stated position; I think it holds.
4. No hanging sentences. Weakest: the close, "...had strengthened and built up the church." Bar: Gregory of Nazianzus, "among them he named Gregory the Theologian." The close is a witness's judgment on his work and ends the pupils' thread; it was not chosen as a tag line, but a reviewer may want the Jerome paragraph last instead.
5. Everything relevant. Weakest: "Eusebius of Caesarea judged that this teacher was Pantaenus, who led the school before Clement." Bar: Methodius, "Jerome says he was bishop of Olympus and later of Tyre". It is kept because it ties the search to the school he then led.

Sentences cut on the sentence test: 9 from the old brief's matter (the Sicilian bee, the namesake, the list of teachers' places in full, McGiffert's 202, the three titles, "Gnostic Notes", the word gnostic and its gloss, the two saving changes, the "remedy against forgetfulness" close), and 3 from my own first draft (the emperor Severus in the opening; the teachers' places one by one; Alexander's "master and benefactor" as a clause of its own).

## Notes for the coordinator

- Order of stances: the philosophy stance and its consequence (Origen, Jerome) come first, then the Valentinian stance with its plain limit, then the pupils' close. Alexander's letter closes because it is a contemporary's judgment of what Clement did for a church.
- "Some people spoke against Greek philosophy, as if it dragged believers away from the faith" rests on Clement's own defensive wording (CA 27684-27691); he does not say who they were, and the brief does not say they were Christians.
- "Another pupil" for Alexander rests on Alexander's "my master" (E 34354). Eusebius himself calls Clement and Pantaenus Alexander's "familiar acquaintances" (E 34330-34333).
- Where Clement was when he "strengthened and built up the church" is not stated in the letter ("Being here"); McGiffert places it in Cappadocia. The brief says only "while with him".
- The Stromata are not held in Greek, so both renderings were made from the printed English alone, with McGiffert's Eusebius as a second English witness for Q1.
- No claim from the old brief was found wrong. Its McGiffert sentence is moved to the card under the no-modern-scholar rule.
