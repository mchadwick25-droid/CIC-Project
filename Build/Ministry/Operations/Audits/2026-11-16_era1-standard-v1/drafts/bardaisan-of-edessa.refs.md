## (a) Moved to the card

- McGiffert and Hort: the dialogue was "undoubtedly written by one of Bardesanes' disciples" (E 26620-26630). The brief rests the point on the text itself, where the narrator is addressed as Philip (A8 68337-68338, 68878-68879).
- That the Book of the Laws of Countries is the dialogue On Fate that Eusebius names: the ANF note (A8 67899-67907) and Holl (EpH note to 56.1.3). The brief says only "a dialogue of his on fate" for Eusebius and does not equate the two. Epiphanius's "against Abeidas the astronomer, on fate" (EpH 26421-26427) matches the Avida of the Laws; card.
- Dates. "About 155 to about 223" comes through McGiffert from the Chronicle of Edessa and Barhebraeus; Holl gives Barhebraeus's year of death as 221/2 (EpH note after 26427). Eusebius puts him "in the same reign", that of Marcus Aurelius (E 26525-26529); Epiphanius has him live past Abgar "to the times of Antoninus Caesar, not the one called Pius but Verus" (EpH 26421-26422). Holl, McGiffert, Hort and Hilgenfeld take this as a confusion of emperors. The brief's "Around 180" is a year both the ancient placing and the modern dates allow; the card should say it is approximate and why.
- Which Abgar, and which emperor's companion (Holl: Abgar IX; Caracalla or Elagabalus): EpH notes after 26334 and 26427. The brief says "Abgar" and "an emperor's companion" only. The companion's name, Apollonius (EpH 26427), is card only.

## (e) Significance and stances

He defended human freedom against rule by the stars (the Laws of Countries, his pupil Philip's report); Eusebius praises a dialogue of his on fate and says his pupils put his works into Greek. He held that the body returns to dust apart from Adam's sin (Ephrem's report, a hostile witness) and, by Epiphanius, denied the resurrection; Ephrem answered him in the fourth century, and Sozomen says Ephrem's hymns were written to displace the songs of his son Harmonius. The record does not show which account of his life, Eusebius's or Epiphanius's, is right.

## (f) Craft self-check

Line of the story: a Syriac teacher at Edessa argued, in his pupil's record, that people are free of the stars; later writers disagreed about when he went wrong, and Ephrem's answer to him gave the Syriac church its hymns.

- Good storytelling. Weakest: "Two later writers tell his life in opposite orders." A paragraph that opens on the sources. Bar: Methodius, "Socrates of Constantinople says Methodius attacked Origen in many places and then, as if taking it all back, praised him in a dialogue."
- Flow. Weakest link: "... put his Syriac works into Greek. Two later writers tell his life ..." Bar: Gregory of Nyssa, "After Basil died, Gregory wrote, he 'inherited the controversy with Eunomius.'"
- No AI tells. Weakest: "He grants the stars some power over health and fortune, yet he denies that they rule what a person chooses." A contrast, but it is the shape of his own argument (A8 68396-68400, 68994-68997). Bar: Athanasius, "If the Son were only a creature, Athanasius argued, his becoming man would have left us as we were, not joined to God."
- No hanging sentences. Weakest: "Bardaisan admits there that he once followed the astrologers." It leads into what he grants the stars. Bar: Origen, "His father died a martyr."
- Sentence test / everything relevant. Weakest: "A man named Avida had asked why God did not make people unable to do wrong." Kept because it is the question the whole argument answers. Bar: Ephrem, "They said they had no one honest enough to run the relief, so Ephrem asked what they thought of him."

Sentences cut on the sentence test: 8 (the quoted greeting, "So begins", the Christians quotation, Eusebius's two praises, McGiffert on the order, McGiffert on Ephrem, "A pupil named Philip wrote down how he taught", "On the body, at least, his opponents agree").
