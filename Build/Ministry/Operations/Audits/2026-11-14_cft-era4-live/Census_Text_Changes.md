- Old: The Royal Frankish Annals, the court’s own record, state it in a sentence: in 782, after the Saxons had destroyed a Frankish army in the Süntel hills, Charlemagne came to Verden on the Aller, demanded the surrender of the rebels, and had four thousand five hundred of them beheaded in one day. The Capitulary for the Saxon Regions, issued within a few years, made the terms of the conquest explicit: death for anyone who burned a church, killed a bishop or priest, cremated the dead in the pagan manner, or ‘scorns to come to baptism and wishes to remain a pagan’. The same document required tithes from a people who had just been conquered. Alcuin of York, who ran that reform, wrote to the king in 796, when the Avars had been defeated and the question of their conversion arose, and his letter is a criticism of what had been done to the Saxons without naming them. Faith, he wrote, ‘is a matter of will, not of compulsion’; a man may be drawn to it but not forced; adults must be taught before they are baptized, or the washing of the body does nothing for the soul; and above all the tithe must not be demanded of new converts — ‘it is better to lose the tithe than the faith’. Both texts are the reform’s: the one it enforced and the one it argued for.
- New: The Royal Frankish Annals, the court’s own record, state it in a sentence. ## Open-Gaps 256: `bagratid-armenian-christianity`, documentedStories.1.text

- Old: Gregory of Narek spent his life in the monastery his father had founded near Lake Van, and in the last years of it he wrote the Book of Lamentations — ninety-five prayers, each headed ‘Speaking with God from the Depths of the Heart’, in a Classical Armenian so dense that Armenians have read it for a thousand years without claiming to understand all of it. The prayers are accusations against himself: he compares his soul to every fallen city in Scripture, catalogs his sins in lists of forty and fifty images, and pleads for a mercy he insists he has no right to. He has written, he says, ‘for the whole assembly of the church, for every rank and condition’: for the wise and the simple, priests and laymen, men and women, the old and the young, the sinner and the righteous, ‘for all the peoples of the earth’. He asks that the book be treated as medicine — kept by the sick, read over them, laid on the body — and that anyone who prays it make it their own words rather than his. The Armenian church did what he asked. Manuscripts of the Narek were kept in houses, placed under pillows, read aloud at deathbeds and carried into battle; the book was said to heal, and copies were treated as relics.
- New: Gregory was raised from infancy in the convent of Narek, in the land of the Rushtunians, and in time became its abbot. So writes Michael Chamich, a monk of the Armenian order at Venice, in his History of Armenia. Chamich tells how the book came to be. Gregory long prayed to see, with his own eyes, the Virgin holding the Son of God, and at last was granted the sight. Then, ‘Filled with the Holy Ghost, he composed that wonderful book of prayers called Narek, the like of which has never till this time appeared in the world.’ Giovanni de Serpos, writing at Venice in 1786, counts ninety-five prayers in it and calls them a masterpiece in every line. In his day, he says, the book was still used in the church’s services. Two of Gregory’s prayers to the Holy Spirit stand in the Armenian Liturgy. In the first, the people assembled before God pray, ‘we implore Thee with out-stretched arms and deep groans.’ Malachia Ormanian, a former Armenian patriarch of Constantinople, wrote that the Narek was used for private prayer; his book was put into English in 1912. Its style, he says, is ‘florid and sublime’, and it ‘is regarded as a potent talisman against all kinds of dangers.’ The book still carries the name of the house that raised him, and his church used it in its services and in private prayer for centuries.
