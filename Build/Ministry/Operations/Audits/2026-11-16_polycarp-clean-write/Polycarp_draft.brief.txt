Polycarp was bishop of the church in Smyrna, a city in the Roman province of Asia. He belonged to the generation the apostles taught, for Irenaeus, who heard him as a boy, wrote that apostles had instructed him.

When persecution reached Smyrna, the crowd shouted, "Away with the atheists!" and demanded Polycarp. Because many wished it, he withdrew to a farm, and then to another. There searchers seized two young slaves of his household, and one gave him away under torture.

When the police came he could have escaped, but he said, "Let the will of God be done." He had food and drink set before them, asked for an hour of prayer, and prayed for two, until many regretted coming out against such a godly old man.

In the stadium the Roman governor pressed him to curse Christ and go free. Polycarp said, "For eighty-six years I have served him, and he has done me no wrong. How can I blaspheme my King, who saved me?" The crowd, calling him the destroyer of their gods, shouted for him to be burned.

The church in Smyrna says its own people saw the flames curve around him like a ship's sail, unable to consume his body, until an executioner killed him with a dagger. They took up his bones, laid them in a fitting place, and resolved to gather there each year and "keep the birthday of his martyrdom."

We still have a letter he wrote to the church at Philippi, calling its readers back from false teaching to "the word handed down to us from the beginning." Irenaeus recommended that letter, and held up Polycarp against Valentinus and Marcion as the steadier witness to the truth.

Polycarp had also visited Rome. He and its bishop, Anicetus, could not agree about keeping Easter, yet they shared communion and parted in peace. Some forty years later Victor, bishop of Rome, tried to cut off the churches of Asia over the date of Easter, and Irenaeus wrote to remind him of that peace.
