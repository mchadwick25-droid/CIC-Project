Polycarp was bishop of the church in Smyrna, a city in the Roman province of Asia. He belonged to the generation the apostles taught. Irenaeus heard him as a boy, and later wrote that apostles had instructed Polycarp and made him bishop.

When persecution reached Smyrna, the crowd shouted, "Away with the atheists!" Then they demanded Polycarp, the man they called the destroyer of their gods, who taught many not to sacrifice.

Because many wished it, he withdrew to a farm, and then to another. There searchers seized two young slaves of his household, and one gave him away under torture.

When the police came, he could have escaped. He said, "Let the will of God be done." He had food and drink set before them and asked for an hour to pray. He prayed for two, and many were sorry they had come for such a godly old man.

In the stadium the Roman governor pressed him to curse Christ and go free. Polycarp said, "For eighty-six years I have served him, and he has done me no wrong. How can I blaspheme my King, who saved me?" The crowd asked for a lion, but the beast shows were over, so they shouted for him to be burned.

The church in Smyrna says its own people saw the flames curve around him like a ship's sail. The fire could not consume his body, they say, so an executioner killed him with a dagger. Later they laid his bones in a fitting place, and resolved to gather there each year and "keep the birthday of his martyrdom."

A letter he wrote to the church at Philippi survives. It calls its readers back from false teaching to "the word handed down to us from the beginning." Irenaeus pointed to Polycarp as a steadier witness to the truth than the false teachers.

Polycarp had also visited Rome. He and its bishop, Anicetus, could not agree about keeping Easter, yet they shared communion and parted in peace. Some forty years later Victor, another bishop of Rome, tried to cut off the churches of Asia over the date of Easter. Irenaeus wrote to remind him of that peace.
