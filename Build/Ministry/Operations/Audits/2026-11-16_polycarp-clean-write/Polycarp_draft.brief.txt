Polycarp was bishop of the church in Smyrna, a city in the Roman province of Asia, in the years just after the apostles. Irenaeus, who heard him as a boy, wrote that apostles had instructed Polycarp, and he could still describe the place where Polycarp used to sit and tell of his time with John and the others who had seen the Lord.

Polycarp once visited Rome while Anicetus was bishop there. They could not persuade each other about the keeping of Easter, because each held to what he had received from those before him. Even so, they shared communion and parted in peace.

Then persecution reached Smyrna. The Roman governor held trials, Christians were thrown to wild beasts in the stadium, and the crowd shouted, "Away with the atheists!" and demanded Polycarp. Because many friends wished it, he withdrew to a farm near the city and prayed there night and day, until a young slave of his household betrayed him under torture.

When the police came for him on a Friday evening he could have escaped, but he said, "Let the will of God be done." He had food and drink set before them, asked for an hour of prayer, and prayed for two, until many regretted coming out against such a godly old man.

In the stadium the governor pressed him: "Swear, and I will set you free. Curse Christ." Polycarp said, "For eighty-six years I have served him, and he has done me no wrong. How can I blaspheme my King, who saved me?"

Threatened with beasts and then with fire, he would not give way, so the crowd built a pyre. The church in Smyrna, which recorded his death, says the flames curved around him like a ship's sail filled by the wind and could not consume his body, until an executioner killed him with a dagger.

The church took up his bones, laid them in a fitting place, and planned to gather there every year on the anniversary of his martyrdom. It sent its account to another church, asking that it be passed on to believers farther away.
