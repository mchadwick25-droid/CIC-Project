"For eighty-six years I have served him, and he has done me no wrong. How can I blaspheme my King, who saved me?"

An old man gave that answer in a stadium in Smyrna, a city in the Roman province of Asia, in the second century. The Roman governor had offered to free him if he would curse Christ. The old man was Polycarp, the bishop of the church there. Irenaeus, who heard him as a boy, wrote that Polycarp had known people who had seen Jesus.

In a time of persecution, the crowd had shouted, "Away with the atheists!" They demanded Polycarp, whom they called the destroyer of their gods.

Because many wished it, he withdrew to a farm. Searchers seized two young slaves of his household, and one gave him away under torture. When the police came, he could have escaped. He said, "Let the will of God be done." He had food and drink set before them and prayed for two hours.

After his answer, the crowd asked for a lion, but the beast shows were over, so they shouted for him to be burned. The church in Smyrna says its own people saw the flames curve around him like a ship's sail. An executioner killed him with a dagger.

The church laid his bones in a fitting place. It resolved to gather there each year and "keep the birthday of his martyrdom," to remember those who had died and to prepare those still to come.

Irenaeus carried his teaching on, and wrote that he kept it in his heart, not on paper. Polycarp had once visited Rome. He and its bishop, Anicetus, could not agree about the date of Easter, yet they parted in peace. Some forty years later Victor, another bishop of Rome, tried to cut off the churches of Asia over that date. Irenaeus wrote to remind him of that peace.

Polycarp's own letter to the church at Philippi survives. It calls its readers back from false teaching to "the word handed down to us from the beginning." Jerome, writing in the late fourth century, said it was still read when the churches of Asia met.
