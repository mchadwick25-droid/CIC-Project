# Polycarp of Smyrna: the two best versions

Both versions carry the minor changes from Mark's review of 16 November.
- The general sentence on households and letters is removed.
- Irenaeus hearing Polycarp is kept apart from what Polycarp told of John.
- The card notes that the scenes are a rendering of the Smyrna church's account.

## Version A: second cold write, approved as the benchmark

An old man stands in a stadium, before a Roman governor and a shouting crowd. The governor offers him his life: "Swear, and I will set you free. Curse Christ." The old man answers, "For eighty-six years I have served him, and he has done me no wrong. How can I blaspheme my King, who saved me?"

His name was Polycarp. In the second century he was bishop of the church in Smyrna, a city in the Roman province of Asia. Polycarp himself had known people who had seen Jesus. A man named Irenaeus heard him teach as a boy. Years later, Irenaeus remembered Polycarp telling of his time with John, the disciple of the Lord.

When Polycarp was a very old man, the governor put the Christians of Smyrna on trial. The crowd called them atheists, because they would not sacrifice to the gods, and shouted for Polycarp.

Many people wanted him to leave the city, so he went out to a farm, and there the police came for him on a Friday evening. He could have escaped, but he said, "Let the will of God be done." He had food and drink set before the men and asked for an hour to pray. He prayed for two hours, and many of them were sorry they had come out against "such a godly old man".

The governor threatened him with wild beasts, and then with fire. Polycarp answered, "You threaten fire that burns for an hour and soon goes out." So they lit the fire. The Christians of Smyrna, who say they saw it, wrote that the flames curved around him "like a ship's sail filled by the wind" and could not consume his body. In the end an executioner killed him with a dagger. The church sent its account out, and asked that it be passed on to Christians farther away.

Years later, Irenaeus called on Polycarp as a witness against the false teachers of his own day. "He had always taught what he had learned from the apostles," Irenaeus wrote, "what the Church hands down, and what alone is true."

Polycarp had once visited Rome, when Anicetus was bishop there, and the two men could not agree about the keeping of Easter. Polycarp kept it as he had with John and the other apostles, and Anicetus followed the elders before him. "Even so, they shared communion," Irenaeus wrote. "And they parted from each other in peace." A generation later, a bishop of Rome named Victor tried to cut off the churches of Asia over the Easter date. Irenaeus wrote to Victor and held up that peace as the reason not to do it.

In his one surviving letter, Polycarp warns against teachers who deny that Jesus Christ came in the flesh, or who say there is no resurrection. "Let us return to the word handed down to us from the beginning," he writes. More than two hundred years later, a writer named Jerome reported that this letter was still read in the church meetings of Asia.

## Version B: round 4

"For eighty-six years I have served him, and he has done me no wrong. How can I blaspheme my King, who saved me?"

An old man gave that answer in a stadium in Smyrna, a city in the Roman province of Asia, in the second century. The Roman governor had offered to free him if he would curse Christ. The old man was Polycarp, the bishop of the church there. Irenaeus, who heard him as a boy, wrote that Polycarp had known people who had seen Jesus.

In a time of persecution, the crowd had shouted, "Away with the atheists!" They demanded Polycarp, whom they called the destroyer of their gods.

Because many wished it, he withdrew to a farm, and then to another. There searchers seized two young slaves of his household, and one gave him away under torture. When the police came, he could have escaped. He said, "Let the will of God be done." He had food and drink set before them and asked for an hour to pray. He prayed for two hours, and many were sorry they had come for such a godly old man.

After his answer, the crowd asked for a lion, but the beast shows were over, so they shouted for him to be burned.

The church in Smyrna says its own people saw the flames curve around him like a ship's sail. The fire could not consume his body, they say, so an executioner killed him with a dagger. Later they laid his bones in a fitting place, and resolved to gather there each year and "keep the birthday of his martyrdom," to prepare those still to come.

In Rome, Polycarp and the bishop, Anicetus, had failed to agree about keeping Easter, yet they parted in peace. Some forty years later Victor, another bishop of Rome, tried to cut off the churches of Asia over it. Irenaeus wrote to remind him of that peace.

Polycarp's letter to the church at Philippi survives. It calls its readers back from false teaching to "the word handed down to us from the beginning." More than two hundred years later, Jerome wrote that it was still read when the churches of Asia met.
