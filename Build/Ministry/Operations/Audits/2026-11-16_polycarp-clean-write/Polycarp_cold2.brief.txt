An old man stands in a stadium, in front of a Roman governor and a shouting crowd. The governor offers him his life: "Swear, and I will set you free. Curse Christ." The old man answers, "For eighty-six years I have served him, and he has done me no wrong. How can I blaspheme my King, who saved me?"

His name was Polycarp. In the second century he was bishop of the church in Smyrna, a city in the Roman province of Asia. This was the church in the time after the apostles, and Polycarp had known people who had seen Jesus. A man named Irenaeus, who heard him teach as a boy, remembered how Polycarp spoke of his time with John, the disciple of the Lord.

Then the governor put the Christians of Smyrna on trial. The crowd called them atheists, because they would not sacrifice to the gods, and it shouted for Polycarp to be found.

Many people wanted him to leave, so he went out to the farms near the city. The police came for him there on a Friday evening. He could have escaped, but he said, "Let the will of God be done." He had food and drink set before the men and asked for an hour to pray. He prayed for two hours, and many of them were sorry they had come out against "such a godly old man".

In the stadium, the governor threatened him with wild beasts, and then with fire. Polycarp answered, "You threaten fire that burns for an hour and soon goes out." So he was burned. The Christians of Smyrna, who wrote down what they saw, say the flames curved around him "like a ship's sail filled by the wind" and could not consume his body. In the end an executioner killed him with a dagger. The church sent its account out, and asked that it be passed on to Christians farther away.

Years later, Irenaeus called on Polycarp as a witness against the false teachers of his own day. "He had always taught what he had learned from the apostles," Irenaeus wrote, "what the Church hands down, and what alone is true."

Irenaeus also used Polycarp's example to keep a peace. Polycarp had once visited Rome, when Anicetus was bishop there, and the two men could not agree about the keeping of Easter. "Even so, they shared communion," Irenaeus wrote. "And they parted from each other in peace." A generation later, a bishop of Rome named Victor tried to cut off the churches of Asia over the date of Easter. Irenaeus wrote to Victor and held up that peace as the reason not to do it.

One letter by Polycarp himself still survives. In it he warns against teachers who deny that Jesus Christ came in the flesh, or who say there is no resurrection. "Let us return to the word handed down to us from the beginning," he writes. More than two hundred years later, a writer named Jerome reported that this letter was still read in the church meetings of Asia.
