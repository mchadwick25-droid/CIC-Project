"Swear, and I will set you free. Curse Christ." A Roman governor said this to an old man in a crowded stadium, and the old man answered him. "For eighty-six years I have served him, and he has done me no wrong. How can I blaspheme my King, who saved me?"

The old man was Polycarp, bishop of the church in Smyrna, a city in the Roman province of Asia. It was the second century, in the church that came after the apostles. Polycarp had been taught by apostles and had known people who had seen Jesus, according to Irenaeus, who heard him teach as a boy.

Now that church was under attack. In Smyrna, some Christians were thrown to wild animals in the stadium, and then the crowd shouted for Polycarp. Because his friends asked him to leave, he went to a farm near the city.

The police found him on a Friday evening. He could have escaped, but he did not want to. "Let the will of God be done," he said. He had food and drink set before the men and asked for an hour to pray. Many of them were sorry they had come out against such a godly old man.

In the stadium, the governor threatened him with wild animals and then with fire. Polycarp replied, "You threaten fire that burns for an hour and soon goes out." So he was tied on a pile of wood, where he prayed, "I bless you, because you have counted me worthy of this day and this hour." The Christians of Smyrna say the flames curved around him like a ship's sail filled by the wind, and did not burn him up. An executioner killed him with a dagger.

The church took up his bones and planned to meet beside them each year, on the day they called the birthday of his martyrdom. They also wrote his story in a letter, and asked that it be sent on to churches farther away.

His witness reached further still. Polycarp had once visited Rome, where he and the bishop, Anicetus, could not agree about how to keep Easter. Even so, they shared communion and parted in peace. Years later another bishop of Rome, Victor, tried to cut off the churches of Asia over the date of Easter. Irenaeus wrote to Victor and told him not to cut off whole churches. He reminded him of Polycarp and Anicetus, who disagreed and still parted in peace.
