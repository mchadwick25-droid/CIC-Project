"For eighty-six years I have served him, and he has done me no wrong. How can I blaspheme my King, who saved me?"

An old man gave that answer in a stadium in Smyrna, a city in the Roman province of Asia, in the second century. The Roman governor had offered to free him if he would curse Christ. The old man was Polycarp, the bishop of the church there. Irenaeus, who heard him as a boy, wrote that Polycarp had known people who had seen Jesus.

In a time of persecution, the crowd had shouted, "Away with the atheists!" They demanded that Polycarp be found.

Because many wished it, he withdrew to a farm, and then to another. There searchers seized two young slaves of his household, and one gave him away under torture. When the police came, he could have escaped. He said, "Let the will of God be done." He had food and drink set before them and asked for an hour to pray. He prayed for two hours, and many were sorry they had come for such a godly old man.

The governor threatened him with wild beasts, and then with fire. Polycarp answered, "You threaten fire that burns for an hour and soon goes out." The crowd cried out against him as the destroyer of their gods. They asked for a lion, but the beast shows were over, so they shouted for him to be burned.

The church in Smyrna says its own people saw the flames curve around him like a ship's sail. The fire could not consume his body, they say, so an executioner killed him with a dagger. Later they laid his bones in a fitting place. They wrote that the Lord would let them gather there to "keep the birthday of his martyrdom," the day he died for Christ. They would do this "to remember those who have already competed, and to train and prepare those still to come."

Years before, Polycarp had visited Rome, where Anicetus was bishop. The two could not agree about keeping Easter. Polycarp kept it as he had with John and the other apostles he had known. Anicetus said he must follow the customs of the elders before him. "Even so, they shared communion," Irenaeus wrote, and the two parted in peace. Decades later Victor, another bishop of Rome, tried to cut off the churches of Asia over the Easter date. Irenaeus wrote to remind him that Polycarp and Anicetus had kept communion though they differed.

Polycarp's letter to the church at Philippi survives. It calls its readers back from false teaching to "the word handed down to us from the beginning." More than two hundred years later, Jerome wrote that it was still read when the churches of Asia met.
