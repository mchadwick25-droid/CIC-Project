# Church Family Tree briefs: the standard (draft for calibration)

Mark, 2026-11-16: process option A, calibration set 1 with a clean write first, Polycarp of Smyrna for the clean write; accessibility option 1 and the Craft line, applied before round 3; the six lens changes from writing for the ear, museum gallery text and global Christian biography, applied before round 3.

**Why we write these.** To bring the church's witness to everyone, not just scholars. These are two thousand years of people following Jesus in their own times and cultures, told honestly. That means not only the good, but we never go looking for the bad.

**The shape.** First the context: when, where and which tradition, set like the opening of a story, not given as a list. Then the recorded story, or stories. Then what came of it: their impact on the church, what they saw differently, what they defended. Tell only what the record supports, and only what this story needs. The sheet will always hold more. A council follows the same shape: the setting, what happened in the room, and what came of it.

**Craft.** It is a story told by someone who knows the record well, not a report about the record. Each sentence leads to the next. The rigor lives in the sheet and on the card; the story carries it without showing the machinery. Give the reason for an act, or for any word or custom that would puzzle a newcomer, in the same sentence. Take the reason from the sheet. A quote earns its place by showing the person, or by letting the listener trust what we say. Name a speaker or writer only when it changes how far the listener should trust the point: a wonder or legend, praise or blame, an enemy's account, a point resting on one witness, or a dispute. Modern scholars, editions and dating arguments belong on the card, not in the story. Tell the hard parts when they are a real part of the story. End when the story ends, with no moral, no summing up and no line picked because it sounds good.

**Rigor.** Every sentence comes from the source sheet. If something is not on the sheet, it is not in the story. The sheet draws on sources from the person's own time, or the nearest the Library holds, and marks a later source as later. A disputed point is told as disputed. Where the record stops, say so plainly. The references go below the story on the card: work, edition and the part used.

**Accessibility.** Write in the voice of a BBC or National Geographic documentary narrator, for a listener anywhere in the world, often in a second language. Stay at grade 10 or below, with reading ease 60 or above with names left out; aim near grade 8, and a lower score is judged by ear, not failed. One thought per sentence, with each subject close to its verb. No sentence past about 30 words. Keep the names the record uses, and say once who each person is. Use plain modern words: simplify the language, never the thought. Tell old English and other languages in our own modern English. A person runs about two minutes aloud and a council about three. Go shorter when the record is thin.

**The three hands.** The researcher builds the source sheet: the recorded moments, quotes checked word for word and rendered under the existing two-reading rule, the impact as the record gives it, what is missing, and where the sources disagree. No prose. The writer reads only this page and the sheet, then writes the story. The reviewer first takes it in once through, as a listener would, without going back, and marks where they got lost or drifted. Does it flow? Does it hold attention? Does it sound like a person speaking? Then the reviewer traces every sentence back to the sheet, and only then checks the reading score.

**Changing this page.** Every change replaces a line. If this outgrows one page, something has gone wrong.
