# Product fit: does each story work as part of the tree?

Reader: a product reader for the Church Family Tree. I read `about.html`, `Pieces.md`, and each piece's page in `cft-website/tree/` (read-only).

My question for each piece: does it place the person in their river and tradition? Does it make the links worth following? Does it suit the page around it? Does it leave the visitor wanting more of the tree?

Two facts about the pages shape what follows.

- **Person pages link out to one place only: their tradition.** (Arius also links to Nicaea.) The stories name dozens of people who have their own pages, such as Athanasius, Augustine, Bede, Aidan, Tertullian, Irenaeus, Tatian, Elishe, Lazar, Barsauma, Palladius, Gregory of Tours, Fortunatus, Ulfila, Origen, Jerome, Ignatius and Wesley. None of them is linked from the page that tells the story. Council pages are the opposite: they carry rich "Traditions It Touched" and "Who" lists.
- **Ten of the pieces sit on their pages as text, with no audio yet:** Antony, Benedict, Clement, Columba, Dhuoda, Hild, Macrina, Marcion, Martin and Radegund. The text matches the piece. The other thirteen with pages have the player.

## Piece by piece

**1. Justin Martyr.** A philosopher who found Christ, defended Christians to the emperor by name and deed, and died for it at Rome about 165.
**Mostly.** It places him well, and the Marcion, Tatian and Edessa thread is a real invitation onward. But it tells his death twice and his Sunday twice, so on one hearing the order feels shuffled.

**2. Vardan Mamikonian.** The Armenian general who pretended to obey Persia, then rose against it and fell at Avarayr in 451. His nephew later won the freedom to worship.
**Meets.** It is gripping, it puts Armenia's own memory at the center, and the ending with Vahan makes the river's next stretch feel earned.

**3. Moses the Black.** A slave and robber who became a great monk of Scete, mocked for his colour and humble under it.
**Meets.** It is vivid and unlike anything else in the batch, and it makes the desert tradition feel peopled.

**4. Alopen.** The man the Xi'an stone names as bringing the Church of the East's books to Tang China in 635.
**Meets.** Framing it through the stone, buried and then found, is the tree's job done well. It shows a river that went quiet and was found again.

**5. Council of Chalcedon.** The council of 451 that defined Christ as "in two natures", and the split with Egypt, Syria and Armenia that still stands.
**Mostly.** The page around it is the best in the batch for links. But the story carries too many names and terms for one hearing (Eutyches, Flavian, Dioscorus, legates, the Tome, Gangra).

**6. Third Council of Toledo.** In 589 the Gothic king and bishops of Spain gave up their church's faith for the Catholic faith of their subjects.
**Meets.** It is clear and fair to the side that lost. The closing "from the Father and the Son" hands the visitor a reason to go looking East and West.

**7. John Wyclif.** The Oxford priest behind the first English Bible, whose followers kept reading it in secret.
**Meets.** Durdant sending the boy outside is the kind of scene that makes a river human. Hus and Tyndale give real reasons to follow the thread. The "Earlier, in 1415" jump back is a small stumble.

**8. Thomas à Kempis.** The quiet canon credited with the Imitation of Christ, a book later read by a Jesuit, a Methodist and a Quaker.
**Meets.** "The book's call crossed the lines that later divided the church" is exactly the tree's idea, said as story.

**9. Narsai of Nisibis.** The poet-teacher who fled Edessa by night and founded the school at Nisibis.
**Mostly.** The opening and the closing "Edessa grew dark; Nisibis grew bright" are strong. But the flight is told twice, out of order, and "in 457 or after 471" is hard to take in by ear.

**10. Pachomius.** The Egyptian monk who first gathered hermits into one shared life under a written rule.
**Meets.** Antony's opening words tie it to the next page over. The Jerome-to-Benedict ending shows the river reaching the West.

**11. Arius.** The Alexandrian priest whose "the Son has a beginning" split the church, and whose name outlived his teaching.
**Meets.** It is fair to a condemned man. It carries the river to the Goths and Toledo, and the last line lands. (See Nicaea for a clash between the two.)

**12. Baudonivia.** A nun of Holy Cross who wrote Radegund's life for her sisters.
**Mostly.** It is Radegund's story more than hers, and it retells the relic episode that Radegund's own page also tells. The frame about "what he had passed over" just saves it.

**13. Antony the Great.** The village Christian who gave everything away, went into the desert, and whose Life later turned Augustine.
**Meets.** The chain from Antony to the Life, to the officials at Trier, to Augustine in the garden is the best "follow the link" moment in the batch.

**14. Benedict of Nursia.** The founder of Monte Cassino, whose short Rule Charlemagne later made the law of Frankish monasteries.
**Mostly.** The Rule is well placed, and the Charlemagne thread works. But his early life arrives after his legacy, so the hearing loses its line.

**15. Clement of Alexandria.** The Alexandrian teacher who held that philosophy was God's gift to the Greeks, and who taught Origen.
**Mostly.** The masks and the grapes are good images, and Origen and Jerome are good threads. But it stays an argument more than a life, so the Alexandrian tradition feels thin around him.

**16. Columba.** The Irish monk who founded Iona, from which Aidan went to the English.
**Meets.** It links Ireland, Iona, Lindisfarne and Whitby in one sweep. The death scene at the copying desk is memorable.

**17. Cyril and Methodius.** The Greek brothers who made letters for the Slavs, and whose disciples carried them to Bulgaria and the Rus.
**Mostly.** The story itself is one of the strongest. But it has no page: there is no `cyril-and-methodius.html`. It is marked "not yet on the tree", yet `cyrillo-methodian-slavic-christianity.html` exists, with its own story and no person list. **The story and the tree pull against each other here: a finished story with nowhere to live.**

**18. Dhuoda.** A Frankish noblewoman who wrote a handbook of faith for her son, sent away to a king's court during a civil war.
**Meets.** It is intimate and grounded in the Carolingian river. The 1923 catechism note opens a thread forward to Luther.

**19. Hild of Whitby.** The abbess of Whitby, who took in Caedmon and hosted the Easter synod of 664.
**Mostly.** It places her well among Iona, Rome and the English. But it says "five bishops" twice, tells Whitby twice, and leaves the Caedmon opening behind.

**20. Macrina the Younger.** The eldest sister of Basil and Gregory, who turned her household into a community, and whose deathbed teaching Gregory wrote down.
**Meets.** Ferrara in 1438 quoting her is a lovely surprise. It shows how far a quiet life can travel through the tree.

**21. Marcion of Pontus.** The second-century teacher who split the Creator from the Father of Jesus, and built churches of his own.
**Meets.** It is honest that only enemies speak. Justin, Tertullian, Irenaeus and Polycarp make a web the visitor wants to walk.

**22. Martin of Tours.** The soldier who would not fight, cut his cloak for a beggar, and pleaded for Priscillian's life.
**Mostly.** Every scene is good. But at 587 words it is the longest piece, and Worms comes twice, so on one hearing it feels like a string of episodes.

**23. Radegund.** A captive princess and queen who became a nun and founded Holy Cross at Poitiers.
**Meets.** The letter frame and the nuns' revolt after her death make it a story about what lasts. It does not point to Baudonivia, which it could.

**24. Council of Nicaea.** The council of 325 that answered Arius with "of one being", and whose rules travelled as law.
**Mostly.** It is clear on the stakes, and the Carthage ending is fresh. But it opens with the same Arius quote as Arius's page, and it reuses Jerome's "groaned" line from that page too. **A visitor who clicks from Arius to Nicaea hears the same opening twice.**

**Counts:** Meets 14 · Mostly 10 · Misses 0.

## Across all twenty-four

### 1. What works, as a pattern
The best pieces follow a life forward into other rivers. They don't stop at the death.

- **Antony the Great.** The Life travels to Trier and turns Augustine. The tree's links become a story you can hear.
- **Thomas à Kempis.** One book reaches a Jesuit, a Methodist and a Quaker. That is the tree's whole case in four sentences.
- **Alopen.** The buried stone and the forgotten church make the visitor feel the gap in a river, and want to see what comes next.

Vardan, Columba and Toledo are close behind. All of them open on a scene, not a summary, and they are honest about who is telling the story.

### 2. What doesn't work, as a pattern
The weaker pieces lose their line. They tell an event, then its legacy, then go back to the youth, and back again. Or they crowd in too many names for one hearing.

- **Chalcedon.** Too many names and terms for the ear, though it sits on the richest page.
- **Hild.** Repeats itself, and drops the Caedmon frame it opened with.
- **Baudonivia.** Her own page tells someone else's story, and repeats a sibling page.

Martin, Benedict, Narsai and Justin share the same order problem in milder form.

### 3. What my listener would want that the pieces don't give
- **A way to follow the people the story names.** The stories build real curiosity about Augustine, Aidan, Tatian, Ulfila and Hus. Then the page offers only the tradition link. Council pages show the right model.
- **Links between sibling stories.** Radegund and Baudonivia, Arius, Nicaea and Toledo, Justin and Marcion, Hild and Columba, and the three desert monks each half-tell one larger story. Nothing on the page says "this continues there."
- **A plain sense of where the river goes next,** near the end of each person piece. Most do this. Clement, Moses and Benedict could do it more.

### 4. One voice or a template? Human or generated?
It sounds like one collection with one voice: plain, concrete, source-aware ("as Lazar gives his words"). It does not read as generated. There is no filler and no assistant cadence.

There is a light template: a quote or scene first, then "almost all we know comes from...", then legacy. It only shows when the order breaks. The source-naming habit is the voice's strength. It is honest and specific, but in the densest pieces (Chalcedon, Nicaea) it adds names the ear can't hold.

### 5. Top three changes for future batches
1. **Write for the links the page actually has.** Before a story is approved, list the people and traditions it names, and check that the page links to each one, or add a "named in this story" list like the council pages have.
2. **Brief sibling pieces together.** When two pieces share an episode or a quote (Radegund and Baudonivia, Arius and Nicaea), give each a different opening, and let one hand off to the other.
3. **Keep one line through the life.** Tell it in an order the ear can follow, with no event told twice. Cap council pieces at a few named actors, and leave the rest to the page's "Who" list.

### Pieces whose story and page pull against each other
- **Cyril and Methodius:** a finished story with no person page. Its tradition page exists, though the piece says it is "not yet on the tree".
- **Council of Nicaea and Arius:** linked pages with the same opening quote.
- **Baudonivia:** her page's "Known for" is about her book, but her story is mostly Radegund's, and repeats Radegund's page.
- **Every person page, mildly:** the story names people with their own pages, and the page links to none of them.
