# Needs read: the churchgoer

Listener: a churchgoer who wants to understand their own church family's roots and how it relates to other Christians. I might be Catholic, Orthodox, evangelical or Coptic. I care that my forebears are treated fairly, and that the stories mean something for faith today, without being preached at.

Verdict counts: **13 Meets, 11 Mostly, 0 Misses.**

## Piece by piece

**Justin Martyr.** A philosopher who found Christ, defended Christians to the emperor, described Sunday worship and died for it in Rome. **Mostly.** The Sunday passage is a gift to any churchgoer, but the order jumps (his death comes before his conversion, and Sunday is mentioned twice), so one hearing leaves a muddle.

**Vardan Mamikonian.** An Armenian general who refused Persia's religion, led a rising and fell at Avarayr; his nephew later won freedom of worship. **Mostly.** It is honest that the princes pretended and that Armenians fought on both sides, but the many episodes (stonings, fire-temples, the nephew) crowd out why Armenians still keep him.

**Moses the Black.** A slave and robber turned desert monk, who faced scorn for his colour and answered with humility. **Meets.** Repentance, racism and grace in one short piece; a Coptic or Ethiopian listener would feel honoured, and anyone would take something home.

**Alopen.** The first recorded Christian missionary to China, from the Church of the East, remembered on a stone found in 1625. **Meets.** The buried-stone frame is gripping, and it gives the Church of the East a proud, warm place in the story.

**Council of Chalcedon.** In 451 bishops confessed Christ "in two natures", and the churches of Egypt, Syria and Armenia refused it and still stand apart. **Mostly.** It is strikingly fair to both sides, but it is dense to hear, and it stops before telling me whether these churches have since drawn closer.

**Third Council of Toledo.** In 589 the Gothic king and bishops of Spain left their Arian church for the Catholic faith, and added "and the Son" to the creed. **Mostly.** The link to the later East–West quarrel is exactly what I want, but the line on baptising children of Jewish marriages passes by flatly and unsettles without comment.

**John Wyclif.** An Oxford priest who said Scripture belongs to everyone in their own tongue; his followers copied English Bibles in secret. **Meets.** The old man sending the boy outside before reciting Paul is unforgettable, and an evangelical listener sees their roots, while a Catholic listener is not caricatured.

**Thomas à Kempis.** A quiet canon credited with the Imitation of Christ, a book later loved by a Jesuit, a Methodist and a Quaker. **Meets.** The best piece in the set for showing how the families are related: one book read across every later line of division.

**Narsai of Nisibis.** A teacher who fled Edessa with his books and founded a great school in Persia for the Church of the East. **Mostly.** Warm to the Church of the East, but "his enemies" are never named, so I can't tell what the quarrel was or how his church came to stand apart.

**Pachomius.** A conscript fed by Christian strangers who later gathered monks into one shared life under a rule. **Meets.** The kindness that converted him, and the blanket he sent back, make the meaning plain without a sermon.

**Arius.** An Alexandrian priest who taught the Son had a beginning; Nicaea answered him, yet his teaching lived on among the Goths. **Mostly.** It is fair to him, but it repeats much of the Nicaea piece word for word, and the abstract argument is hard to follow by ear.

**Baudonivia.** A nun of Poitiers who wrote her foundress Radegund's life for her sisters. **Mostly.** It is lovely, but it is almost all about Radegund, so I learn little about Baudonivia herself, and it overlaps the Radegund piece.

**Antony the Great.** A young Egyptian who heard one Gospel line, gave everything away and went into the desert; his Life later helped turn Augustine. **Meets.** Shared by every family, and the Augustine link shows how one life passed from East to West.

**Benedict of Nursia.** An Italian monk whose short Rule shaped Western monastic life for centuries. **Meets.** The Rule's own words ("the weak do not run away") speak to faith today, and the miracles are clearly Gregory's telling.

**Clement of Alexandria.** A teacher who said philosophy was a gift that led the Greeks to Christ. **Mostly.** The faith-and-reason theme is alive today, but it ends on a thin note about a bishop's letters, and I am left without a picture of the man.

**Columba.** An Irish monk who founded Iona and whose heirs carried faith to the Picts and the English. **Meets.** It handles Whitby fairly to the losing side, and the death scene, copying the Psalms, is moving.

**Cyril and Methodius.** Two brothers who made letters for the Slavs so they could worship in their own tongue. **Meets.** A Catholic and an Orthodox listener can both claim this; the pope's letter defending Slavonic is a fine bridge, though the opening order jumps.

**Dhuoda.** A Frankish noblewoman who wrote a handbook of faith for her son at court, in a time of civil war. **Meets.** A mother's voice across twelve centuries; the family's fate gives it weight without forcing a lesson.

**Hild of Whitby.** An abbess whose house trained bishops, sheltered Caedmon and hosted the Easter debate. **Mostly.** The Caedmon opening is wonderful, but Whitby's debate and the five bishops are each told twice.

**Macrina the Younger.** The eldest of Basil's family, who turned her household into a community and taught her brother on her deathbed. **Meets.** Tender and clear, and the Ferrara moment quietly shows Latins and Greeks still quoting her.

**Marcion of Pontus.** A teacher who split the God of the Old Testament from the Father of Jesus and founded his own churches. **Mostly.** It is fair that we know him only through opponents, but it ends on Polycarp's insult and never says why his question still matters to readers of the Bible.

**Martin of Tours.** A soldier who cut his cloak for a beggar, refused to fight and became a monk-bishop who pleaded against shedding heretics' blood. **Meets.** The cloak and the Priscillian episode carry the meaning on their own, though it runs long.

**Radegund.** A captive princess and queen who founded a convent at Poitiers and wrote a letter to protect it after her death. **Meets.** Strong, clear and dignified; her charge to the hesitant bishop is the best line in the batch.

**Council of Nicaea.** In 325 the bishops answered Arius with a creed calling the Son "of one being with the Father". **Mostly.** Balanced on the debate, but it never says the creed is still said in churches every Sunday, which is the one thing a churchgoer most needs to hear.

## Across all twenty-four

### 1. What works
The best pieces open on one concrete moment and let it carry the meaning: a cloak cut in two, a blanket sent back, robbers carried on a back. Faith today comes through by itself, with no preaching. Best three:
- **Thomas à Kempis:** one book crosses Catholic, Methodist and Quaker lines, so I see the families as related.
- **Moses the Black:** repentance and prejudice, told plainly; it means something on Monday morning.
- **Radegund:** a woman's authority and courage, in her own words, with a clear arc to the end.

### 2. What doesn't work
The weak pieces either repeat a sibling piece or wander through too many episodes. Weakest three:
- **Arius / Nicaea:** the same opening quote and the same Jerome line in both; heard back to back, it sounds copied.
- **Baudonivia:** she is the frame, but Radegund is the subject, and the Radegund piece already does it better.
- **Clement:** a good idea in the middle, then it trails off.

### 3. What this listener wants that isn't there
- **Where my church keeps this person today.** Moses the Black, Antony and Martin are honoured by Copts, Orthodox and Catholics alike; the pieces almost never say so.
- **The thread to the present.** Nicaea's creed is still said on Sundays; Chalcedon's divided churches have since talked again. A closing line like that would make the stories mine.
- **How the Church of the East came to stand apart.** Alopen and Narsai are warm, but nothing tells me why their church is separate.

### 4. One voice, or a template?
Mostly one voice: calm, documentary, honest about who tells each story. It sounds human, not generated. The shared habit of naming the source ("Bede says", "the minutes, kept by the winning side") reads as care, not filler. The template does show in two places: pieces that jump back in time ("Born to…", "Earlier, in 1415…") after the ending, and the near-identical Arius/Nicaea openings.

### 5. Top three changes
1. **End with today.** Close each piece with one plain line on who keeps this person, council or text now, and where it lives in worship.
2. **Tell it in order, once.** Keep one time line per piece and say each fact once; a listener cannot rewind.
3. **Give sibling pieces different openings and jobs.** Arius, Nicaea and Toledo, or Radegund and Baudonivia, should each light a different side of the story, not share quotes.

## Would each church family feel fairly and warmly treated?

- **Orthodox: yes.** Macrina, Antony, Cyril and Methodius, and Nicaea are told warmly. Toledo frames "and the Son" as a cause "or a pretext" of the split, which an Orthodox listener would find fair.
- **Catholic: yes.** Benedict, Kempis, Leo's Tome and Martin come through well. The Wyclif piece reports church bans plainly but does not sneer, and the Ferrara moment shows Latins quoting a Greek saint.
- **Protestant: fairly, but thinly.** Wyclif is warm, and Wesley and Woolman appear inside the Kempis piece. Otherwise an evangelical listener finds little of their own line in this batch. That suits its early era, but they would want later batches.
- **Oriental Orthodox: yes, notably.** Chalcedon gives the Coptic, Syriac and Armenian view in their own words, notes that the minutes were kept by the winners, and names Dioscorus's feast. Moses, Pachomius, Antony and Vardan honour this family too.
- **Church of the East: warmly, but incompletely.** Alopen and Narsai are proud stories. But no piece says how this church came to stand apart, so a member of it would feel admired more than understood.

## Do the pieces show how the families are related?

In places, yes. Kempis, Antony and Augustine, Macrina at Ferrara, Cyril and Methodius with the pope, Ormanian's "one communion" and Toledo's "and the Son" are real bridges. They show the families sharing saints, books and quarrels. But the bridges are left for the listener to find. Most pieces do not say which churches honour this person today, so the family resemblance stays hidden. One closing line per piece would fix that.
