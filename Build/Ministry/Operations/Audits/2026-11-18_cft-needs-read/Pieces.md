## Justin Martyr  (`justin-martyr`)

Told within: `greek-apologists-second-century` · 461 words

In the middle of the second century, Justin wrote to the emperor on behalf of people of every race who were unjustly hated, himself among them.

A philosopher from a Roman town in Samaria, he taught anyone who came to him in Rome. He was a Christian when the name itself was the charge. A name, he argued, is neither good nor bad, so judge Christians by what they do. The Greek logos means both "word" and "reason", and Justin called Christ that Word.

"We have been taught that Christ is the firstborn of God. We have shown already that he is the Word, in whom every race of people has had a share. And those who lived with the Word are Christians, even if they were counted godless."

He counted Socrates among them, and he set out openly what Christians did on Sunday. About the year 165, he was beheaded at Rome for this faith.

On Sunday, he wrote, Christians heard the memoirs of the apostles or the prophets, prayed, shared bread and wine, and gave for orphans, widows, prisoners and strangers.

His road to that faith ran through the philosophers' schools. Then, walking alone by the sea, he met an old man who told him that the prophets, older than any philosopher, had seen and spoken the truth. At once, he says, a fire was lit in his soul. He had found "the only philosophy that is sure and useful", and so he still called himself a philosopher.

He also wrote up his argument with a Jew, Trypho, that the prophets had spoken of Jesus, and told him that the sufferings of his people came to them justly. Yet the two parted as friends.

The account of his trial, by an author we do not know, has him stand with six companions before Rusticus, the prefect of Rome. Rusticus asked, "So you suppose that you will go up into the heavens, to receive some good rewards?" Justin answered, "I do not suppose it. I know it for certain, and I am fully sure of it." All who refused to sacrifice were scourged and beheaded.

Within a generation, Irenaeus was quoting his book against Marcion, defending the one God who made the world. Irenaeus and Hippolytus say that one of his hearers was Tatian, a man from Assyria. In his own book, Tatian praised him and carried on his attack on the gods and the philosophers. Irenaeus, hostile to Tatian, says that after Justin's death he left the Church. But Eusebius says Tatian wove the four Gospels into one book, and a later Syriac account of the church at Edessa says the people came to hear it read.

The church kept the name Tertullian gave him: Justin, philosopher and martyr.

## Vardan Mamikonian  (`vardan-mamikonian`)

Told within: `armenian-christianity-after-avarayr` · 443 words

In the year 450, the king of Persia commanded every people in his lands to worship the sun and keep the laws of the Magi. The princes of Christian Armenia, then ruled by Persia, were summoned to court, among them their general, Vardan Mamikonian. He offered the king his life and goods, but not his faith, as Lazar gives his words: "But I received this religion from God as a child. To abandon it, and change it out of fear of men, is not possible!"

Two Armenian writers tell his story: Elishe, who says he saw it, and Lazar, who wrote a generation later at the order of Vardan's nephew. Vardan led a rising against the order, and fell on the plain of Avarayr in the spring of 451. The Armenian church keeps him as its martyr, and about thirty years later his nephew won from Persia the freedom to keep that faith.

At court, the king threatened chains, elephants and the exile of their families, and the princes pretended to accept his religion. In Lazar, Vardan at first refused in anger to pretend, and gave way in tears only when the others begged him to spare countless people from exile and forced worship.

When Magi and troops tried to break open a church door, the priest Leont led the people in stoning them. The princes rose, stoned a prince who would not join them, burned fire-temples and killed Magi.

Before the battle, Vardan read his army the Maccabees, Jews who fought a Greek king for God's law and died, but were remembered. As Elishe gives his words, he told them: "For a time we deceived that unjust prince. We took him in like a weak child, as if we had outwardly carried out his wicked orders. But the Lord is the hidden witness of our thoughts. He knows we held firm and never parted from him."

Armenians fought on both sides. Vardan was killed, his army scattered to the strongholds, and the historians count 1,036 Armenian dead.

Within about a year, the Persian side ordered Christians left free to worship, perhaps, Elishe says, from fear of the princes in the fortresses. It did not hold: the patriarch and his priests, already captive, were killed in Persia.

His nephew Vahan grew up among the martyrs' sons, and like his forebears outwardly bore the Persian religion's name, which gave him no peace. When he rose, he first demanded that no Armenian be made a Magus, and that Christians worship freely. As Lazar gives the Persian king's words: "Today let all your demands be granted to you forever, confirmed by our writing and our seal."

## Moses the Black  (`moses-the-black`)

Told within: `desert-monasticism` · 353 words

Around the year 400, the desert fathers of Egypt counted Moses among their great ones. He was a priest at Scete, out in the desert, and he left seventy disciples. Yet he began as a household slave, whom Palladius, a bishop writing around 420, calls an Ethiopian by race, and black. His master drove him out for immorality and robbery, and he was even said to have murdered. Palladius tells of these crimes to show what his repentance could do.

People said Moses went on to lead a band of robbers by the Nile. In time, Palladius says only, one of his adventures left him conscience-stricken, and he gave himself to a monastery.

Four robbers once attacked him in his cell, not knowing who he was. Moses tied them up, carried them on his back to the church, and asked the brothers, "I am not allowed to hurt anyone. So what do you tell me to do with these?" When the robbers learned who had caught them, they confessed. "If a man so great and powerful in robbery has feared God," they said, "why should we put off our salvation?" They left the world because of his conversion.

His own fight was long, and by his own account his old lust nearly made him give up. For six years he stood all night in his cell, but still he could not master it. In the end Isidore, the priest of Scete, told him his dreams had ceased and sent him to Communion. Two months later, Moses said he suffered nothing more.

Sayings gathered in the 600s remember the fathers of Scete scorning him. "Why does this Ethiopian come and go among us?" they said. He kept silent, and afterwards said he had been afraid but had not said a word. Once a bishop, wishing to test him, had the clergy drive him out of the sanctuary. Outside, he said to himself, "They have treated you rightly, you whose skin is dark and black. You shall not go back in as if you were a man."

Palladius says he died at Scete, aged seventy-five.

## Alopen  (`alopen`)

Told within: `the-church-of-the-east-on-the-silk-road` · 370 words

In 1625, workmen digging outside Xi'an, in China, came upon a great stone with a cross carved at its top and strange writing beside the Chinese. It told of Christians who had taught in that city a thousand years before, with the favor of the Tang emperors.

The stone named the man who first brought the books of their church, the Church of the East. We know him as Alopen, and his is the earliest Christian mission to China that the record describes. In the year 635 he reached Chang-an, the Tang capital — today's Xi'an — coming from the far west. The emperor Taizong sent his chief minister to meet him outside the capital and bring him to the palace.

The books were translated in the imperial library, and the emperor studied the teaching and ordered it spread. In 638 his decree, quoted on the stone, said of this faith: "It helps every creature, and it does people good. Let it spread freely through the empire." Officials built a monastery in the capital for twenty-one priests, and a foreign faith now had the emperor's open license.

Under the next emperor, the stone says, its monasteries filled a hundred cities. In the years after, Buddhists spoke out against it and others mocked it, but its priests held it together. In 781 they set up the stone, about 146 years after he came, and told his story: his church looking back, in words that also praise the emperors. It pictures his journey: "Watching the blue clouds, he carried the true sacred books. Watching the way the winds blew, he faced hardships and dangers." That is the stone's way of saying the road was long and hard, and he kept the books with him.

After 781, Chinese history keeps only slight traces of the church. One is an edict of 845 that sent more than three thousand foreign teachers, Christians and others, back to ordinary life. The stone lay in the ground until 1625. Then a Chinese Christian official sent a copy to the Jesuit missionaries, and they circulated it through the empire as proof that Christianity had come to China early. So a church the world had forgotten was known again.

## Council of Chalcedon  (`council-of-chalcedon-451`)

Told within: `` · 570 words

Evagrius, a historian on the council's side, wrote that the quarrel turned on one letter: the Greek for "from two natures" against "in two natures". In the autumn of 451, at Chalcedon, just across the water from Constantinople, more than five hundred bishops called by the emperor Marcian answered. "One and the same Jesus Christ, Son, Lord, Only-begotten, is known in two natures: without confusion, without change, without division, without separation."

A nature is what Christ is, as God and as man, and a person is who he is. The bishops confessed one person, fully God and fully human, neither mixed nor split. "From two natures" meant one nature formed out of the two, and they refused to make it the rule. The Greek and Latin churches came to keep this as their fourth council, while the churches of Egypt and Syria, and later Armenia, refused it and still stand apart.

In 448 a synod under Flavian of Constantinople had condemned Eutyches, a monastery head, who said Christ had one nature after the union. A year later a council at Ephesus under Dioscorus of Alexandria restored Eutyches and deposed Flavian. Leo of Rome called it a council of robbers.

Leo did not come, but sent legates and his letter to Flavian, the Tome. It taught that each nature does what belongs to it, in communion with the other: the Word works miracles, while the flesh suffers injuries. As it was read, bishops cried that Leo and Cyril of Alexandria taught the same thing.

Dioscorus stood on trial. As the minutes of 449 were read, he broke in: "I accept 'from two'. 'Two' I do not accept. I am forced to be shameless: my soul is at stake." Leo's legates deposed him for receiving Eutyches, keeping Leo's letter from being read at Ephesus, excommunicating Leo, and refusing three summonses. The minutes, kept by the winning side, record his reply that guards held him back, and his request for the emperor's officials. He was exiled to Gangra.

Most bishops wanted to sign a draft that said "from two natures", but Leo's legates threatened to leave and hold a council in the West. The emperor's judges asked, Leo or Dioscorus? The bishops cried that they believed as Leo, and "two natures" went in. Any other creed was banned. The council also put monks under their bishops, and ranked Constantinople next after Rome, a canon Leo declared void.

Egypt refused at once. Priscus, a historian in Alexandria then, says a crowd burned soldiers alive in the temple of Serapis, and the emperor's troops abused the city's women. The Syriac chronicle says believers were killed at the altar. The Coptic Church's own History says Dioscorus died in exile with the crown of martyrdom, and its calendar of saints keeps his feast.

The Coptic, Syriac and Armenian churches condemned Eutyches too. They confessed Christ as one nature from two, the faith, they said, of Athanasius and Cyril, and to them "two natures" divided him. Their Syriac chronicle says: "That council tore into ten thousand pieces the whole robe of Christ, woven in one piece from the top."

Later emperors set the council aside before the Greek and Latin churches settled on it. Armenia refused it by synod in the sixth century. In 1912 Ormanian, a former Armenian patriarch, still described the Armenian, Syrian, Coptic and Ethiopian churches as one communion, apart from the Greek and Latin.

## Third Council of Toledo  (`third-council-of-toledo-589`)

Told within: `` · 423 words

The Goths' bishops had taught their people to pray, "Glory to the Father, through the Son, in the Holy Spirit." In May 589, at Toledo, the Visigothic kings' capital in Spain, those bishops cursed that prayer. They cursed anyone who would not say, "Glory and honor to the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit." With their king Reccared, his queen and their nobles, they were giving up their church's faith and signing the Catholic faith of their Roman subjects.

The Goths' church had its own bishops, sometimes in the same cities as the Catholic ones. Its opponents named it Arian, after Arius, a priest of Alexandria, and it taught that the Son was less than the Father, and the Spirit less than both. A Frankish bishop, Gregory of Tours, argued with the Gothic king's envoy and reports him: "The Son is always less than the Father, because he does the Father's will. And it is not shown that the Father does his." No Gothic writing survives; we hear that church only through its victors.

Reccared had become Catholic in his first year as king. At his call, sixty-two bishops gathered. They heard his book of faith read out: the Son equal to the Father and eternal with him, the Spirit proceeding "from the Father and the Son". The Gothic bishops, clergy and nobles condemned the faith "that came from Arius", and confessed: "No one doubts that we and those before us went astray in the Arian heresy."

Not every Goth turned. One Gothic bishop, Sunna, was already gone. Paul of Mérida, a hostile later witness, says he refused to turn Catholic and was put on a boat out of Spain. The old church's buildings went to the Catholic bishops, and its married clergy had to live apart from their wives. By the king's edict, a cleric who defied the council was excommunicated, a noble lost half his property, and a lesser man lost all and was exiled. Children of mixed Jewish and Christian marriages were to be baptized, and idolatry was to be punished short of death.

Every congregation was to say the creed before communion, to strengthen, the king said, "our people's new conversion". The chronicler John of Biclaro wrote that the heresy was "cut out by the roots".

The king's words "from the Father and the Son" outlived the Goths' church. Spain said them at Mass. A modern editor traces them through the West, where they became a cause, or a pretext, of "bitter reviling between East and West".

## John Wyclif  (`john-wyclif`)

Told within: `lollardy` · 460 words

In fourteenth-century England, friars said it was heresy to write God's law in English for lay people, everyone who was not clergy. An English tract that goes under John Wyclif's name answers them: "So Christ and his apostles taught the people in the tongue the people knew best. Why should people not do the same now?"

For him, God's law belonged to everyone, in their own tongue, and Scripture was the test of the church's wealth and power. His circle made the first whole Bible in English. A hostile Leicester chronicler of his own day says Wyclif put the Gospel into English himself, for lay people and women who could read. In the chronicler's words, "And so the Gospel's pearl is scattered and trampled by pigs." The Bible's editors of 1850 thought the New Testament was probably his own work, but a later scholar found no real proof, and thought his students made it at his wish.

He taught at Oxford, and from 1374 was priest of Lutterworth, in the English Midlands. He wanted the clergy as poor as the apostles and told the pope to leave worldly lordship to the secular power.

The church taught that at the priest's words the bread of the Mass changed in substance, leaving only its appearance. In 1381 Wyclif held that Christ's body truly is that bread, yet it is still bread. Oxford's ban on his teaching was read out while he sat in the master's chair, arguing the opposite.

In 1382 a London council condemned twenty-four of his conclusions as an earthquake shook the city. Banished from Oxford, he went on writing at Lutterworth until he died there after a stroke, at the end of 1384.

His followers, called Lollards in scorn, carried it on. About 1408 Archbishop Arundel forbade anyone to translate Scripture into English, or read a translation from Wyclif's time, without a bishop's leave. The Bible was copied by hand, not yet printed, and scarce, so a family might own a single gospel. In 1521 the bishop's records show John Grosar examined for an English gospel book, passed to him by a priest, which he lent on. At Iver, old Durdant sent a boy outside, so he could not hear and tell, before reciting passages of Paul and the Gospels to his family.

Long after, in 1526, William Tyndale took up the same work from the Greek and printed it, writing that he had no one's English to follow.

Earlier, in 1415, the Council of Constance declared Wyclif a heretic and ordered his bones dug up. Jan Hus of Prague had built his own book on the Church from Wyclif's. He told that council: "Yet in hope I would wish my soul to be where John Wyclif's soul is."

## Thomas à Kempis  (`thomas-a-kempis`)

Told within: `devotio-moderna-brethren-of-the-common-life` · 425 words

"Whoever follows me does not walk in darkness," says the Lord. So opens the Imitation of Christ, a fifteenth-century book that says these words call us to imitate Jesus's life and ways. It puts a changed heart above argument: "I would rather feel sorrow for sin than know how to define it." And it turns inward: "Learn to scorn outward things and to give yourself to inward things. And you will see the kingdom of God come within you."

The book is credited to Thomas à Kempis, a canon who spent almost all his life in one small house, Mount St Agnes, near Zwolle. There he copied books, among them the house's Bible, and wrote its Chronicle and the Lives of the New Devotion's founders. Johannes Busch, a canon of his own order and time, names him as the book's author. Early copies named John Gerson, a Paris theologian, and scholars still disagree.

The book calls the cross the "royal way", the only road to life and inner peace. That way is dying to self each day: denying oneself, and willingly bearing the pain of body or trouble of spirit that comes.

Born about 1380 in Kempen, near Cologne, Thomas went to school at Deventer. Florens Radewijns, who led the Brothers of the Common Life there, took him in, placed him in the school, gave him books and found him lodging. He lived among the Brothers, who shared all they had and spent their days copying books. In 1399 he came to Mount St Agnes and asked to stay, and there he became a canon, living in community under the Rule of St Augustine.

When a dispute over the bishop of Utrecht brought a pope's ban on church services, the canons kept it. In 1429, for keeping it, they were insulted and driven out, and the community spent three years in Frisia. Thomas died in 1471, in his ninety-second year.

The book's call crossed the lines that later divided the church. Ignatius of Loyola, founder of the Jesuits, knew it as "the little Gerson". He told a fellow Jesuit that after finding it he wanted no other book of devotion, and read one chapter a day. Two centuries later, reading it, the young John Wesley "began to see that true religion was seated in the heart". His Methodist Conference advised its preachers to read it, among other books, between four and five in the morning. The Quaker John Woolman, though told Thomas was a Roman Catholic, judged him "a man of a true Christian spirit".

## Narsai of Nisibis  (`narsai`)

Told within: `the-church-of-the-east-under-persia` · 410 words

In the 400s, on the night he left Edessa, the teacher Narsai asked some Persians to carry his books to Nisibis. They were, a writer of his own church tells a century later, all the treasure he had.

Edessa lay in the Roman Empire, while Nisibis lay just across the border in Persia. Both cities are in what is now southeastern Turkey. There the city's bishop, Barsauma, asked Narsai to found a school and promised him help, and the school counts both men as its founders. Students came from Persia, from Syria and from Edessa. They learned the Bible the way of Theodore of Mopsuestia, a Greek scholar whose commentaries had been put into Syriac. Narsai's enemies had condemned that teaching, but at Nisibis, in the Church of the East, the church under the Persian kings, it went on for more than a century.

Narsai taught in verse too. The school's own account says he wrote more than three hundred sermons in meter, of which forty-seven were printed in 1905. Of God's work in baptism, he wrote: "In baptism he recast our image, as in a furnace. In place of our clay, he has made us spiritual gold."

Born in Persian territory, he had studied at Edessa's school for Persian students and led it for twenty years. Then his enemies called him a heretic and said he sided with Persia. His biographer, Barhadbeshabba, says that when Narsai was warned he would be burned, he fled that night, in 457 or after 471.

In 496, while he was its head, the school at Nisibis took written rules. Yet in Persia he was suspected from the other side, for his biographer says students later denounced him to the king as an enemy of Persia.

He died in the early 500s, after some forty years or more at Nisibis. His kinsman Abraham, who had shared his cell, later led the school, and a century on it was still renewing the rules it kept in his name. About 550, a Latin writer in Constantinople met a Persian trained at Nisibis, where, he wrote, God's law was taught as a set course, step by step.

Barhadbeshabba, speaking to the school about ninety years after Narsai's death, put it this way: "Schools multiplied in the empire of the Persians. Edessa grew dark; Nisibis grew bright. The empire of the Romans filled with error, and that of the Persians with the knowledge of the fear of God."

## Pachomius  (`pachomius`)

Told within: `desert-monasticism` · 450 words

In the Greek Life of Pachomius, by a monk of the next generation, the great hermit Antony speaks to Pachomius's monks after his death. He tells them: "When I first began to live as a monk, there was no community anywhere in which anyone cared or feared for the salvation of others. Once the persecution was over, each of the early monks trained himself in the monk's life on his own. Later, with God's help, your father did this great good."

Before him, in Upper Egypt, each monk lived by his own way, a hermit in a cell, sometimes with a pupil. Pachomius drew such monks into one shared life under one written rule. His brothers lived in houses inside a walled monastery, each house under a head. They wore the same clothes, ate at one table and worked at their trades together. Nine monasteries came to form one fellowship.

Born to pagan parents, he was taken at about twenty for Constantine's war, early in the fourth century. Locked up, he asked who the strangers feeding the recruits were. His own communities' Coptic Life gives the answer: "They are Christian people. They treat us this way out of love for the God of heaven." That night he vowed to serve people all his life. Freed and baptized, he became a hermit's pupil, until at a deserted village called Tabennisi he began to build.

He would have no priests among his monks, and when a bishop wanted him ordained, he hid. At Esneh, monks and bishops questioned him over claims that he read hidden thoughts. As he finished, a man driven by an evil spirit burst in with a sword to kill him, but the Greek Life says the Lord saved him with the help of those present.

In 346 or 348, as plague swept the monasteries, he fell ill and asked for a lighter blanket. When he saw it was finer than the brothers' own, he sent it back: "Take this blanket away from here at once. It is not right that I should be better off in anything than the other brothers." After his death the fellowship split, though Theodore's letters urged the breakaways back.

Palladius, writing about seventy years later, counted some seven thousand monks under the rule. Latin-speaking monks there could not read its Egyptian or Greek, so priests sent Jerome the books, pressing him. About 404 he dictated a Latin version for them, and for his own brothers and Eustochium's nuns. Through that Latin the rule reached the West. In the early ninth century the abbot Benedict of Aniane set it under the chapters of Benedict's Rule, as one of the old rules it agreed with.

## Arius  (`arius`)

Told within: `homoian-arian-christianity` · 455 words

"We are persecuted because we said: the Son has a beginning, but God has no beginning." So wrote Arius, a priest of Alexandria in Egypt, in the early three-hundreds.

In his letters, God alone was without beginning. The Son, begotten by the Father before time as perfect God, was not eternal beside him. The question split the church: was the Son God in the same full sense as the Father, or a being the Father made before all else? In 325 the council of Nicaea answered: the Son is "begotten, not made", "of one substance with the Father". Yet his teaching lived on, in later forms and under a name its bishops refused, as the faith of emperors for a time and of the Goths for two centuries.

Early on, Arius reasoned that two beings without beginning would make two first principles, and a Son who was part of the Father would make the bodiless God divisible. Around 318 he clashed with his bishop, Alexander, whose council deposed him and his allies as "enemies of Christ". His words survive only because opponents copied them, Athanasius to mock his verse.

Nicaea's creed closed by cursing anyone who said "before he was begotten he was not", his own letter's phrase. The emperor Constantine exiled him and ordered his books burned, with death for anyone who hid one.

Constantine took him back after he gave a creed without the disputed phrases. In Constantinople, the bishop refused to admit him, praying that God would take either Arius or himself. Arius died suddenly the day before his reception, in 336 by the usual dating. Athanasius, who was not there, saw God's judgment in it.

Opponents called the bishops who accepted him Arians. At Antioch in 341 they replied: "We have not been followers of Arius. For how could we, who are bishops, follow a priest?" Athanasius answered that they disowned a hated name.

In 359 and 360, under the emperor Constantius, who favored them, councils dropped Nicaea's word "substance" as unscriptural and called the Son "like the Father". Jerome, an opponent, wrote that at Ariminum, the first, the world "groaned, and was astonished to find itself Arian". Ulfila, bishop of the Goths, accepted that creed, and under the emperor Valens gave them letters and the Scriptures in their tongue. His creed calls the Father "alone unbegotten", in Arius's words, and the Son our Lord and God, yet "subject and obedient in all things" to the Father.

In 589 the Gothic bishops of Spain entered the catholic church at Toledo. They cursed all who still held "the faith and communion that came from Arius", held, they said, until then. Churches that had refused his name gave up their faith under it.

## Baudonivia  (`baudonivia`)

Told within: `merovingian-gallic-christianity` · 348 words

"I chose you, daughters. You are my light, my life, my rest and all my happiness. You are the new planting." Radegund spoke those words to the nuns of the convent of the Holy Cross, at Poitiers, in Frankish Gaul. She had been a queen, the wife of King Chlothar, and after leaving him she built the house. A little after the year 600, one of her daughters, a nun named Baudonivia, wrote her life for her sisters, at the bidding of her abbess and the whole community. The poet Fortunatus had already written one, and she meant to tell what he had passed over.

She tells, from what the house had heard, how Radegund, still a queen, had a pagan shrine of the Franks burned. Franks came with swords and clubs to defend it, but she sat her horse unmoved until it burned. When the Frankish kingdoms moved against each other, Radegund sent each king the same letters, that they make peace and the homeland not perish. She set her nuns to pray for them.

She tells, too, how Radegund sought a piece of the wood of the Cross from the emperor, for the safety of the whole homeland. It was kept out of Poitiers, and Baudonivia names no one, blaming the enemy of mankind through his agents. Radegund fasted and wept, her nuns wept every day, and at last the king had the bishop of Tours bring it in. Radegund left it in the house, for the honor of the place and the saving of the people. "What she did in the land of the East," Baudonivia says of the empress Helena, who in the old story found the Cross, "blessed Radegund did in Gaul."

In most manuscripts her book follows Fortunatus's, so later readers met the queen as her daughters remembered her.

Radegund died in 587, with her nuns round the bed crying that their light was taken. "Her eyes were closed, and ours were darkened." Since none of them went out of the gate, they wept from the wall as her body passed below.

## Antony the Great  (`antony-the-great`)

Told within: `desert-monasticism` · 452 words

A young man named Antony, in a village in Egypt, walked into church thinking of the apostles who left everything to follow Jesus. Inside, he heard the Gospel tell a man to sell what he had and give to the poor, and he took it as spoken to him. He gave his family's fields to the villagers and sold the rest for the poor.

He went ever further into the Egyptian desert to live by prayer, work with his hands, and fasting. At last he walled himself into a ruined fort for nearly twenty years. When many wanted to live as he did, his friends forced the door. He persuaded many to take up that life and directed them as a father, until the desert became a city of monks.

One book carried that life far from Egypt. Most of what we know comes from it, a Life written soon after his death, which the church has long credited to Athanasius, bishop of Alexandria. It was written for monks in other lands, as a pattern for their own life, and it was soon put into Latin. Preaching later in the same century, Gregory of Nazianzus called it the laws of the monastic life, set out as a story.

The Life tells how crowds came to him for healing, and how he moved three days further into the desert, to a mountain. There he lived on into the time of the emperor Constantine and his sons, who wrote to him. The Arians taught that the Son of God was created. When they claimed he agreed with them, he came down to Alexandria and taught that the Son was not created.

A historian writing nearly a century later says his disciples flourished in Egypt, Libya, Palestine, Syria and Arabia. And in Augustine's own account, two officials of the emperor's court at Trier found the Life in a monks' cottage and read it. That very hour they resolved to leave the court and serve God. Pontitianus, a fellow official who was there that day, told the story to Augustine in Milan. Augustine never met Antony, but in a garden there, in tears, he remembered him. Then Augustine opened Paul's letters and read, and his doubt was gone. Later he wrote of it in a book addressed to God.
