A man in Rome begins his book by saying that the one who raised him sold him to a woman named Rhoda. Many years later he saw her bathing in the Tiber, drew her out, and thought how happy he would be with such a wife. Then, he writes, she appeared to him from the heavens to accuse him of that one desire.

The man was Hermas, a Christian at Rome in the second century, and all he tells of himself comes from his visions. In them the Church, and then an angel dressed as a shepherd, called the Christians of Rome to repent, to turn from sin and live rightly again, while there was still time. A Christian who sinned after baptism, the washing that made a person a Christian, would have one more chance, and only one. His book, the Shepherd, was read for centuries, quoted as Scripture by some and shut out by others.

He writes that an old woman, the Church, told him a day had been fixed. Those who repented with all their heart before it would be forgiven, but after it a believer who sinned would not be saved. Later he saw the Church as a tower being built, where stones set aside could still be used if their owners repented before it was finished. Rich believers were round stones that had to be cut square, and she turned this on Hermas himself: "When you were rich, you were of no use. But now you are useful, and fit for life."

Then an angel sent to teach repentance came to him dressed as a shepherd, and gave the book its name. Hermas asked him: "Sir, I have heard some teachers say this. There is no other repentance than the one when we went down into the water, and our past sins were forgiven." The angel agreed that the baptized ought to sin no more. But the Lord, knowing human weakness, had granted one repentance to those already called, and someone who kept sinning and repenting gained nothing: "with difficulty will he live."

Churches read it aloud. Irenaeus, bishop of Lyon, quoted it with the words "the Scripture declared", and a teacher in Alexandria carried on its second repentance. An old list of the church's books found it too recent for that: "So it ought indeed to be read. But it cannot be read out to the people in church."

Then its question became a fight. When a bishop forgave Christians who had committed adultery and shown their repentance, Tertullian, in Carthage, scorned the book as "the Shepherd of adulterers". By the late fourth century it was kept for people preparing to become Christians, and the Latin West had almost forgotten it.

Near the end of his book, Hermas says he makes God's great works known to everyone, and hopes that all who have sinned before may hear these words, repent, and receive life again.
