A man in Rome begins his book by saying that the one who raised him sold him to a woman named Rhoda. Years later he saw her bathing in the Tiber, drew her out, and thought how happy he would be with such a wife. Then, he writes, she appeared to him from the heavens to accuse him of that one desire.

The man was Hermas, a Christian at Rome a few generations after the apostles. All he tells of himself comes from his visions, in which the Church, and then an angel dressed as a shepherd, called the Christians of Rome to repent, to turn from sin and live rightly again, while there was still time. A Christian who sinned after baptism would have one more chance, and only one. His book, the Shepherd, was read for centuries, quoted as Scripture by some and shut out by others.

He writes that an old woman, the Church, told him a day had been fixed: sins confessed with a whole heart before it would be forgiven, but not after. In another vision he saw her as a tower built on the water, because their life was saved through baptism, the washing that made a person a Christian. Stones set aside could still be built in if their owners repented before the tower was finished. Round stones were the rich, who had to be cut square, and she turned this on Hermas himself: "When you were rich, you were of no use. But now you are useful, and fit for life."

Later an angel came to his house dressed as a shepherd, and the book takes its name from him. Hermas asked him: "Sir, I have heard some teachers say this. There is no other repentance than the one when we went down into the water, and our past sins were forgiven." The angel agreed that the baptized ought to sin no more, but said that the Lord, knowing human weakness, had granted one repentance, and that whoever kept sinning and repenting gained nothing: "with difficulty will he live."

Later that century Irenaeus, bishop of Lyon, quoted the book with the words "the Scripture declared". An old list of the church's books judged it too recent for that: "So it ought indeed to be read. But it cannot be read out to the people in church."

Then its question became a fight. When a bishop forgave Christians who had committed adultery and shown their repentance, Tertullian, in Carthage, scorned the book as "the Shepherd of adulterers". By the late fourth century it was kept for people preparing to become Christians, and in the Latin West it was almost forgotten.

Near the end of his book, Hermas says he makes God's great works known to everyone, and hopes that all who have sinned may hear these words, repent, and receive life again.
