# Outline: Hermas

Sheet items (S, E, I, L, D) are in `Source_Sheet.md`, which gives the file and line for each. HE is the *Shepherd* in Crombie's English (ANF 2); MUR-L the Muratorian fragment in Latin (Routh); TERT and TERT-L Tertullian in English and Latin.

- **The essence:** Hermas was a Christian at Rome in the second century. His book opens, in the text most editions follow, with the words that he was once sold to a woman in Rome (E1; Documented as that text's words; two other old copies, one Latin and one Greek, say instead that the man who raised him sold a young woman; that he was later freed is never said, D2). He wrote down visions in which the Church, and then an angel dressed as a shepherd, told the Christians of Rome to repent while there was still time. They offered a baptized Christian who sinned one more chance to repent, and only one (E4, E9, E10; Documented as the book's teaching; that the visions happened rests on his word alone, D3). His book, the Shepherd, was read for centuries: some quoted it as Scripture, others shut it out (I1, I3, I6, I7; Documented).
- **Also told:**
  - What the book says, through its images: the Church as an old woman who grows younger as people repent (E8), and the Church as a tower being built of stones. Stones set aside can still be used if their owners repent before the tower is finished; once it is finished there is no room (E6). The rich are round stones that must be cut square, and Hermas himself is told that his riches made him useless (E7; Documented).
  - The question it answers: some teachers said there was no repentance after baptism. The Shepherd agrees that the baptized should sin no more, but says God, knowing human weakness, has granted one more repentance, and that someone who keeps sinning and repenting gains nothing (E10; Documented). It describes no confession or formal penance (DUCH, E10; an editor's reading, Widely Accepted).
  - What it cost and changed: it was read aloud, quoted as Scripture by Irenaeus, and its teaching on a second repentance was carried on by a teacher in Alexandria (I1, I2; Documented). The question it opened became a fight. In Carthage Tertullian, answering a bishop who forgave adultery after repentance, scorned it as "the Shepherd of adulterers" (I6; Documented as his words). An early list of books said it should be read, but never read out in church (I3). By the late fourth century it was kept for newcomers to the faith, and in the Latin West almost forgotten (I7; Documented). It was copied at the end of the New Testament in one of the oldest Bibles (I4; Widely Accepted, editors).
- **Length:** about 2 minutes 45 seconds (about 410 words). Optional, to be cut first: the Church growing younger (beat 3, last sentence), the copies sent to Clement and Grapte (beat 2), and What came 5. Why: almost nothing is known of the man beyond what his visions say, so the life is short. The book's images carry the teaching, and the heart has three parts for the first minute: who he says he was, the call to repent and the one repentance, and the book's divided fate. The rest needs room for the tower and the riches, the Shepherd's answer, and a third for how the churches used and then set aside the book.
- **Setting:**
  - Time: the first half of the second century (S2). The Muratorian fragment puts the book "very recently", while his brother Pius was bishop of Rome; McGiffert gives Pius's years as 139–154. Others date it earlier, to the time of Clement of Rome, about 100. Plain words: "the second century", "a few generations after the apostles". No exact year.
  - Place: Rome, and the roads and farms outside it (S1). Plain words: "Rome", "the countryside outside Rome".
  - Tradition: the church at Rome, still meeting in households, with its elders (S3; the census tradition, "the Scattered Households"). Plain words: "the Christians of Rome". Do not say "the Roman Catholic Church" or "the papacy".

**Words to explain** (the first time each is heard; plain word or short gloss worked into the sentence)

- **repent / repentance**: 'to turn away from sin, sorry for it, and live rightly again' (HE 1975–1976). The forgiveness that follows is said separately. The book's whole subject; gloss it once, early.
- **baptism / baptized**: "the washing in water by which a person became a Christian". The book says "when we went down into the water" (Q1); say that once.
- **one repentance / second repentance**: "one more chance to repent after baptism, and only one". Not "a second baptism".
- **the Church** (as the old woman): "the whole Christian people, seen in his vision as a woman". Not a building.
- **angel of repentance**: "an angel sent to teach repentance", here "an angel dressed as a shepherd".
- **the Shepherd** (the book's title): "the book is named after the angel, who came dressed as a shepherd". The listener may hear "shepherd" as Christ; say plainly it is the angel.
- **visions, commandments, parables**: the book's three parts. Say "visions, commandments and parables"; do not use "Mandates" or "Similitudes".
- **elders** (the book's "presbyters"): "the elders who led the church at Rome". Never "presbyters".
- **the elect / the saints**: say "God's people" or "the Christians". The book uses both words for all believers.
- **Scripture**: plain. But "quoted it as Scripture" needs no gloss; "set it outside Scripture" does: "outside the books read as God's word".
- **apocryphal** (Tertullian's word): do not use it aloud. Say "a false book, outside the Scriptures" (TERT 8143–8144).
- **canon**: do not use. Say "the list of books read as Scripture".
- **bishop**: plain. "Pius, bishop of Rome" may stand; do not call him "pope".
- **Muratorian fragment**: say "an old list of the church's books, found in a library in Milan", or "a list of books whose writer calls Hermas's time 'our times'". Its date in the late second century is the editors' (D6). Do not say "Muratorian" aloud.
- **Codex Sinaiticus**: say "one of the oldest Bibles we have, copied in the fourth century". The name is not needed.
- **those who newly join us** (Athanasius): "people preparing to become Christians".
- **adultery / adulterers**: plain, but say what the quarrel was: whether a Christian who had committed adultery could be forgiven and received back.
- **Sibyl** (if beat 2 uses it): "a pagan prophetess". Optional.
- **done penance**: say 'had shown their repentance' (TERT 7186–7188)
- **council**: 'a meeting of the churches' leaders'

- **Shape:** Teacher or thinker. The question he tried to answer: can a baptized Christian who sinned be forgiven? What he taught: one more repentance, now, before the tower is finished. Who learned it: Irenaeus, the teacher at Alexandria, the Christians who read his book aloud, and Tertullian, who fought it. The trap is a list of books and readers; show the idea through the tower and the stones, and through the teachers' question in his own words (Q1).
- **Sibling:** none in the batch. Link: Tertullian (`tertullian-s-voice`), who rejected the book. The tree's Tertullian story tells his growing strictness, "against what he saw as a church going soft", and his turn to the New Prophecy; it does not mention Hermas. Here Tertullian's attack is told from the book's side, in one or two sentences, credited to him as a hostile witness. None of that story's quotes is used.
- **Openings:**
  1. **Chosen.** The book's first lines (E1). In about three sentences: a man begins his book by saying that the one who raised him sold him to a woman named Rhoda in Rome. Years later he saw her bathing in the Tiber, gave her his hand, and drew her out; he thought how happy he would be with such a wife. Then, in a vision, she appeared to him from the sky to accuse him of that one thought. This leads straight into the essence: the visions that followed were a call to repent.
  2. Not chosen: the teachers' question (Q1, E10). It is the clearest line of the book's teaching, but it comes in the middle of the book; it is kept for beat 4.

**Story beats** (the opening, the essence and beat 1 are the first minute, about 160 words; beats 2 to 4 deepen it)

1. **Accused.** He writes that the Spirit carried him away as he walked, and that the woman he had desired appeared from the heavens and said she had been taken up to accuse him before the Lord. His wrong was the desire in his heart (E2). Then an old woman in a splendid robe, holding a book, told him God was not angry with him for this, but because he had let his household go wrong (E2): he had not corrected his household, whose sins had brought him to ruin in his worldly affairs (E2).
2. **The book, and the deadline.** A year later the old woman gave him a book. He copied it letter by letter without understanding it, and it was snatched from his hands. After fifteen days of fasting its meaning came to him. The book's writing said his children had sinned and "betrayed their parents", his wife could not hold her tongue, and he had neglected them while he was taken up with his wicked dealings (E3; Documented as the vision's words; what the betrayal was is not said). And the sins of God's people up to that day would be forgiven if they repented with all their heart. But a day had been fixed, and after it a believer who sinned would not be saved (E4; Documented). A young man told him the old woman was the Church, old because she was created first of all (E5). He was to send one copy to Clement, who would send it abroad, and one to Grapte, who would teach the widows and orphans, and to read it at Rome with the elders who led the church (E5; Documented).
3. **The tower.** In a third vision he saw a great square tower being built on the water, of stones fitted so closely it looked like one stone. The tower is the Church, built on water because their life was saved through water, that is, baptism (E6). Some stones were set aside but left near the tower: these are people who have sinned and want to repent, and they can still be built in if they repent while the tower is building; once it is finished, there is no room (E6). Round white stones would not fit: believers whose riches made them deny the Lord when trouble came. As a round stone must be cut to be square, their riches must be cut down. Then the Church turned to Hermas (Quote 2; E7). Each time he saw her she was younger, because those who repent with their whole heart become young again (E8; optional).
4. **The Shepherd, and one repentance.** At home after prayer, as he sat on his couch, a man of glorious look came in, dressed like a shepherd in a white goatskin, with a bag on his shoulder and a staff. He was the angel of repentance, sent to live with Hermas for the rest of his life, and he gave him commandments to write down (E9). Hermas asks him a question (Quote 1; E10). The Shepherd says that is sound teaching: the baptized ought to sin no more. But the Lord, knowing human weakness and the devil's tricks, has in mercy set one repentance for those already called. Someone who sins again and again, and repents each time, gains nothing; "with difficulty will he live" (E10; Documented). The same rule in the case of adultery: a husband must take back a wife who repents, "but not frequently. For there is but one repentance to the servants of God" (E10).

**What came of him** (about a third of the story; heaviest first, but told in time order: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5)

1. **Read and quoted as Scripture.** Later in the same century Irenaeus, bishop of Lyon (census dates c. 177–190), quoted its first commandment with the words "the Scripture declared" (I1; EUS-McG 14755–14757; Documented). In Alexandria a teacher (Clement of Alexandria) carried on its teaching almost in its own words: after the first repentance at baptism, God in mercy grants a second repentance to those who fall, but endless sinning and repenting is no repentance at all (I2; Documented that the words follow the Shepherd's; he does not name it here). In Carthage some Christians sat down after prayer because Hermas had written that he sat on his couch (I5; Documented, Tertullian's *On Prayer*).
2. **Read, but not in church.** An old list of the church's books, from a writer who says Hermas wrote "very recently, in our times", while his brother Pius was bishop of Rome, gives its verdict (Quote 3; I3; L1; Documented as the list's words; its date is from its own words and Widely Accepted).
3. **Fought.** Early in the third century a bishop declared that he forgave adultery to those who had done penance. Tertullian, in Carthage, answered that he would give way if the Shepherd had deserved a place among the Scriptures, the one book, he said, that favoured adulterers. He called it the "Shepherd of adulterers", and claimed every council of the churches had judged it a false book (I6; Documented as his words; the councils are his claim alone). Give no motive beyond what he writes. Even his attack shows how widely it was read (Crombie, I6: an editor's point; use only as "his attack shows that others still read it").
4. **Set aside.** Early in the fourth century, Eusebius of Caesarea reported it disputed by some and counted among the rejected books, yet read in churches and used by the oldest writers (I7). In 367 Athanasius placed it outside the list of Scripture, among books the fathers had appointed to be read by people preparing to become Christians (I7; Documented). Jerome, late in the century, called it a useful book, read in some churches of Greece, but almost unknown among the Latins (I7; Documented).
5. **Copied in a Bible.** It was copied at the end of the New Testament in one of the oldest Bibles we have, from the fourth century: part of the book, the visions and some of the commandments (I4; EUS-McG 14826–14828, 67083–67085; Widely Accepted, editors Crombie and Duchesne). Optional.

**Quotes** (3)

Only Quotes 1–3 are quoted aloud, plus these short phrases: "the Shepherd of adulterers" (Tertullian), "but not frequently. For there is but one repentance to the servants of God" (HE 1929–1934), "betrayed their parents" (HE 889), "with difficulty will he live" (HE 2022) and "very recently, in our times" (MUR-E 60178–60179). Tell the rest in paraphrase. The Greek and Latin of the Shepherd are not held; Quotes 1 and 2 are rendered from Crombie's English.

1. Crombie's English (HE 1990–1994, Commandment 4.3): "I heard, sir, some teachers maintain that there is no other repentance than that which takes place, when we descended into the water and received remission of our former sins." Rendering (from the English): "Sir, I have heard some teachers say this. There is no other repentance than the one when we went down into the water, and our past sins were forgiven." Job: beat 4. The question the whole book answers, in his own voice; "went down into the water" carries what baptism was.
2. Crombie's English (HE 1297–1299, Vision 3.6): "When you were rich, you were useless; but now you are useful and fit for life." Rendering (from the English): "When you were rich, you were of no use. But now you are useful, and fit for life." Job: beat 3. The tower's lesson turned on Hermas himself: the man once sold, then rich, then stripped of his riches. The writer gives the sense alongside: a rich stone must be cut square to fit the tower.
3. Latin (MUR-L 22080–22083): "Et ideo legi eum quidem oportet, se publicare [sed publicari] vero in ecclesia populo, neque inter prophetas completum [completos] numero, neque inter apostolos, in finem temporum potest." (English, MUR-E 60180–60185: "And therefore it also ought to be read; but it cannot be made public in the Church to the people, nor placed among the prophets, as their number is complete, nor among the apostles to the end of time.") Rendering (from the Latin, with the editors' correction *sed publicari*): "So it ought indeed to be read. But it cannot be read out to the people in church. It cannot be read among the prophets, whose number is complete, nor among the apostles, to the end of time." Job: What came 2. The book's divided fate in one verdict: worth reading, never Scripture. The narrator gives the sense: it was too recent to stand with the prophets' books, which were closed, or with the apostles' writings.

**Names**

- Hermas: acts. Spelling "Hermas" (census).
- Rhoda: acts (opening and beat 1). Crombie prints "Rhode"; the Palatine Latin "Rada". Use "Rhoda", the English form of the same name (Acts 12:13, which Crombie cites). Not on the tree.
- Pius: link by role, "his brother Pius, bishop of Rome" (What came 2). Not on the tree.
- Irenaeus: link (on the tree as "Irenaeus of Lyon", spoken "Irenaeus").
- Tertullian: link (on the tree as "Tertullian").
- Eusebius of Caesarea, Athanasius, Jerome: link; name at most two of them, the rest by role ("a church historian", "the bishop of Alexandria", "a scholar in the Latin West"). Spellings: "Eusebius of Caesarea", "Athanasius", "Jerome".
- Clement of Alexandria: link; by role ("a teacher in Alexandria") unless the writer has room.
- Clement and Grapte (beat 2): optional, by role or by name only inside the vision ("a man named Clement, who would send it to other cities"). Do not call Clement "Clement of Rome" or "the pope" as fact (L3).

**Connection to carry into the story:** what passed from Hermas was a book and its one teaching: that a baptized Christian who sinned may repent once more and be received back (E10). The listener must understand three things. First, why it mattered: some teachers said there was no repentance after baptism, only the forgiveness given at baptism itself (Q1), so a Christian who fell had no way back; Hermas's book said there was one (E10, Q1). Second, that the churches took it up: they read it aloud, Irenaeus quoted it as Scripture, and a teacher in Alexandria carried on its second repentance almost word for word (I1, I2). Third, that it divided them: when a bishop forgave adultery after repentance, Tertullian made the book his target; an old list set it outside Scripture, and by the late fourth century it was a book for newcomers and almost forgotten in the West (I3, I6, I7). Tell it through these acts: the quoting, the reading aloud, Tertullian's attack, the lists. No "this matters because" sentence, and do not say that the church's later practice of penance came from him (Do not say).

**Ending:** close on his own words near the end of the book, the opening's man seen again. He tells the messenger who had handed him over to the Shepherd that he makes known to everyone the great works of God, and hopes that all who have sinned before may hear these words, repent and receive life again (HE 5196–5199). Or the book's last scene: the messenger who had handed him over to the Shepherd rises, takes the Shepherd with him, and says he will send the Shepherd back to Hermas's house (E11; HE 5270–5273). Paraphrase either; do not quote.

**Links**

- Tertullian: attacked the book as "the Shepherd of adulterers" (I6); earlier mocked those who copied Hermas's sitting after prayer (I5).
- Irenaeus: quoted it as Scripture (I1).
- Clement of Alexandria: quoted it as divine and carried on its second repentance (I1, I2).
- Origen: quoted it, and noted it was "despised by some" (I1).
- Eusebius of Caesarea: reported it disputed and counted it among the rejected books (I7).
- Athanasius: set it among books for those preparing to become Christians (I7).
- Jerome: found it almost unknown among the Latins (I7).
- Pius, bishop of Rome: his brother, by the Muratorian list and the Liberian Catalogue (L1). Not on the tree.

**Who tells it**

- **Hermas himself**, in the Shepherd, is the only witness to his life, and everything he tells of himself is inside his visions. Say so once, inside a sentence about the book, in or right after the essence: "all he tells of himself comes from his own book, and all of it from his visions; an old list adds that he was the brother of Pius, bishop of Rome.". Then credit the visions with "he writes" or "he says he saw": the woman in the sky, the tower, the angel.
- **An unknown writer** of an old list of books, who says the book was written "in our times" (I3). Credited where used, by role.
- **Irenaeus**, **Clement of Alexandria**, **Origen**: readers decades to a century later, who treated it as inspired (I1, I2).
- **Tertullian**, a hostile witness, early in the third century, writing against a bishop's forgiveness of adultery (I6). His claim about "every council" is his alone. Name him where his attack is told.
- **Eusebius**, **Athanasius**, **Jerome**: fourth-century writers who record its loss of place (I7; census dates c. 260–339, 328–373, 374–420).

**Do not say**

- Any word in "Words to explain" used without its plain sense the first time it is heard.
- Not "a freed slave" or "a former slave who won his freedom" as fact. The text says the one who raised him sold him to Rhoda; it never says he was freed. Say "he says he was sold", "the man who raised him sold him". If his later free life is mentioned, say "later he had a wife, children and a business of his own" (E1, E3, D2).
- Not "as a boy" or "as a child" sold. The text gives no age.
- Not "he was Rhoda's slave for years" or "he fell in love with his mistress". He says he met her again "many years after", loved her "as a sister", and had one thought about her (E1).
- Not "Rhoda had died" or "Rhoda in heaven". He says only that she appeared in the sky and said she had been "taken up" (E2).
- Not "God revealed to Hermas" as the narrator's fact. Every vision stays in his voice: "he writes that he saw".
- Not "the Shepherd was Christ" or "the Good Shepherd". The Shepherd is "the angel of repentance" (E9).
- Not "Hermas taught that sins after baptism could be forgiven as often as needed", and not "Hermas allowed no forgiveness after baptism". He allowed one repentance, now, for those already baptized, and said that repeated falling and repenting would not help (E10).
- Not 'those who fell again were damned' or 'could never be saved'. Keep 'with difficulty will he live' (E10).
- Not "one repentance for every Christian for all time". The book offers it to those "called before these days", before a day that has been fixed; later converts have the forgiveness of baptism (E4, E10).
- Not "the great and holy calling" as his sure words. That phrase is in one manuscript only (E10).
- Not "Hermas invented the church's system of penance", "confession" or "absolution". He describes no formal penance (E10, Duchesne).
- Not "the church at Rome adopted his teaching" or "the bishop of Rome approved the book". No held source says so.
- Not 'Irenaeus counted it among the books of the New Testament'. Say he quoted it with the words 'the Scripture declared' (I1).
- Not "Tertullian always rejected the book", and not "he changed his mind" as fact. In *On Prayer* he mocks Christians who copied Hermas's sitting after prayer, without attacking the book; his attack on it as "the Shepherd of adulterers" is in *On Modesty*. Whether his view changed is disputed among editors (I5, I6).
- Not "every council of the churches rejected it" as fact. That is Tertullian's claim (I6).
- Not "the bishop of Rome's edict" or "Pope Callistus" or "Pope Zephyrinus" for the bishop Tertullian attacked. Which bishop it was is disputed (corpus-map row, I6). Say "a bishop".
- Not "Tertullian had become a Montanist", or any motive for his attack beyond his own words against the bishop's forgiveness. The tree's Tertullian story tells the New Prophecy; do not explain it here.
- Not "the Muratorian Canon rejected it" or "banned it". It said it "ought to be read", but not read out in church as Scripture (I3).
- Not "Eusebius rejected it" without the rest: he also says it was read in churches and used by the oldest writers (I7).
- Not "Athanasius excluded it". He set it outside the list of Scripture but among books appointed to be read by those preparing to become Christians (I7).
- Not "it was in the Bible" or "part of the New Testament". It was copied after the New Testament in one Bible manuscript (I4).
- Not 'the whole Shepherd was copied in the Bible'.
- Not "the Hermas whom Paul greets in Romans". That is Origen's guess (L2).
- Not "Pius was pope" or "his brother the pope". Say "his brother Pius, bishop of Rome"; the brotherhood is the old list's word, repeated in a catalogue of 354 (L1).
- Not "written in 140" or "about 150" as fact. Say "in the second century", "while his brother was bishop, by an old list's account" (S2).
- Not "the book was written against Montanism". That is Coxe's thesis (D4).
- Not "his children denied Christ in persecution". The vision says only that they "betrayed their parents" (E3).
- Not 'read in churches for three centuries'. Say 'read for centuries'; some churches read it aloud and others would not (I3, I7).
- Not 'one copy differs'. If the doubt is told, say 'other old copies say it was the young woman who was sold' (HE 649–652, 602–605).

**Leave out**

- The textual history: Simonides, the Athos leaves, the Palatine and Vatican versions, the Ethiopic (EUS-McG 14820–14843). For the card.
- Coxe's theories: the anti-Montanist purpose, the grandson of the Pauline Hermas, the "prose idyl" (D4, L2). An editor's views.
- Vision 4's beast, the seven women around the tower, the willow sticks of Similitude 8, the twelve mountains and the night with the virgins of Similitude 9 (E8, ROB). Real parts of the book, but side visions that would crowd it.
- Maximus, "deny again", and the book of Eldad and Modat (HE 974–989): an obscure passage.
- The *Liber Pontificalis* story of the angel ordering Easter on a Sunday (D7): late legend.
- Robinson's case that Perpetua's visions borrow from Hermas (L6): an editor's argument; Perpetua is not on the tree.
- The stages of composition (Harnack, Duchesne, S2): for the card.
- Origen's lost *Commentary on Romans* and its "divinely inspired" (Library sweep): not held.
