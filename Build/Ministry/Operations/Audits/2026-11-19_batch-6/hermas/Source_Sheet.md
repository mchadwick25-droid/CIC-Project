The job: a Christian of Rome who says he was once sold as a slave; his visions in the Shepherd, the call to repent, one repentance after baptism, and a book some read as Scripture and others set aside. ## 1. Setting

S1. **Rome, and the countryside near it.** "He who had brought me up, sold me to one Rhode in Rome." (HE 648–649). He walks "on my road to the villages" (HE 658–659), goes "to the country" (HE 857), and walks "along the Campanian road" to "a country house" (HE 1568–1569). Crombie's variant note: the Palatine Latin reads "After I had come to the city of Ostia" (HE 663–664), and for Vision 2 the Vatican Latin "From Cumæ" (HE 857–858). OWN. Documented that the book sets him at Rome; the places of the visions vary by manuscript. Plain words: "Rome", "the country outside Rome". "Critics are unanimously agreed that the book was written in Rome" (EUS-McG 14810): editor.

S2. **Date.** The Muratorian fragment: "The Pastor, moreover, did Hermas write very recently in our times in the city of Rome, while his brother bishop Pius sat in the chair of the Church of Rome." (MUR-E 60178–60180). McGiffert gives Pius's years as "139–154 a.d." and says this testimony "is accepted by the majority of scholars, most of whom date the book near the middle of the second century" (EUS-McG 14777–14782). But in Vision 2 Hermas is told to send a copy "to Clemens" (HE 1010–1011), and "From this it is concluded by many that the author must have been contemporary with the well-known Roman Clement" (EUS-McG 14799–14802); "Zahn dates it between 97 and 100" (EUS-McG 14807). Duchesne (following Harnack) holds that the book grew in stages, the oldest part under Trajan, "terminé ... pendant que le frère de l'auteur, l'évêque Pie, siégeait sur la chaire de Rome, c'est-à-dire vers l'an 140" (DUCH 8184–8189, 8198–8204). Crombie: "the reign of Hadrian, or of Antoninus Pius" (HE-Crombie 558–559). The census: "c. 100-160" (CEN). Plain words: "the second century", "the first half of the second century". Exact date: Contested.

S3. **Tradition.** The census places him within `post-apostolic-house-church`, spoken as "the Scattered Households" (CEN, `informalName`; dates 70–200, region "Antioch, Asia Minor, Rome"). Plain words: "the church at Rome, a few generations after the apostles". The book names leaders only loosely: "the presbyters who preside over the Church" (HE 1025–1026); "you who preside over the Church and love the first seats" (HE 1431–1432). Coxe: "Hermas seems, purposely, colourless as to technical distinctions in the clergy" (HE-Coxe 1440–1441).

S4. **The book's form.** Three parts: Visions, Commandments, Similitudes (the div titles, HE 629, 1732, 2822). The first four visions come from a woman who is the Church (HE 998–999); the rest from "the shepherd, even the angel of repentance" (HE 1724–1725). Plain words: "visions, commandments and parables".

## 2. Events (the book's own account, in its order)

E1. **Sold, and Rhoda.** "He who had brought me up, sold me to one Rhode in Rome. Many years after this I recognised her, and I began to love her as a sister. Some time after, I saw her bathe in the river Tiber; and I gave her my hand, and drew her out of the river. The sight of her beauty made me think with myself, 'I should be a happy man if I could but get a wife as handsome and good as she is.' This was the only thought that passed through me: this and nothing more." (HE 648–658, Crombie's variant note removed). **Variant**: Crombie notes, "The commencement varies. In the Vatican: 'He who had brought me up, sold a certain young woman at Rome. Many years after this I saw her and recognized her.' So Lips.; Pal. has the name of the woman, Rada." (HE 649–652). So in the text Crombie translates (Hilgenfeld's Greek, HE 591–592), Hermas himself was sold to Rhoda; in the Vatican Latin, and in the Leipzig manuscript ("So Lips."), which is Greek, from Simonides' Athos text (HE 602–605; EUS-McG 14822–14826), the one sold is the young woman. OWN. Documented as the text's words; the reading is not certain (Contested between manuscripts). **"Freed slave"**: the text says he was sold; it never says he was freed. That he was later free is an inference from his household, his business and his wealth (E3, E5). Duchesne, an editor: "Hermas était un chrétien de Rome, affranchi de condition, propriétaire rural, marié et père d'une famille qui ne lui donnait guère de satisfaction" (DUCH 8191–8193, 8211). Inferential-Thin as fact; the editor's reading.

E2. **The first vision: accused.** He falls asleep on a walk, "the Spirit carried me away" (HE 672), he prays and confesses his sins, "the heavens were opened, and I see the woman whom I had desired saluting me from the sky, and saying, 'Hail, Hermas!'" (HE 680–682). "I have been taken up here to accuse you of your sins before the Lord." (HE 684–685). He protests; she answers, "The desire of wickedness arose within your heart." (HE 698–701). Then "an old woman, arrayed in a splendid robe, and with a book in her hand" (HE 746–747) tells him: "God is not angry with you on account of this, but that you may convert your house, which have committed iniquity against the Lord, and against you, their parents." (HE 769–773). OWN. Documented as his account of a vision.

E3. **His household and his money.** "you have been destroyed by the affairs of this world" (HE 779–780). Vision 2: "Your seed, O Hermas, has sinned against God, and they have blasphemed against the Lord, and in their great wickedness they have betrayed their parents." (HE 886–889). His wife "does not restrain her tongue" (HE 897–900). "And you, Hermas, have endured great personal tribulations on account of the transgressions of your house, because you did not attend to them, but were careless and engaged in your wicked transactions." (HE 956–963). OWN. Documented as the vision's words.

E4. **The second vision: the book to copy, and the deadline.** The old woman gives him a book; "I transcribed the whole of it letter by letter; but the syllables of it I did not catch." (HE 872–878); it is "snatched from my hands" (HE 879–880). After fifteen days of fasting the meaning comes: the sins of his family will be forgiven, "and forgiveness will be granted to all the saints who have sinned even to the present day, if they repent with all their heart" (HE 905–908). "For the Lord has sworn by His glory, in regard to His elect, that if any one of them sin after a certain day which has been fixed, he shall not be saved. For the repentance of the righteous has limits. ... Filled up are the days of repentance to all the saints; but to the heathen, repentance will be possible even to the last day." (HE 909–915, note removed). OWN. Documented.

E5. **Who the woman is; the book sent out.** "Who do you think that old woman is from whom you received the book?" And I said, "The Sibyl." ... "It is the Church." ... "Why then is she an old woman?" "Because," said he, "she was created first of all." (HE 995–1004). She tells him: "You will write therefore two books, and you will send the one to Clemens and the other to Grapte. And Clemens will send his to foreign countries, for permission has been granted to him to do so. ... And Grapte will admonish the widows and the orphans. But you will read the words in this city, along with the presbyters who preside over the Church." (HE 1010–1026, Coxe's note removed). Crombie: "Grapte is supposed to have been a deaconess" (HE 1012): editor. OWN. Documented.

E6. **The third vision: the tower.** "a great tower, built upon the waters, of splendid square stones" (HE 1111–1112). Stones fit so closely that "the building of the tower looked as if it were made out of one stone" (HE 1125–1126). "The tower which you see building is myself, the Church" (HE 1160–1161). It is built on water "because your life has been, and will be, saved through water" (HE 1181–1182). The stones rejected but left near the tower: "These are they who have sinned, and wish to repent. On this account they have not been thrown far from the tower, because they will yet be useful in the building, if they repent. ... if they now repent while the tower is building. For if the building be finished, there will not be more room for any one" (HE 1243–1249). OWN. Documented.

E7. **The rich, and Hermas himself.** The white round stones "are those who have faith indeed, but they have also the riches of this world. When, therefore, tribulation comes, on account of their riches and business they deny the Lord." (HE 1280–1283). "For as a round stone cannot become square unless portions be cut off and cast away, so also those who are rich in this world cannot be useful to the Lord unless their riches be cut down. Learn this first from your own case. When you were rich, you were useless; but now you are useful and fit for life." (HE 1294–1299). OWN. Documented that the vision says he was once rich and is so no longer; how he lost it is not told.

E8. **The Church grows younger.** First "an exceedingly old woman, sitting in a chair"; then "her face was youthful, but her skin and hair betokened age"; then "entirely youthful and exquisitely beautiful, except only that she had the hair of an old woman" (HE 1463–1469). The meaning: "Those, therefore, who repent completely and with the whole heart, will become young and firmly established." (HE 1544–1545). OWN. Documented.

E9. **The Shepherd.** "there entered a man of glorious aspect, dressed like a shepherd, with a white goat's skin, a wallet on his shoulders, and a rod in his hand" (HE 1694–1698). "I have been sent by a most venerable angel to dwell with you the remaining days of your life." (HE 1699–1701). "All these words did the shepherd, even the angel of repentance, command me to write." (HE 1724–1725). Sim. 10: "To him alone throughout the whole world is the power of repentance assigned." (HE 5179–5180). OWN. Documented.

E10. **One repentance (Commandment 4).** On a wife's adultery: if she repents, the husband must take her back, "But not frequently. For there is but one repentance to the servants of God." (HE 1929–1934, note removed). Then: "I heard, sir, some teachers maintain that there is no other repentance than that which takes place, when we descended into the water and received remission of our former sins." (HE 1990–1994, note removed). The answer: "That was sound doctrine which you heard; for that is really the case. For he who has received remission of his sins ought not to sin any more, but to live in purity." (HE 1994–1997). But "to those who have been called before these days, the Lord has set repentance ... The Lord, therefore, being merciful, has had mercy on the work of His hand, and has set repentance for them; and He has entrusted to me power over this repentance." (HE 2002–2012). "And therefore I say to you, that if any one is tempted by the devil, and sins after that great and holy calling in which the Lord has called His people to everlasting life, he has opportunity to repent but once. But if he should sin frequently after this, and then repent, to such a man his repentance will be of no avail; for with difficulty will he live." (HE 2012–2022). **Text notes**: "after that great and holy calling ... everlasting life" "occur only in Pal." (HE-Crombie 2015–2016); Coxe asks "Can the following words be genuine? They reflect the very Montanism here so strictly opposed." (HE-Coxe 2016–2017). The once-only rule stands without the doubted words at HE 1933–1934. Also: the offer is for those "called before these days"; "those who have now believed, and those who are to believe, have not repentance for their sins; but they have remission of their previous sins" (HE 1999–2002). OWN. Documented. Duchesne, editor: Hermas is "pas très catégorique contre les récidifs", and "ne mentionne aucune des formes extérieures de la pénitence ... Il ne parle ni de confession, ni d'absolution" (DUCH 8406–8416).

E11. **The book's last scene.** "After he had spoken with me he rose up from the couch, and taking the Shepherd and the virgins, he departed. But he said to me that he would send back the Shepherd and the virgins to my dwelling." (HE 5270–5273). Before it, the last warning: "lest, while ye delay to do them, the building of the tower be finished, and you be rejected from the edifice" (HE 5264–5266). OWN. Documented.

## 3. Impact, heaviest first (what later witnesses carried on and used)

I1. **Read as Scripture, quoted as Scripture.** Irenaeus: "Truly, then, the Scripture declared, which says, 'First of all believe that there is one God, who has established all things, and completed them...'" (IREN 46864–46869), the first words of Commandment 1 (HE 1746–1747). Eusebius on Irenaeus: "he not only knows, but also receives, The Shepherd" (EUS 28248–28249). Clement of Alexandria: "Divinely, therefore, the power which spoke to Hermas by revelation said..." (CLEM 31437–31440). Origen quotes "that little treatise called The Pastor or Angel of Repentance, composed by Hermas" for its doctrine (ORIG 23580–23584), and cites "the little book of The Shepherd, which seems to be despised by some" (ORIG 32407). Athanasius cites "the most edifying book of the Shepherd" (ATH 16158). LATER. Documented.

I2. **Its teaching on repentance carried on.** Clement of Alexandria, in a chapter titled "On First and Second Repentance" (CLEM 33172–33173), retells the Shepherd's argument closely, without naming it: "He, then, who has received the forgiveness of sins ought to sin no more. ... And the Lord, knowing the heart, and foreknowing the future, foresaw both the fickleness of man and the craft and subtlety of the devil ... Accordingly, being very merciful, He has vouch-safed, in the case of those who, though in faith, fall into any transgression, a second repentance" (CLEM 33175–33188). Compare HE 1994–2011. LATER. Documented that Clement's words follow the Shepherd's; that he draws on it is my comparison of the two texts (Widely Accepted; the parallel is plain).

I3. **Its verdict: read, but not in church.** Muratorian fragment, Latin: "Pastorem vero nuperrime temporibus nostris in urbe Roma Herma (for. Hermas) conscripsit, sedente cathedra (in cathedra, Freindall.) urbis Romae ecclesiae Pio episcopo fratre ejus. Et ideo legi eum quidem oportet, se publicare (for. sed publicari) vero in ecclesia populo, neque inter prophetas completum (for. completos) numero, neque inter apostolos, in finem temporum potest." (MUR-L 22077–22083; OCR ligatures normalised). English: "And therefore it also ought to be read; but it cannot be made public in the Church to the people, nor placed among the prophets, as their number is complete, nor among the apostles to the end of time." (MUR-E 60180–60185). NEAR-CONTEMP. Documented. "sed publicari" is an editor's correction of the manuscript's "se publicare" (MUR-L 22080; MUR-E 60182).

I4. **Copied in a Bible.** "a ms. of the Pastor of Hermas was found at the end of the Sinaitic Codex of Tischendorf" (HE-Crombie 578–579). Duchesne: "Comme Clément, Hermas eut les honneurs de la transcription dans les manuscrits de la Bible. On l'a trouvé à la fin du célèbre codex Sinaïticus." (DUCH 8550–8552). Editors. Widely Accepted. Plain words: "copied at the end of the New Testament in one of the oldest Bibles we have". Robinson thinks it likely stood at the close of the African Bible too: "an additional reason for believing that in the African Bible of the close of the second century ... the Shepherd of Hermas found a place at the close of the Canonical Books" (ROB 2318–2321): editor's inference, Inferential-Thin.

I5. **Its habits copied.** At Carthage some Christians sat down after prayer because Hermas did. Tertullian, *On Prayer* 16: "For what if that Hermas, whose writing is generally inscribed with the title The Shepherd, had, after finishing his prayer, not sat down on his bed, but done some other thing: should we maintain that also as a matter for observance? Of course not." (TERT-O 58564–58570). The line he means: "After I had been praying at home, and had sat down on my couch" (HE 1693–1694). LATER. Documented.

I6. **Rejected by Tertullian.** *On Modesty* opens on a bishop's edict: "I remit, to such as have discharged (the requirements of) repentance, the sins both of adultery and of fornication." (TERT 7186–7188). Against those who hope for "a second repentance" (TERT 8135), Tertullian writes: "I would yield my ground to you, if the scripture of 'the Shepherd,' which is the only one which favours adulterers, had deserved to find a place in the Divine canon; if it had not been habitually judged by every council of Churches (even of your own) among apocryphal and false (writings)" (TERT 8138–8144). Latin: "si scriptura Pastoris, quae sola moechos amat, divino instrumento meruisset incidi, si non ab omni concilio ecclesiarum etiam vestrarum inter apocrypha et falsa iudicaretur" (TERT-L 72450–72456, OCR read). In ch. 20: "the Epistle of Barnabas is more generally received among the Churches than that apocryphal 'Shepherd' of adulterers" (TERT 9329–9331); Latin "receptior apud ecclesias epistola Barnabae illo apocrypho Pastore moechorum" (TERT-L 74596–74597). LATER, hostile; one witness for the "every council" claim. Documented as his words. Crombie: "The only voice of antiquity decidedly opposed to the claim is that of Tertullian ... Even his words, however, show that it was regarded in many churches as Scripture." (HE-Crombie 532–536): editor. Whether Tertullian changed his view between *On Prayer* and *On Modesty*: Robinson cannot see "sufficient ground for supposing, with Gebhardt and Harnack ... that Tertullian materially changed his view" (ROB 2324–2325): Contested among editors.

I7. **Disputed, then set aside.** Eusebius: "...Hermas, to whom the book called The Shepherd is ascribed, it should be observed that this too has been disputed by some, and on their account cannot be placed among the acknowledged books; while by others it is considered quite indispensable, especially to those who need instruction in the elements of the faith. Hence, as we know, it has been publicly read in churches, and I have found that some of the most ancient writers used it." (EUS 14753–14754, 14854–14860, McGiffert's note removed). And: "Among the rejected writings must be reckoned also the Acts of Paul, and the so-called Shepherd" (EUS 18031–18034; Greek "ἐν τοῖς νόθοις"). Athanasius, Festal Letter 39 (367): books "not indeed included in the Canon, but appointed by the Fathers to be read by those who newly join us, and who wish for instruction in the word of godliness ... and that which is called the Teaching of the Apostles, and the Shepherd." (ATH 68817–68823). Rufinus lists "the little book which is called the Book of the Pastor of Hermas" among books "they would have read in the Churches, but not appealed to for the confirmation of doctrine" (JER 58139–58143). Jerome: it "is reputed to be the author of the book which is called Pastor and which is also read publicly in some churches of Greece. It is in fact a useful book and many of the ancient writers quote from it as authority, but among the Latins it is almost unknown." (JER 39625–39629). LATER. Documented.

## 4. Links to other people

L1. **Pius, bishop of Rome**: his brother, per the Muratorian fragment (I3) and the Liberian Catalogue of 354: "Sub huius episcopatu frater eius Ermes librum scripsit in quo mandatum continetur, quae ei precepit angelus, cum venit ad illum in habitu pastoris." (LP 66660–66662): "In his time as bishop his brother Ermes wrote a book holding the command the angel gave him, when he came to him dressed as a shepherd." Also PS-TERT: "After him followed Pius—Hermas his Own brother was; angelic 'Pastor' he, Because he spake the words delivered him" (TERT 15660–15663). Pius is not on the tree.
L2. **The Hermas of Romans 16:14**: Origen's identification, repeated by Eusebius (EUS 14735–14754) and Jerome (JER 39619–39626). McGiffert: "This tradition, however, appears to be no older than Origen, with whom it is no more than a mere guess." (EUS-McG 14793–14795). Coxe's conjecture that Hermas was a grandson of the Pauline Hermas (HE-Coxe 349–351) is his own guess. Leave out.
L3. **Clement** (Vision 2, HE 1010–1013): the one who sends the book "to foreign countries". Often taken as Clement of Rome (EUS-McG 14799–14802). Not on the tree under that name.
L4. **Tertullian** (`tertullian-s-voice`, on the tree as "Tertullian"): rejected the book (I6). The tree's Tertullian story tells his later rigorism "against what he saw as a church going soft" and his alignment with the New Prophecy (CEN `tertullian-s-voice`, `longDescription`); it does not mention Hermas.
L5. **Irenaeus** (`irenaeus-of-lyon`), **Clement of Alexandria** (`clement-of-alexandria`), **Origen** (`origen`), **Eusebius of Caesarea** (`eusebius-of-caesarea`), **Athanasius of Alexandria** (`athanasius-of-alexandria`), **Jerome** (`jerome`): readers of the book (I1, I2, I7). All on the tree.
L6. **Perpetua's visions**: Robinson argues "a strong probability that the Martyrs were familiar with the Visions of Hermas", citing "her vision of the Man in a shepherd's dress" and other parallels (ROB 2169–2195). Editor's argument, Contested. Perpetua is not a census person. Leave out.

## 5. Where sources disagree or come late

D1. **Who he was.** Brother of Pius (MUR, Liberian Catalogue, PS-TERT), or the Hermas of Romans 16 (Origen, Eusebius, Jerome; "a mere guess", McGiffert), or a contemporary of Clement of Rome (Zahn, McGiffert). Contested.
D2. **Slave.** The opening line differs by manuscript (E1). "Freed" is not in the text. Inferential-Thin.
D3. **Vision or fiction.** E1 Witness kinds. Contested. The story keeps every vision in his voice: "he writes that he saw".
D4. **Anti-Montanist purpose.** Coxe reads the whole book as written against Montanism (HE-Coxe 303–313, 5296–5297). Duchesne dates the book's completion to c. 140 (DUCH 8184–8186), before the New Prophecy appeared in the West. An editor's thesis; leave out.
D5. **Coxe's doubt on the once-only words** (E10). Keep to HE 1933–1934 for "one repentance".
D6. **The Muratorian fragment** survives in one seventh- or eighth-century manuscript; its author and original language are unknown (MUR-E 60058–60064; HE-Crombie 538–542).
