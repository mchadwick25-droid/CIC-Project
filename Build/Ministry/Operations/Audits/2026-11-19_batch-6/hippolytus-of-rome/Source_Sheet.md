# Source sheet: Hippolytus of Rome

Census id `hippolytus-of-rome`. Told within **The Roman Church in the Third Century** (`roman-church-third-century`; spoken short name "Third-Century Rome", informal "Callistus' Rome"). Census dates "exiled 235"; place Rome; card role "Presbyter, writer, and rival of Callistus". The card (`Card.existing.json`) was used as a starting list only.

## Setting

**S1. Rome, under three bishops.** The *Refutation*'s author says the teaching of Noetus spread at Rome "during the succession of such bishops", Zephyrinus and Callistus (HIP 13285–13300), and that he was "a spectator" of events at Rome over some twenty years (MacMahon's summary of the author's claim, HIP 796–800: "the author of The Refutation asserts his presence at Rome during the occurrence of events which occupied a period of some twenty years. And not only was he a spectator, but took part in these transactions"; EDITOR). McGiffert's dates: Zephyrinus "bishop of Rome from 198 or 199 to 217" (EUS 35454); Callistus bishop five years, "his accession is to be put into the year 217" (EUS 35482–35504); Pontianus exiled 235 (EUS 35535–35538). EDITOR. Widely Accepted. Plain words: "Rome, in the early two hundreds".

**S2. A Greek writer in a city turning Latin.** McGiffert: "composing all his works in Greek" (EUS 35722–35723). EDITOR, Widely Accepted. The *Refutation* is in Greek (MacMahon, HIP 765–766: the Tertullian attribution "is untenable … because the work is in Greek"). Duchesne: in the next generation the Roman clergy spoke and wrote Latin, and his books soon disappeared from the Roman horizon (DUH 11728–11731: "La littérature d'Hippolyte … disparut bientôt de l'horizon romain. A la génération suivante, le clergé de Rome parle et écrit en latin."). EDITOR. Widely Accepted.

**S3. Tradition.** Census `roman-church-third-century`, spoken "Third-Century Rome". The census tradition entry calls him "a learned presbyter of the same city … eventually functioning as a rival bishop" and says "Nearly everything known about the period comes from him … he is the losing party and a hostile witness." (Census entry; its rival-bishop clause is one side of D2 and must not be carried as settled.)

## Events

**E1. A teacher heard by Origen.** Jerome (JER 40690–40703, *Lives* 61): Hippolytus wrote, among much else, "an exhortation On the praise of our Lord and Saviour, in which he indicates that he is speaking in the church in the presence of Origen." Latin (JERL 56688–56689): "Προσομιλίαν de laude Domini Salvatoris, in qua presente Origene, se loqui in ecclesia significat." LATER (Jerome, 392, about 160 years on), from a title and its contents. Documented as Jerome's report. Harnack places Origen's visit to Rome between 211 and 215/6 (HAR 11957–11960): EDITOR. Jerome also says Ambrose urged Origen to write commentaries "in emulation of Hyppolytus" (JER 40703–40706; JERL 56690–56693: "In hujus emulationem"). Documented as Jerome's report.

**E2. Against Noetus: the question.** HIP 23116–23175 (*Against Noetus* 1): Noetus of Smyrna "alleged that Christ was the Father Himself, and that the Father Himself was born, and suffered, and died." The elders of his church examined him; he answered, "What evil, then, am I doing in glorifying Christ?"; they "expelled him from the Church. And he was carried to such a pitch of pride, that he established a school." OWN (the work's title names Hippolytus; Harnack lists the "Homilie gegen Noetus" among works "niemals bezweifelten", HAR 11866–11868; Duchesne calls it the surviving end of his *Syntagma*, DUH 11444–11445 note). Documented as his account.

**E3. His answer.** HIP 23439–23450 (*Against Noetus* 7): "If, again, he allege His own word when He said, 'I and the Father are one,' let him attend to the fact, and understand that He did not say, 'I and the Father am one, but are one.' … For the word are is not said of one person, but it refers to two persons, and one power." The Greek words in the note: "ἐγὼ καὶ ὁ πατὴρ—ἕν ἐσμεν, not ἕν εἰμι" (HIP 23444–23448). HIP 23508–23514 (ch. 8): "As far as regards the power, therefore, God is one. But as far as regards the economy there is a threefold manifestation". OWN. Documented.

**E4. The quarrel with Zephyrinus and Callistus.** *Refutation* IX (ANF ch. 2, 6–7). Noetus's teaching reached Rome through his pupil Epigonus and then Cleomenes (HIP 13280–13285). "At that time, Zephyrinus imagines that he administers the affairs of the Church—an uninformed and shamefully corrupt man"; bribed, he connived with them, "and he had Callistus as his adviser" (HIP 13285–13295). "Never at any time, however, have we been guilty of collusion with them; but we have frequently offered them opposition" (HIP 13302–13305). Callistus had Zephyrinus say publicly, "I know that there is one God, Jesus Christ; nor except Him do I know any other that is begotten and amenable to suffering," and on another occasion, "The Father did not die, but the Son" (HIP 13619–13625). "We … reproved and withstood him for the truth's sake"; and he "called us worshippers of two gods" (HIP 13631–13640); later Callistus said publicly "in the way of reproach to us, 'Ye are Ditheists'" (HIP 13838–13839). OWN, hostile. Documented as his account; what Zephyrinus and Callistus meant is Contested (DUH 11380–11391 reads Zephyrinus's two sentences as keeping both sides of the tradition; DUH 11450–11466, 11500–11516: Hippolytus's account of Callistus's doctrine is hard to credit).

**E5. Callistus's past, as his enemy tells it.** HIP 13672–13809 (ANF IX ch. 7). Callistus was "a domestic" of Carpophorus, a Christian of Caesar's household, who gave him money to run a bank in the Piscina Publica; widows and brethren deposited with him; he "made away with all", fled to Portus, threw himself into the sea, was pulled out and put in the treadmill (HIP 13672–13709). Released, he caused a disturbance in a synagogue on the Sabbath; the prefect Fuscianus "having scourged Callistus … gave him to be sent to a mine in Sardinia" (HIP 13725–13751). Freed with other confessors through Marcia, though bishop Victor had left his name off the list (HIP 13756–13794); Victor sent him to Antium with a monthly allowance; Zephyrinus "appointed him over the cemetery" (HIP 13794–13806). Hippolytus sneers at it as a "martyrdom" (HIP 13648–13650). OWN, hostile. McGiffert: "a very bitter opponent … it can hardly be doubted that at least the groundwork of the account is true … (and we cannot doubt that it is greatly overdrawn)" (EUS 35509–35521). Duchesne: one must not trust what he says of Callistus; even the facts cannot be accepted without reserve (DUH 11450–11454: "Aussi ne faut-il pas se fier à ce qu'il en dit … les faits eux-mêmes, tels qu'il les rapporte, ne sauraient être admis sans réserves"). Contested in detail; that Callistus had been a slave sent to the Sardinian mines is told without doubt by McGiffert and the census tradition entry (Widely Accepted on one hostile witness).

**E6. Callistus made bishop; Sabellius cut off.** HIP 13816–13822: "after the death of Zephyrinus, supposing that he had obtained (the position) after which he so eagerly pursued, he excommunicated Sabellius, as not entertaining orthodox opinions. He acted thus from apprehension of me, and imagining that he could in this manner obliterate the charge against him among the churches". He never uses "bishop" of Callistus in this sentence; McGiffert: "he never recognizes Callistus as bishop of Rome, but always treats him as the head of a school" (EUS 35665–35667, EDITOR; but see D2: the ANF English has "During the episcopate of this one", HIP 13955). Duchesne: two things are certain by his own testimony: Callistus condemned Sabellius, and did not condemn Hippolytus; Hippolytus separated himself (DUH 11516–11531: "la première c'est que Calliste condamna Sabellius, la seconde c'est qu'il ne condamna point Hippolyte. Celui-ci se ségrégea lui-même."). EDITOR. Documented (Sabellius); Contested (who broke with whom).

**E7. "Too lax": the charges.** HIP 13870–13963 (ANF IX ch. 7). Callistus "established a school of theology in antagonism to the Church"; he "first invented the device of conniving with men in regard of their indulgence in sensual pleasures, saying that all had their sins forgiven by himself" (HIP 13870–13875); those "by us forcibly ejected from the Church" joined him and "served to crowd his school" (HIP 13884–13889); a bishop guilty even of "a sin unto death … ought not to be deposed"; clergy twice and thrice married kept their place; a cleric who married stayed in orders (HIP 13889–13897). He cited "Let the tares grow along with the wheat"; "or, in other words, let those who in the Church are guilty of sin remain in it" (HIP 13903–13909). "But also he affirmed that the ark of Noe was made for a symbol of the Church, in which were both dogs, and wolves, and ravens, and all things clean and unclean; and so he alleges that the case should stand in like manner with the Church." (HIP 13909–13912). He let unmarried women of rank take a slave or a free man as partner without legal marriage; women then used drugs and binding to rid themselves of what they had conceived: "Behold, into how great impiety that lawless one has proceeded, by inculcating adultery and murder at the same time! And withal, after such audacious acts, they, lost to all shame, attempt to call themselves a Catholic Church!" (HIP 13924–13952). "During the episcopate of this one, second baptism was for the first time presumptuously attempted by them" (HIP 13955–13956). His school "continues … indiscriminately offering communion to all", and they are called "Callistians" (HIP 13957–13963). OWN, hostile. Documented as Hippolytus's charge; what Callistus actually ruled is Contested (McGiffert: Callistus "took the liberal view of Christian morals and church discipline, over against the stricter view represented by Hippolytus and his party", EUS 35521–35524; and "favored the readmission to the Church even of the worst offenders upon evidence of repentance and suitable penance", EUS 35713–35715: EDITOR, Widely Accepted).

**E8. His claim to authority.** *Refutation*, Proœmium (HIP 1127–1137): the Holy Spirit "bequeathed unto the Church, which the Apostles, having in the first instance received, have transmitted to those who have rightly believed. But we, as being their successors, and as participators in this grace, high-priesthood, and office of teaching, as well as being reputed guardians of the Church, must not be found deficient in vigilance". The translator's note: "It is not, however, quite certain that the words refer to the episcopal office exclusively" (HIP 1131–1135: EDITOR). Mommsen reads it as the dignity of a bishop (CHR 13736–13737: "archieratian … sibi tribuit, id est dignitatem episcopi"). OWN. Documented as his words; that it means he was a bishop, Widely Accepted among the held editors; what see, D2.

**E9. The *Refutation* and its plan.** Proœmium (HIP 1162–1183): to show the heretics' doctrines "have derived their origin from the wisdom of the Greeks, from the conclusions of those who have formed systems of philosophy, and from would-be mysteries, and the vagaries of astrologers", and "to compare each heresy with the system of each speculator". Book IX turns to "those heresies that have sprung up in our own day" (HIP 13245–13254), ending with Noetus and Callistus. OWN. Documented. Written after Callistus's death in 222 (MacMahon, HIP 878–879: "the work itself was written after the death of Callistus in a.d. 222", EDITOR); Mommsen argues later still, after 234, since it cites his chronicle (CHR 13744–13746). Widely Accepted (after 222). Valentinus's school is among those traced to Greek philosophy (Book VI; the `valentinus` story tells it).

**E10. Easter tables and a chronicle.** Eusebius VI.22 (EUS 35650–35651, 35754–35755, 35778–35780): "At that time Hippolytus, besides many other treatises, wrote a work on the passover. He gives in this a chronological table, and presents a certain paschal canon of sixteen years, bringing the time down to the first year of the Emperor Alexander." Greek (EUSG 4098–4102): "καὶ τὸ Περὶ τοῦ πάσχα πεποίηται σύγγραμμα, ἐν ᾧ τῶν χρόνων ἀναγραφὴν ἐκθέμενος καί τινα κανόνα ἑκκαιδεκαετηρίδος περὶ τοῦ πάσχα προθείς". LATER (Eusebius, about 90 years on). Documented. The first year of Alexander is 222 (Harnack, HAR 11919–11921; Duchesne dates the statue's cycle to 222, DUH 11640–11642: EDITOR). Harnack: the tables "haben sich schon seit dem J. 237 als falsch erwiesen" (HAR 12010–12012: EDITOR). A chronicle ran to the death of Alexander, early 235 (McGiffert, EUS 35690–35693; Duchesne, DUH 11645–11649). Documented (EDITOR, on the surviving Latin versions).

**E11. Exile, 235.** The list of bishops in the book of 354 (CHR 13078 with 13104–13105; LP 108833–108835 gives the later *Liber Pontificalis* form): "Eo tempore Pontianus episcopus et Yppolitus presbiter exoles sunt deportati in Sardinia in insula nociva, Severo et Quintiano cons. In eadem insula discinctus est iiii kal. Octobr. et loco eius ordinatus est Antheros" (OCR "uocina" for "nociva", CHR 13104; Duchesne: one manuscript has *uocina*, the other *nociua*, the second right, LP 109188–109190). ("At that time bishop Pontianus and the presbyter Hippolytus were deported as exiles to Sardinia, the unhealthy island, when Severus and Quintianus were consuls. In that island he [Pontianus] stepped down on 28 September, and Antheros was ordained in his place.") LATER (the list was compiled by 354; Harnack: "die Notiz stammt aus der Mitte des 3. Jahrh.", HAR 11924–11925; Lipsius, via McGiffert, "critically indisputable", EUS 35693–35697). Documented. That this presbyter is the writer: McGiffert, "little doubt" (EUS 35697–35698); Duchesne, "nullement douteux" (LP 108991). Widely Accepted. "Discinctus" read as Pontianus stepping down is the plain sense of the word ("ungirded"); Widely Accepted. That Maximinus, not Alexander, exiled them: Duchesne (LP 109186–109187), Harnack (HAR 11930–11933). EDITOR, Widely Accepted. The list says only that they were exiled to Sardinia; Duchesne writes that they were "condamnés aux mines de Sardaigne" (DUH 11685–11686) and McGiffert that they probably "died in the mines" (EUS 35698–35700): EDITOR, Inferential-Thin for Hippolytus.

**E12. Death and burial.** The calendar of martyrs in the book of 354 (CHR 12786, with the date column at 12882–12883 and the entries at 12909–12913): under "idus Aug." (13 August): "Ypoliti in Tiburtina et Pontiani in Calisti" ("Of Hippolytus, on the Tiburtine Road, and of Pontianus, in [the cemetery] of Callistus"). LATER (calendar of 336/354). Documented: both kept on the same day. How he died is not recorded. McGiffert: "highly probable that both he and Pontianus died in the mines there" (EUS 35698–35700). Harnack: either in Sardinia or soon after in Rome; "nicht als Märtyrer gestorben, sondern sein Martyrium war eben das Exil" (HAR 11977–11981). Duchesne: their bodies were later brought back, perhaps on the same ship, and buried the same day (LP 109034–109040; DUH 11714–11720). EDITOR. Inferential-Thin (place and manner of death); Documented (the shared day).

**E13. Reconciled? (editors only).** Harnack: he was still exiled as a bishop, and the shared fate reconciled him with Pontianus, "und damit war das Schisma erloschen" (HAR 11970–11976). Duchesne: in the misery of the mines the two confessors "finirent par se réconcilier"; at the end he urged his followers to rejoin the other faithful (DUH 11712–11714). McGiffert: after Callistus's death he "undoubtedly returned to the Church" (EUS 35686–35688). EDITOR. Inferential-Thin: no third-century source says so; the urging rests on Damasus's verse (I3).

## Impact, heaviest first

**I1. The *Refutation*: lost, found under another name, argued over.** Coxe: "the discovery, at Mount Athos, in 1842, of the long-lost Philosophumena of this author … Its learned editor, Emmanuel Miller, published it at Oxford under the name of Origen, which was inscribed on the ms." (HIP 638–643). MacMahon: "The Refutation was printed at the Clarendon Press in 1851" (HIP 748); "commentators turned their attention towards Hippolytus, in favour of whose authorship the majority of modern scholars have decided" (HIP 803–806). McGiffert: "it has been proved to the complete satisfaction of almost every scholar that it is a work of Hippolytus" (EUS 35745–35747). McGiffert: "Nothing was known about the character and life of Callistus until the discovery of Hippolytus' Philosophumena" (EUS 35506–35509). MacMahon: it "has restored the Greek text of much of his [Irenaeus's] book Against Heresies, hitherto only known to us in a Latin version" (HIP 944–946). EDITOR. Documented (find and printing); the authorship, D1.

**I2. Remembered as both: elder at Rome, bishop in the East.** Eusebius VI.20 (EUS 35447–35449): "Likewise also Hippolytus, who presided over another church, has left writings." Greek (EUSG 3965–3966): "ὡσαύτως δὲ καὶ Ἱππόλυτος, ἑτέρας που καὶ αὐτὸς προεστὼς ἐκκλησίας" ("he too presiding over some other church"). Jerome (JER 40690–40691): "bishop of some church (the name of the city I have not been able to learn)"; Latin (JERL 56673–56674): "cujusdam Ecclesiae episcopus, nomen quippe urbis scire non potui". Theodoret, *Eranistes* (about 447), lists "Hippolytus and Methodius, bishops and martyrs" among his witnesses (JER 20259–20261) and quotes "the Holy Hippolytus, Bishop and Martyr" (JER 20467–20468). McGiffert: "In the East, from the fourth century on, Hippolytus was commonly called bishop of Rome, but the Western tradition makes him simply a presbyter" (EUS 35660–35662). LATER, EDITOR. Documented.

**I3. Damasus's verse: the link to Novatian.** Damasus, bishop of Rome (366/7–384), set a verse on the tomb on the Tiburtine Road (Duchesne, LP 109003–109008; text LP 109159–109166, with the restored letters in lower case; Mommsen quotes the last line, CHR 13737–13740):

> Hippolytus fertur, premerent cum iussa tyranni, / presbyter in scisma semper mansisse Novati, / tempore quo gladius secuit pia viscera matris. / Devotus Christo peteret cum regna piorum, / quaesisset populus ubinam procedere posset, / catholicam dixisse fidem sequerentur ut omnes. / Sic noster meruit confessus martyr ut esset. / Haec audita refert Damasus: probat omnia Christus.

(OCR at LP 109164 reads "dùrisse" for *dixisse*; the sense requires *dixisse*, "said". Lower-case letters are the editors' restorations of a broken stone; Duchesne cites de Rossi's edition, LP 109007–109008.) LATER (about 140 years on). Documented as Damasus's report; its content Contested. Duchesne: "Damase s'y montre très peu affirmatif et qu'il semble vouloir mettre sa responsabilité de narrateur à couvert derrière les bruits" (LP 109155–109158). Mommsen: Damasus "gravissimo errore eum discipulum facit Novati et ipse sibi fidem denegat" (CHR 13738–13739). De Rossi accepted the verse as history; Duchesne could not (LP 109027–109033). Duchesne's explanation: a short split, long forgotten, was confused with the Novatian church that still existed in the late fourth century (LP 109168–109176; DUH 11720–11727: "comme il n'avait de ce schisme qu'une notion très vague, il l'identifia avec celui de Novatien"). EDITOR.

**I4. Easter tables to Eusebius; the chronicle to Rome's list.** Jerome (JER 40693–40696): "He also discussed the cycle of sixteen years … and gave the cue to Eusebius, who composed on the same Paschal feast a cycle of nineteen years". Latin (JERL 56677–56681): "et Eusebio, qui super eodem Pascha Canonem, decem et novem annorum circulum … composuit, occasionem dedit." LATER. Documented as Jerome's report. Duchesne: the first part of the Liberian list of Rome's bishops, down to Pontianus, derives from Hippolytus's chronicle, which stopped at 235 (LP 108984–108987). EDITOR, Widely Accepted.

**I5. The legend of his death.** MacMahon: "Of his martyrdom, though the fact itself is certain, the details, furnished in Prudentius' hymn, are not historic. Thus the mode of Hippolytus' death is stated by Prudentius to have been identical with that of Hippolytus the son of Theseus, who was torn limb from limb by being tied to wild horses." (HIP 863–868). Harnack: "Alles, was über seinen Märtyrertod von Prudentius ab erzählt worden ist, ist pure Fabel" (HAR 11982–11984). Duchesne: Prudentius drew on Damasus's verse but confused him with another martyr Hippolytus, called Nonnus, honoured at Porto (DUH 11739–11745; LP 109009–109012). Prudentius's hymn itself (*Peristephanon* XI, about 400) is not held. EDITOR. Inferential-Thin (the details); Documented that the legend was told.

**I6. The statue.** Coxe: "In the year 1551, while some excavations were in progress near the ancient Church of St. Lawrence at Rome, on the Tiburtine Road, there was found an ancient statue, in marble, of a figure seated in a chair" (HIP 598–602); "the inscription of Pius IV., who calls him 'Saint Hippolytus, Bishop of Portus'" (HIP 615–617); the chair carries "a calendar indicating the Paschal full moons for seven cycles of sixteen years each … On the back of the chair is a list of the author's works" (HIP 628–633). McGiffert: "The statue, though it bears no name, has been shown to be that of Hippolytus", its list holding "some thirteen works" (EUS 35729–35734). Duchesne: it was raised by "les gens de sa secte"; "La tête est moderne" (DUH 11636–11650; EDITOR). Harnack: raised before 238, since its tables were wrong from 237 (HAR 12010–12015). EDITOR. Documented (find); Widely Accepted (that it is his, in the held editors); the head, Duchesne's note. Later arguments over the statue's original form are not held (Library sweep).

## Links

- **Valentinus**: one of the schools the *Refutation* traces to Greek philosophy (E9). Batch 6 sibling; its story keeps the *Refutation*'s "all love" quote.
- **Irenaeus of Lyon**: a ninth-century writer calls Hippolytus his pupil (PHO 11632, Cod. 121: "Read the tractate of Hippolytus, the pupil of Irenaeus"); Harnack argues for real discipleship, perhaps at Lyon about 190 (HAR 11993–12001). LATER, EDITOR. Contested. Irenaeus's story already tells this clause.
- **Justin Martyr**: the *Refutation* names Tatian as Justin's hearer (HIP 12996 ff., Book VIII, "Tatian"); the live Justin story says "Irenaeus and Hippolytus say that one of his hearers was Tatian". Not told here.
- **Origen**: heard him preach, by Jerome (E1); Ambrose urged Origen to write "in emulation of" him (E1). The 1842 manuscript bore Origen's name (I1).
- **Eusebius of Caesarea**: lists his works, does not know his see (E10, I2); took the cue for his nineteen-year Easter cycle, by Jerome (I4).
- **Jerome**: lists his works, could not learn his city (I2).
- **Damasus of Rome**: the verse on his tomb (I3).
- **Novatian**: Damasus's verse makes Hippolytus a presbyter of "the schism of Novatus" (I3). McGiffert: "We are reminded greatly of Tertullian and of Novatian in studying Hippolytus' character" (EUS 35715–35717). Duchesne: the Logos theology Hippolytus stood for was "ouvertement professée par le prêtre romain Novatien" in the next generation (DUH 11532–11535). EDITOR. No held source says Novatian learned from him.
- **Theodoret of Cyrrhus**: quoted him as "bishop and martyr" (I2).
- **Tertullian**: his *On Modesty* protests an edict on forgiving adultery that Duchesne identifies with Callistus (DUH 11563–11577; the edict, DUH 11572–11577). Duchesne's identification; not told here.

## Where sources disagree or come late

**D1. Who wrote the *Refutation*.** The manuscript bore Origen's name; Miller printed it as Origen's (HIP 641–643). MacMahon lists earlier and rival candidates: Epiphanius (Huet), Didymus (Heuman), Aetius (Gale), Caius the Roman presbyter (Fessler and Baur), Tertullian (Jellabert) (HIP 759–766); "the majority of modern scholars" chose Hippolytus, among them Jacobi, Gieseler, Duncker, Schneidewin, Bernays, Bunsen, Wordsworth and Döllinger (HIP 803–812). Harnack (1904): its Hippolytan origin "bedarf keines Nachweises mehr" (HAR 11865–11866); but he reports that Batiffol (1897) still doubted it, adopting the doubts of de Rossi, Duchesne and Denis (HAR 11871–11874). By 1907 Duchesne writes of it as Hippolytus's (DUH 11404–11407). Eusebius and Jerome do not name the *Refutation* among his works (EUS 35782–35876; JER 40696–40701). Widely Accepted among the held editors; Contested by named nineteenth-century scholars. Later twentieth-century arguments (for two authors, or a Roman school) are not held: the story may say "most scholars since 1851 have given it to Hippolytus; some have doubted it" and no more.

**D2. Bishop of what, and a rival church?** His own words: "successors" of the apostles, sharing their "high-priesthood" (E8); Callistus's following is a "school" that only "attempt[s] to call themselves a Catholic Church" (E7); he and his had "forcibly ejected" some from the Church (E7). Eusebius: he presided "over some other church" (I2). Jerome: bishop, city unknown (I2). The list of 354 calls him "presbiter" in 235 (E11). Views of later writers:
- **Rival bishop of Rome.** Döllinger, called by an editor "the first anti-pope" (JER 36822–36823 note: "According to Döllinger the first anti-pope"); McGiffert: "probable that he was a presbyter of the church of Rome, and was at the head of a party which did not recognize Callistus as lawful bishop, but set Hippolytus up as opposition bishop … The schism … cannot have been a serious one … never amounted to more than talk" (EUS 35669–35686); Harnack: "Gegenbischof des Kallist in Rom" (HAR 11912), the split breaking out at once on Callistus's election (HAR 11950–11952 note); Duchesne: "l'antipape" (DUH 11685), head of a dissident church under Callistus, Urban and Pontianus (DUH 11402–11406, 11676–11679).
- **Bishop of Portus, Rome's harbour, at odds with Callistus but no rival.** Mommsen: he never says he was chosen in Callistus's place (CHR 13749–13757); he was bishop of Portus (CHR 13716–13728). Coxe and MacMahon: bishop of Portus (HIP 852–863). Lightfoot, per Harnack, held Portus: "ist falsch" (HAR 11861–11863). McGiffert: the Portus tradition "is quite worthless" (EUS 35662–35663). Döllinger kept him a bishop but refused Portus (MacMahon, HIP 832–835).
- **Doubt that there was a split at all.** Batiffol, quoted by Harnack, spoke of the "prétendu schisme" and asked why the chronicle's heirs call him a priest in 235 (HAR 11881–11889). Harnack answers him (HAR 11889–11894).
Contested. The story tells the claims as his, and each view credited to its holders (by role where they are not on the tree: "historians", "a German historian of the nineteenth century").

**D3. One Hippolytus or more.** Döllinger, per MacMahon, "shows that there is only one historical Hippolytus", against those who held "more Hippolytuses than one", or a bishop in the East: Le Moyne (seventeenth century) made him bishop of a Portus in Arabia, followed by Dupin, Tillemont and Cave (HIP 841–848). Duchesne: the Roman teacher kept on 13 August must be distinguished from Hippolytus "Nonnus", honoured at Porto on 23 August; he holds that the soldier Hippolytus of Lawrence's legend and Damasus's Novatianist presbyter are the same man as the teacher; de Rossi "conserve des doutes" (LP 108991–109027). McGiffert: the Hippolytus who carried a letter of Dionysius of Alexandria cannot be him (EUS 39080–39087). Some have taken Caius, the Roman presbyter, and Hippolytus for one man (Salmon, citing Lightfoot, via McGiffert, EUS 13987–13999: "no more than a bare hypothesis"). Contested.

**D4. The statue.** That it is his: Coxe ("no reason to doubt", HIP 615), McGiffert ("has been shown", EUS 35733), Duchesne. It bears no name (McGiffert); its head is modern (Duchesne); Pius IV's sixteenth-century inscription calls him bishop of Portus (Coxe). Who raised it: "his sect" (Duchesne), or "contemporaries" (Wordsworth, via Coxe, HIP 623–626). Widely Accepted in the held editors that it is his; the rest Contested.

**D5. His death.** Not recorded. Probably in the Sardinian mines (McGiffert); in Sardinia or Rome (Harnack; MacMahon); torn by horses (the fifth-century poet, not held, per MacMahon), drowned in a canal (MacMahon's "historical testimony", HIP 868–869, unsourced). Inferential-Thin.

**D6. Callistus's portrait.** One witness, his enemy (E5–E7). McGiffert: true at base, "greatly overdrawn"; Duchesne: not to be trusted, and his account of Callistus's doctrine is hard to credit. Contested.

**D7. Whether Hippolytus never called Callistus bishop.** McGiffert says so (EUS 35665–35667). The ANF English of IX has "During the episcopate of this one" (HIP 13955) and "the succession of such bishops" (HIP 13298–13299); the Greek is not held, so the point cannot be checked here. Do not make it a claim.
