# Outline: Hippolytus of Rome

Sheet items (S, E, I, D) are in `Source_Sheet.md`, which gives the file and line for each. HIP is ANF V (the *Refutation* in MacMahon's English, *Against Noetus* in Salmond's; the Greek of both is not held); EUS McGiffert's Eusebius; JER/JERL Jerome's *Lives* 61 in English and Latin; CHR the book of 354 (Mommsen); LP the *Liber Pontificalis* (Duchesne), which holds Damasus's verse; DUH Duchesne's *Histoire ancienne*; HAR Harnack's *Chronologie*.

- **The essence:** Hippolytus was a Greek-writing teacher and elder of the church at Rome in the early two hundreds (S1, S2; Documented). In his *Refutation of All Heresies*, written after 222, he attacked Callistus, bishop of Rome from 217, as a man who forgave sinners too easily and who shared the teaching that Father and Son were one and the same (E4, E6, E7, E9; Documented as his charge; what Callistus taught, Contested). In 235 he was sent into exile on the island of Sardinia together with Pontianus, the bishop of Rome (E11; Documented, the list of 354; that the exile is this Hippolytus, Widely Accepted; that he was set to work in the mines is the historians' reading, DUH 11685–11686, EUS 35698–35700). Whether he led a rival church against the bishops of Rome is disputed (D2; Contested).
- **Also told:**
  - **What he taught.** Against a teacher from Smyrna who said Christ was the Father himself, born, suffering and dying, he answered from Christ's words "I and the Father are one": "are", not "am", so two persons and one power (E2, E3, Quote 2; Documented, his own words).
  - **The quarrel.** He opposed bishop Zephyrinus and his adviser Callistus over that teaching, and they called his side "worshippers of two gods" (E4; Documented as his account). When Callistus became bishop, Callistus cut off another teacher, Sabellius, "from apprehension of me", Hippolytus says (E6). Then he charged Callistus with forgiving every sin to anyone who joined him, keeping bishops in office after grave sin, letting twice-married men stay clergy, and defending all of it with Noah's ark, where clean and unclean lived together (E7, Quote 1; Documented as his charge). He went as far as to accuse Callistus of teaching adultery and murder (E7; his words).
  - **A rival church? Told as disputed.** He wrote as a successor of the apostles who shared their "high-priesthood" (E8), and called Callistus's following a "school" that dared to call itself the catholic church (E7). Eusebius says he led "some other church"; Jerome could not learn which city; the Roman list calls him an elder in 235 (I2, E11). Historians since the 1850s have read this as a rival bishop at Rome; others made him bishop of Portus, Rome's harbour; others doubted there was any split (D2; Contested, each view credited).
  - **The book and its author.** The *Refutation* traced each teaching he opposed back to a Greek philosopher or astrologer, and ended with Callistus (E9). It was lost, found at Mount Athos in 1842, and printed in 1851 under Origen's name; most scholars since have given it to Hippolytus, though some doubted it (I1, D1; Contested in the held record, Widely Accepted among its editors).
  - **What came of him.** The church of Rome kept him and Pontianus on the same day, 13 August (E12; Documented). About 140 years later bishop Damasus set a verse on his tomb saying he had belonged to Novatian's split and, going to his death, told the people to follow the catholic faith; Damasus tells it as hearsay, and historians judge it a confusion (I3; Documented as Damasus's report; content Contested). The Greek East remembered him as a bishop and martyr (I2). His Easter tables prompted Eusebius's own, Jerome says (I4). A seated marble figure found in 1551 on the Tiburtine Road, with his Easter tables and a list of books carved on the chair, is taken by his editors to be his, though it bears no name (I6, D4).
- **Length:** about 3 minutes 20 seconds (about 500 words). Why: a major figure whose own life is thin: no birth, no family, no death scene in the record. What needs room is the quarrel, told through one hostile witness with its force, his one idea in his own words, and the three disputes over what he was (bishop of where, author of what, statue of whom), each needing a credited clause rather than a sentence. A third goes to what came of him, where Damasus and the 1842 find carry the connection. Cut first, if needed: the Easter tables (What came 4), Origen in the audience (beat 1), and the twice-married clergy (beat 4).
- **Setting:**
  - **When:** the early two hundreds; Zephyrinus bishop of Rome from about 199 to 217, Callistus 217 to 222, Pontianus exiled 235 (S1, E11; McGiffert's dates).
  - **Where:** Rome; Sardinia ("Sardinia", the island west of Italy; the list calls it "the unhealthy island", E11).
  - **Tradition:** "Third-Century Rome" (census). He wrote all his books in Greek; in the next generation, Duchesne says, Rome's clergy spoke and wrote Latin (S2).
- **Words to explain:**
  - **elder** (the sources' "presbyter"): "one of the elders who led the church at Rome with its bishop". Never "presbyter" bare, and not "priest".
  - **the teaching that the Father suffered**: "that Father and Son were one and the same, so that the Father himself was born and suffered" (E2). Do not use "Patripassian", "modalism", "Monarchian" or "Sabellian".
  - **"worshippers of two gods"**: the narrator gives it in that plain form; never "ditheists".
  - **school** (his word for Callistus's following): "a school, he called it, not a church" (E7).
  - **catholic church** (in his charge and in Quote 3): "the church as a whole, the one true church". Lower-case "catholic" in sense; do not imply the later Roman Catholic Church.
  - **the mines of Sardinia** (Callistus's sentence, E5): "forced labour in the mines of Sardinia, a sentence for convicts". For Hippolytus and Pontianus the list says only "exile" to Sardinia; "the mines" for them is the historians' reading (E11, E12).
  - **Easter tables**: "tables for finding the date of Easter, year by year".
  - **Portus**: "Portus, Rome's harbour town at the mouth of the Tiber".
  - **confessor** (Quote 3): "one who suffered for the faith". The narrator gives the plain sense beside the quote.
  - **Novatus** (Quote 3): Damasus's name for the split led by Novatian. The narrator says plainly that Damasus means Novatian's split, a generation after Hippolytus.
  - **the tyrant** (Quote 3): "the persecuting emperor".
- **Shape:** Teacher or thinker. The question he was trying to answer: who belongs in the church, and how can Christ be God without the Father himself suffering? What he taught: two persons, one power (Quote 2), and a church kept pure by discipline. Who learned it: later readers, chiefly in the East; and in Rome the memory of him passed into the story of Novatian's split. The trap is a list of books; the book list stays in the card, and only the *Refutation*, *Against Noetus* and the Easter tables are named.
- **Sibling:**
  - `valentinus` (batch 6, outline read; job: the teacher of paired heavenly powers, told from his opponents). The *Refutation*'s account of Valentinus is one clause here, in beat 5: his school is among the teachings Hippolytus traced to Greek philosophy. Its quote "He was all love, and love is not love unless there is something to love" stays with Valentinus, as does "a Platonist, not a Christian". Neither is quoted or paraphrased here.
  - `irenaeus-of-lyon` (batch 6, outline read). It tells that Hippolytus called Irenaeus "the blessed elder" and that a much later writer calls Hippolytus his pupil. None of its quotes is reused; the pupil claim is not told here (Leave out).
  - `novatian` (live, read; job: Rome's split of 251 over receiving back those who had denied the faith). Here Novatian is a link, carried by Damasus's verse in What came 3. None of its quotes is reused ("Swear to me", "the Rule of truth", "another philosophy", "Place a ladder"). The 251 split itself is not told here beyond one clause.
  - `justin-martyr` (live, read). It says "Irenaeus and Hippolytus say that one of his hearers was Tatian". Not told here.
  - Tradition entries `roman-church-third-century` and `modalist-monarchianism` (read) say he functioned "as a rival bishop" and that his charge was "tangled up with his own rivalry for that see". Not quoted; here that is told as disputed.
- **Openings:**
  1. **Chosen.** Noah's ark (Quote 1; E7). In about three sentences: a bishop of Rome, his enemy wrote, said that Noah's ark was a picture of the church, with dogs, wolves and ravens inside, the clean beside the unclean; so the church should keep its sinners too. The man who wrote it down did so to show how far that bishop had fallen. He was Hippolytus, an elder of the same church, and the bishop was Callistus. Then the essence.
  2. The statue (I6): in 1551, digging on the Tiburtine Road outside Rome, workmen found a marble figure seated in a chair, carved with tables for finding Easter and a list of books, and no name. Then: the books were Hippolytus's. Reaches ahead to the end; use only if the writer ends on Damasus.

**Story beats** (after the hook and the essence, in time order)

1. **A teacher at Rome.** In the years around 200 he was one of Rome's elders, writing in Greek (S1, S2; Documented). Jerome, writing about 160 years later, says one of his sermons shows he preached in church with Origen in the audience (E1; Documented as Jerome's report; optional, cut first). Do not give a birthplace, a birth year or a teacher (Leave out).
2. **The Father who suffered.** A teacher from Smyrna, Noetus, had taught that Christ was the Father himself, so the Father was born, suffered and died; his church's elders put him out (E2; Hippolytus's account). His followers brought the teaching to Rome (E4). Hippolytus answered it (Quote 2; E3; Documented): one God in power, but Father and Son two persons. Say "two persons and one power" in plain words once.
3. **Against the bishop's adviser.** At Rome, he says, bishop Zephyrinus let the teaching spread, guided by his adviser Callistus, and was led to say in public both that he knew one God, Jesus Christ, who was born and suffered, and that the Father did not die, but the Son (E4; paraphrase only, not a quote; credit him: "Hippolytus says"). He and his side opposed them, and Callistus called them "worshippers of two gods" (E4). His portrait of Callistus's past, in one or two sentences, credited as his: a slave who ran a bank, lost the savings of widows, was sentenced to the mines of Sardinia, and was later put in charge of the church's burial ground (E5; Contested; credit "his enemy says", and say McGiffert's point plainly: the story is an enemy's and, historians judge, overdrawn). Do not tell the escape by sea or the synagogue scene (Leave out).
4. **Callistus bishop; the break.** When Zephyrinus died in 217, Callistus became bishop (S1, E6). Callistus cut off another teacher, Sabellius, for that same teaching; Hippolytus says he did it "from apprehension of me", to clear himself with the other churches (E6; his voice). Then Hippolytus's charges (E7; credit each as his): Callistus forgave every sin to anyone who came over to him; took in those whom Hippolytus and his side had put out; would not remove a bishop even for a grave sin; let twice-married men remain clergy (optional, cut first); and read the parable of wheat and weeds to mean that sinners should stay in the church. He called Callistus's following a school, not a church, and accused Callistus of teaching adultery and murder (E7; keep this force, credited). No source says that Callistus cut Hippolytus off; Duchesne reads Hippolytus's own account as showing that he left of his own accord, and no neutral witness tells who broke from whom (E6; optional, credited to "a historian"). Then the rival-church question (D2), in two or three credited sentences: he spoke of himself as a successor of the apostles, sharing their "high-priesthood" (E8); many historians since the 1850s have taken this to mean his followers made him a rival bishop at Rome; others say he was bishop of Portus, at odds with Callistus but not his rival, and some doubted there was a split at all. Never settle it.
5. **The book.** After Callistus died in 222, he wrote the *Refutation of All Heresies* (E9; MacMahon's dating, Widely Accepted). It traced each teaching he held false back to a Greek philosopher, a mystery cult or the astrologers; the followers of Valentinus are among them (one clause; E9; the `valentinus` story tells it). It ended with the teachers of his own day: Noetus, and Callistus (E9). About the same time he made tables for finding Easter, with a reckoning of years that, Eusebius says, ran down to the first year of the emperor Alexander, 222; the tables carved on the statue run from 222 (HIP 628–631). He also wrote a chronicle of the world that ended in 235 (E10, I6; Documented; this may sit in What came 4 instead).
6. **Sardinia, 235.** In 235 the bishop of Rome, Pontianus, and "the elder Hippolytus" were sent to Sardinia, "the unhealthy island", according to a list of Rome's bishops kept by 354 (E11; Documented). Historians judge that the new emperor, Maximinus, ordered it (E11; EDITOR; may be left as "the emperor"). There Pontianus gave up his office, and Rome chose a new bishop (E11; Widely Accepted). Hippolytus had told of Callistus's own sentence to the Sardinian mines; now he went to Sardinia himself, with Rome's bishop (E5, E11; a juxtaposition of two documented facts, no cause implied; Duchesne makes the same point, LP 109198–109201). How he died is not recorded; historians think it probable he died there, perhaps in the mines (E12, D5; Inferential-Thin). Rome's calendar of martyrs kept both on the same day, 13 August: Pontianus in the cemetery of Callistus, Hippolytus on the Tiburtine Road (E12; Documented). Some historians think the two were reconciled in exile; no early source says so (E13; credit "some historians think").

**What came of him** (ranked heaviest first; the writer orders them for telling, and may end on 3)

1. **The *Refutation*: the record and its doubt.** His books, written in Greek, soon dropped out of sight at Rome, where the clergy now wrote Latin (S2; Duchesne). Eusebius and Jerome list many of his works but not the *Refutation* (D1). It was found in a manuscript from Mount Athos in 1842 and printed at Oxford in 1851 under the name of Origen (I1; Documented). Scholars argued over who wrote it; most decided for Hippolytus, though some still doubted it (D1; Contested, credited to "most scholars" and "some"). It became almost the only record of Callistus and of Rome's quarrels in those years, which is why an enemy's portrait is nearly all we have of Callistus (I1, D6; Widely Accepted). This is the scene where the doubt is the story; tell it here, once.
2. **Elder in the West, bishop in the East.** Eusebius, less than a century later, says only that he led "some other church"; Jerome could not learn which city (I2; Documented). In the Greek East, historians note, he was remembered as a bishop, even as bishop of Rome (I2; McGiffert, EDITOR, Widely Accepted); Theodoret, in the fifth century, quotes him as "bishop and martyr" (I2; Documented). The Roman list keeps him an elder (E11).
3. **Damasus, and Novatian.** About 140 years after the exile, Damasus, bishop of Rome, set a verse on his tomb (I3; Documented). Quote 3. Say plainly before or after it that "Novatus" is Damasus's name for the split led by Novatian, which came in 251, a generation after Hippolytus. Damasus offers it as something he was told (Quote 3's last line). Historians since have judged that he confused an earlier, short-lived split with Novatian's church, which still existed in his day (I3; Duchesne; Mommsen calls it a grave error; credit "historians"). A Latin poet half a century after Damasus went further and had him torn apart by wild horses, like the Hippolytus of Greek legend; his editors call the story fable (I5; optional; keep in the poet's voice, never as an event).
4. **Easter tables.** His tables set out a sixteen-year cycle for finding Easter; Jerome says they gave Eusebius the idea for a nineteen-year cycle of his own (I4; Documented as Jerome's report). A historian notes the tables were already wrong by 237 (E10; optional). The opening part of Rome's own list of bishops, historians think, came from his chronicle (I4; Duchesne; optional).
5. **The statue.** In 1551 a marble figure seated in a chair was found on the Tiburtine Road, near the place of his tomb; carved on the chair are his Easter tables and a list of books (I6; Documented). It bears no name; his editors take it to be his, and one notes its head is modern (I6, D4). Optional, or use as ending (b).

**Connection to carry into the story:** what passed from Hippolytus was a record and a question. The record is the *Refutation*: written by Callistus's enemy, it is nearly all we know of Callistus and of Rome's quarrels in those years, and it reached us only in 1842, under another man's name (I1, D1, D6). The question is who belongs in the church: Callistus, by his enemy's account, held that the church, like Noah's ark, keeps its sinners; Hippolytus held that grave sinners must be put out (E7). The listener must understand three things. First, that every charge against Callistus comes from one hostile witness, so it is told as his. Second, that the same question split Rome again in 251, when Novatian's party refused to take back those who had denied the faith (Novatian's story, read); the story says this in one clause, and does not say that Novatian learned from Hippolytus, which no held source says. Third, that Rome itself later merged the two splits: Damasus's verse makes Hippolytus an elder of Novatian's split who, at his death, sent the people back to the catholic faith, and Damasus offers it only as what he was told (I3). Tell it through these acts: the writing of the book, the exile together, the verse on the tomb, the find in 1842. Not as a "this matters because" sentence.

**Who tells it:**

The quarrel, Callistus's past, Callistus's teaching and rules, and the motive "from apprehension of me" are all his, a hostile witness's. Say so once, inside a sentence about the book, in beat 3 or What came 1: nearly all we know of Callistus comes from his enemy (E5, D6). Credit "he says" or "he charged" at each charge.

- **The list of Rome's bishops and the calendar of martyrs**, in a Roman book of 354: the exile and the shared day (E11, E12). Credit "a list of Rome's bishops", "Rome's calendar of martyrs".
- **Damasus**, about 140 years on: the verse (I3). He frames it as hearsay; keep that.
