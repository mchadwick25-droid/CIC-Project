Hippolytus, an elder of the church at Rome, set down what, he said, his own bishop, Callistus, taught about Noah's ark. "He also said that Noah's ark was made as a sign of the church. In it were dogs and wolves and ravens, everything clean and unclean. So, he says, it should be with the church." Hippolytus wrote it to show how far, in his eyes, that bishop had fallen.

In the early two hundreds Hippolytus was a Greek-writing teacher, one of the elders who led the church at Rome with its bishop. In his Refutation of All Heresies he charged Callistus with forgiving sinners too easily and with holding that Father and Son were one and the same. In 235 he was exiled to Sardinia with Pontianus, the bishop of Rome. Whether he led a rival church is still disputed.

A teacher from Smyrna had taught that Christ was the Father himself, so that the Father was born and suffered. When the teaching reached Rome, Hippolytus answered from Christ's own words. "Christ did not say, 'I and the Father am one,' but 'are one.' The word 'are' is not said of one person. It speaks of two persons and one power."

Nearly all we know of Callistus comes from this enemy. Hippolytus says that Callistus, as adviser to the bishop before him, let the teaching spread and called Hippolytus's side "worshippers of two gods." He paints him as a former slave who made away with Christians' savings and was later sentenced to the mines of Sardinia, a sentence for convicts. Historians judge the story true at its base but greatly overdrawn.

Callistus became bishop in 217. Callistus, Hippolytus says, forgave every sin to anyone who came over to him and would not remove a bishop even for grave sin. He called Callistus's following a school, not a church, and accused him of teaching adultery and murder. Hippolytus spoke of himself as a successor of the apostles, sharing their "high-priesthood." Many historians think his followers made him a rival bishop at Rome; others place him at Portus, Rome's harbor town, and some doubt there was any split.

After Callistus died, the Refutation traced each teaching Hippolytus held false back to Greek philosophy, and ended with Callistus himself.

Then, in 235, a list of Rome's bishops records that Pontianus and "the elder Hippolytus," as it calls him, were sent to Sardinia, "the unhealthy island." He had told of Callistus's sentence there, and now he went himself, with Rome's bishop. How he died is not recorded, though historians think he probably died on the island. Rome kept both men on the same day, 13 August.

His Greek books soon dropped out of sight in a Rome that now wrote Latin. In 1842 the Refutation turned up in a manuscript from Mount Athos, and it was first printed under the name of Origen. Most scholars have since given it to Hippolytus, though some doubted it.

About 140 years after the exile, Damasus, bishop of Rome, set a verse on his tomb. "It is said that Hippolytus, an elder, while the tyrant's orders pressed hard, had stayed all along in the split of Novatus." Damasus means Novatian, whose party split Rome in 251 by refusing to take back those who had denied the faith. On his way to death, the verse says, Hippolytus told the people to follow the catholic faith, the faith of the one true church. Historians judge that Damasus confused two different splits, and Damasus himself gave it only as hearsay. "Damasus tells what he has heard. Christ tests it all."
