When daylight ended in Nero's gardens, people were burned to light the dark. Others, covered in animal skins, were torn apart by dogs or nailed to crosses, while Nero, dressed as a charioteer, mixed with the crowd. The Roman who tells it, the historian Tacitus, thought they were criminals.

Writing about fifty years after the great fire of Rome in 64, Tacitus gives the fullest account outside the church of what Nero did to the Christians. He despised them and said so. Yet his one chapter, which names their founder as executed under Pilate, became what later historians built on.

After the fire, Rome turned to its gods with prayers and offerings. But nothing stopped the belief that the fire had been set on an order. To kill the rumor, Nero put the guilt on people the crowd hated for their shameful acts and called Christians, and he had them tortured.

Tacitus stops to explain the name, and gives Christ in its Latin form: "Christus, from whom the name came, was put to death in the reign of Tiberius by one of our governors, Pontius Pilatus." A superstition, he calls it, and a deadly one. It began in Judaea, the Roman province around Jerusalem, and broke out even in Rome, where, he says, everything shameful in the world gathers.

First those who admitted it were seized, and then, on their word, a huge number more. They were convicted, he says, less of setting the city on fire than of hatred of the human race, though he never says what they admitted. Their deaths were made a show, as Nero held games at the chariot-racing ground.

Another Roman writer gives this punishment in a single line, without the fire. Almost everything else rests on this one chapter, and Tacitus never says where he learned of Christus.

About the year 200, Tertullian, defending the Christians, answered him by name, though over another history, about where the Jews came from. There Tacitus wrote that the Jews honored an ass's head, and people took it, Tertullian supposed, that the Christians did too. Yet "that same Cornelius Tacitus, who truly talks more lies than anyone," Tertullian noted, says a Roman general found no image in the temple at Jerusalem.

About 400, Sulpicius Severus, a Christian writing a history of the world, told the same deaths in the same order. But where Tacitus called them criminals, he called them innocent.

Later historians made the chapter their main outside record of these deaths, and of Christus put to death under Pilate. In an 1876 debate, a speaker arguing that the Bible was not divine said the passage was open to challenge, but the historians who weighed it treated it as Tacitus's own. In 1890, a translator of Eusebius's history of the early church reported that some historians thought the victims were Jews, not Christians, and he disagreed.

And so the story comes back to the gardens at night, where Tacitus ends with his own judgment: "So even for criminals who deserved to be punished harshly, as an example to all, a feeling of pity arose. For it seemed they were being destroyed not for the public good, but to feed one man's cruelty."
