# Outline: Tacitus

Every line cites `Source_Sheet.md` (S = setting, M = the chapter, I = impact, D = disagreement, N = not held). TAC 68 s1–s12 are the sentences of *Annals* 15.44, in the translation standardly identified as Church and Brodribb.

- **The essence:** Tacitus, a Roman historian writing about fifty years after the great fire of Rome in 64, gives the fullest account outside the church of what Nero did to the Christians. Rumoured to have ordered the fire, Nero put the guilt on them and had a huge number killed as a public show. To explain their name, Tacitus adds that their founder, Christus, was executed under the emperor Tiberius by Pontius Pilate (M2–M6: Documented as Tacitus's account; the year 64, S1: Documented; the date he wrote, S3: Dominant Modern Reconstruction). He despised them and said so, and his one chapter became what later historians built on (M3, M4, M7, D4: Documented; I1: Documented).
- **Also told:** What the chapter says, in its order: the rites that failed to stop the rumour, the blame, the arrests through informers, a verdict of "hatred against mankind" more than of arson, the deaths, and his closing judgement that pity arose because they died for one man's cruelty (M1–M7: Documented as his account). What it cannot tell: he does not say where he learned of Christus, the Library holds only this one chapter, and he wrote decades after the event (D6, S6, S3). What later readers did with it: Sulpicius Severus, a Christian chronicler about 400, told the same deaths in the same order but called the victims innocent (I2: Documented; that he drew on Tacitus is the editors' reading, Widely Accepted). Tertullian, about 200, called Tacitus one "who truly talks more lies than anyone" over another passage, on the Jews (I3: Documented). Modern historians weigh it: some held the victims were Jews, not Christians (D1: Contested), and in 1876 a public critic of the Bible doubted the passage (D2: Contested, one held witness).
- **Length:** about 2 minutes 45 (about 410 words). Reason: the whole record is one chapter of about 375 words and a thin life (S2, S6), but the job is the fire told in full, plus the readers who used and weighed it. Shorter would cut the deaths or the later readers; longer would stretch a few lines past what they say.
- **Setting:** the year 64 for the fire (S1); "about fifty years later, early in the next century" for the writing (S3), never a single year; Rome, Nero's gardens and the circus (S4, M6); Judaea as the place the movement came from, in Tacitus's words (M4). No tradition name: he stands outside the church (S5).

**Words to explain** (work the gloss into the sentence where the word is first heard; or use the plain word instead)
- **procurator** (Q1): say "governor": "one of our governors". Do not use "procurator" in narration (M4).
- **superstition** (M4, I2a): Tacitus's word of contempt for the Christians' religion (D4). If used, say so: "a superstition, he calls it, and a deadly one" ("deadly", from *exitiabilis*, OTTO 12189).
- **circus:** "the chariot-racing ground"; the modern circus misleads (M6).
- **charioteer:** known; keep "dressed as a charioteer" (M6).
- **Annals:** "his history of Rome, the Annals" (S3).
- **Histories** (beat 7): "another of his histories, where he tells where the Jews came from" (I3).
- **Judaea:** "Judaea, the Roman province around Jerusalem" (M4).
- **Tiberius** (Q1): "the emperor Tiberius".
- **Christus** (Q1): keep the Latin form in the quote; the narrator may add "Christ" once beside it (M4, Rolfe's note).
- **hatred against mankind** (M5): "hatred of the human race"; the plain sense, from later historians, is that the inquiry proved they were Christians, not that they set the fire (D3).
- exemplary (Q2): carried in the rendering as 'as an example to all', attached to the punishment.
- **Apology** (beat 7): "his defence of the Christians".
- **Chronicle** (beat 7): "a history of the world".
- heresy (beat 7): 'a teaching the church condemned'
- Church History (beat 7): 'Eusebius's history of the early church'
- Leave out entirely: Sibylline books, Vulcanus, Ceres, Proserpina, Juno (M1). Say "prayers and offerings to the gods".

- **Shape:** Outside witness. The trap is making him a friend or a foe, or stretching his lines past what they say. Say plainly that it is one chapter, that he wrote decades later and names no source, and that his contempt and his pity are both his.
- **Sibling:** `suetonius` (researched after this; live text read). Its job: two short lines in the lives of the emperors, Nero's punishment of the Christians among them; how little they say. This story's job: the fire told in full. Suetonius here is one clause at most: another Roman writer gives Nero's punishment of the Christians in a single line, without the fire (CEN; SCH 24348). Leave "Chrestus" and "Chrestians" to it (D5). `pliny-the-younger` (outside witness in this batch; live text read; batch 6 outline not yet written): no shared event or text. Do not name Pliny here.
- **Openings:** (a) Chosen. When daylight ended in Nero's gardens, people were burned to light the dark. Others, covered in the skins of animals, were torn apart by dogs, or nailed to crosses, while Nero, dressed as a charioteer, mixed with the crowd (M6). The Roman who tells it, the historian Tacitus, thought they were criminals (M7, M3). Then the essence. (b) A Roman historian, writing about a fire in Rome, stops to explain a name. Christus, he writes, was executed under Tiberius by one of Rome's governors, Pontius Pilate (M4). Use (a); (b) gives away Q1 too early.

**Story beats** (beat 1 is the first minute)
1. **The opening and the essence.** After the night scene: Tacitus, writing about fifty years after the great fire of 64, gives the fullest account outside the church of Nero's punishment of the Christians, and in it names Christus, executed under Pilate (S1, S3, M3–M6). He despised them (M3, M7). Documented as his account; the date he wrote, Dominant Modern Reconstruction.
2. **After the fire, the rumour.** Rome turned to the gods, with prayers, offerings, and vigils held at night by married women. But nothing, not the emperor's gifts nor the offerings, stopped the belief that the fire had been set on an order (M1, M2). Tacitus reports a belief; he does not say Nero gave the order. Documented as his account.
3. **The blame, and the name.** To kill the rumour, Nero put the guilt on people the crowd hated for their shameful acts and called Christians, and had them tortured (M3). Tacitus stops to explain the name: Q1 (M4). He adds that this "superstition", held back for a moment, broke out again, in Judaea where it began and in Rome itself, where, he says, everything shameful from all the world gathers (M4). Documented as his account.
4. **Arrests and verdict.** First those who admitted it were seized; then, on what they told, a huge number more. They were convicted, he says, less of setting the city on fire than of hatred of the human race (narrator's plain words, not quoted) (M5). He does not say what they admitted (M5). Documented as his account.
5. **The deaths as a show.** The opening already showed these deaths. Here the story reaches that night: say only that mockery was added, that Nero lent his gardens and held games at the racing ground, standing in a chariot (M6). Do not list the dogs, crosses and burning again. Documented as his account.
6. **The writer.** Tacitus wrote this in his history of Rome. He writes as a Roman insider: Pilate is "one of our governors" (S2). Almost all that is known of these deaths rests on this one chapter, and he does not say where he learned of Christus (S6, D6). Say this once, here. His words are hostile throughout: people hated for their shameful acts; a deadly superstition, as he calls it (narrator's plain words, not quoted) (M3, M4, D4).

**What came of him** (about a third of the story; item 1 is pointed to in beat 1 and told in beat 7)
7. **The readers.** In time order, each credited:
   - About 200, Tertullian, defending the Christians, answered Tacitus by name, over another of his histories, where he tells where the Jews came from. Tacitus had written that the Jews honoured the head of an ass, and, Tertullian supposed, people took it that the Christians, close to the Jews, did too. Tertullian caught him out: the same Tacitus says a Roman general who took Jerusalem went into the temple at Jerusalem and found no image there. Q3 (I3). Documented. It is about the Jews' story, not the fire.
   - About 400, Sulpicius Severus, a Christian writing a history of the world, told the fire, the blame and the same deaths, dogs, crosses and night lights, in the same order. Where Tacitus called them criminals, he called them innocent (I2). He later used Tacitus's phrase for the Christians, deadly superstition (narrator's plain words, not quoted), for a heresy of his own day, his translator notes (I2a: editor's observation).
   - Later historians built on it: a translator of Eusebius's *Church History*, writing in 1890, says Tacitus tells the persecution most fully (narrator's plain words, not quoted); he reports that some historians thought the victims were the Jews as a whole, and he disagrees (I1, D1). Historians of a century ago read the verdict this way: the inquiry proved they were Christians, not that they set the fire (D3: Dominant Modern Reconstruction). In a public debate in 1876, a speaker arguing that the Bible was not divine said the quotation from Tacitus was open to challenge (D2). The historians who weighed it all treated it as Tacitus's own (D2).

**What came of him, ranked**
1. Heaviest: **the outside record historians use.** The chapter is the held moderns' main evidence for Nero's punishment of the Christians at Rome and an outside notice of Christus under Pilate (I1). Told in beat 7, third bullet.
2. **A Christian chronicle carried the scene on, and reversed its verdict** (I2, I2a). Beat 7, second bullet.
3. **A Christian answered him by name** (I3). Beat 7, first bullet.
Nothing defended: he is an outside witness. No line on who keeps him today.
End on the ending below, after item 1 has landed.

**Quotes**
- **Q1.** TAC 68 s7: "Christus, from whom the name had its origin, suffered the extreme penalty during the reign of Tiberius at the hands of one of our procurators, Pontius Pilatus". Rendered from the English of Church and Brodribb (standardly identified); the Latin of this sentence is not held (N1). Rendering: "Christus, from whom the name came, was put to death in the reign of Tiberius by one of our governors, Pontius Pilatus." Job: the outside line naming Christus and Pilate, in the voice of a Roman ("our"), which the narrator cannot carry. Beat 3.
- **Q2.** TAC 68 s12: "Hence, even for criminals who deserved extreme and exemplary punishment, there arose a feeling of compassion; for it was not, as it seemed, for the public good, but to glut one man's cruelty, that they were being destroyed." Rendered from the English (Latin not held, N1). Rendering: "So even for criminals who deserved to be punished harshly, as an example to all, a feeling of pity arose. For it seemed they were being destroyed not for the public good, but to feed one man's cruelty." Job: contempt and condemnation of Nero in one sentence, his own. The ending.
- **Q3.** TERT-L 17915–17916, Tertullian, *Apology* 16: "At enim idem Cornelius Tacitus, sane ille mendaciorum loquacissimus". Rendered from Oehler's Latin, checked against the same words in *To the Nations* 1.11 (TERT-C 4918–4919) and the ANF English ("the very opposite of tacit in telling lies", TERT-E 2583; "most loquacious in falsehood", TERT-E 10094). Rendering: "that same Cornelius Tacitus, who truly talks more lies than anyone". Job: a Christian's answer to him by name. Beat 7. The narrator gives the rest of Tertullian's point in plain words (Pompey found no image).

**Connection to carry into the story:** One hostile chapter passed to the church, and to everyone after, the fullest outside record of how Christians died at Rome under Nero, and a Roman's line that Christus was executed under Pilate (M4–M6, I1). The listener must be told three things for this to make sense: that Tacitus was not a Christian and despised them (M3, M7, D4); that this one chapter is nearly all the outside record of these deaths (S6, I1; Suetonius has a line); and that Christians and historians then took it up and changed or tested it: Sulpicius kept the scene and called the dead innocent (I2), Tertullian called its author a liar on another matter (I3), and moderns argue over who the victims were and what they were convicted of (D1, D3). Carry it through these acts, never as a "this matters because" sentence.

**Ending:** (a) Chosen: the opening image seen again, closed by Tacitus's own last sentence, Q2: the night in the gardens, and pity arising even for those he called criminals, because they died to feed one man's cruelty (M6, M7). (b) Possible: Tacitus's silence on his source: he never says how a Roman historian came to know the name of a man executed by a governor decades before (D6). Use (a).

**Who tells it**
- Tacitus, *Annals* 15.44, about fifty years after the fire: the whole event (M1–M7). He is hostile, writes as a Roman insider, and names no source. Say once, in beat 6, that almost all that is known of these deaths rests on this one chapter.
- Tertullian, about 200, a Christian: credited beside Q3 only.
- Sulpicius Severus, about 400, a Christian chronicler: credited for "innocent" and the retelling; the borrowing of the phrase is credited to "his translator" (I2a).
- The 1890 translator of Eusebius: the "so fully" and the Gibbon–Schiller dispute (I1, D1).
- The 1876 debate: credited to "a speaker" (D2).
- No doubt here is itself a scene; the disputes are one clause each, credited.

**Do not say**
- Not "the Christians set the fire" or "they were convicted of arson". Say Nero put the guilt on them, and they were convicted less of the fire than of "hatred against mankind" (M3, M5).
- Not 'convicted not of arson but of hatred'. Keep 'less of setting the city on fire than of hatred of the human race' (M5).
- Not what the 'abominations' were (no eating of flesh, incest or the like). Tacitus does not say (M3).
- Not "Nero started the fire" in the narrator's voice. Say people believed the fire had been set on an order, and nothing stopped the belief (M2).
- Not 'Tacitus felt sorry for the Christians' or 'came to respect them'. He reports that pity arose, and names no one who felt it; he still calls them criminals, and gives Nero's cruelty as the reason (M7, Q2).
- Not "historians still rely on it today": the held historians run to 1922; say "later historians built on it" (I1).
- Not "the first", "the earliest" or "the only" outside mention of Jesus or Christians. Say "the fullest account outside the church" of these deaths (I1; Josephus and Suetonius are on the tree).
- Not "Tacitus proves Jesus lived" or anything beyond Q1. He says Christus was executed under Tiberius by Pilate; nothing more (M4).
- Not "the governor of Judaea" as Tacitus's words. His words are "one of our procurators"; Judaea comes in the next clause, as the place the superstition began (M4).
- Not "senator", "consul" or any office in the narrator's voice; the only held word is Montaigne's report of "a certaine honourable magistracy" and Whiston's "eminent pleader" (S2). "A Roman insider", from his own "our", is enough.
- Not "in 116" or "in 110" as settled. Say "about fifty years later" (S3).
- Not "burned as torches", "covered in pitch" or "in the Vatican". Tacitus says they were "doomed to the flames and burnt, to serve as a nightly illumination", in Nero's gardens (M6). Pitch and the Vatican are a modern historian's (DUC 2362–2372).
- Not "hundreds" or "thousands". Say "a huge number" (Latin "multitudo ingens", M5).
- Not "they confessed to the fire" or "they confessed to being Christians". Say "those who admitted it" (M5).
- Never drop the dogs, the crosses or the burning, or the informers whose word convicted the rest (M5, M6).
- Not that Tertullian answered this chapter, or called the fire account lies. His words are about Tacitus's account of the Jews and the ass's head, in another work (I3).
- Not that Tertullian's "Consult your records" points to Tacitus. He names no writer (I3a); it is left out.
- Not "Sulpicius copied Tacitus word for word". Say he told the same deaths in the same order (I2).
- Not that Tacitus mentions laws or edicts against Christians, or Peter and Paul. Those are Sulpicius's additions (I2).
- Not that the victims were Jews, or were certainly Christians, in the narrator's voice. Credit both views (D1).
- Not "Bradlaugh proved it a forgery". He said it was "liable to impeachment", in a debate (D2).
- Not that the passage's genuineness is widely doubted. One debater in 1876 doubted it; the historians who used it treated it as his (D2).
- Not "Chrestians" or anything about the spelling of the name (D5); that belongs to the card and the Suetonius story.
- Not that Tacitus learned of Christus from Roman records, from Josephus, or from Christians (D6).
- Any word in "Words to explain" used without its plain sense the first time it is heard, "procurator", "circus" and "superstition" among them.
- Not "hatred of the human race" left unexplained, or its plain sense held back to the later readers. Where it is first heard, give the held historians' reading, credited to them: the inquiry proved they were Christians, not that they set the fire (D3).
- Not "the main" or "the chief" outside record of Christus or of Jesus. The chapter is the main outside record of these deaths and an outside notice of Christus under Pilate (I1).

**Leave out:** the gods and the Sibylline books by name (M1): a list of names that adds nothing. Nero at Antium (only in Sulpicius and the unheld 15.39, N3). Peter and Paul (Sulpicius, Duchesne; not in Tacitus). 1 Clement's "great multitude of the elect" and the Danaids and Dirces: an inside witness tied to the fire only by modern historians (sheet §7). Tertullian's *Apology* 5 on Nero as first persecutor ("Consult your records"): it names no writer, and told beside Tacitus it invites a false link (I3a). The "Chrestianos" reading (D5): the card, and Suetonius's job. Whiston on Josephus as a source (D6): one man's guess, about the Jews. The Shaw–Jones debate (D7): not held. Montaigne's and Rolfe's judgements of his style (I4): not about the Christians. Pliny: no held line ties the two men (N2).
