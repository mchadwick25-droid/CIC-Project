# Batch 6 hearts

The heart of each story, as its researcher set it in `Outline.md`, in plain words. Confidence uses the project's five levels. Shown to Mark on one page, 2026-11-19.

## Roman and Jewish witnesses (shape: Outside witness)

**Pliny the Younger** (about 2:50). Around 112, Pliny, governing Bithynia on the Black Sea, tried people accused of being Christians and had those who would not deny it led away to be punished (Documented, his own letter; that they died is the later writers' reading, Widely Accepted). Unsure of the law, he wrote to the emperor Trajan. His letter and Trajan's reply are a Roman judge's own record of the church on trial (Documented).
Also told: how he tested them; what people who had left the faith told him of the worship before dawn, and the torture of two slave women (Documented, second-hand); his claim that the temples were filling again (his claim); Trajan's rule, not to be hunted out but punished if accused (Documented); Tertullian's answer a century later (Documented); whether the rule protected Christians or condemned them (Contested).

**Tacitus** (about 2:45). Writing about fifty years after the fire of 64, the historian Tacitus gives the fullest account outside the church of the Christians punished at Rome (Documented as his account; the date, Dominant Modern Reconstruction). Nero, rumored to have started the fire, put the blame on the Christians and had a great number killed as a public show. To explain their name, Tacitus says Christus was executed under Tiberius by Pilate (Documented). He despised them.
Also told: the chapter in its order, from the failed rites to his closing judgment (Documented); what it cannot tell, with no source given for Christus; a later Christian writer retelling the same deaths as innocent (Documented); Tertullian calling him a liar (Documented); whether the victims were Christians, and a doubt raised in 1876 (Contested).

**Suetonius** (about 1:50). Around 120, Suetonius, writing the lives of Rome's rulers, gives the Christians two lines. Under Claudius the Jews were driven from Rome for rioting "at the urging of Chrestus"; under Nero the Christians were punished (Documented). They are among the very few outside notices of the first Christians at Rome. Who "Chrestus" was is Contested.
Also told: each line sits in a list of an emperor's measures, and he counts Nero's punishment among his good acts, never tying it to the fire (Documented); Acts tells of an expulsion too (that it is the same, Widely Accepted); Christian writers complained that Romans got the name wrong (Documented).

**Josephus** (about 3:30). Josephus, a priest from Jerusalem, commanded in Galilee against Rome, gave himself up in 67, and wrote his people's history at Rome (Documented, almost all from his own books). His Antiquities gives a few lines each on John the Baptist, on James "the brother of Jesus, who was called Christ", and on Jesus. The lines on John and James are Widely Accepted as his; the passage on Jesus is Contested.
Also told: the cave at Jotapata and the lots, and both sides calling him a traitor (his own account); what the three passages say (Documented); Origen saying he did not accept Jesus as the Christ (Documented); Eusebius first known to quote the passage on Jesus (Documented); the views on it, each credited (Contested).

## Asia, the apostles' line

**Ignatius of Antioch** (about 3:00, Martyr). Ignatius, bishop of Antioch in Syria, was taken under guard to Rome to die by the beasts (Documented, his own words). On the road he wrote seven letters, which are almost all we know of him (Widely Accepted). Polycarp gathered them and sent them on (Documented).
Also told: keep to your bishop, with one altar and one bread (Documented); Christ truly suffered, or his own chains mean nothing (Documented); do not save me, his plea to Rome; his death there is a report (Widely Accepted; how and when are unknown); later writers quoted him, and the quarrel over which letters are his (Widely Accepted that seven are).

**Irenaeus of Lyon** (about 3:20). As a boy in Asia, Irenaeus heard Polycarp (Documented, his own words). After the martyrs of 177 he became bishop of Lyon in Gaul (Documented, Eusebius). There he wrote Against Heresies, the fullest answer we still have to the followers of Valentinus (Documented; "about 180", Widely Accepted).
Also told: his three answers to their claim of a secret, higher teaching: the one faith every church received at baptism, the churches the apostles founded with their bishops counted in order, and four Gospels, no more and no fewer (Documented, his argument); later writers drew on him (Documented); for centuries he was the main witness to the Valentinians, and an opponent (Widely Accepted); his list of Rome's bishops was later argued over (Contested).

**Polycrates of Ephesus** (about 2:45, Leader). Near the end of the second century, Polycrates, bishop of Ephesus, led the bishops of Asia in keeping Easter on the fourteenth day of the moon, the day of the Jewish Passover, while the other churches kept it on Sunday (Documented). When Victor of Rome pressed for one custom, Polycrates wrote naming the apostles and martyrs buried in Asia who had kept that day. Victor tried to cut Asia off from communion (Documented; whether the break held is Contested).
Also told: each custom claimed from the apostles (Documented); his letter's graves, his seven kinsmen who were bishops, and his refusal (Documented, his own words); bishops who objected to Victor, Irenaeus among them (Documented); how the letter outlived the custom, and Nicaea set one day for Easter (Documented).

## Teachers at Rome and Edessa (shape: Teacher or thinker)

**Hermas** (about 2:45). Hermas was a Christian at Rome in the second century. His book opens by saying the man who raised him sold him to a woman named Rhoda in Rome (Documented as the book's words; that he was later freed is an editor's guess, Inferential-Thin). In his visions the Church, and then an angel dressed as a shepherd, call Rome's Christians to repent while there is time, offering a baptized sinner one more repentance, and only one (Documented as the book's teaching; the visions rest on his word alone). Some read the Shepherd as Scripture and others shut it out (Documented).
Also told: the tower, and the stones set aside that can still be built in (Documented); Irenaeus quoting it as Scripture (Documented); Tertullian's scorn (Documented); the old list that said read it, but not in church (Documented).

**Valentinus** (about 2:40). Valentinus taught at Rome in the middle of the second century (Documented as Irenaeus's report). He founded a school that taught a hidden Father and heavenly powers coming forth in pairs, with the world made by a lesser maker (Documented as his opponents' account; how much is his own, Contested). Almost all we know comes from those opponents, and his own words survive only in their quotations (Documented).
Also told: Tertullian alone tells that he hoped to become a bishop and broke away when another man was chosen (his claim Documented; the event Contested); his own surviving lines, such as the heart as an inn (Documented); his school's two branches, and the writers against it (Documented).

**Tatian** (about 3:00). Tatian, born "in the land of the Assyrians" and trained in Greek learning, became a Christian at Rome and heard Justin (Documented, his own words). In his Address to the Greeks he said all their arts were borrowed from foreigners and that Moses was older than Homer (Documented). His opponent Irenaeus says he broke with the church after Justin's death (Documented as his account). He went east (Widely Accepted, on one late source). His Diatessaron, the four Gospels woven into one, was for about two centuries the gospel most Syriac-speaking churches heard read (Widely Accepted).
Also told: the Address's argument, and his charge that a philosopher tried to have Justin and him killed (his charge); Irenaeus's charges against him (Documented, hostile); later bishops removing the Diatessaron from the churches in the 400s (Documented).

**Bardaisan of Edessa** (about 3:00). Bardaisan was a Christian teacher at Edessa around 200 who taught that people are free and not ruled by the stars (Documented). The only text in his voice is a conversation a pupil wrote down, the Book of the Laws of Countries (Widely Accepted). In it nature rules the body and fate rules fortune, but conduct is free: every nation keeps its own laws under the same stars, and Christians keep one law in every land (Documented).
Also told: the dialogue's images, and his admission that he once followed the astrologers (Documented); Eusebius on his pupils and his writing against Marcion's followers (Documented, Eusebius's report); later writers called him a heretic, and disagree on why (Contested); his followers' songs, which Ephrem answered in song, as a clause (Documented as reported).

**Hippolytus of Rome** (about 3:20). Hippolytus was a Greek-writing teacher and elder of the church at Rome around 200 (Documented). After 222 he wrote the Refutation of All Heresies, attacking the bishop Callistus as too quick to forgive and as teaching that Father and Son were one and the same (Documented as his charge). In 235 he was exiled to Sardinia with the bishop Pontianus (Documented; that this is him, Widely Accepted). Whether he led a rival church is disputed (Contested).
Also told: his answer, "two persons, one power" (Documented); his harsher charges against Callistus (his charge); the views on a rival church, each credited (Contested); the book found in 1842 under Origen's name, and the argument over who wrote it (Contested); the feast he shares with Pontianus (Documented).
