In his life of the emperor Claudius, the Roman scholar Suetonius lists what Claudius did. He freed the people of old Troy from tax, and he seated German envoys with the senators at the theater. Between the two comes one sentence: "He drove from Rome the Jews who were constantly rioting at the urging of Chrestus."

Suetonius wrote his lives of twelve of Rome's rulers around 120, decades after Claudius, and the Christians get this line and one other, under Nero. Yet these are among the very few notices of the first Christians at Rome from outside the church, and readers still argue over who Chrestus was.

He was a close friend of Pliny the Younger, and for a time secretary to the emperor Hadrian.

The Claudius line gives no year and no source. In the New Testament, the Acts of the Apostles tells of a Jew named Aquila and his wife, whom the apostle Paul met at Corinth. They had come from Italy "because Claudius had commanded all the Jews to depart from Rome," and historians read the two as one event. But Suetonius never says who Chrestus was. It was a real name, close in sound to Christus, the Latin for Christ. Editors read it as Christ, placed in the wrong city and reign, though one allows that a Jew of that name is meant.

The Nero line sits in another list, between rules for food shops and a ban on chariot drivers' pranks. It reads: "Punishments were inflicted on the Christians, a kind of people given to a new and harmful superstition." Superstition was his word of contempt for their religion. He names no charge and no kind of punishment, and he counts this among Nero's acts that were "beyond criticism." Elsewhere he blames Nero for the great fire, but never ties it to the Christians. Another Roman, Tacitus, tells in full of Christians put to death under Nero.

Historians still weigh both lines, though his English editors noted "how little" even this well-informed Roman knew. Christian writers, meanwhile, complained that Romans said their name wrong. Around 200, Tertullian, defending the Christians, put it to the Romans plainly. "And even when you wrongly say 'Chrestianus', since you do not even know the name for certain, it is formed from sweetness or kindness." The Greek chrestos means kind, or good. Tertullian never names Suetonius; editors set the two side by side.

Seven Latin words, between old Troy's tax and the Germans' theater seats, from a man not writing about Christians at all, still read word by word for one name.
