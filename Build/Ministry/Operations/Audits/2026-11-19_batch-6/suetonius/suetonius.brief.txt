In his life of the emperor Claudius, the Roman scholar Suetonius lists what Claudius did. He freed the people of old Troy, honored as Rome's founders, from tax, and he seated German envoys with the senators at the theater. Between the two comes one sentence: "He drove from Rome the Jews who were constantly rioting at the urging of Chrestus."

Suetonius wrote his lives of twelve of Rome's rulers around 120, decades after Claudius, and the Christians get this line and one other, under Nero. Yet these are among the very few notices of the first Christians at Rome from outside the church, and readers still argue over who Chrestus was.

He was a close friend of Pliny the Younger, and for a time secretary to the emperor Hadrian, until Hadrian dismissed him.

His Claudius line gives no year and no source. The Acts of the Apostles, in the New Testament, tells of a Jew named Aquila and his wife, whom the apostle Paul met at Corinth. They had come from Italy "because Claudius had commanded all the Jews to depart from Rome," and historians read the two as one event. Suetonius never says who Chrestus was. The name was in use among Greeks and Romans, close in sound to Christus, the Latin for Christ. Modern editors read it as Christ, placed in the wrong city and reign, though one allows that a Jew of that name is meant.

The Nero line sits in another list, between rules for food shops and a ban on chariot drivers' pranks: "Punishments were inflicted on the Christians, a kind of people given to a new and harmful superstition." Superstition was his word of contempt for their religion. He names no charge and no kind of punishment, and he counts this among Nero's acts that were "beyond criticism." He blames Nero for the great fire elsewhere, but never ties it to the Christians, whose deaths another Roman, Tacitus, tells in full.

Historians still weigh both lines, and his English editors noted "how little" even this well-informed Roman knew. Christians, for their part, knew that Romans garbled their name. Around 200, Tertullian, defending them, told the Romans: "And even when you wrongly say 'Chrestianus', since you do not even know the name for certain, it is formed from sweetness or kindness." Chrestian was the name said wrong, and the Greek chrestos means kind, or good. Tertullian never names Suetonius; editors set the two side by side.

Seven Latin words, between old Troy's tax and the Germans' theater seats, from a man who was not writing about Christians at all, and still read word by word for one name.
