In his life of the emperor Claudius, the Roman scholar Suetonius lists what Claudius did. He freed the people of old Troy from tax, and he seated German envoys with the senators at the theater. Between the two comes one sentence: "He drove from Rome the Jews who were constantly rioting at the urging of Chrestus."

Suetonius wrote his lives of twelve of Rome's rulers around 120, decades later, and the Christians get this line and one other, under Nero. Yet these are among the very few notices of the first Christians at Rome from outside the church, and readers still argue over who Chrestus was.

He was a close friend of Pliny the Younger, and for a time secretary to the emperor Hadrian.

The Claudius line gives no year and no source. The Acts of the Apostles, in the New Testament, tells of a Jew named Aquila and his wife, whom the apostle Paul met at Corinth, come from Italy "because Claudius had commanded all the Jews to depart from Rome." Historians read the two as one event. But Suetonius never says who Chrestus was. It was a real name, close in sound to Christus, the Latin for Christ. Editors read it as Christ, placed in the wrong city and reign, though one allows that a Jew of that name is meant.

The Nero line sits in another list, between rules for food shops and a ban on chariot drivers' pranks: "Punishments were inflicted on the Christians, a kind of people given to a new and harmful superstition." Superstition was his word of contempt for their religion. He names no charge and no kind of punishment, and he counts this among Nero's acts that were "beyond criticism." He blames Nero for the great fire elsewhere, but never ties it to the Christians, whose deaths another Roman, Tacitus, tells in full.

Historians still weigh both lines, and his English editors noted "how little" even this well-informed Roman knew. Christians knew the Romans garbled their name. Around 200, Tertullian, defending them, put it to the Romans. "And even when you wrongly say 'Chrestianus', since you do not even know the name for certain, it is formed from sweetness or kindness." Chrestian was the name said wrong, and the Greek chrestos means kind, or good. Tertullian never names Suetonius; editors set the two side by side.

Seven Latin words, between old Troy's tax and the Germans' theater seats, from a man not writing about Christians at all, still read word by word for one name.
