# Outline: Suetonius

Every line cites `Source_Sheet.md` (S = setting, E = the two lines, I = impact, D = disagreement). ROL is Rolfe's Latin and English (1914), THF the Thomson–Forester English (1909).

- **The essence:** Suetonius, a Roman scholar who wrote the lives of twelve of Rome's rulers, from Julius Caesar to Domitian, around 120, gives the Christians two short lines and no more. Under Claudius, he writes, the Jews were driven from Rome for rioting at the urging of "Chrestus"; under Nero, the Christians were punished as people of a new and harmful superstition (E1, E3: Documented as his words; the date of the book, S4: Dominant Modern Reconstruction). He is on the tree because these are among the very few outside notices of the first Christians at Rome, and readers still argue over who "Chrestus" was (I1, D1: Documented that they are read so; who Chrestus was, Contested).
- **Also told:** How little the lines say: each sits in a list of an emperor's measures, one between a tax relief for the people of old Troy and seats at the theatre for German envoys, the other between rules for food shops and a ban on chariot drivers' pranks (E2, E3: Documented). He counts the punishment of the Christians among Nero's acts that were beyond blame or even worthy of praise (E5: Documented). He blames Nero for the great fire in another chapter, but never ties the fire to the Christians (E6, D4: Documented). The Claudius line: the Acts of the Apostles tells the same expulsion, through a Jew named Aquila and his wife, whom Paul met at Corinth (I2; Acts 18:1, NT-E 88520: Documented as Acts; the same event, Widely Accepted); a later Roman historian, Dio, says Claudius did not expel the Jews but only banned their meetings (D2: Contested). Chrestus: modern editors read it as Christ, misnamed, with the dates confused; one allows a Jew of that name (D1: Contested). What came of the lines: Christian writers complained that Romans said the name wrong, "Chrestianus", and editors set that beside this line (I3: Documented as their words; the tie, Widely Accepted as the editors'); a German source book of 1911 opens with the Claudius line, and historians print both lines among their testimonies, reading the first beside Acts (I1, I2). Jerome, near the end of the fourth century, modelled his book of Christian writers on Suetonius's lives of Roman men of letters (I4: Documented).
- **Length:** about 2 minutes 20 (about 350 words); cut Jerome, then Dio, first if it runs long. Reason: the record is two lines of sixteen Latin words, and the life is thin (S1, S2). The job is how little they say and why they are still read; the scenes are the lists the lines sit in, and the arguments over Chrestus. Longer would stretch the lines past what they say.
- **Setting:**
  - Time: Claudius ruled 41 to 54 and Nero 54 to 68 (S5). Suetonius gives no year. A later Christian chronicle, Bede's (to 725), following Orosius, sets the expulsion in Claudius's ninth year and ties it to Acts (Bede 23216–23217). The date is Bede's and Orosius's, not Suetonius's or Acts'. The book was published about 120 (S4), "decades later". Plain words: "in the reign of Claudius", "under Nero", "around 120".
  - Place: Rome (S6).
  - Tradition: none; he stood outside the church (S7).

**Words to explain** (the first time each is heard; plain word or short gloss worked into the sentence)
- **Chrestus** (Q1): keep the name as he wrote it. Gloss once, in beat 3: a name in use among Greeks and Romans, close in sound to Christus, the Latin for Christ (THF 20380–20382; D1).
- **Chrestianus** (Q3): "Chrestian", the name said wrong. The narrator gives the plain sense beside it: the Greek word *chrestos* means kind, or good (LAC-E 3165 note; TERT-E 9208 note).
- **superstition** (Q2): superstition, his word of contempt for their religion: a new and harmful one, he says.
- **Ilium:** "old Troy", whose people Rome honoured as its founders (E2).
- **envoys:** known; keep.
- **Lives of the Caesars:** "his lives of the emperors". Do not say "the Caesars" unexplained.
- **secretary** (to Hadrian): known; keep.
- **Acts:** "the Acts of the Apostles, in the New Testament".
- **Dio:** "a later Roman historian, Dio". No further gloss.
- **Apology** (Tertullian): "his defence of the Christians".
- **Lives of Illustrious Men** (Jerome): "a book of short lives of Christian writers".

- **Shape:** Outside witness. Trap: stretching two lines past what they say, or making him a foe. He is brief and he is contemptuous; tell both. Say plainly how short the lines are, that he wrote decades later and names no source, and that "Chrestus" is disputed.
- **Sibling:** `tacitus` (batch 6; outline read). Its job: Nero's fire told in full, with the deaths and Christus under Pilate. Here Nero is one clause, and the Claudius line leads. Name Tacitus once, as another Roman who tells the deaths in full; tell none of his deaths or his words, and do not use his quotes or "mischievous superstition" (his live text). `pliny-the-younger` (batch 6; outline read): its story leaves Suetonius out. A held source does give the tie: Rolfe and the 1909 preface say Pliny's letters show Suetonius as his close friend, and are most of what is known of his life (S1, S3). Here it is one clause.
- **Openings:**
  1. **Chosen.** The line in its list (E1, E2). In about three sentences: in his life of the emperor Claudius, a Roman writer lists what Claudius did. He freed the people of old Troy from tax, and let German envoys sit with the senators at the theatre. Between the two comes one sentence: Q1. Then the name and the essence.
  2. Not chosen: the Nero line in its list, between the food shops and the chariot drivers (E3). Strong, but the Claudius line leads by the job, and this image is kept for beat 4.

**Story beats** (the opening, the essence and beat 1 are the first minute)

1. **The opening and the essence.** After Q1: the writer was Suetonius, around 120. In his lives of the emperors the Christians get this line and one other, under Nero (E1, E3, S4). Documented as his words; the date, Dominant Modern Reconstruction.
2. **Who he was.** A scholar at Rome, whose father was an army officer (his birthplace is unknown, ROL 164–166); a close friend of Pliny the Younger, whose letters are most of what is known of him. He worked for a time as the emperor Hadrian's secretary, until Hadrian dismissed him, about 121 (S1–S3: Widely Accepted, modern editors from letters not held). He wrote of emperors' private lives and acts more than of great events (S4, Rolfe). Keep to one or two sentences.
3. **The Claudius line.** He gives no year, no number and no source (E1). The Acts of the Apostles also tells of Claudius driving out the Jews: the apostle Paul met a Jew named Aquila and his wife at Corinth, who had come from Italy "because Claudius had commanded all the Jews to depart from Rome" (I2; Acts 18:1, NT-E 88520, for Corinth; phrase from NT-E 88523, paraphrase the rest). That it is the same event is the historians' reading (Widely Accepted), not a thing either text says. A later Roman historian, Dio, says Claudius did not drive them out, only banned their meetings (D2: Contested). Then Chrestus: Suetonius does not say who he was. Modern editors read the name as Christ, written as a name in use among Greeks and Romans, close in sound to Christus, the Latin for Christ, and think Suetonius moved him to Rome and to the wrong reign; one editor allows a Jew of that name; a German historian of 1922 holds that the quarrels came as Christianity reached Rome's Jews, and that Rome did not tell Jews and Christians apart (D1: Contested, every view an editor's).
4. **The Nero line.** Q2, told in its list: a limit on spending, public dinners cut to handouts of food, cooked food in taverns banned except beans and greens; then the Christians; then a ban on the chariot drivers who roamed the streets cheating people for sport (E3: Documented). He gives no number, no charge and no kind of death. He sets these among Nero's acts that were "beyond criticism", some even worthy of praise, before he turns to the crimes (E5: Documented; tell in paraphrase). He blames Nero for the great fire in another chapter, but there names no Christians (E6: Documented). Another Roman, Tacitus, tells at length of Christians put to death under Nero after the fire. Historians read the two side by side; Suetonius himself joins nothing to the fire (sibling).

**What came of him** (about a third of the story; heaviest first)

1. **The historians' evidence.** Modern historians gather the two lines among the very few outside notices of the first Christians, and read the Claudius line beside Acts: a German source book on the history of the papacy, in 1911, sets it first of all its documents (I1, I2: Documented). Its 1909 English editors note "how little" even this well-informed Roman knew (I1; THF 19535–19537; a short phrase, credited to the editors). Say "historians still weigh", not "still rely on", and keep it to what the held historians do.
2. **The Christians and the name.** About 200, Tertullian, defending the Christians, told the Romans they said the name wrong: Q3 (I3). A century later Lactantius wrote that the ignorant, "by the change of a letter", call Christ "Chrestus" (I3; LAC-E 3163, a short phrase). Neither names Suetonius; it is editors who set these beside his line (I3). Defended: the Christian name.
3. **Optional, one line.** Near the end of the fourth century, Jerome wrote short lives of Christian writers. Asked to 'follow the example of Tranquillus', another of Suetonius's names, he did for Christian writers what Suetonius had done for Rome's men of letters (JER-E 38773–38779; I4: Documented). It is his lost book on famous writers that Jerome followed, not the emperors' lives.

No line on who keeps him today. End on the ending below, after What came 1 and 2 have landed.

**Quotes** (3)

1. Latin (ROL 25843–25844): "Iudaeos impulsore Chresto assidue tumultuantis Roma expulit." (Rolfe's English, ROL 25907–25909; THF 10639–10640.) Rendering (from the Latin): "He drove from Rome the Jews who were constantly rioting at the urging of Chrestus." Job: the whole of the Claudius line, which the story opens on and turns on. The narrator then gives the plain sense of the name (Words to explain).
2. Latin (ROL 28458–28460; MIR 2570, as 'adflicti'): "afflicti suppliciis Christiani, genus hominum superstitionis novae ac maleficae". (Rolfe's English, ROL 28523–28525; THF 11612–11614.) Rendering (from the Latin): "Punishments were inflicted on the Christians, a kind of people given to a new and harmful superstition." Job: the whole of the Nero line, his contempt in his own words. Beat 4.
3. Latin (TERT-L 12453–12455, Tertullian, *Apology* 3): "Sed et cum perperam Chrestianus pronuntiatur a vobis (nam nec nominis certa est notitia penes vos), de suavitate vel benignitate conpositum est." (ANF English, TERT-E 1713–1716.) Rendering (from Oehler's Latin): "And even when you wrongly say 'Chrestianus', since you do not even know the name for certain, it is formed from sweetness or kindness." Job: a Christian of the next century on the very confusion the Claudius line shows. What came 2. The narrator gives the plain sense beside it: the Greek *chrestos* means kind, or good.

**Connection to carry into the story:** What passed from Suetonius was two lines, and a puzzle in a name. Historians took the lines into their collections of the few outside notices of the first Christians, and read the Claudius line beside Acts, where a Jew named Aquila and his wife, driven from Rome by Claudius, meet Paul at Corinth (I1, I2). The listener must be told three things. First, that Suetonius was not writing about Christians at all: each line is one item in a list of an emperor's measures, and he calls Nero's punishment of them blameless (E2, E3, E5). Second, that "Chrestus" is a real Greek name, close in sound to Christus, so the line may mean Christ, misplaced in time and place, or someone else, and no held source settles it (D1). Third, that Christians themselves knew Romans garbled the name: Tertullian and Lactantius said so, and editors set their words beside this line (I3). Carry it through what the historians and the Christians did, never as a "this matters because" sentence.

**Ending:** (a) Chosen: the opening image seen again. Seven Latin words between old Troy's tax and the Germans' theatre seats, written by a man who was not writing about Christians, still read word by word for one name (E1, E2). (b) Possible: Suetonius dismissed by Hadrian, and after that "we lose sight of" him (ROL 245, Rolfe): the man is lost, and the line is what remains. Use (a).

**Who tells it**
- **Suetonius**, in his lives of the emperors, about 120: both lines, the list around each, his "beyond criticism", and his blame of Nero for the fire (E1–E6). Everything about the two events in this story rests on these lines, Acts and Dio. Say once, in beat 3, that he gives no year, no number and no source.
- **Acts**: the expulsion, and the couple Paul met at Corinth (I2).
- **Dio**: "only banned their meetings", credited by name or role (D2).
- **Modern editors** (Rolfe 1914, the 1909 English editors, a German historian of 1922): every reading of who Chrestus was (D1), and the link of the fire to Christians, which the narrator does not make (D4). Credit as "editors" or "historians".
- **Tertullian**, about 200, and **Lactantius**, a century later: the mispronounced name (I3). Credit the link to Suetonius to "editors".
- **Jerome**: his own preface (I4).
- **Bede**, about 725: the "ninth year" of Claudius for the expulsion, following Orosius; that date is credited to him, not to Suetonius or Acts (Setting; Bede 23216–23217). Not told in the story (Leave out).
- No doubt here is a scene of its own; the Chrestus question is beat 3, told plainly with each view credited.

**Do not say**
- Not "Suetonius says Claudius expelled the Jews because of Christ" or "because of Christians". He says "Chrestus" (E1). That Chrestus means Christ is the editors' reading (D1).
- Not "Chrestus was a misspelling of Christ" as fact. Say editors read it so, and one allows a Jew of that name (D1).
- Not that Chrestus was in Rome, or led the riots in person, in the narrator's voice. Suetonius does not say; "at the urging of" is all (E1, D1).
- Not "all the Jews" as Suetonius's words. His Latin says "the Jews"; "all" is in Acts and in one English translation (D3).
- Not 'in 49' or any year in the narrator's voice. Suetonius and Acts give none; the 'ninth year' is Bede's, from Orosius (not held). Say 'in the reign of Claudius'.
- Not "Suetonius tells of the fire and the Christians" or "Nero blamed the Christians for the fire" as Suetonius's. He blames Nero for the fire in another chapter and never joins the two; the link is Tacitus's and the 1909 editors' (E6, D4).
- Not that Suetonius's punishments were Tacitus's deaths, in the narrator's voice. Historians pair them (MIR 141–151); he does not.
- Not "Suetonius blamed Nero for persecuting the Christians". He counts it among Nero's blameless or praiseworthy acts (E5).
- Not "Nero executed" or "killed" the Christians as Suetonius's words. He says punishments were inflicted, and gives no kind of punishment (E3).
- Not "the first", "the earliest" or "the only" outside mention of Christians or of Christ. Say "among the very few" (I1; Tacitus, Pliny and Josephus are on the tree).
- Not "historians still rely on it today": the held historians run to 1924. Say "historians gather", "historians still weigh" (I1).
- Not "Suetonius proves Jesus lived" or anything about Jesus beyond the editors' reading of one name.
- Not that Tertullian or Lactantius quoted or answered Suetonius. Neither names him; editors set them side by side (I3).
- Not that Jerome modelled himself on the lives of the emperors. It was Suetonius's lost book on famous men of letters (I4).
- Not "Suetonius was a senator", "a lawyer" or "a schoolmaster". Rolfe says he practised at the bar "only for a short time", took no part in political life, and that the schoolmaster claim "seems to lack evidence" (S2). "A scholar" is enough.
- Not "born in 69" or "born in 77" as settled (S1, D5). Leave the birth out. Not "born in Rome": Rolfe says his birthplace is unknown (ROL 164–166).
- Not "the twelve emperors" without care: the book runs from Julius Caesar, who was not an emperor, to Domitian (S4). Say "twelve of Rome's rulers" or "the emperors" loosely.
- Not the reason Hadrian dismissed him as fact, nor anything about the empress. The held editors give one late writer's unclear words (S2). Say "Hadrian dismissed him".
- Not "Pliny's friend Suetonius met the Christians" or any tie between Suetonius and Pliny's trials in Bithynia. None is held.
- Not "a new and mischievous superstition" (Tacitus's live wording is "a most mischievous superstition"). Use Q2's rendering.
- Not "witchcraft" or "magic" as Suetonius's meaning; that is the 1909 editors' gloss on *malefica* (E4).
- Keep the force: his contempt ("harmful superstition") and his approval of the punishment (E3, E5). Do not soften.
- Any word in "Words to explain" used without its plain sense the first time it is heard, "Chrestus", "Chrestianus" and "superstition" among them.

**Leave out**
- The deaths under Nero, the fire's details, Christus under Pilate: the `tacitus` story's.
- Suetonius's *Tiberius* 36 and *Domitian* 12, which editors guess concern Christians: guesses, not his words.
- The Tacitus manuscript reading "Chrestianos": Tacitus's matter and the card's.
- Spartianus, Septicius Clarus and Sabina: the dismissal's detail rests on one unclear late line (S2).
- His lost works by title, Tertullian's use of him on the games, Paulinus, Einhard, Orosius (I5, D6): side notes for the card.
- Eusebius's retelling of the expulsion from Acts (I2): it adds nothing to Acts.
- Bede's chronicle and its "ninth year" (I2, D6): a late chronicle, and its date rests on an unheld Orosius.
- Malech's reading (E5): one modern's paraphrase; the line itself carries it.
- Nero's song over the burning city (E6): vivid, but it pulls the story toward the fire, which is Tacitus's.
