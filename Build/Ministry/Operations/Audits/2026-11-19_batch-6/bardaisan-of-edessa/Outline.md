# Outline: Bardaisan of Edessa

Sheet items (S, E, I, D) are in `Source_Sheet.md`, which gives the file and line for each. BLC is the dialogue (Pratten's English, ANF 8).

- **The essence:** Bardaisan, a Christian teacher at Edessa around the year 200, taught that people are free and not ruled by the stars. In a dialogue that a pupil wrote down, the *Book of the Laws of Countries*, he grants that nature rules the body and fate rules fortune, but says our conduct is our own: every nation keeps its own laws under the same stars, and Christians keep one law in every land (E4, E5, E7, E8; Documented, his words in a pupil's record; the pupil as author Widely Accepted, E11).
- **Also told:**
  - What the dialogue holds (E2–E9). A newcomer, Avida, asks why God did not make people unable to do wrong. Bardaisan answers that a person made so would be like a harp another plays or a ship another steers: the good would not be their own. He admits he once followed the astrologers, and he still grants fate its share: wealth, health, children. Then the laws of countries: the same stars shine on peoples who kill and who refuse to, who marry many and who marry one. Documented, his words.
  - The teacher (E12, S2). Eusebius, about a century later, says he debated the followers of Marcion in Syriac and had very many pupils, who put his works into Greek. Epiphanius says he knew both Greek and Syriac and stood close to the king of Edessa. Documented as their reports.
  - Later called a heretic (I1, I2, D1). Eusebius calls him a "powerful defender of the faith" (McGiffert's English) and also says he never wholly washed off the stain of his old heresy, the teaching of Valentinus. Epiphanius says the reverse: a fine churchman who later fell to the Valentinians and denied the resurrection of the dead. Ephrem, in Edessa a century and a half on, attacked his teaching on the body, the soul and the first beings he said the world was made from, and cursed anyone who said, as he did, that rain and dew come from "the Seven". Documented as each witness's charge; the order and the charge are Contested.
  - Answered in song (I3). Ephrem says Bardaisan made hymns; Sozomen gives the songs to his son Harmonius. Ephrem learned their meters and answered with the church's own. Documented as their reports; whose songs, Contested (D3). A clause here: the Ephrem story tells it.
  The sources will not hold "he rejected astrology" (he keeps fate over fortune, E5), "he wrote the Book of the Laws of Countries" as fact (a pupil did, by the editors' view, E11), or "he was a Gnostic" in the narrator's voice (D1).
- **Length:** about 3 minutes (about 440 words). If it runs long, cut in this order: What came 4 (Theodoret's list) first, then Q2's second part, then beat 5's persecution-works clause. Why: the dialogue gives a real scene and an argument the listener can follow, and the later quarrel over him has force and several voices. Not more: the facts of his life are few, rest on later writers, and their dates conflict (D4); Ephrem's songs are a clause.
- **Setting:**
  - Time: born about 155 (the *Chronicle of Edessa*, S1: 154 by Cowper's reckoning, 155 by Hort's); died in his late sixties, about 222 (S5). "Around the year 200" covers his teaching.
  - Place: Edessa, a city of upper Mesopotamia with its own king; today in south-eastern Turkey (census). Do not call its neighbour "Persia": the dialogue's eastern empire is the Parthians' (BLC 68681, 68795). The river Daisan runs by it; his name means "son of the Daisan" (S1). A king, Abgar, ruled it (S2, S3).
  - Tradition: "the Bardaisanites", the school named after him (census); the city's church is "Syriac Christianity" (census short name "Syriac Edessa"). Syriac: "a form of Aramaic, the language of Edessa" (S4).
- **Words to explain** (the writer works the gloss in where the word is first heard, or uses the plain word):
  - **Syriac**: "a form of Aramaic, the language of Edessa".
  - **fate**: the course God gave the heavenly powers, which shapes a person's fortune from the hour of birth (BLC 68459–68463, 68396). Give the plain sense the first time.
  - **astrologers** / **Chaldaeans**: say "astrologers"; never "Chaldaeans".
  - **the Seven**: in the dialogue, 'those stars which are called the Seven', which astrologers said governed our lives (BLC 68354–68357). In Ephrem's curse they are, in his words, powers 'which Bardaisan confesses' (EPH-G 11261–11263). Give each sense with its own source. Never give Ephrem's line the dialogue's sense, and never say 'planets' or 'aeons'.
  - **dialogue**: "a written conversation".
  - **Valentinus's followers** / **Valentinians**: "the followers of Valentinus, a teacher at Rome whose school taught a heaven of paired divine powers" (from the `valentinus` story's own wording). Avoid "Gnostic" and "aeons".
  - **Marcion's followers**: "followers of Marcion, who taught that the God of the law of Moses, who made the world, was not the Father of Jesus" (from the `marcion-of-pontus` story).
  - **heresy** / **heretic**: "teaching the church condemned"; always a witness's word, never the narrator's.
  - **resurrection of the body**: the belief that the dead will rise in the body. Say "that the body will rise again".
  - **Seres**: 'a people of the far east'. **Brahmans**: 'the Brahmans of India', or 'a class in India who keep their own teaching'. **Geli**, **Hatra**: leave out. **Parthians**: 'the Parthians, whose kingdom lay to the east' (BLC 68681).
  - **Mesopotamia**: "the land between the Tigris and the Euphrates".
  - **confessor**: avoid; say "he refused to deny that he was a Christian".
- **Shape:** Teacher or thinker. Trap: a list of books. Show the one idea, that the stars do not rule what we do, through the dialogue's own scene and his words; then the quarrel over him through what later witnesses wrote.
- **Sibling:**
  - `tatian` (batch 6, outline read): its job is Tatian's *Address* and the Diatessaron. No held source joins the two men or puts Tatian at Edessa. Eusebius tells them in consecutive chapters (EU IV.29–30). Neither story needs the other; do not link them by place.
  - `ephrem-the-syrian` (live, read): it already tells Ephrem's prose against Mani, Marcion and Bardaisan, the charge on the body's resurrection, Harmonius's songs as Sozomen tells them, and Ephrem learning the meters. Here each is at most a clause; none of its quotes or phrases is reused ("slanders God's justice and grace", "the songs of Ephrem"). Ephrem's curse on "the Seven" is not in it and may be told here, narrated.
  - `valentinus` (live, read; batch 6 later): its job is Valentinus's teaching. Here a clause, inside the witnesses' charges.
  - `marcion-of-pontus` (live, read): here a clause, as the party he argued against.
  - `eusebius-of-caesarea` (live, read): here a witness, one or two sentences.
  - `bardaisanite-current` (tradition documented story: same opening scene and dialogue. Here the story goes on to his life and the later quarrel. Its quoted phrase 'wherever they are' is not reused; Q2's first part, 'wherever we are', may stand, and its second part is dropped if the overlap check flags it.)
- **Openings:**
  1. **Chosen.** The dialogue's first scene (E1, E2; BLC 67908–67926). Some pupils are visiting a friend when their teacher, Bardaisan, came in, asked after their host's health, and then asked what they had been talking about, as he always did. One of them answers for a newcomer among his pupils, Avida, who was too shy to ask: if God is one, and made us, and wants us to do right, why did he not make us unable to do wrong? Then say the essence. Note: the text says only that Bardaisan "made inquiries after his health, and ascertained that he was well"; do not make the friend ill.
  2. Ephrem's curse on anyone who says, as Bardaisan said, that rain and dew come from "the Seven" (I2). Not chosen: it opens on the enemy and the end of the story, and needs the argument first to make sense.

**Story beats** (the opening and beat 1 are the first minute, about 150 words; the essence is said by the end of beat 1)

1. **A teacher at Edessa** (S1, S2, S4, E12). Say the essence. Then: born about 155 beside the river Daisan, which gave him his name. He wrote and argued in Syriac; Epiphanius, a bishop writing about 150 years later, says he also knew Greek and stood close to the king, Abgar (credit him). Eusebius says he debated the followers of Marcion, wrote dialogues against them, and had very many pupils, who put his works into Greek (Documented as Eusebius's report). The dialogue was written down by a pupil, Philip, who speaks as "I" in it: say so once, here (E11; Widely Accepted, the editors' view).
2. **The harp and the ship** (E3, E4). He tells Avida that teachers are the ones who are asked, and that it is good to know how to ask. Then his answer: had God made people unable to do wrong, they would not belong to themselves; they would be like a harp another plays, or a ship another steers, where praise and blame belong to the player. The sun never says, "I will not rise"; people can. The good they do is their own (Documented, his words, narrated).
3. **Nature, fate and freedom** (E5, E6; Q1). Nature gives every person the same life: birth, growth, eating and sleeping, old age and death. Fate, the power of the stars from the hour of birth, gives wealth, sickness and children unevenly. He admits he once followed the astrologers himself, and he still says fate is real. But conduct is free. Then Q1. Documented.
4. **The laws of countries** (E7, E8, S3; Q2). The proof is the world's laws. People are born under every star in every land, yet each people keeps its own law: in India the Brahmans eat no meat; Persians marry their sisters; among Britons one wife has many husbands, among Parthians one man many wives. Laws change when kings change them: the Romans, taking Arabia, abolished its laws; at Edessa, he says, when King Abgar became a believer, he ordered that any man who castrated himself in honour of Tharatha should have his hand cut off (the text does not say who Tharatha was: say "in a pagan rite" if a gloss is needed). Then the Christians, Q2. Documented, his words. Keep the force: the laws he lists include stoning and death; he cites them as facts, not as praise.
5. **A stand, and his death** (E13, S5). Eusebius reports that he was said to have written works in a time of persecution. Epiphanius alone says that when a companion of the emperor urged him to deny he was a Christian, he refused, saying he did not fear death, since death would come anyway. Tell it in Epiphanius's voice, with no emperor or date (Contested, D6). He died in his late sixties, about 222 (S5; a medieval chronicle's count).

**What came of him** (about a third of the story; heaviest first)

1. **Called a heretic, by witnesses who disagree.** DEFENDED: the faith, in his dialogues against Marcion's followers (E12, Eusebius's report). Eusebius, about a century on, calls him a powerful defender of the faith, yet says he had first followed Valentinus and never wholly washed off the stain of his old heresy (I1a; render "filth" plainly as "the stain"). Epiphanius tells it the other way: a fine man of the church who later joined the Valentinians and denied that the dead will rise, like a fine ship wrecked at the harbour's edge, with its passengers lost (I1b; narrate). A writer at Rome in the years just after his death already counted him in Valentinus's eastern school (Hippolytus, I1c; by role). Ephrem in Edessa, about 150 years on, wrote against his teaching on the soul, the body and the first beings he said the world was made from, called him the teacher of Mani (I2). Tell the curse in Ephrem's own terms: he cursed anyone who trusted in 'the Seven', powers he said Bardaisan confessed, and who said, as Bardaisan did, that rain, dew, snow and the farmer's seed come from them. Do not set it against the dialogue as though the two meant the same thing. The narrator adds no comment. All Documented as charges; the order and charge Contested (D1). One clause may say the question is still argued (census tradition entry).
2. **The songs** (I3). A clause: Ephrem says he made hymns and a hundred and fifty psalms; Sozomen gives the songs to his son Harmonius; Ephrem learned the meters and answered with the church's own hymns.
3. **The argument kept** (I4). DEFENDED: human freedom against the astrologers' fate (E4–E8, Q2). His pupils put his works into Greek; Eusebius quoted the dialogue in another book (McGiffert's report, the book not held); a later Christian book told under Clement's name, the *Recognitions*, has chapters taken from it in altered form, most editors judge; Jerome, about 170 years on, called it, among his books, the most famous and strongest of them, and asked how much force it must have had in Syriac if it had so much in translation (I4; narrate).
4. **A name in the lists** (I5). In the 400s Theodoret set his name, and his son Harmonius's, beside Valentinus's among those who said that God the Word took nothing from Mary (THD 35058–35063). One clause, if room.

- **Quotes, 2 or 3 at most** (rendered by me from Pratten's English; the Syriac and Eusebius's Greek extract are not held):
  - **Q1.** "So we humans are ruled by nature all alike, by fate each in a different way, and by our freedom each as we choose." Source: BLC 68441–68443. Job: the whole argument in one line, in his words. Give "fate" its plain sense first (Words to explain).
  - **Q2.** "What shall we say of us Christians, the new people Christ planted, when he came, in every country and every region? Wherever we are, we are all called by the one name of Christ: Christians." And: "Wherever they are, in whatever place they are found, the laws of their countries do not stop them obeying the law of their sovereign, Christ." Source: BLC 68966–68969; 68988–68990. Job: the proof that crowns the laws of countries, and what he defended. "New race" is rendered "new people": "race" misleads today. Either part may stand alone.
  - **Q3.** "When that new world is set up, all evil turmoil will cease, and every rebellion will end. The foolish will be convinced, and all that is lacking will be filled. There will be quiet and peace, through the gift of the Lord of all that exists." Source: BLC 69026–69031. Job: the ending, on his words; it echoes Avida's 'I cannot believe unless I be convinced'.

- **Connection to carry into the story:** his argument passed from his teaching circle at Edessa into writing and then across languages. A pupil, Philip, wrote it down; his pupils put his works into Greek (EU 26637–26639); Eusebius used the dialogue; the *Recognitions* reworked it; Jerome praised it (I4). At the same time his name passed into the lists of teachers the church condemned, through Eusebius, Epiphanius and above all Ephrem in his own city (I1, I2). The listener must be told that the only work in his voice is a pupil's record, not his own book; that the same writers who used or praised the argument named him among the heretics; and that the charges the witnesses name are, each in its own witness's voice, about the first beings he said the world was made from, the soul, the body's rising, Christ's body and 'the Seven' (I1, I2). The narrator draws no contrast between these charges and the freedom argument, and does not say whether the charges touched it. It should come through what each witness wrote, never a "this matters because" sentence.
- **Ending:**
  1. **Chosen.** The dialogue's last words (Q3): the new world where "the foolish will be convinced", bringing back Avida from the opening. Then stop. Supporting lines BLC 69023–69033.
  2. Philip's own line near the end, "Of these things, Father Bardesan, thou hast convinced us" (BLC 68870–68872), narrated: the pupils convinced, Avida's answer never recorded (E9). Use if Q3 runs long.
- **Links:** Marcion: argued against his followers. Valentinus: said by later writers to have followed his school. Eusebius: wrote his entry and quoted his dialogue. Epiphanius: wrote against him. Hippolytus: counted him in Valentinus's school. Ephrem: wrote against him in prose and verse and took up the meters of his school's songs. Sozomen, Theodoret: told of his son's songs and Ephrem's answer. Jerome: praised the dialogue. Tatian: none held.
- **Who tells it:**
  - The dialogue, written by a pupil, probably soon after his death (the editors' view): the scene, the argument, his admission, the laws, Abgar's law, the Christians, the new world. Say once that it is a pupil's record.
  - Hippolytus (Rome, about 225–235, just after his death): Valentinus's eastern school; a Marcionite's book addressed to him.
  - Eusebius (early 300s, about a century on): the pupils, the debates with Marcion's followers, "a powerful defender of the faith", and "the old heresy". Note that "powerful defender of the faith" is McGiffert's English; the Greek is not held.
  - Epiphanius (about 375): Greek and Syriac, the king's court, the refusal to deny, the fall, the ship. The emperor in his story is confused (Holl, EPI 26418–26425).
  - Ephrem (Edessa, d. 373): the charges, the curse on "the Seven", the hymns. Hostile; he read Bardaisan's books (I2).
  - Sozomen, Theodoret (the 400s): Harmonius's songs.
  - Jerome (392/3): the praise of the dialogue.
  - The *Chronicle of Edessa* (about 540) and Barhebraeus (13th century): the birth and death dates.
- **Do not say:**
  - "Bardaisan wrote the Book of the Laws of Countries" → "a pupil, Philip, wrote down the conversation"; or "the dialogue in which he speaks".
  - "He rejected astrology" or "denied fate" → "he granted the stars power over fortune, but not over what we do".
  - "He was a Gnostic" or "a heretic" in the narrator's voice → "Eusebius says ...", "Ephrem called him ..."; always a witness's word.
  - "He was first a Valentinian" as fact → "Eusebius says he began among the followers of Valentinus; Epiphanius says the opposite".
  - "Ephrem knew him only through his followers" → leave out (McGiffert's view, which Ephrem's own reading of his books contradicts).
  - "Eusebius called him a powerful defender of the faith" as Eusebius's exact words → "Eusebius praised him as a powerful defender of the faith" is acceptable; do not put it in quotation marks.
  - "He was born in 154" or "155" as settled → "about 155".
  - "He died in 223" → "about 222, in his late sixties".
  - "He lived under Marcus Aurelius" → leave out (the editors call it a confusion).
  - "He was martyred" or "tried before the emperor" → "Epiphanius says he refused to deny he was a Christian".
  - "He addressed his book On Fate to the emperor" → leave out (D5).
  - "He wrote the Hymn of the Soul" → leave out (D3).
  - "Bardaisan converted King Abgar" or "Abgar became a Christian" as fact → "the dialogue says the king became a believer".
  - "Harmonius was a heretic" or "Bardaisan wrote the songs" as fact → "Ephrem says he made hymns; Sozomen says his son Harmonius did".
  - "Tatian" at Edessa, or any meeting with Tatian → none held.
  - 'The Seven are the planets' or 'the stars' in Ephrem's voice → 'the Seven, powers Ephrem says Bardaisan confessed'. The dialogue's 'seven stars' is told only where the dialogue is.
  - "Avida was convinced" at the end → Avida says he will be convinced if it is proved; the dialogue does not record his last word.
  - 'a young man' or 'at Edessa' for the opening scene → 'a newcomer among his pupils'; the text gives no place.
  - Avida as 'the foolish' → never; the dialogue does not call him so.
  - Avida 'sitting apart' or wanting 'to seem to have found the answer himself' → not in the text; he 'was too bashful to ask' (BLC 67944–67946).
  - Do not link Epiphanius's refusal to the persecution Eusebius mentions, to Caracalla, or to any date (Hort's conjecture, EU n. 1341).
  - Any feeling for Bardaisan at the charges: none is in the sources.
  - "fate", "Valentinus", "Syriac" without the plain sense the first time; "Chaldaeans", "aeons", "Gnostic", "docetism", "confessor" at all.
- **Leave out:** the birth legends of the 12th–13th-century chronicles (parents fleeing Persia, the pagan priest of Mabbug, baptism at 25: late; I6); Bardaisan as historian of Armenia (Moses of Khoren, Langlois: late, side story); the archer story (Julius Africanus, not held); the Indian sages (Porphyry not held; a side story); the planetary cycles fragment and the months' names (technical); the soul's seven parts and the "Entities" in detail (technical; one phrase, "the first beings he said the world was made from", is enough); Hort's Caracalla conjecture (an editor's guess); the "days of the readings" fasting (unclear in the text); the dating quarrel beyond "about 155" (D4); Prepon's book (a clause at most). None of the record's conflict is left out: Eusebius's and Epiphanius's charges, Ephrem's curses, and the dialogue's harsh laws are all told.
