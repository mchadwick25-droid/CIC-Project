Some pupils were visiting a friend when their teacher, Bardaisan, came in and asked, as he always did, what they had been talking about. One of them spoke for a newcomer among his pupils, Avida, who was too shy to ask. If God made us and wants us to do right, why did he not make us unable to do wrong?

Bardaisan was a Christian teacher in Edessa, a city in what is now south-eastern Turkey, around the year 200, and his answer was that people are free. Nature, he granted, rules the body, and the stars have power over fortune, but not over what we do. A pupil set down the conversation, the Book of the Laws of Countries. In it his proof is that each nation keeps its own laws under the same stars, and that Christians keep one law in every land.

That pupil, most editors judge, was Philip, one of those in the room, and his record is the only one we have in Bardaisan's voice. Born about 155 beside the river Daisan, which gave him his name, he taught in Syriac, a form of Aramaic and the language of Edessa. Eusebius of Caesarea, a century later, says he wrote other such conversations against the followers of Marcion. Marcion taught that the God of the law of Moses, who made the world, was not the Father of Jesus. His many pupils, Eusebius adds, put his works into Greek.

Had God made people unable to do wrong, Bardaisan told Avida, they would be like a harp that another plays, or a ship that another steers. The sun never says that it will not rise, but people can, and so the good they do is their own.

He admitted that he had once followed the astrologers, and he still gave fate its share. Fate, the course God gave the powers of the heavens, hands out wealth, sickness and children unevenly from the hour of birth. "So we humans are ruled by nature all alike, by fate each in a different way, and by our freedom each as we choose."

He gives his examples. Under the same stars the Brahmans of India eat no meat while Persians marry their sisters, and some peoples stone and kill where others refuse to. At Edessa, he says, when King Abgar became a believer, he ordered that any man who castrated himself in a pagan rite should have his hand cut off. Then he turns to the Christians, found in every land: "Wherever we are, we are all called by the one name of Christ: Christians."

He died about 222, in his late sixties. His argument outlived him: a later Christian book reworked its chapters, most editors judge, and a Latin scholar, about 170 years on, called it the strongest of his books. Yet his name passed into the lists of those the church condemned. Eusebius praised him as a powerful defender of the faith, yet says he began among the followers of Valentinus and never wholly washed off the stain of that old heresy. Valentinus was a teacher at Rome whose school taught a heaven of paired divine powers. A bishop writing about 150 years after him, Epiphanius, says the reverse. To him Bardaisan was a fine churchman who later joined Valentinus's followers and denied that the body will rise again, like a fine ship wrecked at the harbor's edge.

In Edessa itself, at about the same time, Ephrem the Syrian attacked his teaching on the soul, the body and the first beings he said the world was made from. He cursed anyone who said, as Bardaisan did, that rain and dew come from the Seven, powers Ephrem says Bardaisan confessed. He also answered his followers' songs with hymns of his own, in the same meters.

The dialogue never records Avida's last word. It ends on the world to come: "When that new world is set up, all evil turmoil will cease, and every rebellion will end. The foolish will be convinced, and all that is lacking will be filled."
