Some pupils were visiting a friend when their teacher, Bardaisan, came in. He asked after their host's health and then, as he always did, asked what they had been talking about. One of them spoke for a newcomer among his pupils, Avida, who was too shy to ask: if God is one, and made us, and wants us to do right, why did he not make us unable to do wrong?

Bardaisan was a Christian teacher at Edessa around the year 200, and his answer is why he is remembered. People, he taught, are free and are not ruled by the stars: nature rules the body and fate rules fortune, but what we do is our own.

He was born about 155 beside the river Daisan, which gave him his name, in a city with its own king, in what is now south-eastern Turkey. Eusebius of Caesarea, a century later, says he argued against the followers of Marcion and had many pupils, who put his works into Greek. One pupil, Philip, wrote this conversation down, so the dialogue is a pupil's record, not Bardaisan's own book.

Had God made people unable to do wrong, Bardaisan told Avida, they would not belong to themselves. They would be like a harp that another plays, or a ship that another steers, where praise and blame belong to the player. The sun never says that it will not rise, but people can, and so the good they do is their own.

He admitted that he had once followed the astrologers, and he still gave fate its share. Fate, the course God gave the powers of the heavens, shapes each fortune from the hour of birth, handing out wealth, sickness and children unevenly. "So we humans are ruled by nature all alike, by fate each in a different way, and by our freedom each as we choose."

His proof was the laws of countries. The same stars shine on every land, yet in India the Brahmans eat no meat while Persians marry their sisters, and some peoples stone or put to death where others refuse to kill. At Edessa, he says, when King Abgar became a believer, he ordered that any man who castrated himself in a pagan rite should have his hand cut off. And "what shall we say of us Christians, the new people Christ planted, when he came, in every country and every region? Wherever we are, we are all called by the one name of Christ: Christians."

A bishop writing about 150 years later, Epiphanius, says that when urged to deny he was a Christian, Bardaisan refused, since death would come anyway. He died about 222, in his late sixties.

Yet the writers who praised him also named him among those the church condemned. Eusebius praised him as a powerful defender of the faith, but says he had first followed Valentinus, a teacher at Rome whose school taught a heaven of paired divine powers, and never wholly washed off the stain of that old heresy. Epiphanius tells it the other way: a fine man of the church who later joined Valentinus's followers and denied that the body will rise again, like a fine ship wrecked at the harbor's edge.

Ephrem the Syrian, in Edessa a century and a half on, attacked his teaching on the soul and the body and called him the teacher of Mani, the founder of a rival faith. He cursed anyone who said, as Bardaisan did, that rain and dew come from the Seven, powers Ephrem said Bardaisan confessed. Ephrem says Bardaisan also made hymns, and he answered them with the church's own.

Still, the argument lasted. A Latin scholar, Jerome, called the dialogue the most famous and strongest of his books, and asked how much force it must have had in Syriac, if it had so much in translation.

The dialogue never records Avida's last word. It ends on the world to come: "When that new world is set up, all evil turmoil will cease, and every rebellion will end. The foolish will be convinced, and all that is lacking will be filled. There will be quiet and peace, through the gift of the Lord of all that exists."
