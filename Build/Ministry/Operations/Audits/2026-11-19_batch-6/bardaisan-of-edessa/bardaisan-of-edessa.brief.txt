Some pupils were visiting a friend when their teacher, Bardaisan, came in and asked, as he always did, what they had been talking about. One of them spoke for a newcomer among his pupils, Avida, who was too shy to ask: if God made us and wants us to do right, why did he not make us unable to do wrong?

Bardaisan was a Christian teacher at Edessa around the year 200, and his answer was that people are free. The stars, he granted, have power over fortune, but not over what we do.

He was born about 155 beside the river Daisan, which gave him his name, and he taught in Syriac, a form of Aramaic and the language of Edessa. Eusebius of Caesarea, a century later, says his many pupils put his works into Greek. One of them, Philip, wrote this conversation down, so the dialogue is a pupil's record of his teacher's voice.

Had God made people unable to do wrong, Bardaisan told Avida, they would not belong to themselves, but would be like a harp that another plays, or a ship that another steers. The sun never says that it will not rise, but people can, and so the good they do is their own.

He admitted that he had once followed the astrologers, and he still gave fate its share. Fate, the course God gave the powers of the heavens, shapes each fortune from the hour of birth, handing out wealth, sickness and children unevenly. "So we humans are ruled by nature all alike, by fate each in a different way, and by our freedom each as we choose."

His proof was the laws of countries. Under the same stars the Brahmans of India eat no meat while Persians marry their sisters, and some peoples stone and kill where others refuse to. At Edessa, he says, when King Abgar became a believer, he ordered that any man who castrated himself in a pagan rite should have his hand cut off. Then he turns to his own people: "What shall we say of us Christians, the new people Christ planted, when he came, in every country and every region? Wherever we are, we are all called by the one name of Christ: Christians."

A bishop writing about 150 years later, Epiphanius, says Bardaisan refused to deny that he was a Christian, since death would come anyway. He died about 222, in his late sixties.

Yet his name passed into the lists of those the church condemned. Eusebius, who praised him as a powerful defender of the faith, says he began among the followers of Valentinus, a teacher at Rome whose school taught a heaven of paired divine powers, and never wholly washed off the stain of that old heresy. Epiphanius says the reverse: a fine churchman who later joined them and denied that the body will rise again, like a fine ship wrecked at the harbor's edge.

In Edessa a century and a half on, Ephrem the Syrian attacked his teaching on the soul and the body and called him the teacher of Mani, the founder of a rival faith. He cursed anyone who said, as Bardaisan did, that rain and dew come from the Seven, powers Ephrem says Bardaisan confessed.

The argument lasted all the same. Jerome, a Latin scholar, called the dialogue the strongest of his books, and asked how much force it must have had in Syriac, if it had so much in translation.

The dialogue never records Avida's last word. It ends on the world to come: "When that new world is set up, all evil turmoil will cease, and every rebellion will end. The foolish will be convinced, and all that is lacking will be filled. There will be quiet and peace, through the gift of the Lord of all that exists."
