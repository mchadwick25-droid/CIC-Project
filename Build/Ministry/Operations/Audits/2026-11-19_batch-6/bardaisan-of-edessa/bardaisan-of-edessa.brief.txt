Some pupils were visiting a friend when their teacher, Bardaisan, came in and asked, as he always did, what they had been talking about. One of them spoke for a newcomer among his pupils, Avida, who was too shy to ask: if God made us and wants us to do right, why did he not make us unable to do wrong?

Bardaisan was a Christian teacher at Edessa around the year 200, and his answer was that people are free. The stars, he granted, have power over fortune, but not over what we do.

Born about 155 beside the river Daisan, which gave him his name, he taught in Syriac, a form of Aramaic and the language of Edessa. His many pupils, Eusebius of Caesarea says, put his works into Greek, and one of them, Philip, wrote down this conversation, the only record we have in his voice.

Had God made people unable to do wrong, Bardaisan told Avida, they would be like a harp that another plays, or a ship that another steers. The sun never says that it will not rise, but people can, and so the good they do is their own.

He admitted that he had once followed the astrologers, and he still gave fate its share. Fate, the course God gave the powers of the heavens, hands out wealth, sickness and children unevenly from the hour of birth. "So we humans are ruled by nature all alike, by fate each in a different way, and by our freedom each as we choose."

His proof was the laws of countries. Under the same stars the Brahmans of India eat no meat while Persians marry their sisters, and some peoples stone and kill where others refuse to. At Edessa, he says, when King Abgar became a believer, he ordered that any man who castrated himself in a pagan rite should have his hand cut off. Then he turns to the Christians, found in every land: "Wherever we are, we are all called by the one name of Christ: Christians."

He died about 222, in his late sixties, and his name soon passed into the lists of those the church condemned. Eusebius praised him as a powerful defender of the faith, yet says he began among the followers of Valentinus, a teacher at Rome whose school taught a heaven of paired divine powers, and never wholly washed off the stain of that old heresy. A bishop writing about 150 years after him, Epiphanius, says the reverse: a fine churchman who later joined them and denied that the body will rise again, like a fine ship wrecked at the harbor's edge.

In Edessa itself, Ephrem the Syrian attacked his teaching on the soul and the body and called him the teacher of Mani, the founder of a rival faith. He cursed anyone who said, as Bardaisan did, that rain and dew come from the Seven, powers Ephrem says Bardaisan confessed.

The dialogue never records Avida's last word. It ends on the world to come: "When that new world is set up, all evil turmoil will cease, and every rebellion will end. The foolish will be convinced, and all that is lacking will be filled."
