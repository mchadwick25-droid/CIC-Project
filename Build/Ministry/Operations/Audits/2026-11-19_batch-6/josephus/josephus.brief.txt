In 67 the Romans took Jotapata, a walled town in Galilee, and on the third day they found its commander, Josephus, hiding in a pit with forty men. The Roman general Vespasian, later emperor, sent officers to promise him his life, and Josephus writes that he remembered dreams in which God had shown him the ruin of the Jews. Then he prayed in secret: "I declare it openly: I do not go over to the Romans as a deserter from the Jews, but as a servant sent by you."

Josephus was a priest of the Temple in Jerusalem, a priest by birth, who had led the Jewish forces in the north when the Jews rose against Rome. He lived to spend the rest of his life at Rome, under the emperors' favor, writing his people's history. In it he wrote a few lines each on John the Baptist, on James, "the brother of Jesus, who was called Christ", and on Jesus himself. For centuries Christians quoted those lines as the word of a Jewish historian outside the church, and they argued over the passage on Jesus, which is disputed still.

He was born in Jerusalem in 37, into a family of priests. When the war began in 66, the leaders in Jerusalem sent him north to Galilee, and his two books tell it differently: in one he is the commander of the region, while in the other he and two other priests go to persuade armed men to lay down their arms. Then Vespasian came with an army, and when Jotapata fell, forty thousand died there, by his own count.

In the cave, his companions would rather die than surrender, and they offered him a sword to die as their general. When he refused, they called him a coward and turned their swords on him, so he proposed that they kill each other by lot. One by one they bared their necks, until he and one other man were left, he writes, "whether we must say it happened so by chance, or whether by the providence of God", and he persuaded that man to live. The story of the cave is his alone, and no other account of it exists.

Brought before Vespasian, he asked to speak alone and told him that he and his son would be emperors. Vespasian first took it for a trick to save his life, but Suetonius, a Roman writer of the next generation, records that Josephus foretold it. In 69 Vespasian was proclaimed emperor, and he had his prisoner freed.

In 70, at the siege of Jerusalem, Vespasian's son Titus sent Josephus round the walls to call on the defenders, in their own language, to save themselves, their country and their temple. He writes that the Jews wanted him punished, while the Romans, whenever they lost, blamed his "treachery".

At Rome, Vespasian gave him rooms in his own former house, Roman citizenship and a yearly pension. Josephus wrote the history of the war, first in his own language and then in Greek, and then his history of the Jewish people, in twenty books, finished about 93. Inside it are three short passages. John the Baptist was "a good man" who called the Jews to virtue and to washing in water, until the ruler Herod, fearing his hold on the crowds, imprisoned him in a fortress and killed him. James was tried before a council of judges by the chief priest of the Temple and stoned, and those who cared for the law protested until that priest was removed. Then comes Jesus: "About this time there lived Jesus, a wise man, if it is right to call him a man. For he did astonishing deeds, and taught people who gladly accept the truth." The passage goes on: "He was the Christ", that is, the Messiah, the anointed king the Jews awaited. It says the Roman governor Pilate had him crucified, that those who loved him saw him alive on the third day, and that the Christians named after him had not died out. Each passage is only a few sentences long.

About a century and a half later, Origen, the scholar of Alexandria, cited Josephus on John and James against a pagan critic, and wrote: "And this is the remarkable thing. He did not accept that our Jesus was the Christ. Yet he still bore witness that James was so upright." Origen never cites the passage on Jesus at all.

In the early fourth century Eusebius of Caesarea quoted all three passages in his history of the church, the first writer known to quote the one on Jesus. He used it against forged records of Jesus's trial that the church's enemies had spread, because here was a historian who was himself a Hebrew. Eusebius also told the fall of Jerusalem, taken from Josephus, as God's judgment on the Jews for what they had done to Christ.

Later that century, Jerome's Latin said only that Jesus was believed to be the Christ. By 1890 an editor of Eusebius could write that the passage was thought genuine until the sixteenth century, and disputed ever since. Some critics held it wholly genuine, while others, the editor among them, thought it wholly added later by a Christian copyist, and others again took it as partly genuine. The man who translated Josephus in the eighteenth century defended it. The lines on John and James, that editor wrote, were widely accepted as Josephus's own.

When Josephus died is not known. He closed the story of his own life on the emperors' favor and the charges he had outlived, and then left the verdict to his readers: "let others judge of my character."
