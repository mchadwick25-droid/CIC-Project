In 67 the Romans took Jotapata, a walled town in Galilee, and on the third day they found its commander, Josephus, hiding in a pit with forty men. The Roman general Vespasian, later emperor, sent officers to promise him his life. Josephus writes that he remembered dreams God had sent him, and prayed in secret: "I declare it openly: I do not go over to the Romans as a deserter from the Jews, but as a servant sent by you."

Josephus was a priest of the Temple in Jerusalem by birth, and had led the Jewish forces in the north when the Jews rose against Rome. He spent the rest of his life at Rome, under the emperors' favor, writing his people's history. In it are a few lines each on John the Baptist, on James, "the brother of Jesus, who was called Christ", and on Jesus himself. Christians quoted them for centuries as the word of a Jewish historian outside the church, and argued over the passage on Jesus, which is disputed still.

When the war began in 66, the leaders in Jerusalem sent him north, though his two books disagree on why: to command, or to persuade armed men to lay down their arms. Then Vespasian came, and at Jotapata forty thousand died, by his own count.

In the cave his companions would rather die than surrender. They called him a coward and turned their swords on him, so he proposed that they kill each other by lot. One by one they bared their necks, until he and one other man were left, he writes, "whether we must say it happened so by chance, or whether by the providence of God", and he persuaded that man to live. No other account of the cave exists.

Brought before Vespasian, Josephus told him that he and his son would be emperors. Vespasian first took it for a trick, but the Roman writer Suetonius also records the prediction, and in 69, once emperor, Vespasian had him freed. At the siege of Jerusalem in 70, Josephus called on the defenders to surrender. The Jews wanted him punished, he writes, while the Romans, whenever they lost, blamed his "treachery".

At Rome he wrote his history of the Jewish people, in twenty books, finished about 93. John the Baptist is "a good man" who called the Jews to virtue and to washing in water, until the ruler Herod, fearing his hold on the crowds, killed him. James is stoned on the order of the chief priest of the Temple. And then: "About this time there lived Jesus, a wise man, if it is right to call him a man. For he did astonishing deeds, and taught people who gladly accept the truth." The passage goes on, "He was the Christ", the Messiah the Jews awaited, and tells of his crucifixion under Pilate and of his followers seeing him alive on the third day. Each passage is only a few sentences long.

About a century and a half later, the scholar Origen cited Josephus on John and James, and wrote: "And this is the remarkable thing. He did not accept that our Jesus was the Christ. Yet he still bore witness that James was so upright." Origen never cites the passage on Jesus.

In the early fourth century Eusebius of Caesarea became the first writer known to quote it. He used it against forged records of Jesus's trial, since here was a historian who was himself a Hebrew. Eusebius also retold the fall of Jerusalem from Josephus as God's judgment on the Jews for what they had done to Christ.

Later, Jerome's Latin said only that Jesus was believed to be the Christ. By 1890 an editor of Eusebius wrote that the passage had been disputed since the sixteenth century. Some critics held it genuine, others, the editor among them, thought a Christian copyist had added it, and others took it as partly genuine. The lines on John and James, he wrote, were widely accepted as Josephus's own.

When Josephus died is not known. He closed the story of his own life on the emperors' favor and the charges he had outlived, and then left the verdict to his readers: "let others judge of my character."
