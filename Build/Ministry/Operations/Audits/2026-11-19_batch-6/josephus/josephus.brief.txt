In 67 the Romans took Jotapata, a walled town in Galilee, and found its commander, Josephus, hiding in a cave with forty men. The Roman general Vespasian, later emperor, promised him his life. Josephus prayed in secret to God: "I declare it openly: I do not go over to the Romans as a deserter from the Jews, but as a servant sent by you."

He was a priest of the Temple in Jerusalem by birth, who had led the Jewish forces in the north when the Jews rose against Rome. He spent the rest of his life at Rome, under the emperors' favor, writing his people's history, and in it are a few lines each on John the Baptist, on Jesus, and on James. Christians quoted those lines for centuries as the word of a Jewish historian outside the church, and argued over the passage on Jesus, which is disputed still.

His two books disagree on why Jerusalem sent him north in 66: to command, or to persuade armed men to lay down their arms. In the cave, his companions would rather die than surrender, and when they turned their swords on him, he proposed that they kill each other by lot. One by one they bared their necks. He and one other man were left, he writes, "whether we must say it happened so by chance, or whether by the providence of God", and he persuaded that man to live. His own books, written partly to answer the charge of treachery, are the only account.

Brought before Vespasian, he foretold that the general would be emperor, as the Roman writer Suetonius also records, and in 69 the new emperor freed him. At the siege of Jerusalem he called on the defenders to surrender. The Jews wanted him punished, he writes, while the Romans, whenever they lost, blamed his "treachery".

At Rome, about 93, he finished his history of the Jewish people. In it, the ruler Herod kills John the Baptist, "a good man", fearing his hold on the crowds. And of Jesus: "About this time there lived Jesus, a wise man, if it is right to call him a man. For he did astonishing deeds, and taught people who gladly accept the truth." The passage goes on, "He was the Christ", the Messiah the Jews awaited, crucified under Pilate and seen alive on the third day. Elsewhere a high priest has James, "the brother of Jesus, who was called Christ", stoned. Each passage is only a few sentences.

A century and a half later, the scholar Origen cited Josephus on John and James: "And this is the remarkable thing. He did not accept that our Jesus was the Christ. Yet he still bore witness that James was so upright." Origen never cites the passage on Jesus.

Early in the fourth century, Eusebius of Caesarea was the first writer known to quote it, against forged records of Jesus's trial, because here was a historian who was himself a Hebrew. Eusebius also told the fall of Jerusalem, from Josephus, as God's judgment on the Jews for what they did to Christ. Jerome's Latin said only that Jesus was believed to be the Christ.

By 1890 an editor of Eusebius wrote that the passage had been disputed since the sixteenth century, some thinking a Christian copyist had added it, others that it was partly genuine. The lines on John and James, he wrote, were widely accepted.

Josephus ended the story of his own life on the emperors' favor and the charges he had outlived, and left the verdict to his readers: "let others judge of my character."
