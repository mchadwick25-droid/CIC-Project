# Outline: Josephus

Sheet items (S, E, I, D) are in `Source_Sheet.md`, which gives the file and line for each. JW is Whiston's English of Josephus (1859 printing); EH is Eusebius's Church History with McGiffert's notes (1890); CC and OM are Origen in English, OMG Origen's Greek; JV and JVL are Jerome in English and Latin; SOZ is Sozomen; SUET is Suetonius. Josephus's own Greek is not held: every rendering of his words is from Whiston's English.

- **The essence:** Josephus was a priest from Jerusalem who commanded the Jewish forces in Galilee when the Jews rose against Rome. In 67 he gave himself up to the Roman general Vespasian, and he spent the rest of his life at Rome, under the emperors' favour, writing his people's history (E1, E2, E6, S5; Documented, almost all from his own books). In his *Antiquities of the Jews*, finished about 93, he wrote a few lines each on John the Baptist, on James "the brother of Jesus, who was called Christ", and on Jesus himself (E11–E14; the John and James lines Widely Accepted as his). Christians kept and quoted those lines for centuries as a witness from outside the church. The passage on Jesus is the one they argued over. It has been disputed since the sixteenth century, an editor wrote in 1890 (EH-ed 9182–9185). Whether he wrote it as it stands is **Contested** (D2).
- **Also told:**
  - Who he was, and why both sides called him a traitor. He went over in a cave at Jotapata, after his companions resolved to die and he proposed that they die by lot; he and one other were left, and he persuaded that man to live (E3–E5; Documented as his own account, the only one). He told Vespasian he would be emperor; a Roman writer, Suetonius, records that Josephus foretold Vespasian would be emperor and free him (E6; Documented, two witnesses). At Jerusalem he called on the defenders to surrender; the Jews wanted him punished, and the Romans blamed him as a traitor when they lost (E8; Documented as his account).
  - What his books say of the three men. John was "a good man" who called the Jews to virtue and to baptism, and Herod killed him for fear of a rising (E12). James was stoned on the order of a high priest, who was removed for it after three months (E13). The passage on Jesus calls him a wise man, a doer of wonders, "the Christ", crucified under Pilate and seen alive on the third day (E14). Each is only a few sentences (Documented that they are in the text).
  - How Christians used them. Origen, in the third century, cites John and James, and says Josephus "did not accept" Jesus as the Christ (I2; Documented). Eusebius, in the early fourth, is the first known to quote the passage on Jesus, as the word of "one of the Hebrews themselves" (I1; Documented). Jerome's Latin reads "he was believed to be the Christ" (I3; Documented). Eusebius also told the fall of Jerusalem from Josephus's *War* as God's judgment on the Jews for Christ (I6; Documented as Eusebius's use).
  - The argument. By 1890 an editor counted four views: wholly genuine, wholly added, partly genuine, or a hostile passage rewritten as praise (I7; each view credited by name). The eighteenth-century translator of Josephus defended it (I8). No held source says where scholars stand today (sheet, section 6).
- **Length:** about 3 minutes 30 (about 520 words). Why: the slate gives him a fuller life of his own than the Roman witnesses, and the job carries three things that each need a scene: the cave, where he went over; the three passages, told short as they are; and the long Christian use and argument, which is the impact and a third of the story. His own books give the life in vivid detail, so no space goes on guesswork. To cut first: beat 1's sects and the desert teacher, the chain cut to pieces (beat 4), the captives he freed (beat 5), Sozomen (What came 4), and the statue (Leave out already).
- **Setting:**
  - Time: the first century. Born 37 (S2); the war 66–70 (E1–E8); the *Antiquities* finished in "the 13th year of the reign of Caesar Domitian", about 93 (E11). Death not known (D6). Plain words: "the first century", "the great Jewish war against Rome".
  - Place: Jerusalem; Galilee, the hill country to the north; the walled town of Jotapata in Galilee; then Rome, in "his own house" that Vespasian had lived in before he was emperor (S5). Plain words: "Jerusalem", "Galilee in the north", "Rome".
  - Tradition: none. He was a Jew, outside the church (S6). Plain words: "a Jewish historian", "a writer outside the church". Never "a Christian source".

**Words to explain** (the first time each is heard; plain word or gloss of under about 12 words, worked into the sentence)

- **priest**: in his world, "a priest of the Temple in Jerusalem", by birth (S1). Not a Christian minister.
- **Pharisees, Sadducees, Essenes**: "the three main Jewish schools of his day" (S3). Name them only if beat 1 keeps them.
- **Galilee**: "the north of the country".
- **Jotapata**: "a walled town in Galilee".
- **Vespasian, Titus**: "the Roman general Vespasian, later emperor", "his son Titus".
- **sanhedrin**: say "a council of judges" (E13; Whiston's "sanhedrim of judges").
- **high priest**: "the chief priest of the Temple".
- **Antiquities of the Jews**: "his history of the Jewish people, in twenty books" (E11). "Antiquities" alone puzzles: give the gloss once.
- **the Christ**: "the Christ, the Messiah, the anointed king the Jews awaited". Needed for Quote 2, for Origen and for Jerome; the whole argument turns on it.
- **Gentiles**: say "non-Jews" in beat 6's paraphrase (JW 81410–81411).
- **baptism** (of John): "washing in water" (E12). John's washing, in Josephus, was "for the purification of the body"; do not give it the Christian sense.
- **interpolated / interpolation**: never say it. Say "added later by a Christian copyist".
- **manuscripts**: "handwritten copies".
- **Testimonium Flavianum**: never say it. Say "the passage on Jesus".
- **minister** (in Whiston's Quote 1): rendered "servant"; its modern church sense misleads.
- **Church History**: "Eusebius's history of the church".
- **providence**: "God's guiding hand", for beat 3's phrase.
- **Pilate**: "the Roman governor Pilate".

- **Shape:** Outside witness. The trap is making him a friend or a foe of the church, or stretching his lines past what they say. He was neither: a Jewish priest who wrote three short passages that touch the Christians, inside a history of his own people. Say plainly how short they are, and that the one on Jesus is disputed. His fuller life of his own is told because it explains why he wrote and how he wrote, not to make him a hero or a villain.
- **Sibling:** none sharing this story's job. Links on the tree: `eusebius-of-caesarea` (quoted him; his live story never names Josephus) and `origen` (said he did not believe in Jesus; his live story never names Josephus): here each is a scene in What came, and neither story's quotes are used. In batch 6, `suetonius` (job: Claudius and Nero) holds the line on Josephus's prediction, *Vespasian* 5; here it is one clause, credited to Suetonius, and that story's job does not include it. `tacitus` shares no event.
- **Openings:**
  1. **Chosen. The cave at Jotapata, 67.** In about three sentences: the town has fallen, and its commander is hiding in a pit with forty men, found on the third day (E3). Name him in the first sentence the moment allows: Vespasian ordered the officers "to give Josephus their right hands" (JW 110762–110763). He writes that he remembered dreams in which God had shown him the Jews' ruin and what would come to the Roman emperors (JW 110830–110835). He prays in secret, and says he will go over to the Romans, "not as a deserter" but as God's servant (Quote 1; E3, E4). He lived to write his people's history at Rome, and a few lines in it about John, James and Jesus. That leads straight into the essence. The scene's force stays for beat 3: what his companions did next.
  2. Not chosen: the passage on Jesus itself, "a wise man, if it is right to call him a man" (Quote 2). A strong open, but it starts on the argument before the listener knows who wrote it, and the job asks for the man first.

**Story beats** (the opening, the essence and beat 1 are the first minute, about 150 words; beats 2 to 5 deepen it)

1. **A priest's son.** He was born in Jerusalem in 37, into a family of priests, and through his mother, he says, of the old royal line (S1, S2; Documented as his claim). He says the chief priests asked his opinion on the law when he was about fourteen; at sixteen he tried the three Jewish schools, lived three years in the desert with a teacher named Banus, and at nineteen followed the Pharisees (S3; Documented as his own account, "he says"). Optional, cut first.
2. **Commander in Galilee.** When the Jews rose against Rome in 66, the leaders in Jerusalem sent him north to Galilee. His two books tell it differently: in one he is a commander given both Galilees; in the other he and two other priests are sent to persuade armed men to lay down their arms and wait on the Romans (E1, D1; Documented that he commanded; his purpose is told both ways). Vespasian came with an army. Jotapata fell in 67; by his own count, forty thousand died there (E2).
3. **The cave, and the lots.** Back in the cave (the opening has told the officers' promise and the prayer: do not retell them or requote Quote 1). They would die rather than surrender, and offered him a sword to die as their general (E5). His companions called him a coward and turned their swords on him. So he proposed they kill each other by lot. One by one they bared their necks. He and one other were left last, he writes, "whether we must say it happened so by chance, or whether by the providence of God", and he persuaded the man to live (E5; Documented as his own account, the only one). Keep the killing plain.
4. **The prediction, and the chain.** Brought before Vespasian, he asked to speak alone, and told him that he and his son would be emperors; Vespasian at first took it for a trick to save his life (E6). Suetonius, a Roman writer of the next generation, records that Josephus foretold Vespasian would be emperor and free him (E6; two witnesses). In 69 Vespasian was proclaimed emperor (EH-ed 16585–16587). He had the prisoner freed, and on his son Titus's advice the chain was cut to pieces, as for a man wrongly bound (E7; Documented as his account). Optional: the chain.
5. **At the walls, and after.** In 70, at the siege of Jerusalem, Titus sent him round the walls to call on the defenders, in their own language, to save themselves, their country and their temple (E8). He writes that the Jews wanted him punished, and the Romans, whenever they lost, blamed his "treachery" and called for him as a traitor (E8; Documented as his account). When the city fell he asked for his family's freedom and for the holy books, and freed about 190 captive friends (E9). The city fell in 70 (EH-ed 15275). Optional: the captives.
6. **Rome, and the books.** Vespasian gave him rooms in the house he had lived in before he was emperor, Roman citizenship and a yearly pension (S5). He wrote the *Jewish War*, first in his own language and then in Greek, and then the *Antiquities of the Jews*, in twenty books, finished in his fifty-sixth year, about 93 (E10, E11). In it, three short passages. John the Baptist was "a good man" who called the Jews to virtue and to washing in water; Herod feared his hold on the crowds, imprisoned him in a fortress and killed him (E12). James, "the brother of Jesus, who was called Christ", was tried before a council by a high priest and stoned, and those who cared for the law protested until the high priest was removed after three months (E13). And Jesus (Quote 2; E14): a wise man and teacher, followed by many Jews and non-Jews; "He was the Christ"; crucified under Pilate on the accusation of "the principal men among us"; seen alive on the third day by those who loved him; and the Christians, named after him, still not gone. Say each is only a few sentences (E12–E14).

**What came of him** (about a third of the story; heaviest first. Items 1 to 3 are the spine; 4 is one line. Tell them in time order (Origen, then Eusebius); the ranking is weight, not order.)

1. **Eusebius made the passage on Jesus famous.** In the early fourth century, Eusebius of Caesarea quoted all three passages in his *Church History*, and is the first writer known to quote the one on Jesus (I1; McGiffert's statement, EH-ed 9214, and Origen's silence agrees). He used it against forged records of Jesus's trial, recently spread by the church's enemies (EH 8928–8929; the emperor Maximinus's approval is McGiffert's note, pointing to Eusebius's own Book IX): here was "an historian, who is one of the Hebrews themselves" (I1). He also took the fall of Jerusalem from Josephus's *War* and told it as God's judgment on the Jews for what they did to Christ (I6). Tell this plainly and credit it to Eusebius; do not soften it. **Link: `eusebius-of-caesarea`, quoted his book.** Defended: the faith's account of Jesus, against the forged records.
2. **Origen read him as a witness who did not believe.** About a hundred and fifty years later, Origen, the scholar of Alexandria and Caesarea, cited Josephus on John and on James, against a pagan critic (CC 37926–37948). He found it remarkable that Josephus, who did not accept Jesus as the Christ, still praised James (Quote 3; I2). He also cites a line, that Jerusalem fell to avenge James, which is not in the *Antiquities* as we have it (I2, D3). He never cites the passage on Jesus (I2; that this means it was not in his copy is McGiffert's inference, EH-ed 9190–9192). **Link: `origen`, quoted his book.** Defended: that the fall of Jerusalem was for Jesus's death, not James's (CC 37933–37937). If told, credit it to Origen with Eusebius's I6; else Leave out: 'Origen's own reading of the fall, the same judgment as Eusebius's, told once there.'
3. **The argument.** Jerome's Latin, later in the fourth century, does not say "He was the Christ" but "he was believed to be the Christ" (I3). A Syriac chronicle of the twelfth century, by the patriarch Michael the Syrian, gives the passage with 'he was thought to be the Messiah' (Michael, 11233). Optional, one clause; aloud, "a Syriac chronicle" is enough (I9). McGiffert, in 1890, wrote that the passage had been thought genuine until the sixteenth century, and had been "constantly disputed" since (EH-ed 9182–9185). He counted four views: wholly genuine, which he called untenable; wholly added by a Christian, with Hase, Keim and Schürer, and himself; partly genuine, perhaps the most common, with Gieseler, Weizsäcker, Renan, Edersheim and Schaff; and Ewald's, a hostile Jewish passage turned into praise (I7). He did not think Eusebius forged it (I7). William Whiston, who translated Josephus in the eighteenth century, defended it as genuine (I8). By McGiffert's account, the James phrase was "the common opinion of critics" to be genuine (E13), and the John passage "almost universally admitted" (E12); credit both to him. The only held view after 1890 is a one-line note by Michael's editor, Chabot (1899), that a Christian certainly added to the original mention, citing Reinach (1897, not held) (I9); credit it to Chabot, as his view, Contested. Credit each view; the narrator takes no side. Nothing held after 1899. No held source gives today's balance.
4. **One line, optional: Sozomen.** A church historian of the fifth century wrote that Josephus "all but proclaims" that Christ is God (I5). Credited as Sozomen's reading.

Defended (Josephus): his own conduct against the charge of treachery (JW 5334–5345, 5470–5478), and that the Jews' war 'was not voluntary' but forced on them (JW 1655–1658).

**Quotes** (3)

Only Quotes 1–3 are quoted aloud, plus these short phrases: "a good man" (John, JW 82092), "the brother of Jesus, who was called Christ" (JW 91005–91008), "He was the Christ" (JW 81411), "whether we must say it happened so by chance, or whether by the providence of God" (beat 3, JW 111126–111128), "treachery" (beat 5, JW 5342), and, for the ending, "let others judge of my character" (JW 5485–5486). Jerome's "he was believed to be the Christ" may be said as his Latin's sense. Tell the rest in paraphrase.

1. Whiston's English (JW 110853–110856): "And I protest openly, that I do not go over to the Romans as a deserter of the Jews, but as a minister from thee." Rendering (from Whiston's English; the Greek is not held): "I declare it openly: I do not go over to the Romans as a deserter from the Jews, but as a servant sent by you." ("Minister" is not used: its church sense misleads. "You" is God; the narrator says it is a prayer.) Job: the opening. The moment he went over, in his own words, and his own reason for it.
2. Whiston's English (JW 81405–81408): "Now, there was about this time Jesus, a wise man, if it be lawful to call him a man, for he was a doer of wonderful works,—a teacher of such men as receive the truth with pleasure." Rendering (from Whiston's English; the Greek is not held): "About this time there lived Jesus, a wise man, if it is right to call him a man. For he did astonishing deeds, and taught people who gladly accept the truth." ("Lawful" and "wonderful" are not used: their modern senses mislead.) Then, as a short phrase, "He was the Christ" (JW 81411). Job: in beat 6, the passage at the centre of the argument, in its own words, so the listener hears why it is doubted.
3. Origen's Greek (OMG 3731–3733): "Καὶ τὸ θαυμαστόν ἐστιν, ὅτι τὸν Ἰησοῦν ἡμῶν οὐ καταδεξάμενος εἶναι Χριστὸν, οὐδὲν ἧττον Ἰακώβῳ δικαιοσύνην ἐμαρτύρησε τοσαύτην." (ANF English, OM 30627–30629: "And the wonderful thing is, that, though he did not accept Jesus as Christ, he yet gave testimony that the righteousness of James was so great.") Rendering (from the Greek): "And this is the remarkable thing. He did not accept that our Jesus was the Christ. Yet he still bore witness that James was so upright." Job: in What came 2. The first Christian reader on record, telling us Josephus did not believe, which is the seed of the whole argument.

**Connection to carry into the story:** what passed from Josephus to the church was three short passages in a Jewish history, which Christians copied and quoted as testimony from outside their own faith (I1, I2). The listener must understand three things. First, why it mattered: here was a Jew, a priest, not a Christian, who named John, James and Jesus, so Christians used him to answer their opponents; Eusebius did so against forged records of Jesus's trial (I1). Second, what was argued: the passage on Jesus says "He was the Christ", yet Origen, who read Josephus a century and a half after he wrote, two generations before Eusebius, says he did not believe it, and never quotes that passage; Jerome's Latin reads "believed to be" (I2, I3). From this came the long question of whether a Christian copyist added to it, set out with each view credited (I7, I8). Third, that the John and James passages are not in the same doubt (E12, E13). Tell it through what Origen, Eusebius and Jerome did with the text. No "this matters because" sentence.

**Ending:** close on Josephus himself. His *Life*, his account of himself, ends on the favour of the emperors and the accusations he survived, and then: "this is the account of the actions of my whole life; and let others judge of my character by them as they please" (JW 5484–5487). A narrator may say he left the judging to others, and they have done it ever since, of him and of his lines on Jesus: but end on his words, not on a moral. If a line on his death is wanted: when he died is not known; an editor puts it early in the second century (D6).

**Who tells it**

- **Josephus himself**, in the *Life*, the *War* and the *Antiquities*. Almost the whole life rests on him, and he wrote partly to defend himself against the charge of treachery. Say so once, inside a sentence about his books, at beat 3 or 5: the story of the cave is his own, and no other account of it exists (E5). Do not say no one else survived: one other man did. Credit with "he says" or "he writes" for his boyhood praise, the dreams, how the lots fell, and both sides' charges.
- **Suetonius**, a Roman writer of the next generation, credited beside the prediction (E6).
- **Origen**, about 150 years later, credited beside "did not accept" and the James line (I2).
- **Eusebius**, more than two centuries later, credited beside the first quotation of the passage on Jesus, its use against the forged records, and the fall of Jerusalem as judgment (I1, I6).
- **Jerome**, three centuries later, credited beside "believed to be the Christ" (I3).
- **An editor of 1890 (McGiffert)** credited beside "constantly disputed" and the four views; **Whiston**, the eighteenth-century translator, beside the defence (I7, I8); **Chabot**, the editor of Michael's chronicle, in 1899, beside his note (I9).
- The doubt is itself part of the story: What came 3 is the scene. Origen's remark (Quote 3) sets it up in What came 2.

**Do not say**

- Any word in "Words to explain" used without its plain sense the first time it is heard.
- Not "Josephus confirms Jesus was the Messiah", "Josephus believed in Jesus", or "Josephus was secretly a Christian". Origen says he did not accept Jesus as the Christ (I2). Whiston's Ebionite theory is his own, and is left out (I8).
- Not "the passage on Jesus is a forgery" or "the passage is genuine" as the narrator's view. Say it is disputed, and credit each view to its holder (I7, I8, D2).
- Not "most scholars today think it is partly genuine", or any modern balance. No held source says it; McGiffert's "perhaps the most defenders" is his 1890 count (I7).
- Not "Eusebius forged it". It "has been suggested"; McGiffert thought it possible but without reason (I7).
- Not "the earliest outside witness to Jesus" as a settled fact. Say "a Jewish historian of the first century", and leave rankings with Tacitus out.
- Keep "the brother of Jesus, who was called Christ" whole; do not trim to "the brother of Jesus" (E13).
- Not "Josephus says Jerusalem fell because of James" as his text. That is Origen's and Eusebius's citation; it is not in our *Antiquities* (D3).
- Not "Josephus says the Pharisees killed Jesus". That is Jerome's addition (D4). Josephus has "the principal men among us" and Pilate (E14).
- Not "Josephus says Herod killed John for marrying Herodias". In Josephus, Herod feared a rising (E12). (Eusebius says Herod killed John on Herodias's account, EH 9115–9116; that is Eusebius, not Josephus. Leave it out.)
- Not "Josephus betrayed his men" or "Josephus rigged the lots" as fact. He gives "chance, or ... the providence of God" (E5). Say "he writes"; do not add a verdict.
- Not "a hero" or "a traitor" as the narrator's word. The charge of treachery is both sides', as he reports it (E8).
- Not "general" and "peacemaker" as one settled story. His two books disagree on what he was sent to Galilee to do (D1).
- "Forty thousand" dead at Jotapata is his own count: "by his own count" (E2).
- Not "Josephus foresaw Vespasian's rise" as fact. He says he did; Suetonius records that he predicted it; Vespasian first thought it a trick (E6).
- Not "Flavius, the emperors' family name, which he took". No held source says when or why he took it; say "Josephus".
- Not "he wrote in Greek" alone for the *War*. He wrote it first in his own language, then in Greek (E10).
- Not "the *Antiquities* in 93" as an exact year without "about": "about 93".
- Not "he died in 100" or any year (D6).
- Do not quote Tacitus or Suetonius on the Christians; they are their own stories.
- Keep Eusebius's use of the fall of Jerusalem with its force: "God's judgment on the Jews" for Christ, credited to Eusebius (I6). Do not soften it to "a lesson" or leave out "the Jews".
- Not "Origen said the passage was fake". He never mentions it (I2).
- Not 'Michael proves the original said "thought to be"'. Say only that his copy reads so.
- Not that Suetonius names Titus.
- Not 'King Herod'. This is Herod the ruler of Galilee, not the king of the Nativity. Say 'the ruler Herod' (EH-ed 8889, "Antipas ... was Tetrarch").
- Not 'James the apostle'. That is Jerome's word (JV 39724). Josephus has 'the brother of Jesus, who was called Christ'.
- Not 'disputed still', 'still argued', 'to this day' or any present-tense word for the debate. No held source is later than 1899 (Mark, 2026-11-19: 'no claim of a current verdict'). Say 'disputed since the sixteenth century, an editor wrote in 1890'.
- Not the doubting views alone. If the 1890 views are told, include that a few held it wholly genuine (EH-ed 9185–9187), or Whiston's defence (I8).

**Leave out**

- The shipwreck on his first voyage to Rome (S4): a side story.
- His marriages and divorces (JW 5327–5333, 5443–5458): no bearing on the job.
- The three crucified friends (E9): strong, but a second rescue story crowds beat 5.
- The statue at Rome (I4): only Eusebius says it, and an editor can find no authority for it.
- The ambiguous oracle of the *War*, which he applied to Vespasian (JW 126449–126458): it would tell the prediction twice.
- McGiffert's verdict on his character, "flatterer" and "selfishness" (D5): an editor's judgement, for the card.
- Whiston's view that Josephus was a Jewish Christian of the Ebionite kind (I8): his theory; at most "he defended it as genuine".
- The Arabic and Old Russian versions of the passage: not held.
- Photius, Malalas and the later chroniclers (Library sweep): they add no use of the three passages.
