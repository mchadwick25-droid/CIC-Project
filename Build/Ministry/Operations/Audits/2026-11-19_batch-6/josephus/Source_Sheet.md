# Source sheet: Josephus

Researcher's sheet for batch 6 (2026-11-19). The job: a Jewish priest and commander who went over to Rome and wrote his people's history; his lines on John the Baptist, James and Jesus, and how Christians kept them and argued over the passage on Jesus. Shape: Outside witness. The writer uses the outline, the shapes and the standard only; this sheet is the evidence behind the outline.

- LATER, about 300 years = **JV/JVL**: Jerome, *Illustrious Men* 13 (CEN `jerome`, 374–420).
- LATER, about 350 years = **SOZ**: Sozomen (CEN, "c. 370-380 to after 443").

## 1. Setting

S1. **Priest's family, Jerusalem.** "THz [The] family from which I am derived is not an ignoble one, but hath descended all along from the priests" (JW 1384–1386, *Life* 1). He is "of the chief family of that first course" of the twenty-four priestly courses (JW 1393–1397), and "by my mother I am of the royal blood" of the Hasmoneans (JW 1398–1402). OWN. Documented as his own claim. Plain words: "a priest's son from Jerusalem", "a family of priests".

S2. **Born 37.** "as was I born to Matthias in the first year of the reign of Caius Cesar" (JW 1427–1428). McGiffert: "the year beginning March 16, 37 a.d." (EH-ed 15892–15893). Documented. CEN `dates`: "37 to early 2nd c."

S3. **Learning and the sects.** At about fourteen "the high-priests and principal men of the city came then frequently to me ... in order to know my opinion about the accurate understanding of points of the law" (JW 1443–1454). At sixteen he tried "the several sects" — "the Pharisees, ... the Sadducees, and ... the Essenes" (JW 1456–1463); spent three years with a desert ascetic named Banus (JW 1473–1482); and at nineteen "began to conduct myself according to the rules of the sect of the Pharisees" (JW 1483–1486). OWN; the praise of his boyhood is his own. Documented as his claim.

S4. **Rome before the war.** In his twenty-sixth year he sailed to Rome to free some priests sent there in bonds; his ship sank in the Adriatic and about 600 swam all night, and eighty were saved (JW 1503–1530). OWN. Documented as his account. Side story.

S5. **Rome at the end.** Vespasian "gave me an apartment in his own house, which he lived in before he came to the empire. He also honoured me with the privilege of a Roman citizen, and gave me an annual pension" (JW 5407–5413). Titus and Domitian kept the favour (JW 5463–5478). OWN. Documented as his account. Plain words: "Rome, in the emperor's own former house".

S6. **Tradition.** None: CEN `toldWithin` is null; CEN `label` (CARD) "Outside witness", `role` "Jewish historian". The corpus map files him as "Outside witness, not the church's own voice: a first-century Jewish civil historian ... Context only, never tradition" (`cic/corpus-map/alexandria-catechetical.yaml` 4891–4894, and the same row in four other buckets). Plain words: "a Jewish historian", "outside the church".

## 2. Events

E1. **Sent to Galilee, 66.** Two accounts in his own works:
- *Life*: the leading men of Jerusalem "sent me and two others of the priests ... in order to persuade the ill men there to lay down their arms" and to keep arms ready "but still so, that they should wait to see what the Romans would do" (JW 1659–1680).
- *War*: "Josephus, the son of Matthias, of both the Galilees. Gamala also ... was put under his command", in a list of men "sent as general" (JW 106915–106928).
OWN, the two accounts differ in what he was sent to do (D1). Documented that he commanded in Galilee; his purpose there is Contested between his own two books. McGiffert: "governor of Galilee and commander of the forces there, of meeting and opposing Vespasian" (EH-ed 15894–15896).

E2. **Jotapata falls, 67.** "And thus was Jotapata taken, in the thirteenth year of the reign of Nero, on the first day of the month Panemus [Tamuz]" (JW 110707–110710). His figures: "as for those that were slain ... they were numbered to be 40,000", and 1,200 captives (JW 110699–110704). McGiffert: he "gave himself up to the victors, in the summer of 67" (EH-ed 15896–15897). OWN; the numbers are his. Documented (event); the figures are his own count.

E3. **The cave.** He leapt into a pit beside a hidden den "and here he met with forty persons of eminence that had concealed themselves" (JW 110736–110742). On the third day a woman who had been with them was taken, and he was found (JW 110751–110753). Vespasian sent tribunes, then Nicanor, "his familiar acquaintance in old time", to promise his life (JW 110754–110776). He recalled "the dreams which he had dreamed in the night-time, whereby God had signified to him beforehand both the future calamities of the Jews, and the events that concerned the Roman emperors", being "a priest himself, and of the posterity of priests" (JW 110829–110842). Then "he put up a secret prayer to God" (JW 110845; full text E4). OWN, told of himself in the third person. Documented as his account; the dreams and their meaning are his own claim.

E4. **The prayer (Quote 1).** "Since it pleaseth thee, who hast created the Jewish nation, to depress the same, and since all their good fortune is gone over to the Romans; and since thou hast made choice of this soul of mine to foretell what is to come to pass hereafter, I willingly give them my hands, and am content to live. And I protest openly, that I do not go over to the Romans as a deserter of the Jews, but as a minister from thee." (JW 110846–110856). OWN. Documented as his words about himself. English only (Greek not held).

E5. **The companions and the lots.** The men with him cried out: "O Josephus! art thou still fond of life; and canst thou bear to see the light in a state of slavery?" and offered him "our right hand and a sword", to die "as general of the Jews" (JW 110871–110897). After his speech against suicide "they ran upon him with their swords ... and called him a coward" (JW 111060–111066). He proposed death by lot: "He whom the lot falls to first, let him be killed by him that hath the second lot" (JW 111104–111110). "He who had the first lot laid his neck bare to him that had the next" (JW 111115–111117). "Yet was he with another left to the last, whether we must say it happened so by chance, or whether by the providence of God", and he "persuaded him to trust his fidelity to him, and to live as well as himself" (JW 111124–111133). OWN, and the only account. Documented as his account. The killing is told plainly; how the lots fell is his own telling, with his own "chance, or ... providence".

E6. **Before Vespasian: the prediction.** Asking to speak alone, he said: "Thou, O Vespasian, art Caesar [Ceesar] and emperor, thou, and this thy son. Bind me now still faster, and keep me for thyself" (JW 111175–111187). Vespasian "did not believe him, but supposed that Josephus said this as a cunning trick, in order to his own preservation" (JW 111194–111197). He kept him in bonds but gave him clothes and gifts (JW 111240–111246). OWN. Suetonius, a Roman, confirms the prediction as told: "one of his high-born prisoners, Josephus by name, as he was being put in chains, declared most confidently that he would soon be released by the same man, who would then, however, be emperor" (SUET 36662–36665; Latin 36617–36619, "unus ex nobilibus captivis Iosephus, cum coiceretur in vincula, constantissime asseveravit fore ut ab eodem brevi solveretur, verum iam imperatore"). Documented that he made the prediction (two witnesses). Whether he foresaw it, or said it to save himself, is the question Vespasian himself raised (E6, his own text).

E7. **The chain cut, 69.** When Vespasian became emperor he remembered "what Josephus had said to him when he ventured to foretell his coming to the empire while Nero was alive; so he was much concerned that this man was still in bonds" (JW 117836–117841). Titus said that "if we do not barely loose his bonds, but cut them to pieces, he will be like a man that hath never been bound at all"; "so there came a man in, and cut the chain to pieces" (JW 117870–117879). OWN. Documented as his account.

E8. **At the walls of Jerusalem, 70.** Titus "sent Josephus to speak to them in their own language" (JW 121492–121493). "So Josephus went round about the wall ... and besought them, in many words, to spare themselves, to spare their country and their temple" (JW 121497–121502). In his *Life*: he "was frequently in danger of being put to death, while both the Jews were very desirous to get me under their power, in order to have me punished; and the Romans also, whenever they were beaten, supposed that it was occasioned by my treachery, and ... desired that they would bring me to punishment, as a traitor to them" (JW 5334–5345). OWN. Documented as his account. The force: both sides held him a traitor.

E9. **After the fall.** Offered what he wanted from "the ruins of my country", he asked that "my family might have their liberty", and had "the holy books by Titus's concession"; he freed about 190 captive friends from the Temple; and of three acquaintances he found crucified, Titus had them taken down, "yet two of them died under the physician's hands, while the third recovered" (JW 5347–5393). OWN. Documented as his account.

E10. **The War, first in his own tongue.** He undertook "to translate those books into the Greek tongue, which I formerly composed in the language of our country" (JW 91747–91751). His self-introduction: "I, Joseph, the son of Matthias, by birth a Hebrew, a priest also, and one who at first fought against the Romans myself, and was forced to be present at what was done afterward, [am the author of this work.]" (JW 91776–91781, *War*, preface). OWN. Documented. McGiffert: the original was "Aramaic" (EH-ed 15960–15963); Eusebius inverts the order (EH-ed 15963–15964).

E11. **The Antiquities, about 93.** "these Antiquities, which are contained in 20 books, and 60,000 verses" (JW 91627–91629), brought down "to this very day, which is the 13th year of the reign of Caesar Domitian, and the 56th of my own life" (JW 91662–91666). McGiffert: its object "is mainly apologetic, the author aiming to place Judaism before Gentile readers in as favorable a light as possible" (EH-ed 15930–15933; editor's view). Documented (date, his own words).

E12. **John the Baptist (Antiquities XVIII).** "Now some of the Jews thought that the destruction of Herod's army came from God, and that very justly, as a punishment of what he did against John, that was called the Baptist; for Herod slew him, who was a good man, and commanded the Jews to exercise virtue, both as to righteousness toward one another, and piety toward God, and so to come to baptism" (JW 82087–82095). Herod "feared lest the great influence John had over the people might put it into his power and inclination to raise a rebellion" (JW 82104–82107); John "was sent a prisoner ... to Macherus, the castle before mentioned, and was there put to death" (JW 82115–82118). OWN. Documented in the text; genuine: Widely Accepted. McGiffert: "found in all our mss. of Josephus. It is almost universally admitted to be genuine" (EH-ed 9141–9143). McGiffert also notes Josephus "differs with the Evangelists as to the reason for John's imprisonment" (EH-ed 9164–9165). About nine printed sentences.

E13. **James (Antiquities XX).** With the governor Festus dead and Albinus "but upon the road", the high priest Ananus, "a bold man in his temper, and very insolent ... of the sect of the Sadducees" (JW 90991–90995), "assembled the sanhedrim of judges, and brought before them the brother of Jesus, who was called Christ, whose name was James, and some others [or some of his companions]; and, when he had formed an accusation against them as breakers of the law, he delivered them to be stoned" (JW 91002–91010). Those "most uneasy at the breach of the laws ... disliked what was done" and complained to King Agrippa and to Albinus (JW 91010–91041), and Agrippa "took the high-priesthood from him, when he had ruled but three months" (JW 91044–91046). OWN. Documented in the text. The phrase naming James: Widely Accepted as genuine. McGiffert: "Some critics regard the bracketed words as spurious, but Neander ... contends for their genuineness, and this is now the common opinion of critics", since a Christian would hardly write "so-called Christ" (EH-ed 13605–13612). Date "61 or 62 a.d." (EH-ed 13615–13617).

E14. **Jesus (Antiquities XVIII; Quote 2).** "Now, there was about this time Jesus, a wise man, if it be lawful to call him a man, for he was a doer of wonderful works,—a teacher of such men as receive the truth with pleasure. He drew over to him both many of the Jews and many of the Gentiles. He was [the] Christ; and when Pilate, at the suggestion of the principal men among us, had condemned him to the cross, those that loved him at the first did not forsake him, for he appeared to them alive again the third day, as the divine prophets had foretold these and ten thousand other wonderful things concerning him; and the tribe of Christians, so named from him, are not extinct at this day." (JW 81405–81421). OWN as printed; how much is his is **Contested** (D2). McGiffert: "It is found in all our mss. of Josephus, and was considered genuine until the sixteenth century, but since then has been constantly disputed" (EH-ed 9182–9185). Six printed sentences; it sits between an account of a riot and another "sad calamity" (JW 81395–81403, 81423–81425).

## 3. Impact, heaviest first (what later witnesses carried on and used)

I1. **Eusebius quoted all three passages, and was first with the one on Jesus (early fourth century).** Church History I.11 gives the John passage (EH 9146–9160) and the Jesus passage: "And there lived at that time Jesus, a wise man, if indeed it be proper to call him a man. ... He was the Christ" (EH 9229–9232, 9235–9241). His use of it: "Since an historian, who is one of the Hebrews themselves, has recorded in his work these things concerning John the Baptist and our Saviour, what excuse is there left for not convicting them of being destitute of all shame, who have forged the acts against them?" (EH 9242–9246). "Them" are the anti-Christian Acts of Pilate, "forged by their enemies with the approval of the emperor Maximinus" (EH-ed 8928–8932; EH 8928–8935, I.9). In II.23 he gives the James passage (EH 13543–13618). McGiffert: "Eusebius himself, who is the first one to quote this passage" (EH-ed 9214–9215). LATER, about 220 years. Documented (that he quoted it and why).

I2. **Origen knew the John and James passages, said Josephus did not believe in Jesus, and never cites the Jesus passage (mid-third century).**
- *Against Celsus* I.47: "Josephus bears witness to John as having been a Baptist, and as promising purification to those who underwent the rite. Now this writer, although not believing in Jesus as the Christ, in seeking after the cause of the fall of Jerusalem ... says nevertheless—being, although against his will, not far from the truth—that these disasters happened to the Jews as a punishment for the death of James the Just" (CC 37931–37942). Also CC 39893–39897 (II.13).
- *Commentary on Matthew* X.17, Greek (Quote 3): "Καὶ τὸ θαυμαστόν ἐστιν, ὅτι τὸν Ἰησοῦν ἡμῶν οὐ καταδεξάμενος εἶναι Χριστὸν, οὐδὲν ἧττον Ἰακώβῳ δικαιοσύνην ἐμαρτύρησε τοσαύτην" (OMG 3729–3732, OCR: "Καὶ τὸ 4) ϑαυμαστόν ἔστιν; ὅτι τὸν Ἴη- σοῦν ἡμῶν οὐ καταδεξάμενος εἶναι Χριστὸν, οὐδὲν ἣτ- τον ᾿Ιαχώβῳ δικαιοσύνην ἐμαρτύρησε τοσαύτην"; footnote marker "4)" removed). ANF English: "And the wonderful thing is, that, though he did not accept Jesus as Christ, he yet gave testimony that the righteousness of James was so great" (OM 30627–30629; the ANF English leaves out ἡμῶν, "our").
- The line Origen cites on James and the fall of Jerusalem is not in our Antiquities: McGiffert, "This passage is not found in our existing mss. of Josephus, but is given by Origen ... which shows at any rate that Eusebius did not invent the words" (EH-ed 13545–13549; Eusebius repeats it, EH 13543–13544, 13561–13564). Documented that Origen says it; the line itself is not in the held text (D3).
- McGiffert's argument from silence: "Origen, for instance, who mentions Josephus' testimony to John the Baptist in Contra Cels. I. 47, betrays no knowledge of this passage in regard to Christ" (EH-ed 9190–9192). Editor's inference. LATER, about 150 years.

I3. **Jerome's Latin: "he was believed to be the Christ" (392 or after).** *Illustrious Men* 13: "et credebatur esse Christus" (JVL 50907), English "and was believed to be Christ" (JV 39729). Jerome also says Josephus "most openly acknowledges that Christ was slain by the Pharisees on account of the greatness of his miracles", and that Jerusalem fell "because of the murder of James the apostle" (JV 39720–39723; JVL 50852–50857, "propter magnitudinem signorum Christum a Phariseis interfectum"); neither is in the held text of Josephus. The NPNF English reads "eighth book"; the Latin reads "decimo octavo", eighteenth (JVL 50852; JV 39720). The editors of JVL note that learned men admit the Greek "ὁ Χριστὸς οὗτος ἦν" is not to be understood "ut sonant", as it sounds, but in the author's sense, "quem optime Hieronymus expressit dicens: Et credebatur esse Christus" (JVL-ed 50947–50952). LATER, about 300 years. Documented (Jerome's wording); the meaning of the difference is the editors' view.

I4. **Jerome and Eusebius: a statue at Rome.** Eusebius: "honored by the erection of a statue in Rome, and his works were deemed worthy of a place in the library" (EH 15915–15919); Jerome repeats it (JV 39710–39713; JVL 50839–50842). McGiffert: "Eusebius is the only one, so far as we know, to mention this statue in Rome, and what authority there is for his statement we cannot tell" (EH-ed 15917–15919). Inferential-Thin.

I5. **Sozomen: almost a believer (fifth century).** Josephus "may be regarded as a noteworthy witness to the truth concerning Christ; for he hesitates to call Him a man ... but openly calls him Christ ... It appears to me that in narrating these things, he all but proclaims that Christ, by comparison of works, is God. As if struck by the miracle, he ran, somehow, a middle course, assailing in no way those who believed in Jesus, but rather agreeing with them." (SOZ 26287–26304). LATER, about 350 years. Documented as Sozomen's reading.

I6. **Eusebius built the fall of Jerusalem on Josephus's War, as God's judgment.** "the judgment of God at length overtook those who had committed such outrages against Christ and his apostles, and totally destroyed that generation of impious men" (EH 15301–15303); for the details, "any one that wishes may find accurately described in the history written by Josephus" (EH 15317–15318); and the Passover crowd shut up "as in a prison" should "meet with destruction at the hands of divine justice" (EH 15330–15338). He quotes the *War* on the famine at length (EH 15361 onward). Documented as Eusebius's use. Its force is anti-Jewish: tell it plainly and credit it to Eusebius.

I7. **The argument, as an editor of 1890 saw it.** McGiffert lists "Four opinions": "(1) It is entirely genuine. This view has at present few supporters, and is absolutely untenable. ... (2) It is entirely spurious. Such writers as Hase, Keim, and Schürer adopt this view. (3) It is partly genuine and partly interpolated. This opinion has, perhaps, the most defenders, among them Gieseler, Weizsäcker, Renan, Edersheim, and Schaff. (4) It has been changed from a bitter Jewish calumny of Christ to a Christian eulogy of him. This is Ewald's view. The second opinion seems to me the correct one." (EH-ed 9185–9199). His reasons: "A Christian hand is unmistakably apparent", the silence of Christian writers before Eusebius (EH-ed 9187–9190), and "the decided break which the passage makes in the context" (EH-ed 9206–9213). On Eusebius forging it: "This is possible, but there is no reason to suppose it true", since "he would scarcely have dared to insert so important an account in his History had it not existed in at least some mss." (EH-ed 9214–9223). Editor's view, 1890. Contested.

I8. **Whiston, the eighteenth-century translator, defended it.** The passage "has of late been greatly questioned by many, and rejected by some of the learned as spurious", while he has "ever declared my firm belief that these testimonies were genuine" (JW-ed 138244–138250). He reads "This was Christ" as naming which Jesus was meant, not as a confession that he was "the Christ of God" (JW-ed 139286–139298), and holds that Josephus was in mind "a Nazarene or Ebionite Jewish Christian" (JW-ed 139427–139447). Editor's view. Contested.

## 4. Links to other people on the tree

- **Eusebius of Caesarea** (CEN `eusebius-of-caesarea`): quoted his book; first known to quote the Jesus passage (I1). His live story tells his Church History, Nicaea and Tyre, and never names Josephus.
- **Origen** (CEN `origen`): quoted his book; said he did not believe in Jesus as the Christ (I2). Origen's live story tells his life and Scripture work, and never names Josephus.
- **Jerome** (CEN `jerome`): wrote his entry in *Illustrious Men* (I3). Optional link.
- **Sozomen** (CEN `sozomen`): read the passage as almost belief (I5). Optional link.
- **Suetonius** (CEN `suetonius`): records his prediction to Vespasian (E6). Batch 6 sibling, whose job is Claudius and Nero; Vespasian 5 is not in its job.
- **Tacitus** (CEN `tacitus`): batch 6 sibling; his old live story names Josephus once, through McGiffert. No shared event.
- James, John the Baptist and Jesus: not census persons; named because Josephus writes of them.

## 5. Where sources disagree or come late

D1. **What he was sent to Galilee to do.** The *Life* says to persuade rebels to lay down their arms and wait on the Romans (JW 1659–1680); the *War* lists him among the generals sent out, in command of both Galilees (JW 106915–106928). Both are his. Tell both, or say only "he commanded in Galilee", Documented.

D2. **The passage on Jesus.** In all the manuscripts (EH-ed 9182–9183); first quoted by Eusebius (EH-ed 9214); Origen, who read the Antiquities, says Josephus did not accept Jesus as Christ (CC 37933–37934; OMG 3729–3732), which sits badly with "He was [the] Christ" (JW 81410); Jerome's Latin reads "he was believed to be the Christ" (JVL 50907). McGiffert's four views, 1890 (I7); Whiston's defence (I8). **Contested.** No held source gives today's scholarly balance (see section 6).

D3. **James and the fall of Jerusalem.** Origen and Eusebius cite Josephus as saying Jerusalem fell to avenge James (CC 37938–37942; EH 13561–13564). Our text of Antiquities XX does not say so (JW 91002–91048); McGiffert says the line is not in our manuscripts (EH-ed 13545–13546). Documented that it is absent; why it is absent is the editors' guess.

D4. **Jerome's additions.** That the Pharisees killed Christ for his miracles, and "James the apostle" (JV 39720–39723): not in the held text. Jerome's, not Josephus's.

D5. **Character.** McGiffert: "a favorite and flatterer of the Vespasian house, incurring thereby the everlasting contempt of his country men" and "of inordinate selfishness and self-esteem", yet "a historian of unusual ability and conscientiousness" (EH-ed 15899–15910). Editor's judgement, for the card only.

D6. **His death.** Not known. McGiffert: "lived in prosperity there until early in the second century" (EH-ed 15900–15902). NPNF note on Jerome: "died after 97" (JV-ed 39708). No held source gives a year.
