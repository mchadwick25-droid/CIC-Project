## 1. Setting

S1. **Who he was.** McGiffert: "Plinius Cæcilius Secundus, commonly called “Pliny the younger” to distinguish him from his uncle, Plinius Secundus the elder, was a man of great literary attainments and an intimate friend of the Emperor Trajan. Of his literary remains the most important are his epistles, collected in ten books." (EU-ed 19451–19455). EDITOR. Widely Accepted. Trajan's reply calls him "mi Secunde", "my Secundus" (ML 2809). The census: role "Roman governor"; no held primary source gives his birth or death (CEN, card `uncertain`).

S2. **Where and when.** "The epistle was written from Bithynia, probably within a year after Pliny became governor there, which was in 110 or 111." (EU-ed 19457–19459). Mirbt heads the documents "Kaiser Trajan und die Christen (111 — 113)" (ML 2758); Harnack "ep. 96 c. ann. 111/13" (HA 8820); Schanz "ungefähr in die Zeit von 111/13" (SH 6071). The census `activeYear` is 112. Plain words: "about the year 112", or "early in the second century". Exact year: Contested (110–113).
The province: Harnack names it "Bithynien und den Pontus, zwei kleinasiatische Provinzen" (HA 8814–8815) and "der Provinz Bithynia et Pontus" (HA 8851–8852), and thinks the letter was probably written in the east of it (HA 8851–8853; editor's guess). Census place: Bithynia, modern Turkey (CEN `modernCountries`). Plain words: "Bithynia, on the Black Sea coast of what is now Turkey". ("Black Sea" is in Harnack's note on the Pontic cities "am Schwarzen Meer", HA 8848.)

S3. **Christians there before him.** The First Letter of Peter is addressed to the "Dispersion in Pontus, Galatia, Cappadocia, Asia, and Bithynia" (WEB 94902). Harnack: Christians were found there early, but the New Testament tells us no more of them (HA 8815–8818). Pliny's own letter: some said they had left the faith "quidam ante triennium, quidam ante plures annos, non nemo etiam ante viginti" (ML 2787–2788), "some three years, some several years, and one even twenty years before" (EU-ed 19489–19490). OWN. Documented.

S4. **His ban on clubs.** The shared meal had stopped "post edictum meum, quo secundum mandata tua hetaerias esse vetueram" (ML 2794–2795): "after my edict, in which according to thy demands I had prohibited fraternities" (EU-ed 19501–19502). OWN. Documented. Plain words: "clubs and private societies, which he had banned on Trajan's orders".

S5. **He had never seen such a trial.** "Cognitionibus de Christianis interfui numquam. Ideo nescio quid et quatenus aut puniri soleat aut quaeri." (ML 2771–2772). "I have never been present at judicial examinations of the Christians; therefore I am ignorant how and to what extent it is customary to punish or to search for them." (EU-ed 19461–19463). His opening: "Sollemne est mihi, domine, omnia de quibus dubito ad te referre." (ML 2768–2769): "It is my custom, my Lord, to refer to thee all questions concerning which I am in doubt" (EU-ed 19459–19460). OWN. Documented.

## 2. Events (the letter, in its own order)

E1. **His questions.** Whether age makes a difference; whether pardon is given to those who repent, or whether someone who has once been a Christian gains nothing by stopping; "nomen ipsum, si flagitiis careat, an flagitia cohaerentia nomini puniantur" (ML 2772–2775): "whether the mere name, if unaccompanied with crimes, or crimes associated with the name, should be punished" (EU-ed 19464–19468). OWN. Documented.

E2. **How he tried them.** "Interrogavi ipsos, an essent Christiani. Confitentes iterum ac tertio interrogavi, supplicium minatus; perseverantes duci iussi. Neque enim dubitabam, qualecumque esset quod faterentur, pertinaciam certe et inflexibilem obstinationem debere puniri." (ML 2776–2779). McGiffert: "I have asked them if they were Christians, and if they have confessed, I have asked them a second and third time, threatening them with punishment; if they have persisted, I have commanded them to be led away to punishment. For I did not doubt that whatever that might be which they confessed, at any rate pertinacious and inflexible obstinacy ought to be punished." (EU-ed 19470–19476). OWN. Documented. Note: the Latin says only *duci iussi*, "I ordered them led away"; "to punishment" is McGiffert's. Pliny gives no number and does not use the word death. Tertullian reads it as condemnation, *damnatis quibusdam Christianis* (TL 11655), and Eusebius as death (D1). That those led away were executed is the later writers' reading and the plain sense of the next clause ("ought to be punished"): Widely Accepted, not Pliny's word.

E3. **Citizens sent to Rome.** "Fuerunt alii similis amentiae, quos, quia cives Romani erant, adnotavi in urbem remittendos." (ML 2779–2780). "There have been others afflicted with like insanity who as Roman citizens I have decided should be sent to Rome." (EU-ed 19476–19478). OWN. Documented.

E4. **The cases spread; the anonymous list.** "Mox ipso tractatu, ut fieri solet, diffundente se crimine plures species inciderunt. Propositus est libellus sine auctore multorum nomina continens." (ML 2780–2782). "In the course of the proceedings, as commonly happens, the crime was extended, and many varieties of cases appeared. An anonymous document was published, containing the names of many persons." (EU-ed 19478–19481). OWN. Documented.

E5. **The test of denial.** Those who denied being or having been Christians he released "cum praeeunte me deos appellarent et imagini tuae, quam propter hoc iusseram cum simulacris numinum adferri, ture ac vino supplicarent, praeterea male dicerent Christo, quorum nihil posse cogi dicuntur qui sunt re vera Christiani" (ML 2782–2785). "when they had followed my example in invoking the gods and offering incense and wine to thine image,—which I had for that purpose ordered brought with the images of the gods,—and when they had besides cursed Christ—things which they say that those who are truly Christians cannot be compelled to do." (EU-ed 19482–19487). OWN. Documented. "They say" (*dicuntur*): hearsay in Pliny's own letter.

E6. **Those who had left.** Others named by an informer said they were Christians, then denied it; they had been, but had stopped, three years, more years, one twenty years before. "Hi quoque omnes et imaginem tuam deorumque simulacra venerati sunt et Christo male dixerunt." (ML 2786–2789). "All adored thine image and the statues of the gods, and cursed Christ." (EU-ed 19487–19491). OWN. Documented.

E7. **What they told him of their worship.** It is these former Christians who "affirmed" it (*Adfirmabant*): "Adfirmabant autem hanc fuisse summam vel culpae suae vel erroris, quod essent soliti stato die ante lucem convenire carmenque Christo quasi deo dicere secum invicem seque sacramento non in scelus aliquod obstringere, sed ne furta, ne latrocinia, ne adulteria committerent, ne fidem fallerent, ne depositum appellati abnegarent. Quibus peractis morem sibi discedendi fuisse, rursusque coeundi ad capiendum cibum, promiscuum tamen et innoxium; quod ipsum facere desisse post edictum meum..." (ML 2789–2795). McGiffert: "they affirmed that this was the sum of their guilt or error; that they had been accustomed to come together on a fixed day before daylight and to sing responsively a song unto Christ as God; and to bind themselves with an oath, not with a view to the commission of some crime, but, on the contrary, that they would not commit theft, nor robbery, nor adultery, that they would not break faith, nor refuse to restore a deposit when asked for it. When they had done these things, their custom was to separate and to assemble again to partake of a meal, common yet harmless (which is not the characteristic of a nefarious superstition); but this they had ceased to do after my edict" (EU-ed 19491–19502). OWN, reported speech of people who had left the faith. Documented as what they told him.
- **McGiffert's parenthesis**, "(which is not the characteristic of a nefarious superstition)", is not in the Latin as held (ML 2794). It is the translator's, or his text's; never give it to Pliny.
- *carmen ... dicere secum invicem*: "to say a carmen to Christ ... in turn among themselves". *Carmen* is a song or a set form of words; McGiffert has "sing responsively a song", Tertullian reads it as singing, *ad canendum* (TL 11724). Plain words: "to sing or chant a hymn to Christ, as to a god, taking turns". The exact form is uncertain (my note on the Latin).
- *quasi deo*: "as to a god". Tertullian's manuscripts mostly read *Christo et deo* (TL 11724, variants TL 11734); Scaliger and Heraldus preferred *ut deo* from Pliny (TL 11752–11756, editors' note). Do not build anything on the difference.
- *sacramento*: "by an oath". Not "sacrament".
- *ne depositum appellati abnegarent*: not to deny goods left in their keeping when asked to return them.
- *promiscuum tamen et innoxium*: "ordinary and harmless" food. Pliny is answering the rumours of crimes; Tertullian names them, children eaten and incest (TE 1560–1563).

E8. **The two slave women, under torture.** "Quo magis necessarium credidi ex duabus ancillis, quae ministrae dicebantur, quid esset veri et per tormenta quaerere. Nihil aliud inveni quam superstitionem pravam, immodicam." (ML 2795–2797). "I therefore considered it the more necessary to examine, even with the use of torture, two female slaves who were called deaconesses (*ministræ*), in order to ascertain the truth. But I found nothing except a superstition depraved and immoderate" (EU-ed 19502–19506). OWN. Documented. *Ministrae* means women who serve; "deaconesses" is McGiffert's English, and Harnack's ("Diakonissen", HA 2951–2953). Plain words: "two slave women whom the Christians called ministers". Their names are not given and what became of them is not said.

E9. **Why he wrote: the numbers.** "Ideo dilata cognitione ad consulendum te decucurri. Visa est enim mihi res digna consultatione, maxime propter periclitantium numerum. Multi enim omnis aetatis, omnis ordinis, utriusque sexus etiam, vocantur in periculum et vocabuntur. Neque civitates tantum sed vicos etiam atque agros superstitionis istius contagio pervagata est; quae videtur sisti et corrigi posse." (ML 2797–2801). "therefore, postponing further inquiry, I have turned to thee for advice. For the matter seems to me worth consulting about, especially on account of the number of persons involved. For many of every age and of every rank and of both sexes have been already, and will be brought to trial. For the contagion of this superstition has permeated not only the cities, but also the villages and even the country districts. Yet it can apparently be arrested and corrected." (EU-ed 19506–19513). OWN. Documented as his claim. Harnack thinks Pliny had reason to make the spread look as large as possible, but could not have invented it (HA 8861–8866); editor's view.

E10. **The temples filling again.** "Certe satis constat prope iam desolata templa coepisse celebrari et sacra sollemnia diu intermissa repeti pastumque venire victimarum, cuius adhuc rarissimus emptor inveniebatur. Ex quo facile est opinari, quae turba hominum emendari possit, si sit paenitentiae locus." (ML 2802–2805). "it is certainly a fact that the temples, which were almost deserted, are now beginning to be frequented, and the sacred rites, which were for a long time interrupted, to be resumed, and fodder for the victims to be sold, for which previously hardly a purchaser was to be found. From which it is easy to gather how great a multitude of men may be reformed if there is given a chance for repentance." (EU-ed 19513–19519). OWN. Documented as his claim. "Fodder for the victims" = feed for the animals to be sacrificed. "Almost deserted" is his claim, made to argue that pardon works.

E11. **Trajan's reply.** "Traianus Plinio. Actum quem debuisti, mi Secunde, in excutiendis causis eorum, qui Christiani ad te delati fuerant, secutus es. Neque enim in universum aliquid, quod quasi certam formam habeat, constitui potest. Conquirendi non sunt: si deferantur et arguantur, puniendi sunt, ita tamen, ut qui negaverit se Christianum esse idque re ipsa manifestum fecerit, id est supplicando dis nostris, quamvis suspectus in praeteritum, veniam ex paenitentia impetret. Sine auctore vero propositi libelli in nullo crimine locum habere debent. Nam et pessimi exempli nec nostri saeculi est." (ML 2809–2819). McGiffert: "Thou hast followed the right course, my Secundus, in treating the cases of those who have been brought before thee as Christians. For no fixed rule can be laid down which shall be applicable to all cases. They are not to be searched for; if they are accused and convicted, they are to be punished; nevertheless, with the proviso that he who denies that he is a Christian, and proves it by his act (*re ipsa*),—i.e. by making supplication to our gods,—although suspected in regard to the past, may by repentance obtain pardon. Anonymous accusations ought not to be admitted in any proceedings; for they are of most evil precedent, and are not in accord with our age." (EU-ed 19523–19533). OWN. Documented. Five sentences. Trajan answers none of Pliny's questions about age or the name alone.

E12. **How short it is.** Mirbt prints the letter in about thirty-seven lines and the reply in about eight (ML 2768–2805, 2809–2819). Plain words: "a letter of about a page, and a reply of five sentences".

## 3. Impact (heaviest first: what later witnesses carried on and used)

I1. **Tertullian's answer.** In his *Apology*, written in Carthage under Severus, Tertullian summarizes the letter as proof that Christians were innocent, and attacks the ruling. Latin: "Atquin invenimus inquisitionem quoque in nos prohibitam. Plinius enim Secundus cum provinciam regeret, damnatis quibusdam Christianis, quibusdam gradu pulsis, ipsa tamen multitudine perturbatus, quid de cetero ageret, consuluit tunc Traianum imperatorem, adlegans praeter obstinationem non sacrificandi nihil aliud se de sacramentis eorum conperisse quam coetus antelucanos ad canendum Christo et deo, et ad confoederandam disciplinam, homicidium, adulterium, fraudem, perfidiam et cetera scelera prohibentes. Tunc Traianus rescripsit hoc genus inquirendos quidem non esse, oblatos vero puniri oportere. O sententiam necessitate confusam! Negat inquirendos ut innocentes, et mandat puniendos ut nocentes. Parcit et saevit, dissimulat et animadvertit. Quid temetipsam, censura, circumvenis? Si damnas, cur non et inquiris? si non inquiris, cur non et absolvis?" (TL 11653–11658, 11724–11731). Thelwall: "the younger Pliny, when he was ruler of a province, having condemned some Christians to death, and driven some from their stedfastness, being still annoyed by their great numbers, at last sought the advice of Trajan ... explaining to his master that, except an obstinate disinclination to offer sacrifices, he found in the religious services nothing but meetings at early morning for singing hymns to Christ and God, and sealing home their way of life by a united pledge to be faithful to their religion, forbidding murder, adultery, dishonesty, and other crimes. Upon this Trajan wrote back that Christians were by no means to be sought after; but if they were brought before him, they should be punished. O miserable deliverance,—under the necessities of the case, a self-contradiction! It forbids them to be sought after as innocent, and it commands them to be punished as guilty. It is at once merciful and cruel; it passes by, and it punishes." (TE 1566–1585). LATER (about a century). Documented as Tertullian's words.
- "to death" in Thelwall is not in the Latin *damnatis*, "condemned"; "driven some from their stedfastness" renders *gradu pulsis*, which McGiffert reads as "deprived of their dignity" (EU-ed 19581–19595). Tertullian's summary leaves out the anonymous list, the deniers, the cursing of Christ, the two women and the torture.
- In chapter 5 he says these laws were ones "which Trajan to some extent made naught by forbidding Christians to be sought after" (TE 1859–1861); Latin "Traianus ex parte frustratus est vetando inquiri Christianos" (TL 13090). So Tertullian both attacks the ruling and counts it as a partial relief.

I2. **Eusebius carries it into Greek.** "So great a persecution was at that time opened against us in many places that Plinius Secundus, one of the most noted of governors, being disturbed by the great number of martyrs, communicated with the emperor concerning the multitude of those that were put to death for their faith." (EU 19446–19451). Then: "In reply to this Trajan made the following decree: that the race of Christians should not be sought after, but when found should be punished. On account of this the persecution which had threatened to be a most terrible one was to a certain degree checked, but there were still left plenty of pretexts for those who wished to do us harm. Sometimes the people, sometimes the rulers in various places, would lay plots against us, so that, although no great persecutions took place, local persecutions were nevertheless going on in particular provinces, and many of the faithful endured martyrdom in various forms." (EU 19541–19553). "We have taken our account from the Latin Apology of Tertullian" (EU 19556). LATER (about 200 years). Documented as Eusebius's account. He knew the letter through Tertullian, not directly.

I3. **The chronicles.** Eusebius's Chronicle in Jerome's Latin: "Plinius Secundus, cum quandam prouinciam regeret et in magistratu suo plurimos Christianorum interfecisset, multitudine eorum perterritus quaesiuit de Traiano quid facto opus esset, nuntians ei praeter obstinationem non sacrificandi et antelucanos coetus ad canendum cuidam Christo ut deo nihil apud eos repperiri ... ad quae commotus Traianus rescripsit hoc genus quidem inquirendos non esse, oblatos uero puniri oportere: Tertullianus refert in Apologetico." (JC 42246–42260). Prosper copies it word for word, under the consuls of 114 (PC 49063–49072). Bar Hebraeus, in the thirteenth century, still tells it in his Syriac chronicle of the patriarchs of Antioch, just after Ignatius: "tempore Plinii secundi qui multos Christianos ad mortem damnavit et e gradu dejecit ... Igitur praecepit Trajanus Christianorum genus haud esse inquirendum" (BH 3900–3913; editors' Latin). LATER. Documented as their words.

I4. **A softened memory.** Sulpicius Severus, about 400, does not name Pliny: "tertia persecutio per Traianum fuit. qui cum tormentis et quaestionibus nihil in Christianis morte aut poena dignum repperisset, saeuiri in eos ultra uetuit." (SS 4912–4914). "the third persecution under Trajan. But he, when after torture and racking he found nothing in the Christians worthy of death or punishment, forbade any further cruelty to be put forth against them." (SE 10761–10764). LATER. Documented as his words. Pliny's tortures and Pliny's findings have become Trajan's, and the ruling has become an end to cruelty, which it was not (E11).

I5. **Historians who still weigh it.** (All EDITOR.)
- McGiffert: the Fathers' reading of the reply as favourable "is a decided mistake. Trajan's rescript expressly made Christianity a *religio illicita*, and from that time on it was a crime in the sight of the law to be a Christian ... Trajan, it is true, advises moderation in the execution of the law; but that does not alter the fact that his rescript is an unfavorable one" (EU-ed 19561–19576).
- Schanz–Hosius: the emperor recognized that Christianity was punishable, "im Einklang mit der bisherigen Uebung"; being a Christian was enough to convict; but Trajan softened it, since anyone could deny and sacrifice, and he forbade the search (SH 24485–24505). The correspondence was soon published, so the ruling spread widely (SH 24507–24511).
- Harnack: "Ein bekanntes, aber nie hoch genug zu schätzendes nichtchristliches Zeugnis" (HA 8812–8813); Christians in the countryside "von Plinius zuerst genannt" (HA 182–183); the letter shows women called *ministrae* (HA 2951–2953) and "utriusque sexus" (HA 3021). Pliny "hatte Grund, die Verbreitung möglichst kräftig darzustellen" and wanted the emperor to approve "sein relativ mildes Verfahren" (HA 8861–8862, 8897–8898).
- Whiston, in a dissertation printed with his Josephus, calls the two letters "two heathen records in their favour" (WH 142719–142721): a later reader making the witness a friend.

## 4. Links

- **Tertullian** (`tertullian-s-voice`): answered Trajan's ruling (I1). His census story tells the *Apology* as "dismantling Roman slanders against Christians" and does not mention Trajan (CEN `tertullian-s-voice`, longDescription, read).
- **Eusebius of Caesarea**: retold it from Tertullian (I2).
- **Jerome**: his Latin of Eusebius's Chronicle (I3).
- **Sulpicius Severus**: the softened memory (I4).
- **Ignatius of Antioch** (batch 6): Eusebius's Chronicle and Bar Hebraeus set Pliny's letter just after Ignatius's death under Trajan (JC 42244–42246; BH 3888–3901). Not told.
- **Suetonius** (batch 6): Rolfe says Suetonius "secured a military tribuneship through Pliny's good offices", that Pliny in a letter to Trajan calls him *contubernalis*, an intimate friend, and that Pliny's letters about him run from about 96 to 112 (RO 191–216). The live `suetonius` text already says Pliny's letters show him practising law (CEN). Not told here.
- **Post-Apostolic Household-Church Christianity** (`post-apostolic-house-church`, on the tree): its live story already tells this letter in one paragraph: worship before dawn, the oath, the meal, the two women under torture, with the quotes 'as God', 'common yet harmless' and 'a superstition depraved and immoderate' (CEN, longDescription). This story must not reuse those quotes, and tells the worship in paraphrase.

## 5. Where sources disagree or come late

D1. **How many died.** Pliny gives no number and writes only "led away" (E2). Tertullian: "some condemned" (*damnatis quibusdam*, TL 11655). Eusebius: "the great number of martyrs ... the multitude of those that were put to death" (EU 19449–19451). Jerome's Chronicle: he "had killed very many Christians" (*plurimos Christianorum interfecisset*, JC 42247–42248). The number grows with each retelling. Contested; the story says Pliny does not count.

D2. **Was the ruling a relief?** Tertullian attacks it as self-contradictory (I1) yet says it partly blunted the laws (TE 1859–1861). Eusebius says persecution was "to a certain degree checked" (EU 19543–19545). Sulpicius makes it an end of cruelty (I4). McGiffert calls that reading "a decided mistake" (EU-ed 19570). Contested.

D3. **What was found.** Pliny found "nothing except a superstition depraved and immoderate" (E8). Tertullian turns it into "nothing else" against them but their refusal to sacrifice and their dawn meetings (I1). Sulpicius gives the finding to Trajan, after "torture and racking" (I4). The force of Pliny's own words, *superstitio prava, immodica*, must stay.

D4. **Date.** 110 or 111 for the governorship (EU-ed 19458–19459); the letter 111–113 (ML 2758; HA 8820; SH 6071). Prosper's copy files it under 114 (PC 49065–49067). Say "about 112".

D5. **Where in the province.** Harnack's guess, the east of the province (HA 8851–8853). Not told.

D6. **Gaps.** No held text of the rest of Pliny's letters (only 10.96–97 in Latin, through Mirbt). No held text of Pliny's life beyond McGiffert's note and Rolfe. What happened to the two women, to the citizens sent to Rome, or to the Christians of Bithynia after the reply: no held source says.
