# Outline: Pliny the Younger

Sheet items (S, E, I, D) are in `Source_Sheet.md`, which gives the file and line for each. ML is the Latin of the two letters in Mirbt (1911); EU-ed is McGiffert's English of them (1890); TL and TE are Tertullian's Latin and English.

- **The essence:** Around the year 112, Pliny, a Roman man of letters governing the province of Bithynia on the Black Sea, was trying people accused of being Christians, and having those who would not deny it led away to be punished. Unsure of the law, he wrote to the emperor Trajan to ask what to do (S2, S5, E2; Documented, his own letter; that those "led away" died is the later writers' reading, Widely Accepted). His letter and Trajan's short reply are a Roman judge's own record of the church on trial: how he tested them, what people who had left the faith told him of their worship before dawn, and the emperor's rule that Christians were not to be hunted out, but punished if they were accused and would not deny it (E5, E7, E11; Documented).
- **Also told:**
  - How he tried them: he asked three times, threatened them, and had those who held firm led away; citizens were sent to Rome. An anonymous list named many more. Those who denied it prayed to the gods, offered incense and wine to Trajan's statue, and cursed Christ, which, he had heard, real Christians cannot be made to do (E2–E6; Documented).
  - What he learned, and how: people who had left the faith told him they met on a fixed day before dawn, sang a hymn to Christ as to a god, swore an oath not to steal, rob or commit adultery, and later met to eat ordinary, harmless food, until his ban on clubs. He then had two slave women who were called ministers tortured, and found nothing but a twisted, excessive cult (E7, E8; Documented as their account and his finding).
  - Why he wrote: so many were in danger, of every age and rank, men and women, in towns, villages and countryside. He claimed the temples were beginning to fill again, and thought many could be won back if pardon were allowed (E9, E10; Documented as his claims; Harnack thinks he had reason to make the numbers large, an editor's view).
  - Trajan's answer: no fixed rule; not to be hunted out; if reported and proved, punished; anyone who denied it and prayed to the gods was to be pardoned; no anonymous accusations (E11; Documented).
  - What can and cannot be told from it: it is about a page, and the reply five sentences (E12). He gives no number of the dead (D1). What he says of the worship came from people who had left, and from torture (E7, E8).
  - What came of it: Tertullian answered the ruling less than a century later; Eusebius carried it into Greek; the chronicles carried it on, the dead growing in number with each telling; historians have argued since whether the reply protected Christians or made their faith itself a crime (I1–I5, D1, D2; Documented as each writer's view).
- **Length:** about 3 minutes 30 (about 520 words). Why: the record is one short letter and a five-sentence reply, but the letter is full of scenes (the three questions, the statue, the list, the dawn meeting, the two women), and the reply and its reading by Christians are the story's point. The impact needs about a third: Tertullian's answer, and how the chronicles grew it. Nothing is spent on Pliny's wider life, which the Library barely holds. Optional, to be cut first: What came 3 (Sulpicius), and the twenty-year-old ex-Christian in beat 3.
- **Setting:**
  - Time: about 112, early in the second century (S2). McGiffert puts his governorship in 110 or 111; others date the letter 111 to 113 (D4). Plain words: "around the year 112".
  - Place: Bithynia, a Roman province on the Black Sea coast, in what is now northern Turkey (S2; census). Christians were there before him: the First Letter of Peter is addressed to them (S3). Plain words: "Bithynia, on the Black Sea".
  - Tradition: none; he stood outside the church. The Christians he tried belong to the early household churches of Asia Minor (census `post-apostolic-house-church`). Plain words: "the Christians of the province".

**Words to explain** (the first time each is heard; plain word or gloss of under about 12 words, worked into the sentence)

- **governor / province**: "governing the province for the emperor" (ML 2795), i.e. the region Rome ruled. Not "proconsul" (Whiston's word, WH 142724; not used).
- **Bithynia**: "a Roman province on the Black Sea, in today's Turkey".
- **Trajan**: "the emperor Trajan". No gloss needed beyond "emperor".
- **rescript**: do not use. Say "Trajan's reply" or "his ruling".
- **Roman citizen**: say why it mattered as the record does: citizens were sent to Rome for trial (E3). No more.
- **incense and wine to the emperor's image**: "burned incense and poured wine before Trajan's statue", which stood among the statues of the gods (E5).
- **curse Christ**: plain. Keep it.
- **clubs** (Latin *hetaeriae*): "private clubs and societies, which he had banned on Trajan's orders" (S4).
- **oath** (Latin *sacramentum*): "a solemn oath". Never "sacrament".
- **deposit**: "not to keep goods left in their care" (E7).
- **hymn / carmen**: "sang a hymn, taking turns" (E7). Do not explain the Latin.
- **ministrae**: "two slave women who were called ministers". Not "deaconesses" as Pliny's word; that is McGiffert's and Harnack's English (E8).
- **superstition**: Pliny's word for a cult that is wrong and runs to excess. Say "a twisted, excessive cult", or "superstition, as he called it". Do not quote "a superstition depraved and immoderate" (barred, see Quotes).
- **feed for the sacrificial animals**: "people were buying feed for the animals to be sacrificed again" (E10). Not "fodder for the victims".
- **Apology**: "his Defence of the Christians, written to Rome's governors" (I1; TL 11034).
- **chronicle**: "a year-by-year history".
- **Syriac**: 'in Syriac, the language of many churches of the Middle East'

- **Shape:** Outside witness. Trap: making Pliny a friend or a foe of the church, or stretching a page past what it says. He was a careful official who killed people for stubbornness and tortured two women, and who also found no crime and asked for pardon to be allowed. Tell both. Say plainly how short the letter is, that the worship comes second-hand from people who had left the faith and from torture, and that later Christians changed it.
- **Sibling:** `tacitus` and `suetonius` (batch 6, outside witnesses, no outlines yet): Nero's fire and Rome are theirs; this story keeps to Bithynia and leaves Suetonius, Pliny's friend, out. `tertullian-s-voice` (live; read): its story is his Latin and his rigorism, and never names Trajan. Here his answer to the ruling is told, a sentence or two. `post-apostolic-house-church` (live; read; not named in the slate): its story already tells this letter in one paragraph, as evidence of how the early house churches lived: the dawn hymn, the oath, the meal, the two women. Here the letter is the spine and the trial is told whole; the worship is told in paraphrase, and none of that story's quotes is used. A question for Mark (see the reply).
- **Openings:**
  1. **Chosen.** His own admission (S5). In about three sentences: Pliny, governing a Roman province on the Black Sea, wrote to the emperor Trajan that he had never once attended a trial of Christians, and so did not know what was punished, or how hard to search. By then he had already been trying them, and had sent those who held firm away to be punished. That was around the year 112, in Bithynia. This leads straight into the essence.
  2. Not chosen: the anonymous list posted with many names (E4). Strong, but it is the middle of the trial; opening on it would tell the list twice.

**Story beats** (the opening, the essence and beats 1 and 2 are the first minute, about 150 words; beats 3 to 6 deepen it)

1. **The governor, and the trials.** Pliny, a man of letters and a close friend of Trajan, became governor of Bithynia in 110 or 111 (S1, S2; Widely Accepted, McGiffert). People were brought before him as Christians. He asked each whether they were Christians. Those who said yes he asked a second and a third time, threatening punishment. Those who held firm he had led away (Quote 1; E2). Roman citizens he sent to Rome (E3). His reason, in his own words: whatever it was they confessed, their stubbornness deserved punishment (Quote 1; Documented).
2. **The list, and the test.** As the trials went on, the charges spread, and an anonymous list was posted with many names (E4). Those who denied it he released once they had called on the gods after him, offered incense and wine before Trajan's statue, which he had brought in for this among the statues of the gods, and cursed Christ. He had heard that real Christians cannot be made to do any of it (E5; Documented; "he had heard" is his own hearsay).
3. **What the ones who had left told him.** Others named by an informer said they had been Christians but had stopped, some three years before, some longer, one twenty years (E6). They too worshipped the statues and cursed Christ. They told him this was the whole of their fault: on a fixed day they met before dawn, sang a hymn to Christ as to a god, taking turns, and swore an oath, not to a crime, but not to steal, rob, commit adultery, break their word, or keep what was left in their care. Then they parted, and met again to eat ordinary, harmless food, until his ban on clubs stopped it (E7; Documented as what they said).
4. **The two women.** So he thought it all the more needed to find the truth by torture, from two slave women who were called ministers. He found nothing but a twisted, excessive cult (E8; Documented). Their names, and what became of them, are not recorded (D6).
5. **Why he wrote.** He stopped the hearings and wrote. So many were in danger: every age, every rank, men and women, and the cult had spread through the towns, the villages and the countryside. It seemed to him it could be stopped and set right: the temples, almost deserted, were beginning to fill again, and people were buying feed for the sacrificial animals. A great crowd could be set right, he wrote, if there were room for repentance (E9, E10; Documented as his claims). He had asked: should age matter; should those who stopped be pardoned; was the name alone a crime, or only crimes that went with it (E1). Defended, Pliny's own: room for repentance and pardon (E10).
6. **Trajan's answer.** Five sentences. Pliny had acted rightly; no fixed rule could be laid down for every case. Christians were not to be hunted out; if reported and proved, they were to be punished (Quote 2; E11). Whoever denied it and proved it by praying to the gods was to be pardoned, even if suspected for the past. Anonymous lists had no place in any charge: they set the worst example, and did not belong to "our age" (E11; Documented). He said nothing about age. He did not say in so many words whether the name alone was a crime, but his rule punished anyone proved a Christian who would not deny it (ML 2811–2818; that this made the name itself punishable is the editors' reading, SH 24494–24497, EU-ed 19570–19576).

**What came of him** (about a third of the story; heaviest first. Items 1 and 2 are told; item 3 is one line, optional)

1. **Tertullian answered the ruling** (the Christians' defence, Tertullian's). Less than a century later, in Carthage, Tertullian wrote his *Apology*, a defence of the Christians addressed to Rome's governors (TL 11034). He used Pliny's letter as proof of their innocence: the governor had found nothing against them but their refusal to sacrifice and their dawn hymns to Christ, and a shared rule of life forbidding murder, adultery and fraud (TL 11724–11726). Then he turned on Trajan's ruling (Quote 3; I1). He leaves out the list, the cursing and the tortured women (I1; Documented). He also counted it as partial relief: Trajan, he wrote, had to some extent blunted the laws by forbidding the search (I1; TE 1859–1861).
2. **The chronicles carried it on, and grew it.** Eusebius of Caesarea, writing in Greek about two hundred years after Pliny, took it from Tertullian's Latin. He calls Pliny one of the most noted governors, "disturbed by the great number of martyrs", and says the ruling checked the persecution to a degree, while local persecutions went on (I2). Pliny himself gives no number. In Jerome's Latin of Eusebius's Chronicle, Pliny "had killed very many Christians" (I3, D1). The story was still being told in a Syriac chronicle in the thirteenth century (I3; Bar Hebraeus).
3. **Optional, one line.** About 400, Sulpicius Severus told it without Pliny's name: Trajan, finding after torture nothing in the Christians worthy of death, forbade any further cruelty (I4). The ruling had become a mercy. A modern historian may be set against it: Arthur McGiffert, Eusebius's translator, calls that reading a mistake: the reply made being a Christian a crime in law (I5, D2).

**Quotes** (3)

Only Quotes 1–3 are quoted aloud, plus at most three short phrases: "our age" (Trajan), "disturbed by the great number of martyrs" (Eusebius, EU 19449) and "had killed very many Christians" (Jerome, rendered from JC 42247–42248). Everything else in the letter, above all the worship before dawn, is told in paraphrase.

1. Latin (ML 2777–2779): "Confitentes iterum ac tertio interrogavi, supplicium minatus; perseverantes duci iussi. Neque enim dubitabam, qualecumque esset quod faterentur, pertinaciam certe et inflexibilem obstinationem debere puniri." (McGiffert's English, EU-ed 19472–19476.) Rendering (from the Latin): "Those who confessed, I asked a second and a third time, and I threatened them with punishment. Those who held firm, I ordered to be led away. For I did not doubt that, whatever it was they confessed, their stubbornness and unbending obstinacy surely deserved punishment." Job: how he tried them, in beat 1. A judge's reason in his own words: not a crime, but refusal.
2. Latin (ML 2811, 2816): "Conquirendi non sunt: si deferantur et arguantur, puniendi sunt" (the sentence goes on, "ita tamen, ut qui negaverit..."; the quote stops there). (McGiffert's English, EU-ed 19526–19527.) Rendering (from the Latin): "They are not to be hunted out. If they are reported and proved guilty, they are to be punished." Job: Trajan's ruling, in beat 6. The heart of the reply, which the rest of the story turns on.
3. Latin (TL 11728–11730): "O sententiam necessitate confusam! Negat inquirendos ut innocentes, et mandat puniendos ut nocentes. Parcit et saevit, dissimulat et animadvertit." (Thelwall's English, TE 1583–1587.) Rendering (from the Latin): "What a ruling, confused by necessity! It forbids a search for them, as if they were innocent, and it orders them punished, as if they were guilty. It spares and it rages; it looks away and it punishes." Job: the Christian answer, in What came 1. The narrator gives the plain sense alongside the first line: need had forced the ruling to contradict itself.

**Connection to carry into the story:** what passed from Pliny was a ruling and a record. Trajan's reply, answering Pliny's question, set how Christians were to be tried: not searched for, but punished if accused and they held firm, and pardoned if they denied it and prayed to the gods (E11). Less than a century later Tertullian, defending the Christians, took Pliny's own findings as proof they had done no crime, and attacked the ruling for treating them as innocent and guilty at once (I1). The listener must understand two things. First, the Christian retellings came one from another: Tertullian summarized the letter, Eusebius took it from Tertullian, and the chronicles from Eusebius. Along the way the torture and the cursing dropped out, and the dead grew in number (I1, I2, I3, D1). Second, the reply was not a law of protection; Christians themselves argued whether it was a relief or a trap, and historians have argued it since: McGiffert, Eusebius's translator, read the reply as making the faith itself a crime (EU-ed 19570–19576). Tell it through what Tertullian wrote and what the chronicles changed. No "this matters because" sentence.

**Ending:** close on Pliny, with the opening image seen again: the governor who had never attended a trial of Christians, asking the emperor how hard to search (S5). Or close on his own last line: a great crowd could be set right, if there were room for repentance (E10; told in paraphrase, as in beat 5 if not used there: use it in one place only). If the second is chosen, beat 5 keeps the temples and moves the "room for repentance" line here.

**Who tells it**

- **Pliny himself**, in his letter of about 112, writes as the judge. The whole story rests on his letter and Trajan's reply. Say so once, inside a sentence about the letter: what he knew of their worship he heard from people who had left the faith, and from two women under torture (E7, E8).
- **Trajan**, in his reply (E11).
- **Tertullian**, less than a century later, a Christian advocate answering the ruling (I1). Credit his reading of the letter to him.
- **Eusebius**, about two hundred years later, from Tertullian (I2). "Great number of martyrs" is his.
- **Jerome**, in his Latin of Eusebius's Chronicle: "very many" killed (I3).
- **Sulpicius Severus** (about 400), **McGiffert** (1890), **Harnack** (1906): optional, each credited by name or role.

**Do not say**

- Any word in "Words to explain" used without its plain sense the first time it is heard.
- Not "Pliny executed many" or any number. He gives none; he writes "led away". Say "he had those who held firm led away", and, if death is said, "to their deaths, as later Christians understood it", or "to be punished" (E2, D1).
- Not "Pliny persecuted the Christians in a great persecution". That is Eusebius's framing (I2). Say what Pliny did.
- Not "Trajan forbade persecution", "protected the Christians" or "ended the persecution". He forbade the hunt and ordered punishment of those accused and proved (E11). The "mercy" reading is Sulpicius's (I4) and Eusebius's "to a certain degree checked" (I2), credited.
- Not "Pliny found the Christians innocent". He found no crime he names, but a twisted, excessive cult, and punished their stubbornness (E2, E8).
- Not "the Christians told him". It was people who said they had left the faith (E6, E7). And not "the Christians called it harmless" as a quote: barred, and the words come in Pliny's report.
- Not "deaconesses" as Pliny's word, and not "who held office". Say "who were called ministers" (E8).
- Not "the two women were executed" or "released". The record does not say (D6).
- Not "the earliest outside account of Christians", and no ranking against Tacitus or Suetonius (the batch's other outside witnesses; no held source here dates them against Pliny). Say "a Roman judge's own report".
- Not "every Christian in Bithynia", "the temples were empty". He says "almost deserted", and he says it to argue for pardon (E10).
- Not "Pliny was cruel" or "Pliny was fair-minded" as the narrator's verdict. Tell what he did.
- Not "Tertullian quoted the letter". He summarized it, and left out the torture, the cursing and the list (I1).
- Not "Trajan's law made Christianity illegal" as fact. That is McGiffert's reading (I5); credit it, or leave it out.
- Not "the governor of Bithynia and Pontus" stacked with dates and titles in one sentence. Give the province, then the date, a clause at a time.
- Not "proconsul" or "senator".  Say "governor", as the census does; "proconsul" is Whiston's word (S2, WH 142724). No held source calls him a senator.
- Not "born in 61" or "died in 113", or anything about Vesuvius or his uncle's death. No held source gives his dates, and the elder Pliny's death is not in the held record of this man.
- Keep the force: the threat repeated three times, the cursing of Christ demanded, the torture of two slave women. Do not soften "tortured" to "questioned".
- Keep his hope too: he thought many could be won back if pardon were allowed (E10). Do not make him only a persecutor.
- Not "the letter is disputed" or "may be forged". No held source disputes that it is his. What is disputed is the number of dead, the date, and what the reply meant (D1, D2, D4).
- Not "a song" or "a creed" as the settled sense of *carmen*. Say "a hymn".
- Not 'Trajan left the question of the name open' or 'did not answer it'. Say he said nothing about age, and that his rule punished those proved to be Christians who would not deny it.
- Not 'Trajan's rule became the law of the empire'. He wrote to Pliny and said no general rule could be laid down (ML 2810–2811). Schanz–Hosius say it spread once the letters were published (SH 24507–24511), an editors' view.
