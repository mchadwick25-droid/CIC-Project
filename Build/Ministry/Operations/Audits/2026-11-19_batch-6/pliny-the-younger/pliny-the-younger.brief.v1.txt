Pliny the Younger wrote to the emperor Trajan with an admission. He had never once attended a trial of Christians, so he did not know what was punished, or how hard to search, though he had already been trying them.

That was around the year 112, in Bithynia, a Roman province on the Black Sea in today's Turkey. Pliny, a man of letters, was governing it for the emperor, and his letter and Trajan's short reply are a Roman judge's own record of the church on trial.

He asked each of the accused whether they were Christians. "Those who confessed, I asked a second and a third time, and I threatened them with punishment. Those who held firm, I ordered to be led away. For I did not doubt that, whatever it was they confessed, their stubbornness and unbending obstinacy surely deserved punishment." He does not say what became of them, though later Christians took it that they died.

As the trials went on, the charges spread, and an anonymous list was posted with many names. Those who denied being Christians he released once they had prayed to the gods, burned incense and poured wine before Trajan's statue, and cursed Christ. Real Christians, he had heard, cannot be made to do that.

Others said they had once been Christians but had stopped, and what he knew of the worship came from them. On a fixed day, they said, they had met before dawn to sing a hymn to Christ as to a god. They swore a solemn oath, not to any crime, but never to steal, rob or commit adultery. Later they met to eat ordinary, harmless food, until he banned private clubs on Trajan's orders. Then he had two slave women who were called ministers tortured, and found nothing but a twisted, excessive cult. What became of the women is not recorded.

So many were in danger, of every age and rank, in towns and countryside alike, that he stopped the hearings and wrote. Yet the temples, almost deserted, were beginning to fill again, he claimed.

The letter fills about a page, and Trajan's reply runs to five sentences. No fixed rule could be laid down for every case, the emperor wrote. "They are not to be hunted out. If they are reported and proved guilty, they are to be punished." Whoever denied being a Christian and prayed to the gods was to be pardoned, and anonymous lists did not belong to "our age".

Less than a century later, Tertullian wrote his Defense of the Christians, addressed to Rome's governors. He used Pliny's findings as proof that they had done no crime, and left out the list, the cursing and the tortured women. Then he turned on the ruling: "What a ruling, confused by necessity!" Need, in other words, had forced it to contradict itself. "It forbids a search for them, as if they were innocent, and it orders them punished, as if they were guilty. It spares and it rages; it looks away and it punishes."

Eusebius of Caesarea, writing in Greek two centuries after Pliny, took the story from Tertullian. His Pliny was "disturbed by the great number of martyrs", though Pliny gives no number. In Jerome's Latin of Eusebius's year-by-year history, he "had killed very many Christians". Historians have argued since whether the reply protected Christians, and Arthur McGiffert, Eusebius's translator, read it as making the faith itself a crime.

The governor who had never attended a trial of Christians had ended his letter with a hope. A great crowd could be set right, he wrote, if there were room for repentance.
