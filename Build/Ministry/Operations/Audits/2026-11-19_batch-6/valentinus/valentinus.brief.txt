Valentinus, a teacher at Rome, once wrote that the heart is like an inn on the road, rutted and filled with dung by guests who care nothing for a place that is not theirs. So it is, he wrote, with a heart no one looks after: "It is unclean, the home of many demons. But when the Father, who alone is good, visits it, it is made holy and shines with light." Those words survive because Clement of Alexandria quoted them to argue against him.

In the middle of the second century, Irenaeus says, Valentinus taught at Rome for some twenty years. He founded a school, a circle of students around a teacher, which taught that a hidden Father lived beyond the world, that heavenly powers came from him in pairs, and that a lesser maker made this world. Almost everything we know of him comes from the men who wrote against him.

Tertullian, writing at Carthage about fifty years later, alone tells the turn in his life: "Valentinus had hoped to become a bishop, for he was able in mind and in speech. But another man won the place, by the claim his suffering for the faith had given him. Angry at this, Valentinus broke away from the church of the true rule." That was the church that kept the true teaching, and Tertullian puts the break down to ambition and a wish for revenge.

As Irenaeus tells the teaching, an unnamed pair, Unspeakable and Silence, gave rise to Father and Truth, then Word and Life, and more pairs after them. When one of the last fell away, its fall brought forth the world and its maker. Hippolytus, another opponent, tells it differently: the Father was alone, and "he did not love being alone. For, he says, he was all love, and love is not love unless there is something to love." Tertullian says Valentinus kept these powers inside God, as his thoughts and feelings, until his pupil Ptolemy made them separate beings. The accounts do not agree, and we cannot always tell the teacher from his students.

In a sermon, Valentinus told his hearers they were immortal from the beginning, children of eternal life, who had wished for death to be given to them so that "death may die in you." Clement answered that this made some people saved by their nature, not by Christ.

No source says when or how he died. His students carried the teaching on, and the school split into an Italian branch and an Eastern one. Irenaeus, Tertullian and Hippolytus wrote long books against it, and in answering they set out what the church held, that one God made the world. Those same books kept the only account of the school for eighteen centuries, until its own writings were found in Egypt in 1945.

By Tertullian's day, he says, Valentinus himself was now nowhere, though his followers still bore his name. Only one of them, Axionicus at Antioch, kept his teaching whole.
