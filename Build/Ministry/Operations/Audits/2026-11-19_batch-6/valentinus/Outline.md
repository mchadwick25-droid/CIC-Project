# Outline: Valentinus

Sheet items (S, E, I, D) are in `Source_Sheet.md`, which gives the file and line for each. CL is Clement's *Stromata* in the ANF English (the Greek is not held); IR is Irenaeus in the ANF English, HOLL the Greek of Epiphanius (with Irenaeus Book I); HIP Hippolytus in the ANF English (the Greek is not held); TE Tertullian in the ANF English, KR and OE his Latin.

- **The essence:** Valentinus was a teacher at Rome in the middle of the second century, there, Irenaeus says, from the time of bishop Hyginus to that of Anicetus (S1; Documented as Irenaeus's report; "about 140 to 160" is the editor's reckoning, Widely Accepted). He founded a school that taught a hidden Father beyond the world, from whom heavenly powers came forth in pairs, and a world made by a lesser maker (E4, E6; Documented as his opponents' account; how much of the scheme is his own and how much his pupils', Contested, D3). Almost everything known of him comes from the men who wrote against him (I3, E8–E12; Documented).
- **Also told:**
  - **The bishopric he missed.** Tertullian, writing about half a century later, says Valentinus hoped to become a bishop, being able in mind and speech; when another man won the place by the claim of having suffered for the faith, he broke with the church (E2; Documented as Tertullian's claim; the event Contested, one hostile witness). Tertullian names no city (D2). Tertullian also says Rome expelled him more than once (E3; one clause, Marcion's story tells it).
  - **What he taught, in his opponents' words.** First an unnamed pair, Unspeakable and Silence; from them Father and Truth; then Word and Life, Man and Church; from these, more powers; one of the last fell away, and from its fall came the world below and its maker (E4; Irenaeus, Documented as his account). In Hippolytus's account the Father was alone, but he was all love, and love needs something to love, so he brought forth a pair (E5, Quote 3; Hippolytus's report of the school). Tertullian says Valentinus kept these powers inside God, as his thoughts and feelings; his pupil Ptolemy made them separate beings (E7; Tertullian's claim).
  - **His own words.** A few letters and sermons, quoted by Clement to refute them: the heart as an inn that the Father makes shine with light (E8, Quote 1); the angels who made Adam and took fright at the seed from above that spoke in him (E9); "you are immortal from the beginning", so that "death may die in you" (E10). Clement charged that he taught some people were saved by nature (E13; Clement's reading).
  - **What came of him.** A school that outlived him and split: Ptolemy and Heracleon in Italy, Axionicus and others in the East (I1; Documented). The church's longest answers of the century were written against it: Irenaeus, Tertullian, Hippolytus, and much later Epiphanius (I2; Documented). For eighteen centuries the school was known almost only through them (I3; the tree's tradition entry, Widely Accepted).
- **Length:** about 2 minutes 40 seconds (about 400 words). Why: the life is thin. Irenaeus gives his years at Rome, Tertullian alone the break, and only a rumour two centuries later gives his birthplace and his end; no time goes on a childhood, a journey or a death the record does not hold. But a teacher's story must let the teaching be heard, and here it needs both his own words and his opponents' account of the pairs, each credited, plus the school and the answer it drew, which carry about a third. Cut first, if needed: beat 1's Theudas clause, beat 5's angels fragment, and What came 3's Bardaisan clause.
- **Setting:**
  - Time: the middle of the second century, under the emperors Hadrian and Antoninus (S3); at Rome from about 140 to about 160 (S1, editor's years). Plain words: "in the middle of the second century". Do not give a birth or death year.
  - Place: Rome (census). Egypt and Alexandria only as a report (S4); Cyprus only as a report (E16).
  - Tradition: the tree's tradition is "Valentinian and other Gnostic Christianities", short name "Gnostic Currents" (census). The writer may say "one of the teachers later grouped as Gnostics"; see Words to explain.
  - Language: he wrote in Greek (the fragments are Greek, quoted by Clement; only the English is held).

**Words to explain** (the first time each is heard; plain word or short gloss, worked into the sentence)

- **aeon(s)**: "heavenly powers" throughout. Never "aeons" bare. If the word is used once, gloss it: "powers his followers called aeons, from a Greek word for an age".
- **pair / syzygy**: "pairs", "two by two". Never "syzygy". Epiphanius's word means a yoked pair (E6); "paired" is enough.
- **Pleroma**: do not use. "the fullness of God", or "the heavenly world".
- **Demiurge**: "the world's maker", "a lesser maker". Never "Demiurge".
- **Gnostic**: credit it: "the teaching Irenaeus called Gnostic, from the Greek word for knowledge" (E4; the gloss is a plain fact of the word). Not as the narrator's own label for him.
- **heresy / heretic**: credit it to the opponent: "what Irenaeus called a heresy", or "a teaching he held false".
- **confessor** (Tertullian's "martyrii praerogativa"): "a man who had suffered for the faith". Not "martyr": he was alive.
- **school**: "a school, a circle of students around a teacher".
- **Platonist**: "a follower of Plato's philosophy", if used (S6).
- **homily**: "a sermon".
- **bishop of Rome**: Hyginus and Anicetus are given by role: "from the time of one bishop of Rome to another, some twenty years".
- **Quote 1's inn** (ANF "caravanserai"): "an inn on the road, where travellers stay". The narrator gives the point in the next sentence: in his teaching, a heart full of evil spirits, until the Father visits.
- **the true rule** (Quote 2, Tertullian's *authentica regula*): the church's rule of faith, the teaching it held as handed down. The narrator gives the plain sense alongside: "the church that kept the true teaching".

- **Shape:** Teacher or thinker. The question he answered: how does a hidden, perfect God relate to this world and to the human heart? What he taught: a Father beyond the world who brought forth powers in pairs; a world made by a lesser maker; a seed from above in human beings. Who learned it: Ptolemy, Heracleon, Axionicus and the two branches of his school. The trap is a list of books or a catalogue of powers; show the idea through his own image of the heart (Quote 1) and the love that needs something to love (Quote 3). Name at most the first four or six powers, never the thirty.
- **Sibling:**
  - `irenaeus-of-lyon` (batch 6 outline, read; job: the answer to the Valentinians). There the answer; here the teaching. There his teaching is one clause, and the mosaic of the king, the "one house", and the Florinus letter are Irenaeus's quotes: none is reused here. Here Irenaeus is a link: credited for the years at Rome (S1) and the account of the pairs (E4), and named once among the opponents (What came 2). Do not retell the rule of faith, the bishops' lists or the four Gospels. Both stories carry the eighteen centuries and the 1945 find in one clause; no wording or image is shared.
  - `hippolytus-of-rome` (researched after this; job includes the *Refutation*). Here the *Refutation* is used for the pairs (E5, Quote 3) and named once. Quote 3 and the vision of the child (E14) are this story's; Hippolytus's story should not reuse Quote 3.
  - `marcion-of-pontus` (live, read). It already says Tertullian has Rome expelling Marcion more than once, and "He says this of Valentinus too, who taught at Rome with him." Here that is one clause, in paraphrase, and Marcion is one clause; its quotes ("Separating the law from the gospel", "the defenders of Judaism", "There is a certain Marcion", "Do you know me?") are not used.
  - `clement-of-alexandria` (live, read). It does not quote Valentinus. Here Clement is one clause: the opponent who kept his words (opening). None of Clement's own lines is used.
  - `polycarp-of-smyrna` (approved text): it says Polycarp turned many at Rome away from Marcion and Valentinus. Not told here, or one clause at most, not quoted.
  - Tradition entry `valentinian-and-other-gnostic-christianities`: it paraphrases Tertullian's bishopric and tells the 1945 find. This story keeps Tertullian's account (batch README) and quotes it; the 1945 find is one clause at most.
  - `bardaisan-of-edessa` (batch 6): it tells that Eusebius says Bardaisan began as his follower. Here, if used, one clause.
- **Openings:**
  1. **Chosen.** His own image (Quote 1; E8). In about three sentences: the heart is like an inn on the road, dug with holes and ruts and filled with dung by guests who do not care for a place that is not theirs; so is a heart full of evil spirits. But when the Father visits it, it is made holy and shines with light. Name him in the first sentence ("Valentinus, a teacher at Rome, once wrote that the heart is like an inn ..."). End the opening on Clement keeping the words, so that it leads into the essence. Then the essence.
  2. Not chosen: Tertullian's bishopric (Quote 2). Strong, but it opens on a hostile witness's motive, half a century late, and it belongs in time order at beat 3.

**Story beats** (the opening, the essence and beat 1 are the first minute, about 150 words)

1. **Before Rome.** He may have come from Egypt: two centuries later, Epiphanius reported that some said he was born on the Egyptian coast and educated in Greek learning at Alexandria, and admits that most people did not know (S4; Inferential-Thin, credit Epiphanius). His followers said he had been taught by a teacher said to have learned from Paul (S5; their claim, as Clement reports it; cut first). One sentence for both, or leave the gap: "no early source says where he was born".
2. **At Rome.** He came to Rome while Hyginus was bishop, was at his height under Pius, and stayed until Anicetus, some twenty years (S1; Irenaeus). Justin, writing at Rome in those years, already spoke of "Valentinians", named after him (E1; optional clause; do not quote). His opponents called him a follower of Plato's philosophy; Hippolytus went further and judged him "a Platonist, not a Christian" (S6; Tertullian, Hippolytus; optional, but if used keep the judgment's force, credited, in paraphrase).
3. **The bishopric (Tertullian's account).** Tertullian, in Carthage, about half a century later, says he hoped to become a bishop, because he was able in mind and in speech; another man won the place by the claim his suffering for the faith had given him; angry, Valentinus broke with the church (E2; Quote 2). Tertullian alone tells this, and he gives the motive: ambition, and a wish for revenge (E2, the clause after the quote; keep its force in paraphrase, credited). He names no city and no year (D2). Tertullian also says the church at Rome expelled him more than once (E3; one clause, credited). Irenaeus gives no motive.
4. **The paired powers.** What he taught, as his opponents tell it. Irenaeus: first an unnamed pair, Unspeakable and Silence; then Father and Truth; then Word and Life, Man and Church; from these, more powers; one of the last fell away, and from that fall came the world below, made by a lesser maker (E4; credit Irenaeus). Hippolytus gives the reason for the pairs: the Father was alone, and he was all love (Quote 3; credit "Hippolytus, another opponent, reports the school's teaching"). Tertullian adds that Valentinus kept these powers inside God, as his thoughts and feelings; it was his pupil Ptolemy who made them separate beings (E7). Say plainly that we cannot always tell his teaching from his students' (D3).
5. **The seed from above, in his own words.** The fragments show the teaching from inside: the angels who made Adam were terrified when a seed from above, placed in him unseen, made him speak greater things than they had given him, and they spoiled their work (E9; paraphrase; cut first). In a sermon he told his hearers they were immortal from the beginning, children of eternal life, and wished to have death given out to them, so that "death may die in you" (E10; paraphrase; the short phrase may stand). Clement answered that this made some people saved by their nature, not by Christ's coming (E13; CL 40954–40960; Clement's charge).
6. **Scripture kept whole, read anew (Tertullian's charge).** Tertullian set him beside Marcion, also at Rome: Marcion cut Scripture with a knife; Valentinus kept it whole, and changed it by giving every word a new sense (E15; paraphrase; Tertullian's view, credited). One clause for Marcion.
7. **The end of the record.** Irenaeus says only that he stayed at Rome until Anicetus (S1). Epiphanius, two centuries later, says that he went to Cyprus and left the faith there (E16; Inferential-Thin; an account that does not fit Irenaeus's). No source says when or how he died.

**What came of him** (about a third of the story; heaviest first)

1. **A school that outlived him, and changed him.** His students carried the teaching on: Ptolemy, Heracleon and others, Tertullian says, each striking off a path of his own; Hippolytus says they split into an Italian branch and an Eastern one (I1; Documented). Heracleon wrote on John's Gospel, and Origen, in the next century, answered him in his own commentary on John (I1; Documented; "the earliest known commentary on John" is the tradition entry's claim, Widely Accepted, may stand credited to "historians" or be left out).
2. **The answer it drew.** Writer after writer wrote against his school: Irenaeus in five books, about 180; Tertullian a whole book *Against the Valentinians*; Hippolytus at Rome; and Epiphanius, two centuries later (I2; Documented). In answering them, the church set out against them what it held: that one God made the world (I2; the tradition entry's `legacy`, Widely Accepted; carry it through what the opponents wrote, not as a moral). What Irenaeus answered is his story; here one clause.
3. **The record and the answer are the same books.** In writing against his school, its opponents kept the only account of it; for eighteen centuries it was known almost only through them, until its own books were found in Egypt in 1945 (I3; tradition entry; one clause). Bardaisan of Edessa, Eusebius says, began as his follower (I1; optional clause).

**Quotes** (3)

Only Quotes 1–3 are quoted aloud. "Death may die in you" (E10) may stand as a short phrase inside the narrator's sentence. Everything else is paraphrase.

1. CL 34296–34302 (*Stromata* II.20; Valentinus, a letter; ANF English; the Greek is not held, so this is rendered from the English). ANF: "And the heart seems to be treated somewhat like a caravanserai. For the latter has holes and ruts made in it, and is often filled with dung; men living filthily in it, and taking no care for the place as belonging to others. So fares it with the heart as long as there is no thought taken for it, being unclean, and the abode of many demons. But when the only good Father visits it, it is sanctified, and gleams with light." Rendering: "The heart seems to be treated somewhat like an inn. An inn has holes and ruts dug in it, and is often filled with dung. Men live in it filthily and take no care of the place, since it belongs to others. So it is with the heart, as long as no one takes thought for it. It is unclean, the home of many demons. But when the Father, who alone is good, visits it, it is made holy and shines with light." The writer may use the last two sentences alone, with the rest in paraphrase. Job: the opening. His own voice and his own image; it shows his teaching from inside, not through his enemies.
2. KR 9633–9634, 9652–9653 (*Against the Valentinians* 4; Latin, Kroymann): "sperauerat episcopatum Valentinus, quia et ingenio poterat et eloquio, sed alium ex martyrii praerogatiua loci potitum indignatus de ecclesia authenticae regulae abrupit". (TE 43109–43114: "Valentinus had expected to become a bishop, because he was an able man both in genius and eloquence. Being indignant, however, that another obtained the dignity by reason of a claim which confessorship had given him, he broke with the church of the true faith.") Rendering (from the Latin): "Valentinus had hoped to become a bishop, for he was able in mind and in speech. But another man won the place, by the claim his suffering for the faith had given him. Angry at this, Valentinus broke away from the church of the true rule." Job: beat 3. The one turning point of his life, in the voice of the hostile witness who alone tells it; credit Tertullian in the same sentence.
3. HIP 9170–9173 (*Refutation* VI.29; ANF English; the Greek is not held, so this is rendered from the English): "for (the Father) was not fond of solitariness. For, says he, He was all love, but love is not love except there may be some object of affection. The Father Himself, then, as He was solitary, projected and produced Nous and Aletheia, that is, a duad". Rendering: "For he did not love being alone. For, he says, he was all love, and love is not love unless there is something to love. So the Father, alone as he was, sent forth Mind and Truth: a pair." The writer may stop after "something to love" and give the pair in paraphrase. Job: beat 4. Why the powers come in pairs, in the school's own reasoning; credit Hippolytus's account of the school, and do not say the words are certainly Valentinus's (the "he" is not named in the text, and "(the Father)" is the translator's bracket, E5).

**Connection to carry into the story:** what passed from Valentinus was a school and the argument it set off. He taught that a hidden Father brought forth heavenly powers in pairs, that the world was made by a lesser maker, and that a seed from above lives in some people (E4–E10). His students carried that teaching on and changed it, Ptolemy and Heracleon in Italy, Axionicus in the East, so that by Tertullian's day the school bore his name but hardly held his teaching (I1, E7). Against that school the church's leading writers wrote their longest answers: Irenaeus, Tertullian, Hippolytus, and Epiphanius long after (I2). The listener must understand two things the story cannot leave to inference. First, that we know him almost only through those opponents: his own words survive because Clement quoted them to argue against him, and his teaching is told in the words of men who thought it false (I3). Second, that the answer and the record are the same books: in writing against his school, they preserved it. Tell it through these acts (the quoting, the writing against, the school's splitting), not as a "this matters because" sentence.

**Ending:** close on the person. Either (a) Tertullian's jibe, from the Latin, in paraphrase: Valentinus is now nowhere, and yet the Valentinians are named after him; only Axionicus at Antioch keeps his teaching whole (I1; KR 9663–9666; credit Tertullian; do not use ANF's "as yet nowhere"); or (b) his own words: the sermon's hope that "death may die in you" (E10; CL 39091–39094), if beat 5 leaves it unused. Recommended: (a), told after What came, since beat 5 already carries the sermon and no event is told twice. Use (b) only if beat 5 leaves the sermon out, so the story ends in his own voice.

**Who tells it**

- **Valentinus himself**, in a few fragments of letters and a sermon, all kept by Clement, about 200 (the Clement census story's date), who quotes them to refute them (E8–E12). Credit "he wrote", and say once, in the opening, that an opponent kept them.
- **Irenaeus**, about 180, an opponent: the years at Rome and the account of the first pairs (S1, E4). Credit "Irenaeus says".
- **Tertullian**, at Carthage, about fifty years after (the ANF editor dates *Against the Valentinians* "not earlier than a.d. 207", TE 42916; "about fifty years" is reckoned from that): the bishopric, the expulsions, the pupil Ptolemy, the knife and the pen, Axionicus (E2, E3, E7, E15, I1). His hostility is open; credit every claim to him, and say he alone tells the bishopric.
- **Hippolytus**, at Rome in the early third century, an opponent: the love that needs a pair, the two branches, the vision (E5, I1, E14). Credit him; he reports the school, not always Valentinus alone.
- **Epiphanius**, about 375 (the corpus-map bucket's date): birthplace and Cyprus, as hearsay (S4, E16). Credit him and his doubt.
- The doubt is part of the story: say once, in the essence, that almost all we know comes from his opponents. No stock how-we-know sentence after that.

**Do not say**

- Any word in "Words to explain" used without its plain sense the first time it is heard.
- Not "the true rule" heard without its plain sense.
- Do not quote the mosaic of the king, "as if it lived in one house", or the Florinus letter (Irenaeus's quotes); or Marcion's lines (Sibling); or any line of the approved Polycarp story.
- Not "he nearly became bishop of Rome", "he lost the election for pope" or "he ran for bishop of Rome". Tertullian names no see and describes no election. Say "he hoped to become a bishop, Tertullian says, and another man won the place" (E2, D2).
- Not "a martyr was chosen instead". The man had suffered for the faith and lived: "a man who had suffered for the faith" (Words to explain).
- Not "he left the church because he was passed over" as fact. It is Tertullian's account and Tertullian's motive (E2, D5). Do not soften it either: Tertullian says he broke away in anger, out of ambition and a wish for revenge.
- Not "Rome expelled him under Eleutherus" as fact, and not any year for the break. Tertullian's dating does not fit Irenaeus's (D1).
- Not "born in Alexandria", "an Egyptian", or "he died in Cyprus" as fact. Only Epiphanius, two centuries later, on hearsay (S4, E16).
- Not "in his last years he went to Cyprus" as fact.
- Not "a pupil of Theudas, who was taught by Paul" as fact. His followers claimed it (S5).
- Not "Valentinus believed …" or "he taught …" for the system in the narrator's voice without credit. Say "Irenaeus says he taught", "in Hippolytus's account" (E4, E5).
- Not "thirty aeons" as his own teaching, and do not list them. The thirty are Epiphanius's and the school's account (E6, D3).
- Do not merge Irenaeus's account and Hippolytus's into one sequence ("Unspeakable and Silence, then the lonely Father brought forth Mind and Truth"). They are two accounts that do not agree. Tell each under its own witness, and say plainly that they differ (D3).
- Not "Gnostic" as the narrator's label for him. Irenaeus says he adapted "the heresy called Gnostic" to his own school (E4).
- Not "he taught that matter is evil" or "the body is a prison". No held fragment says so in those words.
- Not "the Valentinians were a rival church". The held sources do not say how they met; the tradition entry says they mostly stayed in ordinary congregations (census): leave out, or credit it.
- Not "he wrote the Gospel of Truth". Irenaeus says only that his followers had a recent book of that name; who wrote the one found in 1945 is disputed (I4).
- Not "Clement admired him" or "Clement was a Valentinian". Clement quoted him to refute him.
- Not "Valentinus was as yet nowhere" (ANF's misreading). From the Latin: "now nowhere" (I1).
- Not "Heracleon wrote the first commentary on John" as fact. If used, credit it ("historians count it the earliest") or leave it out (I1).
- Not "the church's creed was written against him". The opponents set out the faith against his school; no held source says a creed was made to answer him.
- Not "the vision of the child" told as an event. If told at all, in Hippolytus's voice: "Hippolytus says Valentinus claimed …" (E14).
- Not "the only account of the school" or "known only through them". Say "known almost only through them" (I3).

**Leave out**

- The thirty powers by name, Sophia's fall, Achamoth, the two Limits and the left-hand power (E4, E6): the system's detail is the card's; a catalogue is the shape's trap.
- The letter to Agathopus on Jesus's eating and drinking (E12): needs too much explaining.
- "The common books" fragment (E11): hard to hear once.
- The vision of the newborn child (E14): a wonder told by one hostile witness; optional at most, in Hippolytus's voice.
- Ptolemy's Letter to Flora, and the Excerpts of Theodotus: his pupils' works, not his.
- Polycarp at Rome turning many from him (I2): Polycarp's and Irenaeus's stories.
- Florinus: Irenaeus's story.
- Jerome's Chronicle, McGiffert's and Holl's reckonings by name (S1, S2, E16): for the card.
- The 1945 find beyond one clause: the tradition entry's story.
- The editors' praise of his system's "depth and power of mind" (EUS 22169–22171, HIP 9159–9165 notes): editors.
