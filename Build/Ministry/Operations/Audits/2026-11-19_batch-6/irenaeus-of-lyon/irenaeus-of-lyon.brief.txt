"It is as if a skilled artist had carefully made a fine portrait of a king from choice small stones. Then someone broke up the man's likeness. He moved those stones, fitted them together again, and made the shape of a dog or a fox, and a poor one at that."

The portrait is a mosaic, a picture made of small colored stones, and he insists the fox is still the king. That, wrote Irenaeus, bishop of Lyon in Roman Gaul, today's France, is what the followers of Valentinus did with Scripture. Valentinus had taught at Rome a generation earlier, and his followers claimed, Irenaeus says, that their secret teaching went deeper than the apostles'.

As a boy Irenaeus had heard the old bishop Polycarp of Smyrna, and he became bishop himself after a persecution in 177. His five books against these teachers, written about 180, are the fullest answer we still have. Against their secret teaching he set three open things: the faith received at baptism, the churches the apostles founded, and four Gospels.

In the year 177 a persecution struck the Christians of Lyon and Vienne, on the river Rhône. Their bishop, Pothinus, more than ninety years old, the survivors wrote, was beaten and died in prison. From prison they asked Irenaeus, one of the elders who led the church with its bishop, to carry their letters to Rome. Others were put to death, and their burned remains were swept into the Rhône, so that, the persecutors said, they could not hope to rise again.

Irenaeus became bishop after Pothinus, Eusebius says, and most of what we know of his life comes from that historian, writing more than a century later. The new bishop read his opponents' books. They taught, he says, that the world's maker was a lesser power below a perfect, hidden God. He called their system madness and blasphemy against Christ, and likened them to wolves in sheep's clothing.

His first answer was the faith received from the apostles, in one God the maker, in Christ Jesus who became flesh, suffered and rose, and in the Holy Spirit. A Christian received this short summary at baptism, he says, as a rule to measure by. "The church has received this preaching and this faith, as I said. Though it is scattered through the whole world, it guards them with care, as if it lived in one house. It believes them alike, as if it had one soul and one heart. It preaches them, teaches them and hands them on in harmony, as if it had one mouth." From Germany and Spain to Egypt and Libya, he says, the churches hand on no other.

His second answer anyone could check. If the apostles had secret teachings, he argued, they would have given them to the men they left in charge of the churches. Those bishops could be counted, one after another, to his own day. He counted Rome's, down to the bishop of his own time, in "the twelfth place from the apostles". Historians still argue whether Rome then had a single bishop.

His third answer was four Gospels, by Matthew, Mark, Luke and John, for as there are four winds, he says, the church has four pillars. The followers of Valentinus claimed more, and Marcion fewer.

Florinus, a man Irenaeus had seen as a boy at Polycarp's side, had become an elder at Rome. He seemed, Eusebius says, to teach that God was the author of evil, and he was being drawn toward Valentinus. Irenaeus wrote to him that the elders before them, who had known the apostles, never handed on such teachings. "I remember that time more clearly than recent years. For what boys learn grows with the mind and becomes one with it. So I can describe the very place where the blessed Polycarp sat as he taught." He could describe, he wrote, how Polycarp came and went, how he lived and looked, and his talks to the people. "And I can tell how he spoke of his time with John and the others who had seen the Lord."

Later writers drew on his book for generations: Hippolytus called him "the blessed elder", and Tertullian "a very exact inquirer". About two centuries on, Epiphanius copied his opening account of the Valentinians word for word, and that copy is how much of its Greek survives. For eighteen centuries the Valentinians were known almost only through opponents such as him, until books of their own were found in Egypt in 1945.

A Christian who holds the rule received at baptism, Irenaeus wrote, will know the stones of the broken mosaic, but will not take the fox for the king. He puts each one back in its place, and the false picture falls apart.
