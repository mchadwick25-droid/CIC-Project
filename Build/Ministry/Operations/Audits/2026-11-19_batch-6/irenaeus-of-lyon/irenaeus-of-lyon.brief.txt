"It is as if a skilled artist had carefully made a fine portrait of a king from choice small stones. Then someone broke up the man's likeness. He moved those stones, fitted them together again, and made the shape of a dog or a fox, and a poor one at that."

The portrait is a mosaic, a picture made of small colored stones, and the man insists the fox is still the king. That, wrote a bishop in Roman Gaul about the year 180, is what the followers of Valentinus did with Scripture.

The bishop was Irenaeus of Lyon, and his five books against them are the fullest answer we still have. Valentinus had taught at Rome a generation earlier. His followers taught, Irenaeus says, that the world's maker was a lesser power below a perfect, hidden God, and that their own secret teaching went deeper than the apostles'. Against it Irenaeus set three open things: the faith received at baptism, the churches the apostles founded, and four Gospels.

As a boy, in the Roman province of Asia, on the west coast of today's Turkey, he had heard the old bishop Polycarp of Smyrna. How he came to Gaul, no early source says.

In the year 177 a persecution struck the Christians of Lyon and Vienne, on the river Rhone. Their bishop, Pothinus, more than ninety years old, was beaten and died in prison. While still in prison, the Christians sent letters to Rome about a quarrel that troubled the churches, and chose Irenaeus, one of the elders who led the church with its bishop, to carry them. Others died for their faith, the survivors wrote, and their ashes were swept into the Rhone, so that, the persecutors said, they could have no hope of rising again.

Irenaeus became bishop after Pothinus, Eusebius says, and most of what we know of his life comes from that historian, writing more than a century later. Irenaeus read his opponents' books, called their system madness and blasphemy against Christ, and likened them to wolves in sheep's clothing. At a friend's request, he wrote to expose and answer them.

His first answer was the faith the church had received from the apostles, in one God who made heaven and earth, in Christ Jesus who became flesh, suffered and rose, and in the Holy Spirit. A Christian received this short summary at baptism, he says, as a rule to measure by. "The church has received this preaching and this faith, as I said. Though it is scattered through the whole world, it guards them with care, as if it lived in one house. It believes them alike, as if it had one soul and one heart. It preaches them, teaches them and hands them on in harmony, as if it had one mouth." From Germany and Spain to Egypt and Libya, he says, the churches hand on no other.

His second answer was one anyone could check. If the apostles had secret teachings, he argued, they would have given them to the men they left in charge of the churches. And anyone could count those bishops, one after another, to his own day. He counted Rome's, from Linus to the bishop of his own time, in "the twelfth place from the apostles".

His third answer was four Gospels, by Matthew, Mark, Luke and John. As there are four winds, he says, the church has four pillars. The followers of Valentinus claimed more, and Marcion fewer.

Florinus, a man Irenaeus had seen as a boy at Polycarp's side, had become an elder at Rome and then fallen from that office. He seemed, Eusebius says, to teach that God was the author of evil, and he was being drawn toward Valentinus. Irenaeus wrote to him that the elders before them had never handed on such teachings. "I remember that time more clearly than recent years. For what boys learn grows with the mind and becomes one with it. So I can describe the very place where the blessed Polycarp sat as he taught. And I can tell how he spoke of his time with John and the others who had seen the Lord." Polycarp, he wrote, would have stopped his ears and fled. Later, in a quarrel over the date of Easter, he urged the bishop of Rome not to cut off whole churches. Then the record falls silent.

Later writers drew on his book for generations. Hippolytus called him "the blessed elder", and Tertullian "a very exact inquirer". About two hundred years on, Epiphanius copied his opening account of the Valentinians word for word, and that copy is the main way the Greek of the first book survives. For eighteen centuries the Valentinians were known almost only through opponents such as him, until books of their own were found in Egypt in 1945. His list of Rome's bishops fed later arguments over who holds the apostles' teaching, though historians still argue whether Rome then had a single bishop.

Irenaeus had his own answer to the broken mosaic. A Christian who holds the rule received at baptism, he wrote, will know the stones, but will not take the fox for the king. He puts each one back in its place, and the false picture falls apart.
