"It is as if a skilled artist had carefully made a fine portrait of a king from choice small stones. Then someone broke up the man's likeness. He moved those stones, fitted them together again, and made the shape of a dog or a fox, and a poor one at that."

The portrait is a mosaic, a picture made of small colored stones, and the man who broke it then insisted it was still the king. That, wrote a bishop in Roman Gaul about the year 180, is what the followers of Valentinus did with Scripture.

The bishop was Irenaeus of Lyon. Valentinus had been a teacher at Rome a generation earlier. His followers taught, Irenaeus says, that the world's maker was a lesser power below a perfect, hidden God, and that their own secret teaching went deeper than the apostles'. Against them Irenaeus wrote five books, Against Heresies, the fullest answer to them we still have. He set three things against a secret teaching: the faith every church received at baptism, the churches the apostles founded, with their bishops counted back to them, and four Gospels, no more and no fewer.

As a boy, in the Roman province of Asia, on the west coast of today's Turkey, he had heard the old bishop Polycarp of Smyrna. How and when he came to Gaul, no early source says.

In the year 177, a persecution struck the Christians of Lyon and Vienne, on the river Rhone. Their bishop, Pothinus, more than ninety years old and barely able to breathe, was beaten and died in prison two days later. While still in prison, the Christians sent letters to Eleutherius, bishop of Rome, about a quarrel that troubled the churches. They chose Irenaeus, one of the elders who led the church with its bishop, to carry them, and asked Eleutherius to honor him as one zealous for Christ's covenant. Others died for their faith by the sword or the wild beasts, the survivors wrote. Their bodies were burned and the ashes swept into the Rhone, so that, the persecutors said, they could have no hope of rising again.

Irenaeus became bishop of Lyon after Pothinus, Eusebius says, and most of what we know of his life comes from that historian, writing more than a century later. Irenaeus wrote in Greek, but he lived, he says, among the Celts, the people of Gaul, busy mostly with a foreign tongue. Even along the Rhone, the followers of one teacher of that school had led many women astray. Such teachers claimed, he says, that the truth had come down not in writing but by word of mouth, to them, who were wiser than the apostles. He called their system madness and blasphemy against Christ, and likened them to wolves in sheep's clothing. He read their books and talked with some of them, and at a friend's request he wrote to expose and answer them.

His first answer was the faith the church had received from the apostles: one God, maker of heaven and earth; one Christ Jesus, the Son of God, who became flesh, suffered, rose and will come again; and the Holy Spirit, who spoke through the prophets. A Christian received this short summary at baptism, he says, as a rule, a measure to test by. He wrote: "The church has received this preaching and this faith, as I said. Though it is scattered through the whole world, it guards them with care, as if it lived in one house. It believes them alike, as if it had one soul and one heart. It preaches them, teaches them and hands them on in harmony, as if it had one mouth." The churches in Germany and Spain, among the Celts, in the East, in Egypt and in Libya hand on no other. The most gifted speaker adds nothing to it, and the weakest takes nothing away.

His second answer was open to anyone who cared to check. The apostles' teaching could be seen in the churches they founded, where anyone could count the bishops, one after another, to his own day. If the apostles had known secret teachings, he argued, they would have given them above all to the men they left in charge. Listing every church would take too long, so he gave Rome's, from Linus to Eleutherius, in "the twelfth place from the apostles", and then named Polycarp at Smyrna and the church at Ephesus. The followers of Valentinus, he added, had not existed before Valentinus.

His third answer was four Gospels, by Matthew, Mark, Luke and John. As there are four winds, he says, and the church is spread through the whole world, it has four pillars. The followers of Valentinus claimed more, and called a recent book of theirs the Gospel of Truth, while Marcion made them fewer. Yet even they built on these Gospels, the Valentinians above all on John's.

Florinus, a man Irenaeus had seen as a boy at Polycarp's side, had become an elder at Rome and then fallen from that office. He seemed, Eusebius says, to teach that God was the author of evil, and he was being drawn toward Valentinus. On the first, Irenaeus wrote to him that the elders before them, who had known the apostles, never handed on such teachings. "I remember that time more clearly than recent years. For what boys learn grows with the mind and becomes one with it. So I can describe the very place where the blessed Polycarp sat as he taught. And I can tell how he spoke of his time with John and the others who had seen the Lord." Polycarp, he wrote, would have stopped his ears and fled the place where he heard such words. Later, in a quarrel over the date of Easter, he urged Victor of Rome not to cut off whole churches. After that, the record falls silent.

Writers against false teaching drew on his book for generations. Hippolytus, at Rome in the next generation, called him "the blessed elder", and Tertullian, in Carthage, "a very exact inquirer". About two hundred years later, Epiphanius gave up setting out the same teachers' ideas himself and copied Irenaeus's long opening account of them word for word. He called Irenaeus a noble athlete who had wrestled their teaching to the ground. That copy is the main way the Greek of the first book survives, since the whole work lives only in an old Latin translation. For eighteen centuries the followers of Valentinus were known almost only through opponents such as him, until books of their own were found in Egypt in 1945 and let them speak for themselves. Eusebius copied his list of Rome's bishops, and it later became a starting point for arguments over who holds the apostles' teaching, though historians still argue whether Rome then had a single bishop at its head.

Irenaeus had his own answer to the broken mosaic. A Christian who holds the rule received at baptism, he wrote, will know the stones, but will not take the fox for the king. He puts each one back in its place, and the false picture falls apart.
