# Source sheet: Irenaeus of Lyon

Researcher's sheet for batch 6 (2026-11-19). The job: from Polycarp's Smyrna to Lyon after the martyrs of 177: *Against Heresies*, answering the Valentinians with the apostles' churches, one rule of faith and four Gospels. Shape: Teacher or thinker. The writer uses the outline, the shapes and the standard only; this sheet is the evidence behind the outline.

## 1. Setting

S1. **Asia, and Polycarp.** "For when I was a boy, I saw thee in lower Asia with Polycarp, moving in splendor in the royal court, and endeavoring to gain his approbation" (EUS 30792–30805; the note on "royal court" cut). "Polycarp also was not only instructed by apostles, and conversed with many who had seen Christ, but was also, by apostles in Asia, appointed bishop of the Church in Smyrna, whom I also saw in my early youth" (ANF 39857–39860). OWN. Documented that he saw and heard Polycarp as a boy in the Roman province of Asia. **No held line says he was born at Smyrna or lived there**; he says "lower Asia", and that Polycarp was bishop of Smyrna. Birth "in Asia Minor, probably between the years 120 and 130" is McGiffert's, following Lipsius (EUS-ed 24470–24476); NPNF3-ed gives "between 140 and 145" (NPNF3 40168 note); PHOT-ed "c. 120-140" (PHOT 11596). Contested; editors only. Plain words: "as a boy, in the Roman province of Asia, on the west coast of today's Turkey, where Polycarp was bishop of Smyrna".

S2. **Lyon and Vienne, in Gaul.** "The country in which the arena was prepared for them was Gaul, of which Lyons and Vienne are the principal and most celebrated cities. The Rhone passes through both of them, flowing in a broad stream through the entire region" (EUS 26795–26801; McGiffert's place note cut). CONTEMP via Eusebius. Documented. The letter is addressed "to the brethren through out Asia and Phrygia" (EUS 26870–26874). That Gaul had many people from Asia Minor and close ties with it is McGiffert's (EUS-ed 26845–26853); a brisk trade between Marseilles and Smyrna is Coxe's (ANF-ed 29428–29429). Editors.

S3. **Among the Celts, in Greek.** Greek: "Οὐχ ἐπιζητήσεις δὲ παρ᾽ ἡμῶν, τῶν ἐν Κελτοῖς διατριβόντων χαὶ τερὶ βάρβαρον διάλεχτον τὸ πλεῖστον ἀσχολουμένων, λόγων τέχνην ἣν 00% ἄϑομεν" (HOLL 30142–30144, OCR as printed). ANF: "Thou wilt not expect from me, who am resident among the Keltæ, and am accustomed for the most part to use a barbarous dialect, any display of rhetoric, which I have never learned" (ANF 29884–29890; note cut). OWN. Documented that he says he lived among the Celts and was mostly busy with a non-Greek tongue. **Which tongue, and whether he preached in it, he does not say.** Plain words: "he lived among the Celts, he says, and spent most of his days in a foreign tongue".

S4. **The year 177.** "In the seventeenth year of the Emperor Antoninus Verus" (EUS 26706–26707); McGiffert: "i.e., the seventeenth year of the reign of Marcus Aurelius, a.d. 177" (EUS-ed 26707). The Chronicle puts it at 167; McGiffert argues 177 (EUS-ed 26708–26716). Widely Accepted for 177. Census: `activeYear` 177, `dates` "c. 177-190" (CEN).

S5. **Tradition.** Census `toldWithin`: `post-apostolic-house-church`, spoken name "The Scattered Households" (CEN). Plain words: "the churches of the generations after the apostles". The census `status` says his story is told inside that tradition (CEN). Do not say "post-apostolic" aloud.

S6. **Who the Valentinians were, as he tells it.** "I refer especially to the disciples of Ptolemæus, whose school may be described as a bud from that of Valentinus" (ANF 29872–29873). Their teaching in his account: "in the invisible and ineffable heights above there exists a certain perfect, pre-existent Æon" (ANF 29919–29921); the maker of the world, the Demiurge, is a lesser being, formed within their system (ANF 30544–30545, chapter title; ANF-ed 29820–29822). Their own claim: that the truth "was not delivered by means of written documents, but vivâ voce", and that they are "wiser not merely than the presbyters, but even than the apostles" (ANF 39723–39724, 39740–39742). OWN, a hostile witness. Documented as his account. The teaching itself belongs to the `valentinus` story; here only what the listener needs to follow the answer.

## 2. Events

E1. **A boy who heard Polycarp.** S1 above. The Polycarp story already tells that he heard Polycarp as a boy; here it is a clause (sibling).

E2. **How he came to Gaul: not known.** No held line of his own or of Eusebius says when or why he went to Gaul. Gregory of Tours, about four centuries later, says he was "sent to the city by the blessed Polycarp" (GT 1077–1078): late legend. Coxe says Polycarp sent Pothinus to Gaul and that Irenaeus joined him "having been his fellow-pupil under Polycarp" (ANF-ed 29429–29431): editor, no source given. Inferential-Thin; leave out.

E3. **The persecution of 177.** The letter of Vienne and Lyon: "The blessed Pothinus, who had been entrusted with the bishopric of Lyons, was dragged to the judgment seat. He was more than ninety years of age, and very infirm" (EUS 27172–27174). Asked who the God of the Christians was, "he replied, 'If thou art worthy, thou shalt know.'" He was beaten, "cast into prison and died after two days" (EUS 27185–27193). Of the others, "some were beheaded, others cast to the wild beasts, and others fell asleep in prison" (EUS 27742–27744). "The bodies of the martyrs … were afterward burned and reduced to ashes, and swept into the Rhone … so that no trace of them might appear on the earth" (EUS 27482–27486), so that, the persecutors said, "they may have no hope of a resurrection" (EUS 27489–27490). CONTEMP. Documented. **The letter does not say Irenaeus was in Lyon when they died.** The number who died is not given; "forty-eight" is Ado's, and McGiffert thinks Ado's list "largely imaginary" (EUS-ed 26824–26831).

E4. **Sent to Rome with their letter.** Eusebius: the brethren in Gaul "published also several epistles from the witnesses that had been put to death among them. These they sent, while they were still in prison, to the brethren throughout Asia and Phrygia, and also to Eleutherus, who was then bishop of Rome, negotiating for the peace of the churches" (EUS 27674–27680). The question was the "new prophecy" of Montanus in Phrygia (EUS 27669–27675). The martyrs' own words: "We have requested our brother and comrade Irenæus to carry this letter to you, and we ask you to hold him in esteem, as zealous for the covenant of Christ. For if we thought that office could confer righteousness upon any one, we should commend him among the first as a presbyter of the church, which is his position" (EUS 27733–27739). Eusebius: "already at that time a presbyter of the parish of Lyons" (EUS 27729). Jerome: "sent to Rome as legate by the martyrs of this place, on account of certain ecclesiastical questions" (NPNF3 40168–40171). CONTEMP and LATER. Documented. **Which side of the Montanist question the letter took is disputed**: "commonly assumed" that it favoured them; Salmon and McGiffert argue it opposed them (EUS-ed 27680–27717). Contested; leave out. Coxe's claims that he was sent "during this persecution" with letters "against the rising pestilence of heresy", and that he met at Rome a Valentinian friend from Polycarp's school, are Coxe's (ANF-ed 29434–29442).

E5. **Bishop of Lyon.** "Pothinus having died with the other martyrs in Gaul at ninety years of age, Irenæus succeeded him in the episcopate of the church at Lyons" (EUS 27953–27955). "Afterwards when Pothinus, nearly ninety years of age, received the crown of martyrdom for Christ, he was put in his place" (NPNF3 40172–40174). LATER. Documented as Eusebius's account. Exact year not known; "he cannot have succeeded immediately" but no great time passed (EUS-ed 24491–24497). Note the two witnesses differ on Pothinus's age: the letter says "more than ninety", Eusebius "at ninety", Jerome "nearly ninety". Say "more than ninety", the letter's own words.

E6. **Why he wrote.** A friend asked him: "Thou hast indeed enjoined upon me, my very dear friend, that I should bring to light the Valentinian doctrines, concealed, as their votaries imagine" (ANF 39633–39635). He read their books and met them: "after reading some of the Commentaries, as they call them, of the disciples of Valentinus, and after making myself acquainted with their tenets through personal intercourse with some of them" (ANF 29851–29854). Teachers of that school were active near him: "in our own district of the Rhone, they have deluded many women" (ANF 31912–31913). This is said of the followers of Marcus (*Against Heresies* I.13, ANF 31734 chapter title), one teacher among those he treats after Valentinus; not of Valentinians in general. Their danger, as he saw it: "their language resembles ours, while their sentiments are very different" (ANF 29850–29851). OWN. Documented as his account.

E7. **The book and its date.** Its title, as Eusebius gives it: "Refutation and Overthrow of the Knowledge Falsely So-called" (EUS 28053–28056); ANF-ed: "generally referred to under the shorter title, Against Heresies" (ANF 29720–29722). Five books (EUS 28053; PHOT 11571–11583). Book III names Eleutherius as bishop of Rome "now, in the twelfth place from the apostles" (ANF 39849–39851); Eusebius: "under whom he composed his work" (EUS 27959–27962). McGiffert: "It was written—or at least the third book was—while Eleutherus was bishop of Rome, i.e. between 174 and 189" (EUS-ed 24537–24539), and "The author's primary object was to refute Valentinianism" (EUS-ed 24541–24542). Documented that Book III was written under Eleutherius; "about 180" Widely Accepted (CEN tradition entry `valentinian-and-other-gnostic-christianities`: "Around 180"). Do not give a single year as fact.

E8. **Florinus, a man from Polycarp's circle, drawn to Valentinus.** Eusebius: Irenaeus wrote "another to Florinus On Monarchy, or That God is not the Author of Evil. For Florinus seemed to be defending this opinion. And because he was being drawn away by the error of Valentinus, Irenæus wrote his work On the Ogdoad" (EUS 30656, 30718–30721; notes cut). Florinus "fell from the presbyterate of the Church" at Rome (EUS 29286–29288). The letter: "These doctrines, O Florinus, to speak mildly, are not of sound judgment. … These doctrines, the presbyters who were before us, and who were companions of the apostles, did not deliver to thee" (EUS 30784–30790). Then the memory of Polycarp (T7 below), and "if that blessed and apostolic presbyter had heard any such thing, he would have cried out, and stopped his ears … And he would have fled from the place" (EUS 30824–30829). OWN, via Eusebius. Documented. **Date: Contested.** Lightfoot made the letter one of his earliest writings; Lipsius, followed by McGiffert, puts it under Victor, after *Against Heresies* (EUS-ed 30696–30714). Editors.

E9. **The Easter quarrel: one clause here.** When Victor of Rome cut off the churches of Asia, Irenaeus, "sending letters in the name of the brethren in Gaul over whom he presided", held that Easter should be kept on the Lord's day, yet "fittingly admonishes Victor that he should not cut off whole churches of God which observed the tradition of an ancient custom" (EUS 31436–31441). Eusebius: "Thus Irenæus, who truly was well named, became a peacemaker in this matter" (EUS 31659–31664); the name is from the Greek word for peace (EUS-ed 31660–31662). Jerome agrees (NPNF3 40192–40197). Documented. The Polycarp story tells this letter; `polycrates-of-ephesus` tells the quarrel. Here: a clause.

E10. **His death: not known.** "As we have no trace of him, however, subsequent to the time of the paschal controversy, it is probable that he died, at the latest, soon after the beginning of the third century" (EUS-ed 24512–24515). Jerome is first to call him a martyr, in a later commentary on Isaiah (not held), and Gregory of Tours gives the first full account; McGiffert: "the silence of Tertullian, Hippolytus, Eusebius, and all the earlier Fathers … must be accepted as conclusive evidence that he was not a martyr" (EUS-ed 24501–24512; McGiffert's view). Gregory: "This executioner, after causing divers tortures to be inflicted on the blessed Irenaeus in his presence, dedicated him by martyrdom to Christ the Lord" (GT 1087–1089). PHOT-ed: "He is said to have been martyred under Severus, but this is not regarded as certain" (PHOT 11597–11598). Contested; the early record is silent.

## 3. The teaching (his own words)

T1. **The mosaic of the king.** Greek: "ὅνπερ τρόπον εἴ τις βασιλέως εἰχόνος χαλῆς κατεσχευασμένης ἐπιμελῶς ἐχ Ψηφίδων ἐπισήμων ὑπὸ σοφοῦ τεχνίτου, λύσας τὴν ὑποχειμένην τοῦ ἀγϑρώπου ἰδέαν μετενέγχοι τὰς ψηφῖδας ἐχείνας χαὶ μεϑαρμόσοι χαὶ ποιήσοι μορφὴν χυνὸς ἢ ἀλῴπεχος χαὶ ταύτην φαύλως χατεσχευασμένην, ἔπειτα διορίζοιτο χαὶ λέγοι ταύτην εἶναι τὴν τοῦ βασιλέως ἐχείνην εἰχόνα τὴν καλήν, ἣν ὃ σοφὸς τεχνίτης χατεσχεύασεν" (HOLL 31907–31913). ANF: "Their manner of acting is just as if one, when a beautiful image of a king has been constructed by some skilful artist out of precious jewels, should then take this likeness of the man all to pieces, should rearrange the gems, and so fit them together as to make them into the form of a dog or of a fox, and even that but poorly executed; and should then maintain and declare that this was the beautiful image of the king which the skilful artist constructed" (ANF 30993–31000). What it is about: the Valentinians "disregard the order and the connection of the Scriptures, and so far as in them lies, dismember and destroy the truth" (ANF 30989–30990; Greek HOLL 31904–31905); "In like manner do these persons patch together old wives' fables, and then … adapt the oracles of God to their baseless fictions" (ANF 31006–31010; Greek HOLL 31918–31920). OWN. Documented. *Against Heresies* I.8.1.

T2. **The rule of truth received at baptism.** Greek: "οὕτω δὲ χαὶ ὃ τὸν κανόνα τῆς ἀληϑείας ἀχλινῆ ἐν ἑαυτῷ κατέχων, ὃν διὰ τοῦ βαπτίσματος εἴληφεν … χαὶ γὰρ εἰ τὰς ψηφῖδας γνωρίσει, ἀλλὰ τὴν ἀλώπεχα ἀντὶ τῆς βασιλικῆς εἰκόνος οὐ παραδέξεται" (HOLL 32635–32639). ANF: "he also who retains unchangeable in his heart the rule of the truth which he received by means of baptism, will doubtless recognise the names, the expressions, and the parables taken from the Scriptures, but will by no means acknowledge the blasphemous use which these men make of them. For, though he will acknowledge the gems, he will certainly not receive the fox instead of the likeness of the king. But when he has restored every one of the expressions quoted to its proper position, and has fitted it to the body of the truth, he will lay bare, and prove to be without any foundation, the figment of these heretics" (ANF 31353–31364; note cut). The word for "rule" is κανών, a builder's straight rule (ANF-ed 31353–31356). OWN. Documented. *Against Heresies* I.9.4.

T3. **The one faith.** What the church received "from the apostles and their disciples": "in one God, the Father Almighty, Maker of heaven, and earth, and the sea, and all things that are in them; and in one Christ Jesus, the Son of God, who became incarnate for our salvation; and in the Holy Spirit, who proclaimed through the prophets" the birth from a virgin, the passion, the resurrection and the ascension in the flesh, and his coming again to judge (ANF 31388–31417). Greek HOLL 32655–32660 and following. OWN. Documented. *Against Heresies* I.10.1.

T4. **One house, one mouth.** Greek: "τοῦτο τὸ χήρυγμα παρειληφυῖα χαὶ ταύτην τὴν πίστιν ὡς προέφαμεν ἡ ἐχχλησία, χαίπερ ἐν ὅλῳ τῷ χόσμῳ διεσπαρμένη, ἐπιμελῶς φυλάσσει: ὡς ἕνα οἶχον οἰχοῦσα χαὶ ὁμοίως πιστεύει τούτοις ὡς μίαν ψυχὴν χαὶ τὴν αὐτὴν ἔχουσα χαρδίαν χαὶ συμφώνως ταῦτα χηρύσσει χαὶ διδάσχει χαὶ παραδίδωσιν ὡς ἕν στόμα χεχτημένη" (HOLL 32715–32719; the OCR's line breaks and the editor's margin numbers removed). Then: "οὔτε αἱ ἐν Γερμανίαις ἱδρυμέναι ἐκχλησίαι ἄλλως πεπιστεύχασιν ἢ ἄλλως παραδιδόασιν οὔτε ἐν ταῖς Ἰβηρίαις οὔτε ἐν Κελτοῖς οὔτε χατὰ τὰς ἀνατολὰς οὔτε ἐν Αἰγύπτῳ οὔτε ἐν Λιβύῃ" (HOLL 32721–32723). ANF: "the Church, having received this preaching and this faith, although scattered throughout the whole world, yet, as if occupying but one house, carefully preserves it. She also believes these points [of doctrine] just as if she had but one soul, and one and the same heart, and she proclaims them, and teaches them, and hands them down, with perfect harmony, as if she possessed only one mouth. For, although the languages of the world are dissimilar, yet the import of the tradition is one and the same. For the Churches which have been planted in Germany do not believe or hand down anything different, nor do those in Spain, nor those in Gaul, nor those in the East, nor those in Egypt, nor those in Libya" (ANF 31419–31429). The Greek says "among the Celts" where ANF says "in Gaul". He adds that neither the most eloquent leader adds to it nor the least able takes from it (ANF 31434–31441). OWN. Documented. *Against Heresies* I.10.2.

T5. **The apostles' churches and their bishops.** "It is within the power of all, therefore, in every Church, who may wish to see the truth, to contemplate clearly the tradition of the apostles manifested throughout the whole world; and we are in a position to reckon up those who were by the apostles instituted bishops in the Churches, and [to demonstrate] the succession of these men to our own times" (ANF 39768–39773). The argument: "if the apostles had known hidden mysteries, which they were in the habit of imparting to 'the perfect' apart and privily from the rest, they would have delivered them especially to those to whom they were also committing the Churches themselves" (ANF 39775–39778). Because listing every church "would be very tedious", he gives Rome's (ANF 39785–39794), from Linus to "Eleutherius does now, in the twelfth place from the apostles, hold the inheritance of the episcopate" (ANF 39823–39851), then Polycarp at Smyrna and the church at Ephesus (ANF 39857–39898). "Should we not have recourse to the most ancient Churches with which the apostles held constant intercourse?" (ANF 39926–39928). And the peoples who believe "without paper or ink", keeping the old tradition, would "stop their ears" at the heretics' words (ANF 39934–39958). Against them: "prior to Valentinus, those who follow Valentinus had no existence" (ANF 39964–39965). OWN, from the Latin only. Documented. *Against Heresies* III.3–4. **The clause on Rome's "pre-eminent authority" (ANF 39794–39796) is a famous crux**: the Latin's meaning and its Greek original are disputed, by the translators' own note and Coxe's (ANF-ed 39796–39816). Contested; leave out of the story.

T6. **Four Gospels, no more and no fewer.** Who wrote them: Matthew "among the Hebrews in their own dialect", Mark "the disciple and interpreter of Peter", Luke "the companion of Paul", and John, "during his residence at Ephesus in Asia" (ANF 39686–39698; Eusebius quotes it, EUS 28174–28198). "It is not possible that the Gospels can be either more or fewer in number than they are. For, since there are four zones of the world in which we live, and four principal winds, while the Church is scattered throughout all the world, and the 'pillar and ground' of the Church is the Gospel and the spirit of life; it is fitting that she should have four pillars" (ANF 41044–41054). The Valentinians "boast that they possess more Gospels than there really are", and call "their comparatively recent writing 'the Gospel of Truth'" (ANF 41173–41178). Marcion makes them fewer (ANF 41148–41150). Even heretics start from these Gospels: "those … who follow Valentinus, making copious use of that according to John" (ANF 41037–41040). OWN, from the Latin. Documented. *Against Heresies* III.1.1, III.11.7–9. McGiffert notes he "is unable to say that Matthew translated his Gospel into Greek" (EUS-ed 28175–28186): editor's criticism.

T7. **"The very place where Polycarp sat"** (the letter to Florinus). "I remember the events of that time more clearly than those of recent years. For what boys learn, growing with their mind, becomes joined with it; so that I am able to describe the very place in which the blessed Polycarp sat as he discoursed, and his goings out and his comings in, and the manner of his life, and his physical appearance, and his discourses to the people, and the accounts which he gave of his intercourse with John and with the others who had seen the Lord" (EUS 30807–30815). "These things being told me by the mercy of God, I listened to them attentively, noting them down, not on paper, but in my heart" (EUS 30821–30823). OWN, via Eusebius; the Greek is not held. Documented. Reserved to this story in batch 6 (README).

T8. **Not a stylist.** He says he never learned rhetoric (S3), and writes "simply, truthfully, and in my own homely way", leaving his friend to expand what he gives "only the seminal principles" (ANF 29891–29898). OWN. Documented.

## 4. Impact (heaviest first)

I1. **The storehouse for later writers against heresy.** McGiffert: "the most important of the polemical writers of antiquity, and his works formed a storehouse from which all subsequent heresiographers drew" (EUS-ed 24517–24519). Hippolytus, early third century: "the blessed presbyter Irenæus, having approached the subject of a refutation in a more unconstrained spirit, has explained such washings and redemptions" (HIPP 10028–10031). Tertullian names him among those who answered the heretics: "Irenæus, that very exact inquirer into all doctrines" (TERT 43167); Coxe: "Tertullian appropriates the work of Irenæus, (B. i.) against the Gnostics without further ceremony" (TERT, Coxe note 43167–43169; editor). Photius, ninth century: Hippolytus was "the pupil of Irenaeus", and his book was "a synopsis" of Irenaeus's lectures (PHOT 11632–11636; "It is said" for the pupilship: Photius's own wording). Documented for Hippolytus's and Tertullian's words; pupilship Widely Accepted on Photius, a late witness.

I2. **His Greek kept by Epiphanius.** Epiphanius, about two centuries later, says he did not wish to set out the rest of Valentinus's subtleties himself, "εὐρὼν παρὰ τῷ αγιωτάτῳ Eion-ναίῳ τῷ ἀρχαίῳ τὴν κατ΄ αὐτοῦ πραγματείαν γεγενημένην" ("having found the treatise made against him by the most holy Irenaeus of old"; HOLL 29966–29970, OCR as printed), and that he will give the passage of "the servant of God, Irenaeus I mean" in full ("τὴν παραϑεσιν 040-σχερῶς ποιήσομαι", read ὁλοσχερῶς; HOLL 29979–29981). He heads it "From the works of the holy Irenaeus" (HOLL 29984), and ends "Here end the words of Irenaeus against the Valentinians" (HOLL 32949). He calls him "the elder Irenaeus … like a noble athlete … who wrestled down and overcame their whole foolish system" (HOLL 32952–32957; ἀϑλητὴς 32954). This is how most of Book I survives in Greek (ANF-ed 29789–29793; EUS-ed 24533–24536). Documented.

I3. **The witness to the Valentinians for eighteen centuries.** The tree's tradition entry: "For eighteen centuries, these groups were known almost entirely through such opponents. Then, in 1945, farmers digging near Nag Hammadi … found a sealed jar … Among them was the Gospel of Truth" (CEN `valentinian-and-other-gnostic-christianities`). Census `legacy`: "His account of the Valentinians and of Marcion remains a main witness to both, but it is the witness of an opponent" (CEN). The Nag Hammadi books are **not held**; the claim rests on the tree's tradition entry. Widely Accepted.

I4. **Eusebius's authority for the second century.** "He is quoted very frequently by Eusebius as an authority for events which happened during the second century" (EUS-ed 24519–24522). Eusebius copies his list of Rome's bishops (EUS 27959–27990) and his account of the four Gospels (EUS 28168–28198). Documented.

I5. **The list of Rome's bishops, argued over.** Census `legacy`: "His list of Rome's bishops became a starting point for later arguments about who holds the apostles' teaching. Historians still weigh it. Peter Lampe argues that Rome had no single bishop in charge until about 189 to 199" (CEN). Lampe is **not held**. Coxe's polemic on the Rome clause (ANF-ed 39803–39813; ANF-ed 29444–29454) shows how the passage was used in later quarrels: editor. The tree's own tradition entry says that in these generations "Some communities were guided by a single bishop alongside elders and deacons, others by a circle of elders alone" (CEN `post-apostolic-house-church`). Contested; credit "historians", never settle it.

I6. **Used as a witness by later bishops.** Theodoret, fifth century, lists him among his witnesses: "Irenæus, who enjoyed the teaching of Polycarp, and became a light of the western Gauls" (NPNF3 20259–20260). Documented.

I7. **The care he asked for his books.** His adjuration at the end of *On the Ogdoad*: "I adjure thee who mayest copy this book … to compare what thou shalt write, and correct it carefully by this manuscript, and also to write this adjuration, and place it in the copy" (EUS 30768–30772). Eusebius and Jerome both copied it (EUS 30752–30777; NPNF3 40185–40191). Documented. Minor.

## 5. Links to other people

- **Polycarp of Smyrna**: heard him teach as a boy (S1, T7); told of his visit to Rome under Anicetus (ANF 39871–39875); wrote his story for later readers. Sibling.
- **Valentinus**: wrote against his followers (E6, T1–T6; ANF 39964–39970). Sibling.
- **Marcion of Pontus**: named him among those who make the Gospels fewer (T6); told his meeting with Polycarp (ANF 39882–39884, barred). Live story.
- **Justin Martyr**: quoted his book against Marcion (EUS 24336–24343, Eusebius 4.18). Live story; not told here.
- **Tatian**: told his break with the church (ANF 33642). Batch 6; not told here.
- **Ignatius of Antioch**: quoted his wheat line without his name (sibling outline, I2). Not told here.
- **Hermas**: quoted his Shepherd as Scripture (hermas outline). Not told here.
- **Polycrates of Ephesus / Victor of Rome**: wrote to Victor in the Easter quarrel (E9). One clause.
- **Hippolytus of Rome**: called him "the blessed presbyter" (I1); his pupil, by Photius. Batch 6.
- **Tertullian**: praised and used him (I1). Live as `tertullian-s-voice`.
- **Eusebius of Caesarea**: the main source for his life; quoted him (I4).
- **Epiphanius of Salamis**: copied Book I word for word (I2).
- **Theodoret of Cyrrhus**: cited him as a witness (I6).
- **Jerome**: wrote his life in brief (E4, E5, E9).
- **Gregory of Tours**: told his martyrdom, as legend (E10).

## 6. Where sources disagree or come late

D1. **Birth and age.** No held line of his gives a year or a place. Editors range from 120–130 to 140–145 (S1). Leave it out; say "as a boy".

D2. **Smyrna or "lower Asia".** He says he saw Polycarp "in lower Asia" (S1). That he grew up at Smyrna is inferred from Polycarp's see. Say "in the province of Asia, where Polycarp was bishop of Smyrna".

D3. **How he came to Gaul.** Gregory (legend) and Coxe (editor) only (E2). Leave out.

D4. **Was he in Lyon in 177, and did he write the martyrs' letter?** Eusebius has him sent to Rome "while they were still in prison" (E4). Whether he saw the deaths is not said. Authorship: Valesius's guess, Coxe "probably", McGiffert "highly improbable" (EUS-ed 26860–26868; ANF-ed 29436). Contested; do not say he wrote it or saw the deaths.

D5. **The Montanist side of the embassy.** Contested (E4). Leave out.

D6. **Florinus's date.** Contested (E8). Tell the letter without a year.

D7. **Rome's single bishop, and the "pre-eminent authority" clause.** Contested (T5, I5). The list is told as his list; its use in later arguments is told as "historians still argue over it".

D8. **His death.** Contested; the early record is silent; martyrdom first in Jerome and Gregory (E10).

D9. **Pothinus's age.** "More than ninety" (letter), "at ninety" (Eusebius), "nearly ninety" (Jerome) (E3, E5). Use the letter.

## 7. Wanted but not held

- The Latin of *Against Heresies* (Massuet, Stieren or Harvey), and the Greek fragments of Books II–V outside Epiphanius.
- The Greek of Eusebius, *Church History* I–V (for the letters to Florinus and Victor, and the martyrs' letter).
- *Demonstration of the Apostolic Preaching*, found in Armenian in 1904. Eusebius names it (EUS 31732–31733); McGiffert in 1890 thought it lost (EUS-ed 31737). Photius's editor lists an English version (PHOT 13721) but the text itself is not held.
- Jerome's *Commentary on Isaiah* (the first call him a martyr; EUS-ed 24501–24503).
- Peter Lampe on the Roman bishops (CEN `legacy`).
- The Nag Hammadi *Gospel of Truth* (CEN tradition entry).
