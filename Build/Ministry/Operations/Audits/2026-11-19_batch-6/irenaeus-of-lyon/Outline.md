# Outline: Irenaeus of Lyon

Sheet items (S, E, T, I, D) are in `Source_Sheet.md`, which gives the file and line for each. ANF is the Ante-Nicene Fathers English of *Against Heresies*, made from the old Latin; HOLL is the Greek of Book I as Epiphanius copied it (Holl's edition); EUS is Eusebius in McGiffert's English. The Latin of *Against Heresies* is not held.

- **The essence:** Irenaeus heard the old bishop Polycarp as a boy in the Roman province of Asia, and became bishop of Lyon in Gaul after the martyrs of 177 (S1, E4, E5; Documented, his own words and Eusebius). There he wrote five books *Against Heresies*, about 180, the fullest answer we still have to the followers of Valentinus, who claimed a secret, higher teaching (E7, S6; the book Documented; its date under bishop Eleutherius Documented, "about 180" Widely Accepted; "fullest answer we still have", census, Widely Accepted). Against them he set three things: the faith every church received at baptism, the churches the apostles founded with their bishops in an unbroken line, and four Gospels, no more and no fewer (T2–T6; Documented as his teaching).
- **Also told:**
  - **One faith, one house.** The church, scattered across the world, keeps one faith received from the apostles: one God who made heaven and earth, one Christ Jesus who became flesh, suffered, rose and will come again, and the Holy Spirit who spoke through the prophets. The churches in Germany, Spain, among the Celts, in the East, in Egypt and Libya believe and hand on the same (T3, T4; Documented). A believer holds it as the rule of truth received at baptism, and can tell the true use of Scripture from the false (T2; Documented).
  - **The apostles' churches.** If the apostles had secret teachings, they would have given them to the men they left in charge of the churches. Anyone can count the bishops from the apostles to his own day. He counts Rome's, from Linus to Eleutherius, "the twelfth place from the apostles", then names Polycarp at Smyrna and the church at Ephesus (T5; Documented as his argument). Whether Rome then had a single bishop, historians still argue (I5; Contested).
  - **Four Gospels.** Matthew, Mark, Luke and John, and these four only: as there are four winds, he says, the church has four pillars. The Valentinians claim more, and call a recent book of theirs the Gospel of Truth (T6; Documented as his argument).
  - **What came of him.** Writers against heresy drew on his book for generations: Hippolytus called him "the blessed elder", Tertullian "a very exact inquirer", and Epiphanius copied his first book in full, which is how its Greek survives (I1, I2; Documented). For eighteen centuries the Valentinians were known almost only through opponents such as him (I3; the tree's tradition entry, Widely Accepted). Eusebius drew on him for the second century, and his list of Rome's bishops became a starting point for later arguments over who holds the apostles' teaching (I4, I5; Documented for Eusebius; the later arguments, census, Contested in substance).
- **Length:** about 3 minutes 20 seconds (about 500 words). Why: the life is thin, resting mostly on Eusebius, so no time goes on a childhood, a journey or a death the record does not hold. But he is a major figure, and the job carries three answers, each needing a plain sentence and his own words, plus the martyrs of 177 that made him bishop. A third goes to what came of him, where the later writers and the long life of his witness carry the connection. Cut first, if needed: beat 7's Easter clause, What came 4's Theodoret, and the barbarians "without paper or ink" in beat 5.
- **Setting:**
  - Time: the second half of the second century. Lyon in 177 (S4); the book about 180 (E7); his last trace in the Easter quarrel near the century's end (E9, E10). Plain words: "in the late second century", and "in the year 177" for the persecution.
  - Place: as a boy, the Roman province of Asia, on the west coast of today's Turkey, where Polycarp was bishop of Smyrna (S1, D2). Then Lyon, in Roman Gaul, on the Rhone, today in France (S2; CEN `modernCountries`). Plain words: "Lyon, in Roman Gaul, on the river Rhone".
  - Tradition: the churches of the generations after the apostles; the tree's name is "The Scattered Households" (S5). Do not say "post-apostolic".
  - Language: he wrote in Greek, living, he says, among the Celts and busy mostly with a foreign tongue (S3).

**Words to explain** (the first time each is heard; plain word or short gloss, worked into the sentence)

- **Valentinians / followers of Valentinus**: "the followers of Valentinus, a teacher at Rome a generation earlier". Their teaching in one clause: "who taught that the world's maker was a lesser power below a perfect, hidden God, and that their own secret teaching went deeper than the apostles'" (S6). Never "Gnostics" in the narrator's voice for them; Irenaeus uses the word for other groups.
- **heresy / heretics**: "teachings he held false", or credit it to him: "those he called heretics". The title may stand as "*Against Heresies*", with the plain sense once: "a book against teachings he held false".
- **elder** (Eusebius's "presbyter"): "one of the elders who led the church with its bishop". Never "presbyter", and not "priest".
- **rule of faith / rule of truth**: "the short summary of the faith a Christian received at baptism", which he calls a rule, a measure to test by (T2; the word is κανών, a measuring rule; that gloss is the translators', ANF-ed 31353–31356). Work it into the sentence; do not use the phrase bare.
- **succession / unbroken line of bishops**: "the bishops of each church, one after another, back to the apostles" (T5).
- **Asia**: "the Roman province of Asia, on the west coast of today's Turkey". The modern sense misleads.
- **Gaul / the Celts**: "Roman Gaul, today's France"; "the Celts, the people of Gaul".
- **martyrs** / the letter's "witnesses": "those who died for their faith" the first time (EUS-ed 26804–26810 notes the letter's word means "witnesses").
- **Gospel of Truth**: "a recent book of theirs that they called the Gospel of Truth" (T6).
- **Quote 1's "portrait of small stones"**: the narrator says plainly it is a mosaic, a picture made of small coloured stones, and gives the point in the next sentence: that, he said, is what they did with Scripture (T1 context).
- **Easter quarrel**, if used: "a quarrel over the date of Easter" (E9).

- **Shape:** Teacher or thinker. The question he answered: when teachers claim a secret, higher teaching, how can a church tell the apostles' faith from what is new? What he taught: the faith received at baptism, the apostles' churches and their bishops, the four Gospels. Who learned it: Hippolytus, Tertullian, Epiphanius, Eusebius. The trap is a list of books; show the idea through his mosaic (Quote 1), the one house (Quote 2) and his memory of Polycarp (Quote 3). The other books are named only in passing, if at all (Leave out).

- **Openings:**
  1. **Chosen.** His own image (Quote 1; T1). In about three sentences: a skilled artist makes a portrait of a king from small stones; someone breaks it up and makes a dog or a fox, and swears it is the king. Then the turn: that, wrote a bishop in Roman Gaul about the year 180, is what the followers of Valentinus did with Scripture. Then the name and the essence.
  2. Not chosen: the bodies of the martyrs of Lyon swept into the Rhone (E3). Strong, but it belongs in time order at beat 2, and opening on it would make the martyrs swallow a teacher's story.

**Story beats** (the opening, the essence and beat 1 are the first minute, about 160 words; beats 2 to 7 deepen it)

1. **A boy in Asia.** As a boy, in the Roman province of Asia, he heard Polycarp, the bishop of Smyrna (S1; Documented, his own words). One clause; the Polycarp story tells the rest. How and when he came to Gaul, no early source says (E2; leave the gap unspoken or say it plainly in a clause).
2. **Lyon, 177.** In 177 a persecution struck the Christians of Lyon and Vienne, on the Rhone (S2, S4). Their bishop, Pothinus, more than ninety years old and barely able to breathe, was beaten and died in prison two days later (E3). Some were beheaded, some thrown to wild beasts; their bodies were burned and the ashes swept into the Rhone, so that, the persecutors said, they could have no hope of rising again (E3; Documented, the survivors' letter). While still in prison, the Christians sent letters to Eleutherius, bishop of Rome, about a quarrel that troubled the churches, and they chose Irenaeus, one of their elders, to carry them. They asked Eleutherius to honour him as one zealous for Christ's covenant (E4; Documented). Do not say he saw the deaths, wrote the letter, or which side of the quarrel it took (D4, D5).
3. **Bishop, and the book.** He became bishop of Lyon after Pothinus (E5; Documented, Eusebius). He lived, he says, among the Celts, and spent most of his days in a foreign tongue; he claims no skill as a writer (S3, T8). Teachers of that school were close at hand: the followers of one of them, Marcus, had led many women astray "in our own district of the Rhone", he says (E6; Marcus's followers, not Valentinus's in general). They taught a perfect, hidden God beyond the world and a lesser maker below him, and claimed that the truth came down not in writing but by word of mouth, to them, who were wiser than the apostles (S6; his account, credit him). He read their books and talked with some of them, and at a friend's request he wrote five books to expose and answer them (E6, E7; Documented). He wrote the third while Eleutherius was bishop of Rome (E7). The sense of Quote 1 is the problem he names: they took Scripture's words out of their order to build a different picture (T1).
4. **One faith, one house.** His first answer was the faith the church had received from the apostles and their followers: one God, maker of heaven and earth; one Christ Jesus, the Son of God, who became flesh, suffered, rose and will come again; and the Holy Spirit who spoke through the prophets (T3). A Christian received it at baptism, he says, as a rule to measure by (T2). Then Quote 2. Then, in paraphrase: the churches in Germany, in Spain, among the Celts, in the East, in Egypt and in Libya believe and hand on no other; the most gifted speaker adds nothing to it and the weakest takes nothing away (T4; Documented).
5. **The apostles' churches.** His second answer: anyone can see the apostles' teaching in the churches they founded, and count their bishops, one after another, to his own day. If the apostles had secret teachings, he argued, they would have given them above all to the men they left in charge (T5; Documented). Listing every church would take too long, so he gave Rome's, from Linus to Eleutherius, "the twelfth place from the apostles", and then named Polycarp at Smyrna and the church at Ephesus (T5). Even peoples who believe "without paper or ink", he says, keep that old faith and would stop their ears at the new teachers (T5; optional, cut first). And the Valentinians had not existed before Valentinus (T5).
6. **Four Gospels.** His third answer: four Gospels, written by Matthew, Mark, Luke and John (T6). There cannot be more or fewer, he says: as there are four winds and the church is spread through the whole world, it has four pillars (T6; paraphrase only; do not quote). The Valentinians claimed more, and called a recent book of theirs the Gospel of Truth; Marcion, he says, made them fewer (T6; Marcion one clause). Even his opponents, he notes, built on these Gospels: the Valentinians on John's (T6).
7. **Florinus, and the end of the record.** Florinus, a man Irenaeus had seen as a boy at Polycarp's side in Asia, had become an elder at Rome. He fell from that office, and Eusebius says he was being drawn to Valentinus's teaching (E8; S1 for Polycarp's side, his own words; "fellow pupil" is McGiffert's inference, EUS-ed 30659–30660, so do not say it). Irenaeus wrote to him that the elders before them, who had known the apostles, never handed him such teachings (E8; Documented). Then Quote 3. Polycarp, he wrote, would have stopped his ears and fled the place where he heard such words (E8). Tell it without a year (D6). Then one clause: in the quarrel over the date of Easter he wrote to Victor of Rome not to cut off whole churches for an old custom; Eusebius says he was well named, a peacemaker (E9; that the name comes from the Greek word for peace is McGiffert's note, a plain fact of the word, and may be said). After that the record falls silent (E10).

**What came of him** (about a third of the story; heaviest first)

1. **The storehouse.** Writers against false teaching drew on his book for generations (I1; McGiffert's "storehouse", credit as an editor's view or say "later writers drew on it"). Hippolytus, at Rome in the next generation, called him "the blessed elder Irenaeus" (I1; Documented). Tertullian, in Carthage, named him "a very exact inquirer into all doctrines" (I1; Documented). A much later writer says Hippolytus was his pupil (I1; Photius, ninth century, "it is said").
2. **Epiphanius.** About two hundred years later, Epiphanius, writing against the same teachers, chose not to describe them in his own words and copied Irenaeus's first book in full; that copy is how most of its Greek survives, while the whole work lives only in an old Latin translation (I2; Documented). He called Irenaeus a noble athlete who wrestled their teaching to the ground (I2; Epiphanius's words, paraphrase).
3. **The Valentinians' witness.** For eighteen centuries the Valentinians were known almost only through their opponents, above all him. Then, in 1945, their own books were found in Egypt, among them a Gospel of Truth (I3; the tree's tradition entry; the finds are not held). Say plainly that his account is an opponent's (CEN `legacy`).
4. **Eusebius and the Rome list.** Eusebius, in the fourth century, took him as a chief witness for the second century and copied his list of Rome's bishops and his account of the four Gospels (I4; Documented). That list later became a starting point for arguments over who holds the apostles' teaching; historians still argue over whether Rome then had a single bishop at its head (I5; census; credit "historians", never settle it). Theodoret, in the fifth century, called him "a light of the western Gauls" (I6; optional).

**Connection to carry into the story:** what passed from Irenaeus was a way of telling the apostles' faith from a new teaching, and the fullest record of the teaching he opposed. The followers of Valentinus said the truth had come down secretly, by word of mouth, to the wise, beyond the apostles and beyond the written Gospels (S6). Irenaeus answered that the apostles' teaching was public: it was the faith every Christian received at baptism, the same in every church from Germany to Libya; it was kept in the churches the apostles founded, whose bishops could be counted back to them; and it was written in four Gospels, no more and no fewer (T2–T6). The listener must understand three things. First, why that answer fitted that claim: a secret tradition is met with an open one that anyone can check, church by church. Second, that for Irenaeus the chain was not abstract: he had heard Polycarp, who had known John, and he used that memory against a man he had known at Polycarp's side, who was being drawn to Valentinus (T7, E8). Third, that later writers took up both his answer and his record: Hippolytus and Tertullian drew on him, Epiphanius copied his first book whole, Eusebius made him a chief witness, and for eighteen centuries the Valentinians were known almost only through him and writers like him; his list of Rome's bishops was later used in arguments he never saw (I1–I5). Tell it through these acts: the writing, the letter to Florinus, the copying and quoting. Not as a "this matters because" sentence.

**Ending:** close on the person. Either (a) the opening image seen again, in paraphrase: a Christian who holds the rule received at baptism, he wrote, will know the stones but will not take the fox for the king; he puts each one back in its place, and the false picture falls apart (T2, ANF 31360–31364; HOLL 32638–32641); or (b) the record falling silent: after his letters on Easter, he is lost to view; no early writer says how or when he died, and only a writer four centuries later made him a martyr (E10; in Gregory's voice, credited). Recommended: (a), if What came is placed before it, so the last words are his.

**Who tells it**

- **Irenaeus himself**, in *Against Heresies* and his letters. The teaching, the Valentinians' claims, the Celts, and the memory of Polycarp are his. Credit "he says" or "he argued" wherever he describes his opponents: their teaching and their motives are his hostile account (S6, E6).
- **The Christians of Lyon and Vienne**, in their own letter of 177, kept by Eusebius (E3, E4). Credit the persecution scene to them: "the survivors wrote".
- **Eusebius**, about a century and a half later. Most of his life rests on Eusebius; say so once, inside a sentence about Eusebius, in beat 3 or What came 4 ("Most of what we know of his life comes from Eusebius", CEN `sourcing`). Credit him beside the succession to Pothinus (E5) and the "well named" line (E9).
- **Hippolytus, Tertullian, Epiphanius, Theodoret**: later readers, credited by name where they praise him (I1, I2, I6).

**Do not say**

- Any word in "Words to explain" used without its plain sense the first time it is heard.
- Do not quote "Do you know me?" / "the firstborn of Satan", or "Matthew published his Gospel among the Hebrews", or "It is not possible that the Gospels can be either more or fewer". The first is in the Marcion story; the four-Gospel lines are told in paraphrase (Sibling).
- Do not quote any line of the approved Polycarp story, including "Even so, they shared communion".
- Not "born in Smyrna" or "grew up in Smyrna". He saw Polycarp "in lower Asia" (S1, D2). Say "in the Roman province of Asia, where Polycarp was bishop of Smyrna".
- Not "born in 130" or any birth year (D1).
- Not "Polycarp sent him to Gaul" or "he went to Gaul as a missionary". Only a sixth-century writer and a modern editor say so (E2, D3).
- Not "he survived the persecution", "he watched the martyrs die" or "he wrote the letter of the martyrs". The letter says only that they sent him to Rome while still in prison (E4, D4).
- Not "the martyrs sent him to Rome to plead for the Montanists" or "against the Montanists". Say "about a quarrel that troubled the churches" (E4, D5).
- Not "forty-eight martyrs" or any number. The letter gives none (E3).
- Not "Pothinus, aged ninety". Say "more than ninety", the letter's words (D9).
- Not "he became bishop at once" or "in 178". Say "after Pothinus" (E5).
- Not "he preached in the Celtic language" or "learned Gaulish". He says only that he was busy mostly with a foreign tongue (S3).
- Not "written in 180" as fact. Say "about 180" or "while Eleutherius was bishop of Rome" (E7).
- Not "the Valentinians believed …" in the narrator's voice. Say "they taught, he says" (S6). Do not soften his hostility either: he calls their system "an abyss of madness and of blasphemy against Christ" (ANF 29865) and likens them to wolves in sheep's clothing (ANF 29844–29849); keep that force in paraphrase where it is told.
- Not "Gnostics" for the Valentinians in the narrator's voice (Words to explain).
- Not "the Valentinians were spreading along the Rhone". He says this of the followers of Marcus, one teacher of that school (E6).
- Not "the first to name four Gospels" or "he fixed the New Testament". He argued for four; no held source says he was first or that he settled a list (T6).
- Not "Rome had authority over all the churches", and do not quote or paraphrase the clause on Rome's "pre-eminent authority". Its meaning is disputed (T5, D7).
- Not "the first list of popes" or "the bishops of Rome", as if a papacy. Say "Rome's bishops" or "the bishops of Rome, one after another".
- Not "Rome had one bishop from the start" or "Rome had no bishop then" as fact. Say "historians still argue over it" (I5).
- Not "the apostolic succession" as a doctrine he founded. Say what he did: he counted the bishops back to the apostles (T5).
- Not "Florinus became a Valentinian" as settled. Eusebius says he "was being drawn away" (E8). Not "his fellow student" or "his old friend": he says only that he saw Florinus with Polycarp (S1).
- Not "Irenaeus wrote to Florinus in 190", or any year for the letter (D6).
- Not "Victor backed down" or any outcome of the Easter letter (E9).
- Not "he was martyred" or "he died in 202". The early record is silent (E10, D8).
- Not "Hippolytus was his pupil" as fact. Say "a much later writer says" (I1).
- Not "his book survives only in Latin", unqualified. Say it survives whole only in an old Latin translation, with much of the first book in Greek through Epiphanius (I2).
- Not "the Nag Hammadi books proved him wrong" or "right". Say only that they let the Valentinians speak for themselves for the first time (I3).
- Not "the father of theology" or any title of praise in the narrator's voice.

**Leave out**

- His other works by name (*On the Ogdoad*, *On Schism*, the *Demonstration*, the lost works in 5.26): a list of books is the shape's trap.
- His adjuration to copyists (I7): charming, but a side story.
- John and Cerinthus at the bath-house, and Polycarp and Marcion (ANF 39878–39884): Polycarp's and Marcion's stories.
- The Montanist side of the embassy to Rome (D5).
- Blandina, Attalus and the other martyrs of Lyon by name: the Lyon martyrs are told in two sentences, from the letter, as the setting of his becoming bishop.
- The "pre-eminent authority" clause and the later quarrels over it (T5, D7): for the card.
- Coxe's and McGiffert's verdicts and reconstructions (Coxe on his embassy and a Valentinian friend at Rome; McGiffert on Matthew): editors.
- Gregory of Tours's claims that he made all Lyon Christian and that streets ran with blood (E10): legend, beyond ending (b)'s one clause.
- Peter Lampe by name (I5): for the card.
- Recapitulation, Adam and Christ, Eve and Mary, "the glory of God is a living man": his other great themes, outside this story's job.
