# Outline check: Ignatius of Antioch

**The essence.** Early in the second century Ignatius, bishop of Antioch in Syria, was taken under a guard of soldiers to Rome to be killed by wild animals. On the road, at Smyrna and Troas, he wrote seven letters to the churches that came out to meet him: keep together under your one bishop with the elders and deacons, hold to one Eucharist, and hold that Christ was truly born, truly suffered and truly died, against those who said he only seemed to; and, to Rome, do not save me from the beasts. He is on the tree because those letters, gathered by Polycarp and sent on, are among the earliest Christian writings after the apostles, the earliest to set out the church under one bishop, and were read for centuries as a martyr's own voice.

**Also told.**
- The churches of Asia sent bishops, elders and deacons to meet him at Smyrna; he met Polycarp there (Documented, OWN).
- What the letters teach: the bishop presides with elders and deacons; nothing done without the bishop; one Eucharist, one altar (Documented, OWN).
- Christ's real suffering, tied to his own coming death: if Christ only seemed to suffer, "why am I in bonds?" (Documented, OWN).
- The letter to Rome: do not hinder me; "I am the wheat of God" (Documented, OWN).
- At Troas, news that the church at Antioch had peace; he asked the churches to send envoys; then sailed on (Documented, OWN).
- His death at Rome is known only by report; Polycarp soon spoke of him among the blessed dead and asked for news (Widely Accepted; manner and date not known).
- What came of him: Polycarp collected and sent on the letters; Irenaeus quoted the wheat line unnamed; Eusebius made him a guardian of the apostles' tradition; Athanasius and Theodoret cited him on Christ's flesh; Antioch kept his feast (Chrysostom); the letters were long argued over, and seven shorter Greek letters came to be widely accepted (Widely Accepted, with dissent).
