# Outline: Ignatius of Antioch

Sheet items (S, E, I, D) are in `Source_Sheet.md`, which gives the file and line for each. ANF is the Ante-Nicene Fathers English of his letters (shorter form unless marked); EUS is Eusebius in McGiffert's English. The Greek of his letters is not held.

- **The essence:** Ignatius, bishop of the church at Antioch in Syria, was taken under armed guard across the Roman world to Rome, condemned to die by wild beasts in the arena (E1, E2, S6; Documented, his own words). On the road he wrote seven letters to the churches that came out to meet him, and they are almost all we know of him (E3, D2; the letters Documented; that these seven are his, Widely Accepted, long disputed). Polycarp, bishop of Smyrna, gathered them and sent them on, and later writers quoted them (I1, I2–I4; Documented).
- **Also told:**
  - **Keep to your bishop.** Each church, he wrote, should act as one around its bishop, with its elders and deacons: one bishop, one altar, one shared bread and cup. Do nothing without the bishop (E5; Documented as his teaching). He says that at Philadelphia he cried this aloud, and that it came from God's Spirit, not from any man's report of their quarrels (E5; Documented as his claim).
  - **Christ truly suffered.** Some said Christ only seemed to suffer. Ignatius answered that he was truly born, truly crucified under Pontius Pilate and truly raised, and that if it were only a show, his own chains and his coming death would mean nothing (E6; Documented). He says those teachers kept away from the shared bread and cup and cared nothing for widows, orphans or the hungry (E6; Documented as his charge against them).
  - **Do not save me from the beasts.** From Smyrna he wrote ahead to the church at Rome, begging them not to work for his release: let him be food for the beasts, God's wheat ground into bread (E7; Documented). Whether he died as he expected, no early witness saw: Eusebius, two centuries later, writes that "report says" he was given to the beasts at Rome (E9; Widely Accepted that he died a martyr at Rome; the manner and year not known).
  - **What came of him.** Polycarp kept and sent on his letters (I1). Within a lifetime Irenaeus quoted his wheat line, without naming him, as the words of a man condemned to the beasts (I2). Eusebius counted him a guard of the apostles' teaching (I3), and later bishops used his words on Christ's real flesh in their own quarrels (I4). Antioch kept his feast (I5). And because his letters speak so plainly of bishops, scholars argued for centuries over which were really his (I7, D2; the argument Documented; the verdict for seven Widely Accepted).
- **Length:** about 3 minutes (about 450 words). Why: the record holds only his last journey, so no time goes on an earlier life. But that journey is rich: the guard, the churches on the road, three teachings that each need a plain sentence and a scene, and the letter to Rome. A third goes to what came of him, where the letters' survival and the long quarrel over them carry the connection. Cut first, if needed: beat 6's request to send a messenger to Antioch, and What came 4 and 5.
- **Setting:**
  - Time: early in the second century (S3, D3). No letter gives a year. A later chronicle puts his death under the emperor Trajan, read as 107; some scholars say later. Plain words: "early in the second century".
  - Place: Antioch in Syria (S1). Then the road west: Smyrna and Troas, cities of the Roman province of Asia, on the west coast of today's Turkey (S5). Then Rome. Plain words: "from Antioch in Syria to Rome".
  - Tradition: the churches of the generation after the apostles; the tree's name for them is "The Scattered Households" (S4). Do not say "post-apostolic".

**Words to explain** (the first time each is heard; plain word or short gloss, worked into the sentence)

- **elders** (his "presbytery", "presbyters"): "the council of elders who led each church with its bishop". Never "presbyters", and not "priests".
- **Eucharist**: "the shared bread and cup of the Lord's Supper". He calls it the flesh of Christ (E6); give that only as his words.
- **Asia**: "the Roman province of Asia, on the west coast of today's Turkey". The modern sense of Asia misleads.
- **condemned to the beasts / fight with beasts**: "sentenced to be killed by wild animals in the arena" (S6). In Quote 1 the beasts are his guards: the narrator says so in the next sentence (E2: "I mean a band of soldiers").
- **martyr**: "one who died as a witness for Christ", the first time it is used, if used.
- **seemed to suffer**: say "some taught that Christ only seemed to have a body and to suffer" (E6). Never "Docetists" or "Gnostics": he names no group.
- **chains / "in bonds"**: say "in chains" or "a prisoner". Never "bonds".
- **Troas**: "a port on the coast of Asia" (E8: he sails from there).
- **Theophorus**, if used: "his other name, which means God-bearer" (S7).
- **feast**: "the yearly day on which a church remembered him".
- **recension / forgery**: say "a longer and a shorter form", and "letters written later in his name".
- **wheat … pure bread** (Quote 3): the narrator gives the plain sense alongside: as grain is ground to make bread, his body torn by the beasts would make him an offering to God (E7, "a sacrifice [to God]", ANF 7046–7047).

- **Shape:** Martyr. The trap is the death swallowing the story. Here the death is not even seen: what fills the record is the road and the letters. So the three teachings and what came of them get as much room as the beasts, and the end is told as he foresaw it and as report gave it, not as a scene.
- **Sibling:** `polycarp-of-smyrna` (approved story; job: the old bishop's martyrdom at Smyrna, his Easter visit to Rome, his letter to Philippi). That story does not mention Ignatius. Here Polycarp gets one sentence where they meet at Smyrna and one clause where he gathers and sends the letters. None of the Polycarp story's quotes is used. `irenaeus-of-lyon` is researched after this; here Irenaeus is one clause (What came 2). Tradition entry `post-apostolic-house-church` already quotes "ten leopards" and "show themselves all the worse": not quoted here (Do not say).
- **Openings:**
  1. **Chosen.** His own line from the letter to Rome (Quote 1; E2). In about three sentences: a prisoner on the road writes that from Syria all the way to Rome he fights with beasts, by land and sea, by night and day. Then the turn: the beasts on the road were his guards, a squad of ten soldiers, and the real beasts waited at the end, in the arena at Rome. Then the name and the essence.
  2. Not chosen: the bishops coming to meet him at Smyrna (E3). True and strong, but it belongs in time order at beat 2; opening on it would tell it twice.

**Story beats** (the opening, the essence and beat 1 are the first minute, about 170 words; beats 2 to 6 deepen it)

1. **Bishop, and prisoner.** He was bishop of the church at Antioch in Syria; he calls himself "the bishop of Syria" (S1). A historian two centuries later counted him the second bishop there (S2). Of his life before his arrest nothing is known (E1). He does not say why or by whom he was condemned, only that he was led in chains from Syria to Rome, "from the east unto the west" (E1; Documented). Others from Syria had gone ahead of him to Rome (E7, Rom. 10).
2. **The churches on the road.** Churches came out to meet him, he says, city by city, even those not on his road (E3). At Smyrna, where Polycarp was bishop, he met him face to face (E4). Bishops came there from three other cities, with their elders and deacons: from Ephesus its bishop, Onesimus, and from Magnesia and Tralles theirs (E3; Documented). Ephesus sent a deacon on with him (E3).
3. **The letters from Smyrna: keep to your bishop, and Christ truly suffered.** He wrote back to those three churches (E4). He told them to act as one around the bishop, with the elders and deacons: the bishop presiding in God's place, the elders like the council of the apostles; one prayer, one bread and cup, one altar (E5). He warned them against teachers who said Christ only seemed to suffer, and wrote that Christ was truly born, truly crucified under Pontius Pilate and truly raised (E6). Then Quote 2: if it was only a show, why is he in chains? (Quote 2; E6; Documented.)
4. **The letter to Rome.** Also from Smyrna, on 23 August, he wrote ahead to the church at Rome (E7). He was afraid of their love, he wrote: if they spared him, it would be hard for him to reach God (E7, Rom. 1–2). He begged them not to be kind to him at the wrong time, and to let him be food for the beasts (E7, Rom. 4). Then Quote 3, with its plain sense (Words to explain). He adds that he does not command them, as Peter and Paul did: they were apostles, and he a condemned man (E7; Documented).
5. **Troas.** Moved on to Troas, he wrote three more letters: to Philadelphia, to the church at Smyrna and to Polycarp (S5, E8). To Smyrna he wrote against those who denied Christ's real body, and said that they kept away from the shared bread and cup and cared nothing for the widow, the orphan or the hungry (E6). To Philadelphia, which he had passed through, he recalled how he had cried out among them, in a loud voice, to heed the bishop, the elders and the deacons; and he said it was the Spirit, not any man's report of their quarrels, that told him to say it: do nothing without the bishop (E5; Documented as his claim).
6. **Peace at Antioch, and the sudden sailing.** Word reached him that the church at Antioch was now at peace (E8). He asked the churches, and Polycarp, to choose a messenger to go to Syria and share their joy (E8). He could not write to every church, he told Polycarp, because he had to sail suddenly from Troas (E8). That is the last we hear in his own words (E8; Documented).

**What came of him** (about a third of the story; heaviest first. The death, item 1, comes first because the record gives it as report)

1. **His end, and Polycarp's letters.** No early witness saw his death. Polycarp, writing to the church at Philippi, named "the blessed Ignatius" among those who had suffered with the Lord, yet asked for any surer news of him (E9, D1). He sent Philippi the letters Ignatius had written to Smyrna, with the others he held, as they had asked (I1; Documented). That is how the letters were carried on. Two centuries later Eusebius wrote that "report says" he was given to the wild beasts at Rome (E9). Tell the death only as report.
2. **Irenaeus.** Within a lifetime, Irenaeus quoted the wheat line, without naming him, as the words of a man of the church condemned to the beasts (I2; Documented). One clause; the writer may echo Quote 3's image but does not quote it twice.
3. **Eusebius and later bishops.** Eusebius wrote that on the road he warned the churches against new teachings and urged them to hold to the apostles' teaching, and set it down in writing (I3; Eusebius's view, credit it). Bishops in the fourth and fifth centuries, such as Athanasius, quoted his words on Christ's real flesh in their own arguments over who Christ was (I4; Documented).
4. **Antioch.** By the late fourth century Antioch kept his feast. John Chrysostom preached on it there, and said the city had sent out a bishop and received back a martyr (I5; Documented for the feast; that his remains came home is a tradition traced no earlier than then). The Greek prayer book of the Eastern Church kept him on 20 December in its 1851 edition (I6). No held source says who keeps the day now: do not say "today".
5. **The quarrel over the letters.** Fifteen letters came down under his name. Eight were later written in his name, and the seven had a longer, padded form and a shorter one (I7). Because they speak so plainly of bishops, scholars fought over them for three centuries; by 1890 most accepted the seven shorter letters as his, though some still did not (I7, D2; credit "scholars", not the narrator).

**Quotes** (3)

Only Quotes 1–3 are quoted aloud. "The bishop of Syria", "from the east unto the west", "the blessed Ignatius" and "report says" may stand as short phrases inside the narrator's sentences. "Do nothing without the bishop" is told as his words in indirect speech (beat 5), not as a set-piece quote. The Greek of the letters is not held, so Quotes 1 and 2 are rendered from the ANF English of the shorter form, checked against the longer form and against Eusebius's quotation; Quote 3 is rendered from Greek.

1. ANF (Rom. 5, shorter, ANF 7083–7086): "From Syria even unto Rome I fight with beasts, both by land and sea, both by night and day," (Eusebius, EUS 19994–19995: "From Syria even unto Rome I fight with wild beasts, by land and by sea, by night and by day"). Rendering: "From Syria all the way to Rome I fight with beasts, by land and by sea, by night and by day." The quote stops before "being bound to ten leopards" (Do not say). Job: the opening. A prisoner's own line about his road; the next sentence explains that the beasts on the road were his guards.
2. ANF (Trall. 10, shorter, ANF 6671–6676): "But if, as some that are without God, that is, the unbelieving, say, that He only seemed to suffer (they themselves only seeming to exist), then why am I in bonds? Why do I long to be exposed to the wild beasts? Do I therefore die in vain?" Rendering: "Some who are without God, that is, unbelievers, say that he only seemed to suffer. They themselves only seem to exist. But if they are right, why am I in chains? Why do I long to face the wild beasts? Do I die for nothing, then?" ("Bonds" is rendered "chains": its modern sense misleads.) Job: beat 3. His teaching that Christ truly suffered, tied to his own chains and death.
3. Greek (HOR 15547–15552, the Horologion citing Eusebius; Eusebius gives it from Irenaeus): "Σῖτός εἰμι τοῦ Θεοῦ, καὶ δι᾽ ὀδόντων θηρίων ἀλήθομαι, ἵνα καθαρὸς ἄρτος εὑρεθῶ." (ANF shorter, Rom. 4, ANF 7040–7042: "I am the wheat of God, and let me be ground by the teeth of the wild beasts, that I may be found the pure bread of Christ.") Rendering (from the Greek): "I am God's wheat, and I am ground by the teeth of wild beasts, so that I may be found pure bread." Job: beat 4. The plea not to be saved, in the image the church remembered; the narrator gives its plain sense (Words to explain).

**Connection to carry into the story:** what passed from Ignatius was seven letters, carried on by the churches he wrote to. Polycarp, who met him at Smyrna, gathered the ones he held and sent them to Philippi when that church asked (I1). Through them, later churches received three things he pressed on the road: one church gathered around one bishop, with elders and deacons, at one table; a Christ who truly suffered in the flesh; and the pattern of a martyr who would not be rescued (E5–E7). The listener must understand three things. First, the letters are almost all we know of him; without Polycarp's gathering there would be no Ignatius to tell (E1, I1). Second, later writers used them: Irenaeus quoted his last plea within a lifetime, Eusebius read him as a guard of the apostles' teaching, and later bishops used his words on Christ's real flesh (I2–I4). Third, what he wrote about bishops is why the letters were fought over. Questions of church order had long divided Christians, and the letters show churches each led by one bishop with elders and deacons. Some scholars thought that too developed for so early a date; others defended the letters; the argument ran for centuries (I7; EUS-ed 19848–19852 via sheet E5; ANF-ed 4479–4483). Tell it through these acts: the gathering and sending, the quoting, the long argument. Not as a "this matters because" sentence.

**Ending:** close on the person. Either (a) his words seen again: the prisoner who wrote that he fought with beasts all the way to Rome, now gone on from Troas toward the real ones, the last we hear of him (E2, E8); or (b) Polycarp, at Smyrna, sending the letters on and asking for surer news of "the blessed Ignatius" (E9, I1), if What came is ordered to end there. Do not end on the quarrel of the scholars.

**Who tells it**

- **Ignatius himself**, in seven letters written on the road, at Smyrna and at Troas. Almost the whole life rests on him. Say so once, inside a sentence about the letters, in or right after the essence: almost all we know of him comes from the letters he wrote on the road (E1, EUS-ed 19817–19820). Credit "he says" or "he writes" where he praises or judges: the churches meeting him, his opponents, the Spirit's word at Philadelphia.
- **Polycarp**, the bishop who met him, wrote after Ignatius had passed through, while still waiting for news (E9, I1). Credited at What came 1.
- **Irenaeus**, about seventy years later (I2). Credited at What came 2.
- **Eusebius**, about two hundred years later, the first to give a full account of him; he adds little the letters do not hold (I3). Credit his view of the journey as his.
- **Chrysostom**, late fourth century, preaching at Antioch (I5).
- **Scholars** from the seventeenth to the nineteenth century, on which letters are his (I7). Credit as "scholars"; the narrator does not settle it.

**Do not say**

- Any word in "Words to explain" used without its plain sense the first time it is heard.
- Do not quote "ten leopards" or "show themselves all the worse". Both are quoted in the Scattered Households story. Say "a squad of ten soldiers" or "his guards".
- Do not quote "wherever Jesus Christ is, there is the Catholic Church", and do not say he was "the first to use the words 'Catholic Church'". That is an editor's note (I8); it stays on the card.
- Not "he died in the Colosseum", "he was eaten by lions", "in the amphitheatre" or "on 20 December". The manner of death is only in late writers (E9, D5). Say "report said he was given to the beasts at Rome".
- Not "he died in 107" or "in 107 or 116" as fact. Say "early in the second century" (D3).
- Not "Trajan condemned him", "he stood before Trajan" or "he gave himself up". Only the late *Martyrdom* tells it (E1).
- Not "a disciple of John", "Peter made him bishop" or "the child Jesus held in his arms" (D4, D6, S7).
- Not "Ignatius created bishops", "the first bishop", "invented the threefold ministry" or "he founded church hierarchy". Say he urged the churches to keep to the bishop, the elders and the deacons they already had (E3, E5).
- Not "Docetists" or "Gnostics"; he names no group and will not write their names (E6). Say "some who said Christ only seemed to suffer".
- Not "the persecution at Antioch ended". He says only that the church there was "at peace" (E8, D7).
- Not "the emperor ordered him to sail". "Of the emperor" is the translators' word (E8). Say "he had to sail suddenly".
- Not "he was eager to die" or "he longed for death" as the narrator's verdict, and no comment on his state of mind. Give his words: he begged them not to save him. Do not soften them either: he says he will coax the beasts, and force them if they will not attack (E7, Rom. 5).
- Not "Polycarp saw him die" or "Polycarp buried him". Polycarp asked for news (E9).
- Not "Polycarp collected all his letters" or "published" them. He sent the ones written to Smyrna and the others he held, at Philippi's request (I1).
- Not "Irenaeus named him". He quotes him as "a certain man of ours" (I2).
- Not "fifteen letters" as his. Seven; the other eight were written later in his name (I7).
- Not "scholars agree" or "it is settled". Say "by 1890 most scholars accepted seven as his; some still did not" (I7, D2).
- Not "his body was brought home to Antioch" as fact. Chrysostom says so; it is a tradition traced to the late fourth century (I5).
- Not "Eastern Christians keep his feast today". The held source is the 1851 prayer book (I6).
- Not "the wheat of Christ". Quote 3 is "God's wheat", from the Greek. Irenaeus's Latin has "of Christ" (I2).
- Not "bishop of Antioch for forty years" or any start date. The date is "entirely unreliable" (S2).
- Not "he visited Ephesus, Magnesia and Tralles". Their bishops came to him at Smyrna (E3; route note S5).
- Not "on his way he passed through Philadelphia before Smyrna" as fact. He says only that he had been among them (E5); the route is an editor's reconstruction (S5).

**Leave out**

- The late *Martyrdom of Ignatius*: the trial before Trajan, the voyage, the scene in the amphitheatre, the remains carried to Antioch (E1, E9; EUS-ed 19761–19766). It is unreliable and centuries later.
- Socrates's story that he brought singing in alternate choirs to Antioch after a vision of angels (Library sweep): a fifth-century wonder story.
- Malalas's claim that he died at Antioch (EUS-ed 19946–19950).
- The bishop lists (second or third bishop; made by Peter or by Paul) beyond one clause (D4).
- "Medicine of immortality" (E5): a vivid phrase, but a fourth quote; keep the story to three.
- "My love has been crucified" (E7): moving, but it needs explaining and the story has its three quotes.
- The names Damas, Polybius, Burrhus, Philo, Agathopus, Crocus: side names.
- The Syriac three-letter version and Cureton by name, Daillé and Pearson by name: for the card. The story says only "scholars argued for centuries".
- Coxe's and Schaff's verdicts on his character (ANF-ed 4445–4448; EUS-ed 19889–19904): editors' judgements.
- Harnack's dating under Hadrian by name (D3): for the card.
