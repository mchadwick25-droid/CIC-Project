"From Syria all the way to Rome I fight with beasts, by land and by sea, by night and by day." A prisoner wrote that early in the second century. The beasts on his road were his guards, a squad of ten soldiers, while the real beasts waited in the arena at Rome.

His name was Ignatius, bishop of the church at Antioch in Syria, and he had been sentenced to be killed by wild animals in the arena. On the road he wrote seven letters to the churches that came out to meet him, and almost all we know of him comes from them. Polycarp, bishop of Smyrna, gathered the ones he held and sent them on, and later writers quoted them.

Of his life before his arrest nothing is known. He does not say why he was condemned, or by whom, only that he was led in chains "from the east unto the west."

Churches came out to meet him, he says, city by city. At Smyrna, in the Roman province of Asia, on the west coast of today's Turkey, he met Polycarp face to face, and bishops came there from three other cities. From Smyrna he wrote back to their churches. Each church, he told them, should act as one around its bishop, with the council of elders who led it with him and its deacons, at one altar, with one bread broken together.

He also warned them against teachers who said that Christ only seemed to have a body and to suffer. Christ, he wrote, was truly born, truly crucified under Pontius Pilate, and truly raised. "Some who are without God, that is, unbelievers, say that he only seemed to suffer. They themselves only seem to exist. But if they are right, why am I in chains? Why do I long to face the wild beasts? Do I die for nothing, then?"

From Smyrna, too, he wrote ahead to the church at Rome, and begged them not to work to spare him but to let him be food for the beasts. He would coax the beasts, he said, and force them if they held back. "I am God's wheat, and I am ground by the teeth of wild beasts, so that I may be found the pure bread of Christ." As grain is ground into bread, his torn body would become an offering to God.

At Troas, a port farther up the coast, he called the teachers he opposed beasts in human shape, who cared nothing for the widow, the orphan or the hungry. He told another church to do nothing without the bishop, and said that God's Spirit had told him to cry it aloud among them.

No early witness saw his death. Polycarp, writing soon after to the church at Philippi, asked for surer news of "the blessed Ignatius" and sent on his letters, as Philippi had asked. Within a lifetime, Irenaeus quoted the wheat line as the words of a man condemned to the beasts. Two centuries later Eusebius wrote that "report says" he was given to the beasts at Rome, and later bishops used his words on Christ's real flesh in their own quarrels.

Because his letters show each church led by one bishop with elders and deacons, some scholars thought them too developed for so early a date, and the argument over which were really his ran for centuries. By 1890 most accepted seven as his, though some still did not.

The last we hear from him in his own words, he has to sail suddenly from Troas: the prisoner who fought beasts all the way from Syria, sailing on toward the real ones at Rome.
