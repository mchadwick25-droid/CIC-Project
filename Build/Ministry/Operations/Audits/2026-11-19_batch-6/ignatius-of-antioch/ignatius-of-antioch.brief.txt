"From Syria all the way to Rome I fight with beasts, by land and by sea, by night and by day." A prisoner wrote that early in the second century. The beasts on his road were his guards, a squad of ten soldiers, while the real beasts waited in the arena at Rome.

His name was Ignatius, and he was bishop of the church at Antioch in Syria, sentenced to be killed by wild animals in the arena. On the road he wrote seven letters to the churches that came out to meet him, and almost all we know of him comes from them. Later churches kept them, and later writers quoted them.

Of his life before his arrest nothing is known. He says only that he was led in chains "from the east unto the west."

At Smyrna, on the west coast of today's Turkey, he met its bishop, Polycarp, face to face, and bishops came to him from three other cities. He wrote back to their churches that each should act as one around its bishop, its deacons and the elders who led it with him, at one altar, breaking one bread.

He also warned them against teachers who said that Christ only seemed to have a body and to suffer, and wrote that Christ was truly born, truly crucified under Pontius Pilate, and truly raised. "Some who are without God, that is, unbelievers, say that he only seemed to suffer. They themselves only seem to exist. But if they are right, why am I in chains? Why do I long to face the wild beasts? Do I die for nothing, then?"

From Smyrna he also wrote ahead to the church at Rome. He begged them not to work to spare him but to let him be food for the beasts, which he would coax, and force if they held back. "I am God's wheat, and I am ground by the teeth of wild beasts, so that I may be found the pure bread of Christ." As grain is ground into bread, his torn body would become an offering to God.

At Troas, a port on the same coast, he called the teachers he opposed beasts in human shape, who cared nothing for the widow, the orphan or the hungry. To another church he wrote that God's Spirit had moved him to cry aloud among them: do nothing without the bishop.

No early witness saw his death. Polycarp, writing soon after to the church at Philippi, asked for news of "the blessed Ignatius" and sent on the letters he held, as Philippi had asked. Within a lifetime Irenaeus quoted the wheat line, without naming him, and two centuries later Eusebius wrote that "report says" he was given to the beasts at Rome. Later bishops used his words on Christ's real flesh in their own quarrels.

Because the letters show each church led by one bishop, some scholars thought them too developed for so early a date, and they argued for centuries over which were his. By 1890 most accepted seven, though some still did not.

The last we hear in his own words, he has to sail suddenly from Troas: the prisoner who fought beasts all the way from Syria, sailing on toward the real ones at Rome.
