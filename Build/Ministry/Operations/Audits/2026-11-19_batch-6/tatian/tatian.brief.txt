Do not sneer at the barbarians, as you call all who are not Greek, Tatian told the Greeks, for which of your arts did not come from them? You owe the stars to the Babylonians, geometry to the Egyptians, and your very alphabet to the Phoenicians.

Tatian was born in the land of the Assyrians, east of the Euphrates, yet he had mastered Greek learning. At Rome, in the middle of the second century, he became a Christian and heard the teacher Justin. Then, in a book addressed to the Greeks, he turned on all he had studied, calling it borrowed and younger than Moses. Later he broke with the church, and his weaving of the four Gospels into one story became the gospel most churches speaking Syriac, a form of Aramaic, heard read.

He had been admitted to the mysteries, the secret rites of a god's cult, and at Rome, he says, he saw a Jupiter who delighted in human blood. Searching alone for the truth, he found old writings he calls barbarian writings, which his editors take to be the Hebrew Scriptures. He trusted their plain language and their one God. A philosopher at Rome, he adds, tried to have both Justin and him put to death.

His book mocks the philosophers as vain and greedy, while Christians, he says, teach the poor for free, and their women study philosophy. Then comes his turn: "So I said goodbye to the boasting of the Romans and the lifeless talk of the Athenians, teachings that do not hang together. I made our barbarian philosophy my own." He then sets out to prove, from Greek, Babylonian and Egyptian writers, that Moses was older than Homer.

Irenaeus, who wrote as his opponent, says that after Justin's martyrdom, about 165, Tatian left the church, puffed up with wanting to be a teacher. He taught invisible heavenly powers, Irenaeus says, as the followers of Valentinus did, and called marriage corruption, as Marcion did. He was also the first, Irenaeus says, to teach that Adam was not saved. A church historian dates the break to about 172, and later writers called him the founder of the Encratites, "the self-controlled".

A later writer says Tatian went east, to the land between the Tigris and the Euphrates, and only that writer puts him there. Tatian wove the four Gospels into one continuous story, the Diatessaron, the fourfold gospel, though whether in Greek or Syriac, and before or after the break, is disputed.

In the churches that spoke Syriac it was widely read aloud as the Gospel, and Ephrem the Syrian wrote a commentary on it. In the 400s, two bishops moved against it. A bishop of Edessa ordered the separate Gospels read in every church, and the words we have of his order give no reason. Theodoret of Cyrrhus, in northern Syria, charged that it cut out Jesus's descent from David, a charge the surviving texts only partly bear out. People used it in all innocence, he says, as a handy short book. Then he tells what he did, and by evangelists he means the four Gospel writers. "I myself found more than two hundred such books, held in honor in our churches. I gathered them all and put them away. In their place I brought in the Gospels of the four evangelists."

Still, the fourfold gospel outlived them, and it survives in Arabic and Latin forms. Near the close of his book to the Greeks, the man from the land of the Assyrians stands before them once more. "Now that I know who God is, and what he has made, I stand before you, ready to be questioned on what I teach. I hold to the way of life that follows God, and I will not deny it."
