Do not sneer at the barbarians, Tatian told the Greeks, for which of your arts did not come from them? You owe the stars to the Babylonians, geometry to the Egyptians, and your very alphabet to the Phoenicians.

To a Greek, a barbarian was anyone who was not Greek. Tatian was born in the land of the Assyrians, east of the Euphrates, yet he had mastered Greek learning. At Rome he became a Christian and heard the teacher Justin. Then, in a book addressed to the Greeks, he turned on all he had studied, calling it borrowed and younger than Moses. Later he broke with the church, and his weaving of the four Gospels into one story became the gospel most Syriac-speaking churches heard read.

He had been admitted to the mysteries, secret rites of initiation into a god's cult, and at Rome, he says, he saw a Jupiter who delighted in human blood. He searched for the truth alone, until he found old writings he calls barbarian writings, which his editors take to be the Hebrew Scriptures. Their plain language and their one God won him, and they set people free, he says, from ten thousand tyrants. A philosopher at Rome, he adds, tried to have both Justin and him put to death.

His book mocks the philosophers as vain and greedy, while Christians, he says, teach the poor for free, and their women study philosophy. Then comes his turn: "So I said goodbye to the boasting of the Romans and the lifeless talk of the Athenians, teachings that do not hang together. I made our barbarian philosophy my own."

After Justin's martyrdom, about 165, Tatian left the church. So says Irenaeus, who wrote as his opponent. Irenaeus says Tatian was puffed up with wanting to be a teacher, called marriage corruption, and first taught that Adam was not saved. Later writers called him the founder of the Encratites, a name meaning "the self-controlled".

A later writer says Tatian went east, to the land between the Tigris and the Euphrates. He wove the four Gospels into one continuous story, the Diatessaron, the fourfold gospel. Whether he made it in Greek or Syriac, and before or after the break, is disputed.

In the churches that worshipped in Syriac, a form of Aramaic, this was the book read aloud. A Christian writer in Persia quotes it as the Gospel, and Ephrem the Syrian wrote a commentary on it. In the 400s, two bishops moved against it. A bishop of Edessa ordered the separate Gospels read in every church. Theodoret of Cyrrhus, in northern Syria, charged that it cut out Jesus's descent from David, a charge the surviving texts only partly bear out. He tells what he did with it, calling the four Gospel writers the evangelists: "I myself found more than two hundred such books, held in honor in our churches. I gathered them all and put them away. In their place I brought in the Gospels of the four evangelists."

Still, it survives in Arabic and Latin forms, and in Ephrem's commentary. Near the close of his book to the Greeks, the man from the land of the Assyrians stands before them once more: "Now that I know who God is, and what he has made, I stand before you, ready to be questioned on what I teach. I hold to the way of life that follows God, and I will not deny it."
