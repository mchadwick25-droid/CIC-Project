# Outline: Tatian

Sheet items (S, E, I, D) are in `Source_Sheet.md`, which gives the file and line for each.

- **The essence:** Tatian, a man born "in the land of the Assyrians" and trained in Greek learning, became a Christian at Rome and a hearer of Justin. In his *Address to the Greeks* he turned on everything he had studied: their gods, their philosophers and their arts, all borrowed, he said, from the peoples they called barbarians, and all younger than Moses (S2, E3, E5; Documented, his own words). After Justin's death he broke with the church, by the account of his opponent Irenaeus, about 172 by Eusebius's date (E6, S4; Documented as Irenaeus's account and Eusebius's date). He went back east, and his *Diatessaron*, the four Gospels woven into one continuous story, became for about two centuries the gospel most Syriac-speaking churches heard read (E7, E8, I1; the journey east Widely Accepted, resting on Epiphanius two centuries later; the harmony's use Documented; "very widely if not universally read" is Hogg's judgment, Widely Accepted).
- **Also told:**
  - What the *Address* says (E5). Every Greek art came from non-Greeks. The philosophers are vain and greedy. One law should serve all people. The Christians teach the poor for free, and old women and boys too, and their women study philosophy. Moses is older than Homer, proved from Greek, Chaldean and Egyptian writers. His own road: the cults of Rome, a search alone, and "barbarian writings", the Hebrew Scriptures, older and more divine than Greek teaching (E1, E2; Documented, his words; the identification is the editors', Widely Accepted). The cost of it at Rome: a philosopher, Crescens, tried to have both Justin and Tatian put to death (E4; Documented as Tatian's charge).
  - The break (E6). Irenaeus says that after Justin's martyrdom Tatian left the church, "puffed up" by wanting to be a teacher; that he taught invisible heavenly powers like the followers of Valentinus, called marriage corruption, and denied that Adam was saved. Later writers made him the founder of the Encratites, "the self-controlled" (I5). All Documented as the hostile witnesses' accounts; founding the Encratites Contested (D4).
  - The harmony and its fate (I1, I2). Aphrahat, writing between 336 and 345, quotes "the beginning of the Gospel": "In the beginning was the Word". Ephrem wrote a commentary on it. In the 400s two bishops took it away: Rabbula of Edessa ordered the separate Gospels read in every church, and Theodoret gathered more than two hundred copies in his own churches and put the four Gospels in their place (Q3). Documented. It survives today only in translation (I3; Documented as Hogg's 1896 report).
  The sources will not hold "the only gospel the Syriac churches read" (Hogg: "very widely if not universally"; Aphrahat sometimes cites the separate Gospels, AP-P), or "Tatian went to Edessa" (no source places him there; the harmony was read there).
- **Length:** about 3 minutes (about 450 words). Why: his own book survives and can carry the turn and the argument in scenes; the harmony's afterlife has real force (two bishops, two hundred copies) and is the connection. Not more: the facts of his life are few and their order is disputed (D3), and Justin's story already tells the hearer, the break and the Edessa reading.
- **Setting:**
  - Time: "the middle of the second century" (S4); Justin's death about 165; the break about 172 (Eusebius's date, S4). The harmony's removal, the 400s: Rabbula d. 435, Theodoret's book 453 (I2).
  - Place: born "in the land of the Assyrians" (S2): say "east of the Euphrates" if a gloss is needed. Rome, "the great city" (S6, E4). Later "the East", Mesopotamia, "the land between the rivers" (E7). Theodoret's churches: Cyrrhus, in Syria (I2).
  - Tradition: "Syriac Christianity", the Syriac-speaking churches around Edessa (S5). Syriac: "a form of Aramaic".
- **Words to explain** (the writer works the gloss in where the word is first heard, or uses the plain word):
  - **barbarian**: in Greek, anyone who was not Greek. Tatian takes the insult as a badge: Christianity is "our barbarian philosophy" (E5f). Always give the plain sense the first time.
  - **philosophy**: Greek schools of thought on God, nature and how to live. Tatian calls Christianity a philosophy too (E5d, E5f).
  - **the Address** / **apology**: use "a book addressed to the Greeks". Do not use "apology".
  - **the mysteries**: "secret rites of initiation into a god's cult" (E1).
  - **rhetoric**: "the art of public speaking" (S3).
  - **Diatessaron**: Greek for "through four"; the Arabic copy calls it "the Fourfold" (E8). Say "the Diatessaron, the fourfold gospel".
  - **harmony**: "the four Gospels woven into one continuous story" (E8).
  - **aeons**: avoid the word; "invisible heavenly powers" (E6).
  - **Encratites**: "a name meaning 'the self-controlled'", for Christians who refused marriage, meat and wine (IR 33656–33661; EU-ed 26283–26286).
  - **heresy** / **heretic**: "teaching the church condemned"; always a witness's word, never the narrator's.
  - **Mesopotamia**: "the land between the Tigris and the Euphrates".
  - **Syriac**: "a form of Aramaic, the language of the churches around Edessa".
  - **evangelists** (in Q3): "the four Gospel writers".
  - **Cyrrhus**: "a city in northern Syria", Theodoret's see.
  - **Chaldean**: "Babylonian".
- **Shape:** Teacher or thinker. Trap: a list of books. Show the one idea, that the "barbarians" were older and truer than the Greeks, through his own words and his own turn; then the harmony through what churches did with it.
- **Sibling:**
  - `justin-martyr` (live new-kit story, read): its job is Justin's own life and death. It already tells, in four sentences, that Irenaeus and Hippolytus name Tatian as his hearer, that Tatian praised him and carried on his attack on the gods and philosophers, that Irenaeus says he left the church, that Eusebius says he wove the Gospels into one, and that a Syriac account of Edessa says people came to hear it read. Here: Justin is a clause (hearer; Crescens's threat to both); Tatian's own book carries the story; the *Doctrine of Addai* line is at most a clause and not quoted; none of Justin's quotes is reused.
  - `bardaisan-of-edessa` (batch 6, researched after this one): Eusebius tells him in the next chapter (EU 26525–26529). No tie between the two men is held; neither story needs the other.
  - `marcion-of-pontus` (live, read): it tells Irenaeus's fourfold Gospel against Marcion. Here Marcion is a clause inside Irenaeus's charge only.
  - `irenaeus-of-lyon` (live text read; batch 6 outline later): here Irenaeus is the hostile witness to the break, one beat.
  - `ephrem-the-syrian` (live, read): famine, Bardaisan, hymns. Here only "Ephrem wrote a commentary on it".
  - `valentinus`, `hippolytus-of-rome` (batch 6, later): a clause each at most.
- **Openings:**
  1. **Chosen.** His own first lines (E5a, AG 5684–5693). Tatian, born in the land of the Assyrians and once a master of Greek learning, wrote to the Greeks: do not sneer at the barbarians, for which of your arts did not come from them? You owe the stars to the Babylonians, geometry to the Egyptians, and your very alphabet to the Phoenicians. Narrator's paraphrase, not a quote. Leads straight into the essence.
  2. The two hundred copies (I2): a fifth-century bishop gathers more than two hundred copies of a gospel from his churches and puts them away. Not chosen: it is the end of the harmony's story and cannot open without telling the ending early.

**Story beats** (the opening and beat 1 are the first minute, about 150 words; the essence is said by the end of beat 1)

1. **A Greek-trained man from the East** (S2, S3). Born "in the land of the Assyrians", trained first in Greek teaching, a traveler and a speaker; Jerome, two centuries on, says he won fame teaching oratory (credit Jerome). He had been admitted to the mysteries, and at Rome he saw, he says, a Jupiter delighting in human blood (E1). Alone, he searched for the truth (Documented, his words). Then say the essence.
2. **"Barbarian writings"** (E2, E3). He came upon old foreign writings (the Hebrew Scriptures: the editors' identification, Widely Accepted; he says only "barbarian writings") and trusted them for their plain language, their prophecies, their precepts and their one God; they set people free, he says, from "ten thousand tyrants" (his words, narrated). He became a Christian at Rome and heard Justin, whom he calls "the most admirable Justin" (narrate, no quote; Justin's story tells the praise). A philosopher named Crescens, Tatian says, tried to have both Justin and him put to death (E4). Documented as Tatian's account.
3. **The Address** (E5). What the book argues, told as substance: the Greeks' philosophers vain; their laws differ city to city, when there should be one law for all; Christians teach the poor without fee and admit old women and boys, and their women study philosophy. Then the turn in his words (Q1). He then sets out to prove from Greek, Chaldean and Egyptian writers that Moses is older than Homer, and so older than all Greek learning. Documented, his words. Give no date or place of writing (D2).
4. **The break** (E6, S4). After Justin's martyrdom, about 165, Tatian left the church, Irenaeus says; Irenaeus writes as his opponent and calls him "puffed up" with wanting to be a teacher. He taught invisible heavenly powers, as Valentinus's followers did; he called marriage corruption, as Marcion did; and he was the first, Irenaeus says, to deny that Adam was saved, a view that was his own invention. Eusebius dates the break to about 172. Documented as Irenaeus's account and Eusebius's date. Encratites: one clause, as "later writers called him their founder" (Contested, D4).
5. **East, with the harmony** (E7, E8). A later writer, two centuries on, says he went from Rome to the East and set up a school in Mesopotamia; it is the only source for the place (Widely Accepted). He wove the four Gospels into one continuous story, the Diatessaron, beginning "In the beginning was the Word" (Bar Salibi via Hogg; and as Aphrahat quotes it). Whether he made it before or after the break, and in Greek or Syriac, is disputed: say so in one clause (D5).

**What came of him** (about a third of the story; heaviest first)

1. **The gospel of the Syriac churches.** Aphrahat, writing inside Persia between 336 and 345 (CEN tradition), quotes it as "the Gospel" (AP 24764–24766; Parisot's reading). Ephrem wrote a commentary on it, which survives in Armenian (I1; Widely Accepted). A modern editor, Hope Hogg, judged it "very widely if not universally read in the Syriac churches" (credit Hogg). The *Doctrine of Addai* line: a clause at most (sibling).
2. **Taken away, by force of office.** Rabbula, bishop of Edessa, ordered "the Distinct Gospel", the separate Gospels, read in every church (I2; Documented as Hogg quotes it). Theodoret says those who kept to the apostles' teaching, as he puts it, used it in all innocence, simply as a handy short book, and he gathered the copies (Q3). Tell it plainly: bishops removed a book their people honored. Theodoret's charge that it cut out Jesus's descent from David: one clause, credited to Theodoret, with "a charge the surviving texts only partly bear out" (D6).
3. **It outlived them.** New Syriac copies were made in the 800s; it was put into Arabic, and that Arabic, with a Latin form and Ephrem's commentary in Armenian, is how it survives (I3; Documented as Hogg's report).
4. **The Address kept for its dates.** DEFENDED: Christian teaching as a "barbarian philosophy" older and truer than Greek learning, open to the poor, to women and to the old (E5). Clement used his dating of Moses; Eusebius called it the most useful of his many books; of all he wrote, only the Address survives whole (I4). One clause.

- **Quotes, 2 or 3 at most** (each rendered by me from the Greek, checked against Ryland's English):
  - **Q1.** "So I said goodbye to the boasting of the Romans and the lifeless talk of the Athenians, teachings that do not hang together. I made our barbarian philosophy my own." Source: *Address* 35, AG 7258–7260 (Ryland); AG-G 12199–12201 (Greek, second witness). Job: the turn, in his words; "barbarian" taken as a badge. The narrator gives "barbarian" its plain sense just before (Words to explain).
  - **Q2.** "Now that I know who God is, and what he has made, I stand before you, ready to be questioned on what I teach. I hold to the way of life that follows God, and I will not deny it." Source: *Address* 42, AG 7464–7471 (Ryland); AG-G 14236–14241 (Greek, second witness). Job: the ending, on the person.
  - **Q3.** "I myself found more than two hundred such books, held in honor in our churches. I gathered them all and put them away. In their place I brought in the Gospels of the four evangelists." Source: Theodoret, *Compendium of Heretical Fables* I.20, Greek as quoted by Maran, TH 14984–14987; Maran's Latin, TH 14994–14997 (Theodoret's own book is not held). Job: the force of the harmony's removal, and how honored it had been.

- **Connection to carry into the story:** the Diatessaron passed from Tatian to the Syriac-speaking churches, which read it in worship as their gospel, quoted it (Aphrahat) and wrote commentaries on it (Ephrem), until bishops of the 400s set out to remove it. Rabbula of Edessa ordered the separate Gospels read in every church, and the order as quoted gives no reason. Theodoret, writing against heresies, says Tatian's harmony left out the passages showing that Jesus was descended from David, and that faithful Christians used it without seeing the harm. A later editor finds that charge only partly borne out by the surviving texts. Its copies were still being made centuries later (Hogg). The listener must be told what a harmony is (one continuous story made from all four Gospels, so a church heard one account, not four books), that this was the book read aloud in their churches, and why bishops moved against it, as the witnesses above give it. Supporting passages: I1, I2 (AP 24764–24766; DI-ed 1914–1931; TH 14976–14984).
- **Ending:**
  1. **Chosen.** His own closing words (Q2), the opening's man seen again: the Greek-trained Assyrian standing before the Greeks to be questioned, holding to his way of life (AG 7464–7471). It follows the afterlife without moralizing; the narrator adds no comment on what became of that promise.
  2. Theodoret's two hundred copies put away, if the writer must close on the harmony. Not preferred: the kit ends on the person.
- **Links:** Justin: heard him teach. Irenaeus: wrote against him. Valentinus, Marcion: likened to them by Irenaeus. Eusebius: wrote of the Diatessaron and the Address. Ephrem: wrote a commentary on his book. Theodoret: removed his book from the churches. Clement of Alexandria: used his dates. Hippolytus: repeated Irenaeus's charges.
- **Who tells it:**
  - Tatian himself, the *Address* (his own time): the conversion, Rome, Crescens, the argument, his closing stand.
  - Irenaeus (within about a decade; his opponent): the hearer, the break and its three charges, and the motive. Credit him by name, and say once he wrote as an opponent.
  - Eusebius (about 150 years later): the date of the break, the Diatessaron's name.
  - Epiphanius (about 200 years later; often wrong on dates): the school in Mesopotamia. Say "a later writer", and that the place rests on him.
  - Jerome (about 200 years later): his fame as a teacher of oratory.
  - Aphrahat and Ephrem (the 300s): the harmony in use.
  - Rabbula and Theodoret (the 400s): its removal. Theodoret in his own words.
  - Hope Hogg (1896, editor and translator of the Arabic): the judgment "very widely if not universally read", and the survival in translation.
- **Do not say:**
  - "the only gospel the Syriac churches read" → "the gospel most of them heard read", or Hogg's "very widely if not universally".
  - "Tatian went to Edessa" or "took it to Edessa" → "went east, to Mesopotamia, a later writer says"; the harmony was read at Edessa.
  - "Crescens had Justin killed" → "Crescens tried to have Justin, and Tatian too, put to death, Tatian says".
  - "Tatian founded the Encratites" as fact → "later writers called him their founder".
  - "He was proud / puffed up" in the narrator's voice → "Irenaeus, his opponent, says".
  - "a heretic" or "heresy" in the narrator's voice → always a witness's word.
  - Any date for the *Address*, or where it was written.
  - "He wrote the Diatessaron in Syriac" or "after he left the church" → "whether in Greek or Syriac, and before or after the break, is disputed".
  - "Tatian cut out Jesus's family line" as fact → Theodoret's charge, only partly borne out.
  - "Greek by birth" or "a Syrian" → "born in the land of the Assyrians", his words.
  - "Theodoret burned the copies" → "gathered them all and put them away".
  - "barbarian" without its plain sense the first time; "aeons", "apology" at all.
  - Any feeling for Tatian at the break (bitterness, disappointment): none is in the sources.
  - "he alone denied Adam's salvation" → "he first taught it, Irenaeus says".
  - "bishops removed it because Tatian was a heretic" → "Theodoret says it cut out Jesus's descent from David; Rabbula gives no reason".
  - "after the break he went east" as fact → "a later writer, Epiphanius, says that after Justin's death he went east".
  - "the orthodox" in the narrator's voice.
  - "for exactly two hundred years" → "from his time until bishops moved against it in the 400s".
- **Leave out:** Epiphanius's route through Antioch, Cilicia and Pisidia (late, and a list); the Severians (Eusebius; McGiffert shows the link is doubtful); the *On Perfection* fragment and the arguments on Genesis (technical, hostile quoters); Rhodo, his pupil at Rome (a side figure); the editors' sympathy and censure (Coxe, Ryland, D9); Eusebius's "I know not how" and the debate over it (D7); the Codex Fuldensis and the Arabic manuscripts' detail; 'Abdisho's praise (late); Moore's verse counts. None of the record's conflict is left out: the break, the charges, and the bishops' removal of the book are all told.
