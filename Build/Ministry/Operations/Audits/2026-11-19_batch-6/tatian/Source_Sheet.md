**Witness kinds.**
- OWN: the *Address to the Greeks* (AG, AG-G). The *Diatessaron* is his work, but it is "an impersonal work" (DI-ed 1946–1947) and survives only in later translations; it carries no words of his own.
- CONTEMPORARY, hostile: Irenaeus, *Against Heresies*, written within about a decade of the break (Irenaeus writes while Eleutherius is bishop of Rome, about 175–189, census entry `irenaeus-of-lyon`). Irenaeus is Tatian's opponent.
- LATER, about one generation: Clement of Alexandria (c. 200); Hippolytus (c. 220s), who follows Irenaeus closely.
- LATER, about 150 years: Eusebius (c. 325); Aphrahat (336–345), Ephrem (d. 373).
- LATER, about 200 years: Epiphanius (c. 375), whom McGiffert calls error-prone; Jerome (392).
- LATER, about 280 years: Theodoret, book on heresies, 453 (DI-ed 1789–1790).
- LATE: Isho'dad of Merv (c. 852), Bar Salibi (12th c.), 'Abdisho (d. 1318), all only as quoted by Hogg; the Arabic *Diatessaron* (Ibn at-Tayyib, 11th c.).
- MODERN editors: Ryland and Coxe (ANF 2), McGiffert (1890), Hogg (1896), Holl (1922), Parisot (1894), Phillips (1876), Maran (18th c., in Otto 1872), Mitchell (1912–21). Their views are marked as theirs.

## 1. Setting

S1. **Name.** "I Tatian, a disciple of the barbarian philosophy" (AG 7457–7458); Greek "Τατιανὸς" (AG-G). Census name "Tatian" (CEN). Documented.

S2. **Birth and learning.** "I was born in the land of the Assyrians, having been first instructed in your doctrines, and afterwards in those which I now undertake to proclaim" (AG 7460–7464; AG-G 14225–14233 "γεννηθεὶς μὲν ἐν τῇ τῶν Ἀσσυρίων γῇ, παιδευθεὶς δὲ πρῶτον μὲν τὰ ὑμέτερα"). OWN. Documented. "Assyria" here is the Greek name for the land east of the Euphrates: plain, "the land of the Assyrians, east of the Euphrates" (Coxe: "from Euphrates and the Tigris", AG-ed 5545–5546). Whether he was Greek or Syrian by birth is disputed: McGiffert follows Harnack that he "was of Greek parentage", against Zahn's "Syrian by race" (EU-ed 26191–26196); Epiphanius says "Syrian by race" (EP 15995), and so does Clement (EP-ed 16012, citing Strom. III 81). **Contested: say only what he says, "born in the land of the Assyrians".**

S3. **Travels and rhetoric.** "I have visited many lands; I have followed rhetoric, like yourselves; I have fallen in with many arts and inventions; and finally, when sojourning in the city of the Romans, I inspected the multiplicity of statues brought thither by you" (AG 7250–7253). OWN. Documented. Jerome: "while teaching oratory, won not a little glory in the rhetorical art" (JE 40088–40089). LATER. Documented as Jerome's account. "though I was once a great proficient in it" (AG 5716), of Greek wisdom.

S4. **Time.** Census: "broke with the church c. 172"; active 172 (CEN). Eusebius's *Chronicle*, twelfth year of Marcus Aurelius, 172: "Tatianus hæreticus agnoscitur, a quo Encratitæ" (quoted EU-ed 26218–26220; the *Chronicle* itself is not held). McGiffert: "no reason to doubt that this represents with reasonable accuracy the date of Tatian's break" (EU-ed 26220–26222). Justin's death about 165 (EU-ed 26223). Jerome: "He flourished in the reign of Marcus Antoninus Verus and Lucius Aurelius Commodus" (JE 40098–40099). Plain: "the middle of the second century"; the break "about 172". Death: "Of the date, place, and circumstances of Tatian's death, we know nothing" (EU-ed 26234–26235).

S5. **Tradition.** Census `toldWithin`: `syriac-edessa-nisibis`, spoken name "Syriac Christianity" (CEN `informalName`), "Christians who spoke Syriac" in Edessa and Nisibis, 200–410 (CEN tradition). The tradition entry: "They met the Gospel as one continuous story rather than four separate books, in a harmony a Greek-trained convert named Tatian had woven a generation earlier" (CEN tradition `longDescription`). Plain words: "the Syriac-speaking churches of upper Mesopotamia, around Edessa"; Syriac is "a form of Aramaic".

S6. **Places.** Rome: AG 7252 ("the city of the Romans"); the Address names "Latiarian Jupiter" and "Artemis not far from the great city" (AG 6965–6967). The East: see E7. Census places: Rome, Edessa (CEN). **No held source places Tatian himself at Edessa.** Edessa enters only as the place where the Diatessaron was read (I3).

## 2. Life events, in order

E1. **The search, in his own words.** Having "been admitted to the mysteries", and found among the Romans "their Latiarian Jupiter delighting in human gore and the blood of slaughtered men ... retiring by myself, I sought how I might be able to discover the truth" (AG 6962–6971). OWN. Documented. Words: "the mysteries" = secret rites of initiation into a god's cult.

E2. **The conversion: "barbarian writings".** "I happened to meet with certain barbaric writings, too old to be compared with the opinions of the Greeks, and too divine to be compared with their errors; and I was led to put faith in these by the unpretending cast of the language, the inartificial character of the writers, the foreknowledge displayed of future events, the excellent quality of the precepts, and the declaration of the government of the universe as centred in one Being" (AG 6971–6978). Greek: "συνέβη γραφαῖς τισιν ἐντυχεῖν βαρβαρικαῖς, πρεσβυτέραις μὲν ὡς πρὸς τὰ Ἑλλήνων δόγματα, θειοτέραις δὲ ὡς πρὸς τὴν ἐκείνων πλάνην" (AG-G 10387–10390). "these put an end to the slavery that is in the world, and rescue us from a multiplicity of rulers and ten thousand tyrants" (AG 6993–6994). OWN. Documented. The writings are the Hebrew Scriptures (Coxe: "the Holy Scriptures", AG-ed 6979; Ryland: "the sacred books of the Christians", AG-ed 5641–5642): editors' identification, Widely Accepted.

E3. **Justin's hearer.** Irenaeus: "He was a hearer of Justin's, and as long as he continued with him he expressed no such views" (IR 33664–33665). CONTEMPORARY, hostile. Documented. Tatian himself: "And the most admirable Justin has rightly denounced them as robbers" (AG 6526–6530); Coxe: "The language of an affectionate pupil: ὁ θαυμασιώτατος Ιουστινος" (AG-ed 6526–6529). Hippolytus: "being himself a disciple of Justinus the Martyr" (HI 13005). Jerome: "a follower of Justin Martyr and was distinguished so long as he did not leave his master's side" (JE 40089–40091). Documented. **Sibling `justin-martyr` already tells this; here a clause.**

E4. **Crescens.** "Crescens, who made his nest in the great city, surpassed all men in unnatural love ... and was strongly addicted to the love of money. Yet this man, who professed to despise death, was so afraid of death, that he endeavoured to inflict on Justin, and indeed on me, the punishment of death, as being an evil, because by proclaiming the truth he convicted the philosophers of being gluttons and cheats" (AG 6550–6556). OWN. Documented as Tatian's charge. Eusebius read it as saying Crescens caused Justin's death (EU 23875–23877); McGiffert: as the text stands, "the passage is far from proving that Justin's death was caused by the machinations of Crescens, for Tatian puts himself on a level with Justin as the object" (EU-ed 23895–23898). **Do not say Crescens had Justin killed.** Words: "the great city" = Rome.

E5. **The Address to the Greeks: what it says.** Date and place of writing: Harnack via McGiffert, 152–153, written in the East after a first stay at Rome (EU-ed 26227–26228, 26494); others put it later. **Contested: give no date and no place of writing.** Its arguments, all OWN, Documented:
- a. Every Greek art came from "barbarians" (non-Greeks): "Be not, O Greeks, so very hostilely disposed towards the Barbarians, nor look with ill will on their opinions. For which of your institutions has not been derived from the Barbarians? ... To the Babylonians you owe astronomy; to the Persians, magic; to the Egyptians, geometry; to the Phœnicians, instruction by alphabetic writing" (AG 5684–5693). "On this account we have renounced your wisdom, though I was once a great proficient in it" (AG 5715–5716).
- b. The philosophers mocked: "What noble thing have you produced by your pursuit of philosophy?" (AG 5738–5739), with tales of Diogenes, Plato and Aristotle (5739–5756).
- c. One law for all, against Greek codes: "there ought to be one common polity for all; but now there are as many different codes as there are states" (AG 6948–6950).
- d. Free teaching for all: "Not only do the rich among us pursue our philosophy, but the poor enjoy instruction gratuitously ... we admit all who desire to hear, even old women and striplings" (AG 7076–7081).
- e. Christian women: "you may not treat the women with scorn who among us pursue philosophy" (AG 7131–7133); "all our women are chaste, and the maidens at their distaffs sing of divine things more nobly than that damsel of yours" (AG 7140–7141, 7157–7158).
- f. The turn, in one sentence (Q1): "So, bidding farewell to the arrogance of Romans and the idle talk of Athenians, and all their ill-connected opinions, I embraced our barbaric philosophy" (AG 7258–7260). Greek: "Διόπερ χαίρειν εἰπὼν καὶ τῇ Ῥωμαίων μεγαλαυχίᾳ καὶ τῇ Ἀθηναίων ψυχρολογίᾳ, δόγμασιν ἀσυναρτήτοις, τῆς καθ᾽ ἡμᾶς βαρβάρου φιλοσοφίας ἀντεποιησάμην" (AG-G 12199–12201). He expects the reply: "Tatian, aspiring to be above the Greeks ... has struck out a new path, and embraced the doctrines of Barbarians" (AG 7269–7271).
- g. Moses older than Homer: "it seems proper for me to demonstrate that our philosophy is older than the systems of the Greeks. Moses and Homer shall be our limits ... we shall find that our doctrines are older, not only than those of the Greeks, but than the invention of letters" (AG 7026–7032); "I will not bring forward witnesses from among ourselves, but rather have recourse to Greeks" (7033–7034), then Chaldean, Phoenician and Egyptian dates (chapters 36–41).
- h. His standing, at the close (Q2): "Henceforward, knowing who God is and what is His work, I present myself to you prepared for an examination concerning my doctrines, while I adhere immoveably to that mode of life which is according to God" (AG 7464–7471). Greek: "Γινώσκων δὲ λοιπὸν τίς ὁ θεὸς καὶ τίς ἡ κατ᾽ αὐτὸν ποίησις, ἕτοιμον ἐμαυτὸν ὑμῖν πρὸς τὴν ἀνάκρισιν τῶν δογμάτων παρίστημι, μενούσης μοι τῆς κατὰ θεὸν πολιτείας ἀνεξαρνήτου" (AG-G 14236–14241).
- Tone: McGiffert: "he treats Greek philosophy with the greatest contempt, and finds nothing good in it", unlike Justin (EU-ed 26489–26491). Editor's view; Widely Accepted. The text itself bears it out (E5a, b, f).

E6. **The break, by Irenaeus.** "A certain man named Tatian first introduced the blasphemy. He was a hearer of Justin's, and as long as he continued with him he expressed no such views; but after his martyrdom he separated from the Church, and, excited and puffed up by the thought of being a teacher, as if he were superior to others, he composed his own peculiar type of doctrine. He invented a system of certain invisible Æons, like the followers of Valentinus; while, like Marcion and Saturninus, he declared that marriage was nothing else than corruption and fornication. But his denial of Adam's salvation was an opinion due entirely to himself" (IR 33663–33675). Also: "False, therefore, is that man who first started this idea, or rather, this ignorance and blindness—Tatian" (IR 43912–43913). CONTEMPORARY, hostile. **Documented as Irenaeus's account; the motive ("puffed up") is Irenaeus's judgment, and must be credited to him.** Hippolytus repeats the three points (HI 13005–13012; 15349–15358). Jerome: "inflated by a swelling of eloquence, he founded a new heresy which is called that of the Encratites" (JE 40091–40093). Words: "Æons" = invisible heavenly powers; "Adam's salvation" = whether the first man was saved.
- E6a. **On marriage, in his own (lost) book, as Clement quotes it:** a fragment of *On Perfection according to the Saviour* (FR 7488–7499, from Strom. III.12; title at EU-ed 26251–26255). LATER (one generation), hostile quoter. Leave out: the argument is technical.

E7. **The East.** Epiphanius (c. 375): "Syrian by race ... he set up his school first in Mesopotamia, about the twelfth year of Antoninus ... For from Rome after the death of holy Justin he crossed to the parts of the East and stayed there"; most of his preaching "from Antioch by Daphne and in the parts of Cilicia, and still more in Pisidia" took hold, and from him came "the so-called Encratites" (EP 15995–16008, my rendering of: "Σύρος τὸ γένος ... τὸ δὲ αὐτοῦ διδασκαλεῖον προεστήσατο ἀπ᾽ ἀρχῆς μὲν ἐν τῇ Μέσῃ τῶν ποταμῶν ... ἀπὸ Ῥώμης γὰρ μετὰ τὴν τοῦ ἁγίου Ἰουστίνου τελείωσιν διελθὼν ἐπὶ τὰ τῆς ἀνατολῆς μέρη"). LATER, about 200 years. Holl: "in Mesopotamia" is "nur durch Epiph. überliefert", found only in Epiphanius (EP-ed 16018). McGiffert: Epiphanius "falls into grave errors (especially in his chronology). The only trustworthy information that can be gathered from him is that Tatian, after becoming a Christian, returned to Mesopotamia and taught for a while there" (EU-ed 26209–26214). Hogg: "after Justin's death left Rome and returned to Mesopotamia" (DI-ed 1960–1961). **Widely Accepted that he went east to Mesopotamia; it rests on Epiphanius alone, two centuries later.** The sequence of his life is told two ways: McGiffert reports Harnack (converted about 150, went east, wrote the Address there, came back to Rome, broke about 172) against Zahn (at Rome once, became a heretic in the East) (EU-ed 26224–26234). **Contested.**

E8. **The Diatessaron: what it is.** Eusebius: "their original founder, Tatian, formed a certain combination and collection of the Gospels, I know not how, to which he gave the title Diatessaron, and which is still in the hands of some" (EU 26424–26479). LATER, about 150 years. Documented. McGiffert: "Eusebius clearly means to imply in these words that he was not acquainted with the Diatessaron" (EU-ed 26427–26429); Lightfoot read them as disapproval (26429–26431): editors. The name: "Diatessaron, i.e., The Fourfold, a compilation from the four Gospels" (DI 10422–10424, the Arabic subscription). Hogg: "a word meaning 'fourfold'" (DI-ed 2110). It began "In the beginning was the Word" by Bar Salibi's report (DI-ed 1770–1771, 1838–1840; LATE, via Hogg); the Arabic text in its present form also opens there (DI 2195–2196). Hogg's count from the Arabic: of 3780 verses in the four Gospels, "the Diatessaron quotes 2769 and omits 1011" (DI-ed 1981–1983; G. F. Moore's figures). It was divided "into fifty-four almost equal chapters ... a feature that agrees well with what we have learned of the work as being of old the lectionary of the Syrian church" (DI-ed 1988–1991): Hogg's inference, Dominant Modern Reconstruction. Words: "harmony" = the four Gospels woven into one continuous story; "lectionary" = the book read aloud in worship.
- E8a. **Language and date of making.** Hogg: "It was probably here [Mesopotamia] that he issued in Syriac his most important work" (DI-ed 1961–1963); "the view favoured by an increasing majority of scholars, that it was written in Syriac" (DI-ed 1995–1997). McGiffert: Zahn held Syriac, Harnack Greek, with "very strong reasons" (EU-ed 26460–26464); "Both Zahn and Harnack agree, as do most other scholars, that the work was written before Tatian became a heretic, and with no heretical intent" (EU-ed 26464–26466). **Contested (language, and whether before or after the break). Do not say which.**
- E8b. **Theodoret's charge of cutting.** Theodoret (453): "This [writer] also composed the gospel which is called Diatessaron, cutting out the genealogies and whatever other passages show that the Lord was born of the seed of David according to the flesh" (DI-ed 1790–1794; Greek TH 14973–14976). Hogg: the claim about the genealogies agrees with the Ephrem source, but the wider claim "conflicts with all three texts" that survive (DI-ed 1845–1851). **Contested; told only as Theodoret's charge, if at all.**

## 3. What came of him (heaviest first)

I1. **The Diatessaron as the Syriac churches' gospel.** DEFENDED: the four Gospels told as one.
- Aphrahat, writing in Persia in the 340s: "as is written in the beginning of the Gospel of our Saviour:—In the beginning was the Word" (AP 24764–24766). OWN (Aphrahat). Parisot reads this as the Diatessaron's opening and finds Aphrahat's Gospel order follows Tatian's harmony, while Aphrahat sometimes cites the separate Gospels too (AP-P 2253–2281). Editor's view, Dominant Modern Reconstruction.
- Ephrem: "upon this gospel Mar Ephraem commented" (Isho'dad of Merv, c. 852, quoted at DI-ed 1764–1768; Bar Salibi repeats it, 1770). The Chronicle of Seert, an anonymous Nestorian history written after the ninth century (Scher, PO 4 file 12095–12096, 12167), lists among Ephrem's works "un Commentaire de l'Évangile appelé Diatessaron, lequel est formé du mélange des quatre Évangiles et avait été composé par le grec Tatien" (`barhadbeshabba_cause-de-la-fondation-des-ecoles-po4-syr-fra_scher1908.txt` 15906–15907; the file holds all of PO 4, and this passage is the Chronicle, not Barhadbeshabba), and says again "Il expliqua le Diatessaron" (`chronicle-of-seert_histoire-nestorienne-premiere-partie-fasc2-patrologia-orientalis-5-ara-fra_scher-dib1910.txt` 15751). LATE, read directly. Its "le grec Tatien" is not to be used: Tatian says he was born in the land of the Assyrians (S2). Ephrem's Syriac commentary is lost; an Armenian version survives in manuscripts of 1195 (DI-ed 1799–1806); AP-ed: Ephrem treated the Gospels "not in their separate form, but in the continuous narrative known as the 'Diatessaron'" (AP 12592–12595). Mitchell: Ephrem "habitually used the Diatessaron, not the four separate Gospels" (MI 2630). That Ephrem commented on it: Widely Accepted (late witnesses plus a surviving Armenian version). **Ephrem's commentary itself is not held.**
- Edessa: the *Doctrine of Addai* has crowds come daily "to the reading of the Old and New Testament, of the Diatessaron" (AD 65). Phillips: Cureton's manuscript read "Ditornon" and was "not quite clear"; the St Petersburg manuscript confirms "Diatessaron" (AD-ed 219). Hogg dates the work "third to fourth century" (DI-ed 1924). **Sibling `justin-martyr` already tells this line ("the people came to hear it read"); here at most a clause, no quote.**
- Hogg's summary: "at a quite early stage the Diatessaron was very widely if not universally read in the Syriac churches, and commented on by scholars as the gospel ... Outside of the Syriac speaking churches we find no signs of any such use" (DI-ed 1927–1931). Editor's judgment, Widely Accepted. The tradition entry: read "for as long as this Christian tradition lasted" (CEN tradition). **The sources hold "very widely if not universally", not "the only gospel".**

I2. **Its removal: two bishops in the fifth century.** Force told plainly.
- Rabbula, bishop of Edessa (d. 435): "Let the presbyters and deacons give heed that in all the churches there be provided and read a copy of the Distinct Gospel" (DI-ed 1914–1919, quoting Overbeck; Overbeck's Syriac is **not held**). Hogg: "the harmonized or mixed gospel" was what it replaced (1919–1920). Documented as quoted by Hogg.
- Theodoret, bishop of Cyrrhus (453), in Greek (TH 14976–14984): "Ἐχρήσαντο δὲ τούτῳ οὐ μόνοι οἱ τῆς ἐκείνου συμμορίας, ἀλλὰ καὶ οἱ τοῖς ἀποστολικοῖς ἑπόμενοι δόγμασι, τὴν τῆς συνθήκης κακουργίαν οὐκ ἐγνωκότες, ἀλλὰ ἁπλούστερον ὡς συντόμῳ τῷ βιβλίῳ χρησάμενοι. Εὗρον δὲ κἀγὼ πλείους ἢ διακοσίας βίβλους τοιαύτας ἐν ταῖς παρ᾽ ἡμῖν ἐκκλησίαις τετιμημένας, καὶ πάσας συναγαγὼν ἀπεθέμην, καὶ τὰ τῶν τεσσάρων εὐαγγελιστῶν ἀντεισήγαγον εὐαγγέλια." Maran's Latin, same lines: "Nactus sum etiam ipse libros huiusmodi supra ducentos in honore habitos in ecclesiis nostris, quos omnes in unum congestos seposui, et pro his quatuor evangelistarum evangelia introduxi" (TH 14994–14997). Hogg's English of part: "found more than two hundred copies of the work 'held in respect in the churches'" (DI-ed 1922–1923). LATER (about 280 years), OWN words of Theodoret. Documented. Census name: "Theodoret of Cyrrhus" (CEN).
- Hogg: these leaders "were trying to suppress traditional practice"; "it could not be suppressed"; new Syriac copies were made in the 800s, an Arabic version in the early 1000s, and Armenian copies to the end of the 1100s (DI-ed 1920–1941). Editor's judgment.

I3. **It survives in translation.** "No copy of a Syriac Diatessaron has yet been shown to have survived" (DI-ed 1731–1733; Hogg, 1896). What survives: the Arabic of Ibn at-Tayyib, from a Syriac copy of about 900 (DI-ed 1715–1722; DI 10422–10435), a Latin form in the Codex Fuldensis of Victor of Capua (d. 554) (DI-ed 1822–1828), and Ephrem's commentary in Armenian (I1). 'Abdisho (d. 1318): "of his own he did not add a single saying" (DI-ed 1904–1908). Documented as Hogg reports, for his time. **The writer must not state the present-day state of the manuscripts; Hogg's is a 1896 report.**

I4. **The Address read for its dates.** Clement: the age of Moses "has been discussed with accuracy by Tatian in his book To the Greeks" (CL 29726–29727; also CEN edge `greek-apologists-second-century--alexandria-catechetical`). Eusebius: "Of these the one most in use among many persons is his celebrated Address to the Greeks, which also appears to be the best and most useful of all his works. In it he deals with the most ancient times, and shows that Moses and the Hebrew prophets were older than all the celebrated men among the Greeks" (EU 26484–26505). Jerome: "a most successful book Against the nations, is extant, and this is considered the most significant of all his works" (JE 40095–40098). McGiffert: "It was largely on account of these chapters on the antiquity of Moses that Tatian's Oratio was held in such high esteem, while his other works disappeared" (EU-ed 26513–26515): editor, Dominant Modern Reconstruction. Jerome: "innumerable volumes" (JE 40095–40096); Eusebius: "a great many writings" (EU 26484). Only the Address survives whole (AG-ed 5660). Documented.

I5. **Remembered as a heretic.** Irenaeus (E6); Hippolytus; Eusebius: "It is said that Tatian was the author of this false doctrine" of the Encratites (EU 26185–26186); Jerome, "Tatian the heresiarch" (JE 40084). The name: "Encratites (self-controlled)" (IR 33656–33657). McGiffert: Irenaeus says they "sprung from Marcion and Saturninus, evidently implying that they were not founded by Tatian, but that he found them already in existence"; and "the word was used very commonly in a wider sense" for anyone teaching extreme abstinence (EU-ed 26302–26313). Hogg: "Among the Greek scholars, however, he became more and more regarded as a heretic, Encratite (ascetic), and Gnostic" (DI-ed 1964–1965). **That he founded the Encratites: Contested (Eusebius and Jerome say so; Irenaeus's own text implies otherwise, per McGiffert).**

## 5. Where sources disagree or come late

D1. **Greek or Syrian by birth** (S2): Contested. Use his own words.
D2. **When and where the Address was written** (E5): Contested; no date, no place.
D3. **The shape of his life**: one stay at Rome or two; heretic at Rome or in the East (E7): Contested. Hold to what all sources share: Justin's hearer at Rome; broke with the church after Justin's death (Irenaeus); about 172 (Eusebius's *Chronicle*); went east (Epiphanius).
D4. **Founder of the Encratites** (I5): Contested.
D5. **The Diatessaron's language, and whether made before or after the break** (E8a): Contested.
D6. **What it left out** (E8b): Theodoret's charge, partly contradicted by the surviving texts (Hogg).
D7. **"I know not how"** (E8): whether Eusebius had seen it (McGiffert) or disapproved (Lightfoot): Contested; leave out.
D8. **Crescens and Justin's death** (E4): Eusebius's reading is not what the text says.
D9. **The editors' sympathy.** Coxe's preface calls Tatian "half Father and half heretic" and blames his fall on "infirmity of mind and body" (AG-ed 5530–5533); Ryland says he "unfortunately fell under the influence of the Gnostic heresy" and excluded passages on Christ's humanity (AG-ed 5649–5667). These are editors' views and the second is not supported by the surviving text (D6). Not for the story.
