"We keep the day exactly, neither adding nor taking away. For in Asia, too, great lights have fallen asleep." He meant the saints who had died there. Near the end of the second century, Polycrates, bishop of Ephesus, wrote this to Victor, bishop of Rome. The day he meant was Easter, the feast of Christ's death and rising.

Polycrates led the bishops of the Roman province of Asia, in today's Turkey, in keeping Easter on the day of the Jewish Passover, whatever day of the week it fell. The other churches whose letters Eusebius saw kept it only on a Sunday. When Victor pressed for one custom and Polycrates refused, Victor tried to cut the churches of Asia off from communion.

Asia ended the fast before Easter on the fourteenth day of the Jewish month, counted by the moon, when the Jews sacrificed the Passover lamb. The others waited for Sunday, the day of the resurrection, and each side claimed the apostles. At Victor's wish, bishops met from Palestine to Gaul and decided for Sunday, while the bishops Polycrates gathered held to the old custom.

He wrote for them all. His great lights were Philip, whom he calls one of the twelve apostles, and John, who leaned on the Lord's breast, and Polycarp of Smyrna, who died for the faith. All of them, he wrote, had kept the fourteenth day, and so had his own family: seven of his relatives were bishops, and he was the eighth. Then he answered Victor: "So, brothers, I have lived sixty-five years in the Lord. I have met the brothers all over the world, and I have gone through all of holy Scripture. I am not frightened by what is said to terrify us. For those greater than I have said, 'We must obey God rather than human beings.'"

The bishops of Asia agreed to his letter. Then, Eusebius writes, Victor at once tried to cut off the churches of all Asia as straying from the true faith. He wrote declaring them out of communion, refusing to share the Lord's Supper with them or count them as one church with Rome. Some bishops rebuked him sharply, and Irenaeus of Lyon, who held that Easter belonged on Sunday, urged him not to cut off whole churches for an old custom. Whether the break took hold, historians disagree, and no source says how it ended.

The letter survives only because Eusebius copied it more than a century later, and the whole story rests on him. He first quoted it to show where John was buried, and later writers read it the same way. The custom lost. At Rome a generation later, Hippolytus put its keepers in his book against heresies, and in 325 the Council of Nicaea set one day for Easter everywhere. Later councils did what Victor had tried, and cast out any bishop who kept Easter with the Jews.

The great lights of Asia, Polycrates wrote, lie asleep, waiting for the day the Lord comes with glory from heaven. Of his own death and grave, no early writer says anything.
