# Outline: Polycrates of Ephesus

Sheet items (S, E, L, I, D) are in `Source_Sheet.md`, which gives the file and line for each. EUS is Eusebius's *Church History* in McGiffert's English; ANF8 the Ante-Nicene Fathers English of the two fragments; PL23 Jerome's Latin with the Greek columns; JER Jerome in English. Eusebius's Greek of Books I–V is not held as a clean text: only key Greek words are (sheet, Language).

- **The essence:** Near the end of the second century, Polycrates, bishop of Ephesus, led the bishops of the Roman province of Asia in keeping Easter on the fourteenth day of the moon, the day of the Jewish Passover, whatever day of the week it fell; the other churches whose letters Eusebius saw kept it only on a Sunday (S1, S2, S4, E1, E3; Documented, Eusebius and the letter). When Victor of Rome pressed for one custom, Polycrates answered in a letter naming the apostles and martyrs buried in Asia who had kept that day, and Victor tried to cut all the churches of Asia off from communion (L1–L5, E5; Documented; whether the break took hold, Contested, D1).
- **Also told:**
  - **Two customs, both claimed from the apostles.** Asia ended its fast before Easter on the fourteenth, the day the Jews sacrificed the lamb; the others ended it only on the day of the resurrection (S4; Documented). Asia's churches claimed John; the others, Eusebius says, "apostolic tradition" (S4, E3; Documented as their claims).
  - **The synods.** At Victor's wish, bishops met in many places. Palestine, Pontus, Gaul, Osrhoene and Corinth, and Rome itself, decided for Sunday; Asia's synod alone held to the old custom (E1–E3; Documented, Eusebius saw the letters).
  - **The letter.** We keep the day exactly; the great dead of Asia kept it: Philip, whom he calls one of the twelve apostles, and his daughters; John, who leaned on the Lord's breast; Polycarp of Smyrna; other bishops and martyrs. Seven of his relatives were bishops, and he the eighth. After sixty-five years in the Lord he is not frightened: "We must obey God rather than human beings." The bishops he gathered agreed to it (L1–L6, E4; Documented, his own words).
  - **Victor's answer.** Victor tried to cut off the churches of Asia as straying from the faith, and wrote declaring them all out of communion. Other bishops sharply rebuked him; Irenaeus urged him not to cut off whole churches for an old custom (E5, E6; Documented). Whether the break took hold, historians disagree (D1; Contested). How it ended, no source says (E8).
  - **What came of him.** The letter survives only because Eusebius copied it, and he copied it first to show where John was buried; later writers used it the same way (I1; Documented). The custom lost: Hippolytus at Rome put its keepers in his book against heresies, Nicaea in 325 set one day for Easter everywhere, and later councils cast out those who kept it with the Jews (I2; Documented). Eusebius and Jerome still praised him (I3; Documented).
- **Length:** about 2 minutes 45 seconds (about 410 words). Why: the record is one letter, in two fragments, and Eusebius's account around it; nothing is known of his life before or after, so the story is the quarrel alone. It needs room for the two customs (a listener cannot follow the quarrel without them), the letter's argument in his own words, Victor's act told with its force, and a third on what came of it. Cut first, if needed: Quote 3 (tell the consent in one clause), the Palestinian line on Alexandria (E7), and What came 3.
- **Setting:**
  - Time: near the end of the second century, in the 190s, while Victor was bishop of Rome (S2). Plain words: "near the end of the second century". No single year (D5).
  - Place: Ephesus, in the Roman province of Asia, in today's Turkey (S1; CEN tradition entry). Hierapolis and Smyrna, cities of the same province, come in only inside his list.
  - Tradition: the churches of the generations after the apostles; the tree's name is "The Scattered Households" (S3). Do not say "post-apostolic".
  - Language: he wrote in Greek; the letter survives only as Eusebius quotes it (sheet, Language).

**Words to explain** (the first time each is heard; plain word or short gloss, worked into the sentence)

- **Asia**: "the Roman province of Asia, in today's Turkey". The modern sense misleads.
- **Easter / Passover**: "Easter, the feast of Christ's death and rising"; "the Jewish Passover". Say once that the two fell close together because Christ died at Passover time, only if the outline's sources are kept: Eusebius calls Easter "the feast of the Saviour's passover" (S4). Do not add a theology of the date.
- **the fourteenth day of the moon**: "the fourteenth day of the Jewish month, counted by the moon, the day the Jews sacrificed the Passover lamb" (S4). "Whatever day of the week it fell."
- **the Lord's day**: "Sunday, the day of the resurrection" (E1).
- **the fast**: "the fast before Easter" (S4). Asia ended it on the fourteenth; the others on Sunday.
- **synod**: "a meeting of bishops". Do not use the word bare.
- **communion / cut off / excommunicate**: "to cut them off from communion, refusing to share the Lord's Supper or count them as one church with Rome" (E5). Never "excommunicate" without that plain sense.
- **"fallen asleep"** (Quote 1): the narrator gives its plain sense, "died", alongside.
- **"great lights"** (Quote 1): the great saints of Asia; the narrator says plainly that he means the dead he then names.
- **"the day when the people put away the leaven"** (L4), if used: "the day the Jews cleared out the yeast for Passover". Better told in paraphrase: "on the day of the Jewish Passover".
- **martyr**: "who died for the faith", the first time.
- **heterodox** (Eusebius's word for Victor's charge): "as straying from the true faith". Do not use the word.
- **Pontus, Osrhoene**: give by region if named at all: 'from Palestine and Mesopotamia to Gaul'; Osrhoene was 'a region of country in northwestern Mesopotamia' (EUS-ed, EUS 31289).
- **Quartodeciman**: do not use. It is a later label, from the Latin for "fourteenth", carried back onto them (I2, EUS-ed 31190–31194).

- **Shape:** Founder or leader. What he led: the bishops of Asia, in a synod he called and a letter written for them all. How it ran: by custom handed down through graves and families, his own among them. Who it served: the churches of Asia, whose way of keeping Easter he defended. The impact is in what outlasted him: not the custom, which lost, but the letter, which kept Asia's memory of its apostles' graves. The trap is titles in place of acts; here the acts are the synod, the letter, and the refusal.
- **Sibling:**
  - `polycarp-of-smyrna` (approved text, read; job: the old bishop's martyrdom, his visit to Anicetus at Rome, his letter to Philippi). It already tells, in four sentences, that Polycarp and Anicetus could not agree about keeping Easter, that Victor later tried to cut off the churches of Asia, and that Irenaeus reminded him that Polycarp and Anicetus had kept communion. Here: the quarrel is told from Asia's side, through Polycrates's own letter. Anicetus is not told. Polycarp appears only as a name in Polycrates's list of the great dead. Irenaeus's letter is one clause, without Anicetus. None of its quotes is reused, including "Even so, they shared communion".
  - `irenaeus-of-lyon` (batch 6 outline, read; job: Lyon and *Against Heresies*). There Polycrates is one clause, not by name, and the Victor letter one clause, with Eusebius's "well named, a peacemaker". Here Irenaeus is one clause, by name, and the "peacemaker" line is not used (it stays with Irenaeus).
  - `ignatius-of-antioch`: no held source ties the two. Not read; not told.
  - `hippolytus-of-rome` (researched after this): here one clause, his listing the keepers of the fourteenth in his book against heresies (I2). Its job is Callistus and the *Refutation*.
  - `council-of-nicaea-325` (live, read for Easter): it already says Easter "would fall on one day everywhere". Here one clause; no quote from it.
- **Openings:**
  1. **Chosen.** His own words (Quote 1; L1, L2). In about three sentences: "We keep the day exactly…", to "fallen asleep", with the plain sense, "died". Then the turn, with the name: the bishop of Ephesus, Polycrates, wrote this to Victor, the bishop of Rome, near the end of the second century, and the day he meant was Easter. Then the essence.
  2. Not chosen: Eusebius's line that Victor "immediately attempted to cut off from the common unity the parishes of all Asia" (E5). Strong, but it is the story's climax and belongs in time order at beat 5.

**Story beats** (the opening, the essence and beat 1 are the first minute, about 150 words; beats 2 to 5 deepen it)

1. **Two customs.** The churches of Asia ended their fast before Easter on the fourteenth day of the moon, the day the Jews sacrificed the Passover lamb, whatever day of the week it fell. The other churches ended it only on the day of the resurrection, Sunday (S4; Documented, Eusebius). Eusebius calls Asia's custom "an older tradition" and the other the practice "from apostolic tradition" (S4): each side claimed the apostles. Polycrates's own words for his day: the day the Jewish people put away the leaven, that is, Passover (L4). Do not explain which is older or right.
2. **Victor's call, and the synods.** At Victor's wish, bishops met in many places (E2; "whom I summoned at your desire", Polycrates's words). Those in Palestine, Pontus, Gaul, Osrhoene and Corinth, and at Rome, decided that the resurrection should be kept only on Sunday, and wrote letters saying so (E1; Documented, Eusebius saw them). Optional: the bishops in Palestine wrote that Alexandria kept the same day as they did (E7). Asia's bishops, gathered by Polycrates, held to the old custom, and he wrote for them all (E3); their agreement to the letter is told once, in beat 5.
3. **The letter: the graves.** He wrote to Victor and the church of Rome for all of them (E3). Quote 1 has opened the story; here, in paraphrase, the list it begins: Philip, whom he calls one of the twelve apostles, buried at Hierapolis, with two daughters who grew old unmarried and a third buried at Ephesus; John, who leaned on the Lord's breast, buried at Ephesus; Polycarp, bishop and martyr, at Smyrna; and other bishops and martyrs in the cities of Asia. "All these", he wrote, kept the fourteenth day "according to the Gospel" (L2, L3; Documented as his words). The point the listener must get: for Asia, the apostles who had lived and died among them were the proof of what the apostles did. Credit Philip as "whom he calls" (D2). Name at most Philip, John and Polycarp; give the rest by role.
4. **The letter: his family, and his refusal.** He followed the custom of his relatives: seven of them had been bishops, he says, and he was the eighth (L4; Documented; which churches they led is not known, D4). Then Quote 2 (L5). The "frightening" words he refuses are Victor's (L5; that Victor's letters threatened is read from this line, E2; tell it as Polycrates's answer, not as a quoted threat).
5. **The bishops' consent, and Victor's act.** The bishops he had called, too many, he says, to name, agreed to the letter (E4). Quote 3, optional (cut first). Then Victor at once tried to cut off the churches of all Asia, and those that agreed with them, as straying from the true faith, and wrote declaring them all out of communion (E5; Documented, Eusebius). This did not please all the bishops: they urged him toward peace, and some rebuked him sharply in letters Eusebius still had (E6). One clause: Irenaeus of Lyon, who held that Easter belonged on Sunday, wrote that he should not cut off whole churches for keeping an old custom (E6). Whether Victor's break took hold, historians have disagreed; how and when it ended, no source says, and nothing is known of Polycrates after the letter (D1, E8).

**What came of him** (about a third of the story; heaviest first)

1. **The letter kept Asia's graves.** It survives only because Eusebius, more than a century later, copied it; and the first time he quotes it, in his third book, it is not for Easter but to show where John was buried (I1; Documented). Later writers used it the same way, in Latin, English and Greek: a Latin writer of the twelfth century (Abelard), an Oxford scholar in 1680 answering those who doubted that John had died at Ephesus, and a Greek Orthodox book of saints' lives, in an edition of 1900 (SYNAX), which cites it to show "that John truly died" (I1; Documented). The listener must hear that the letter outlived the custom it defended, as a witness to the graves of the apostles in Asia. Do not settle which Philip (D2). (Abelard and Thomas Smith by role, not name.)
2. **The custom lost.** At Rome, a generation later, Hippolytus put those who kept the fourteenth into his *Refutation of All Heresies*, as quarrelsome by nature and ill-informed, though, he granted, they accepted all else the apostles handed down (I2; Documented, his view). In 325 the Council of Nicaea set one day for Easter everywhere. By then, Constantine's own letter from the council counts Asia among the provinces already keeping the common day (EUS 64680–64681; Documented). When and how Asia changed, no source says (E8). Constantine's own letter from the council urged the churches to have nothing in common with the Jews, in words of open contempt (I2; Documented). If told, say it plainly and credit Constantine; do not soften it or leave out its hostility once it is mentioned. A council at Antioch in 341 cast out any bishop who kept Easter with the Jews, and another, at Laodicea, counted the keepers of the fourteenth among heresies (I2; Documented). The listener must hear that what Victor tried, later councils did.
3. **Praised all the same.** Eusebius counted him among those whose sound faith had come down in writing, and Jerome, two centuries on, copied the letter, he said, to show the man's genius and authority (I3; Documented; paraphrase, not quoted). Optional.

**Quotes** (2, with a third optional)

1. EUS 31324–31328 (*Church History* V.24.2): "We observe the exact day; neither adding, nor taking away. (Greek words held: ἀῤῥαδιούργητον ἄγομεν τὴν ἡμέραν, ANF8 72579–72581, PL23 55043; στοιχεῖα, ANF8 72583. Jerome's Latin, PL23 55119–55124.) Rendering: "We keep the day exactly, neither adding nor taking away. For in Asia, too, great lights have fallen asleep. They will rise again on the day the Lord comes, when he comes with glory from heaven and seeks out all the saints." The writer may stop after "fallen asleep" and give the plain sense, "died", and the rest in paraphrase. Job: the opening. Asia's whole argument in his own image: the day is kept as received, and the proof lies in Asia's graves.
2. EUS 31396–31400 (V.24.7): "I, therefore, brethren, who have lived sixty-five years in the Lord, and have met with the brethren throughout the world, and have gone through every Holy Scripture, am not affrighted by terrifying words. EUS 31410–31413 (V.24.8): "And they, beholding my littleness, gave their consent to the letter, knowing that I did not bear my gray hairs in vain, but had always governed my life by the Lord Jesus." (ANF8 72628–72632; Greek τὸν μικρόν, ANF8 72628–72629.) Rendering: "Seeing how lowly I am, they agreed to the letter. They knew I had not worn my gray hair for nothing, but had always lived my life by the Lord Jesus." Job: beat 5. **Connection to carry into the story:** what passed from Polycrates was Asia's memory of its apostles, set down in a letter written to defend a custom that then lost. Two things must be clear to the listener. First, what the quarrel was about, and why graves answered it. Every church claimed to keep Easter as the apostles had; Asia's proof was that the apostles and martyrs who kept the fourteenth day had lived and died among them, and that his own family had kept it for seven bishops before him (L2–L4). Victor's answer was to make Rome's custom the condition of communion, and he tried to cut off a whole province that would not follow it (E5). Second, what outlasted it. The custom did not survive: within a generation a writer at Rome listed it in a book against heresies; by the time Nicaea set one day for all, Asia itself had come to keep the common day; and later councils cast out those who still kept the fourteenth, so that what Victor tried, the later church did (I2). The letter did survive, only because Eusebius copied it, and he and later writers read it as the oldest witness that John was buried at Ephesus (I1). Tell it through these acts: the synod, the letter, Victor's letters, the copying and the councils. Not as a "this matters because" sentence.

**Ending:** close on the person. Either (a) the opening image seen again, in paraphrase: the great lights of Asia, he wrote, lie asleep in their cities, waiting for the day the Lord comes with glory from heaven; of Polycrates's own death and grave, no early writer says anything (L2, E8); or (b) his own measure of himself, if Quote 3 was not used in beat 5: the bishops agreed to his letter, he wrote, because they knew he had not worn his gray hair for nothing (E4; paraphrase only, as the event is earlier: use it as the last image of the man, not as a new event). Recommended: (a), placed after What came, so the last thing heard is his own image and the silence after him.

**Who tells it**

- **Polycrates himself**, in the two fragments Eusebius quotes. The list of graves, the family, the sixty-five years and the refusal are his. Credit "he wrote" where he speaks; Philip as an apostle is his claim (D2).
- **Eusebius**, writing early in the fourth century, about 130 years later. The whole story rests on him: the customs, the other synods, Victor's act, and the rebukes. Say so once, inside a sentence about Eusebius, where What came 1 tells that the letter survives only because he copied it. Credit him beside Victor's act (E5), where the force of the record sits.
- **Irenaeus**, a contemporary, as Eusebius quotes him (E6): one clause.
- **Hippolytus**, at Rome a generation later: credit his judgment to him (I2). In the ANF English the word "(heretics)" is the translator's supplement; the chapter stands in his book against heresies, so say that.
- **Constantine, the Nicene bishops, and the councils of Antioch and Laodicea**, in the fourth century: their own letters and canons (I2).
- **Historians**, on whether Victor's break took hold (D1). The narrator does not settle it.

- Not "Asia celebrated Christ's death, Rome his resurrection", or any theology of the two customs: that is McGiffert's and Schaff's gloss (S4). Say what Eusebius says: when each ended the fast.
- Not "the rest of the world kept Sunday" as fact. Say "the other churches whose letters Eusebius saw" (D6).
- Not "the Quartodecimans" for Polycrates's churches (Words to explain).

- Not "Victor demanded" or "Victor threatened" as a quoted fact. Say Victor asked for the synods (his words, "at your desire"), and that Polycrates answered that he was not frightened by threats (E2, L5).
- Not "Philip the apostle was buried at Hierapolis" in the narrator's voice. Say "Philip, whom he calls one of the twelve apostles" (D2).
