In 335, at a council in Tyre, Athanasius's accusers held up a severed hand. They said he had murdered a bishop named Arsenius and used the hand for magic. Socrates, who tells it with plain sympathy, says Arsenius was found alive and led in with his hands under his cloak, and Athanasius showed both. Sozomen adds a rumor that Athanasius had hidden the man himself. Either way, the council deposed him in his absence.

He had been a young deacon at Nicaea in 325, where Socrates says he spoke strongly against the party of Arius, and three years later, at about thirty, he became bishop of Alexandria. Athanasius quotes Arius's poem, which said God was not always a Father and that the Son was made out of nothing. Nicaea answered that the Son is "begotten not made, One in essence with the Father."

If the Son were only a creature, Athanasius argued, then becoming man would have left humanity just as it was, not joined to God. In his own words: "For He was made man that we might be made God."

But the price was high. Constantine sent him to Trier, Socrates says, on a charge of threatening the grain ships. Socrates gives another account too: some affirm the emperor did it for the unity of the church, since Athanasius refused any communion with Arius and his followers. Four more exiles followed. In one, the emperor's agents searched towns, monasteries and tombs for him, and no one gave him up. From hiding he wrote more than half of his surviving works. He came home for good in 366, named Peter as his successor, and died in May of 373.

Years later, Gregory of Nazianzus began a sermon: "In praising Athanasius, I shall be praising virtue." Sixty years after his death, as Cyril of Alexandria made peace with the bishops of the East, he wrote that he followed, especially, "our blessed and all-celebrated Father Athanasius."
