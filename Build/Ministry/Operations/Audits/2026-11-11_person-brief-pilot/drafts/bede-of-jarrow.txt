In the year 664, at the monastery of Whitby, King Oswy gathered the clergy of Northumbria to settle the day for keeping Easter. Bishop Colman said his Easter came from his elders and was the one John the Evangelist kept. Wilfrid answered for Rome and for Peter. The abbess Hilda stood with Colman. Then Oswy asked whether the keys of heaven were given to Peter, and both men said yes. The king would not oppose the doorkeeper, he said, "lest, when I come to the gates of the kingdom of heaven, there should be none to open them."

That scene comes to us from Bede, who was born some years later on the land of the monastery at Wearmouth and Jarrow. At seven he was given to Abbot Benedict to be raised there, and he spent the rest of his life in that house, studying Scripture. In his own words, "I always took delight in learning, teaching, and writing." He died on Ascension Day in 735, just after finishing a translation of the Gospel of John, and his pupil Cuthbert wrote an account of his last hours.

When King Ceolwulph asked to read his History of the English church, Bede sent it, naming his helpers; one of them searched the archives in Rome for letters of Pope Gregory. He calls himself an impartial historian, and he writes of Bishop Aidan with love, yet he says plainly that he detests Aidan's Easter.

About the year 800, Alcuin, writing against the bishop Felix, turned twice to blessed Bede the priest for his reading of the Acts of the Apostles. A Roman Missal printed in English in 1843 prays to God, who enlightened his church "by the learning of blessed Bede," that his servants may always be enlightened by his wisdom.
