Hugo Grotius, a law officer of Holland, was in England on business for the East India Company. The Remonstrants, the Arminian side in the Dutch quarrel over grace and predestination, chose him to plead their cause there. Le Vassor says English divines received him well, all but the Archbishop of Canterbury. King James told him in several audiences that the views of Calvin and Beza were very harsh, and that on their reasoning God would be the author of sin. Yet the king wanted proof that the Remonstrants had no leaning toward Socinianism.

Faustus Socinus said God can forgive sin as a creditor forgives a debt, with no payment. In 1617, at Leiden, Grotius answered him. To punish or to pardon, he argued, belongs to a ruler as ruler, not to a wronged party, and God acts here as a ruler. So Christ's death was a real penalty paid for believers, and it showed how God hates sin. God had good reasons, Grotius writes, "that sin should not be thought of less importance, if so many great sins should be remitted without an example."

Then Prince Maurice's party won the struggle for power, and the men who had led the government were thrown into prison. Barneveldt, grown grey in his country's service, died on the scaffold, and Grotius was condemned to prison for life, though Mosheim says he does not know on what charge. Grotius escaped when his wife sent him out in a chest that usually carried books.

The leading Dutch Remonstrants rejected his argument about the atonement, while English Arminians largely took it up. It came to be called the governmental theory, and it reached New England through Samuel Hopkins.
