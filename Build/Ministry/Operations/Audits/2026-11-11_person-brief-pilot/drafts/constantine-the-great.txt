Marching on Rome against his rival Maxentius, Constantine prayed, Eusebius says, that God would show him who he was. Constantine told Eusebius the rest himself, years later and under oath. At midday a cross of light stood above the sun, with the words "Conquer by this," and that night Christ came to him in sleep and told him to make a likeness of the sign. Eusebius admits the story would be hard to believe from anyone else. Lactantius knows only a dream, telling Constantine to mark the sign of Christ on his soldiers' shields.

Maxentius was driven into the Tiber at the broken bridge, Lactantius says. Soon Constantine and Licinius met at Milan and decreed that Christians and all others should be free to follow the religion each thought best.

At Alexandria the presbyter Arius argued that if the Father begat the Son, there was a time when the Son was not. Constantine wrote to Arius and his bishop that the quarrel was trifling and should stay in private thought. Then he called the bishops to Nicaea and sat among them, "your fellow-servant," as he wrote. Eusebius, the bishop of Caesarea, told his church that the emperor urged one added word, homoousios, "of the same essence," and explained that the Son did not come from the Father by division or cutting off.

Only when he knew he was dying, Eusebius says, did he seek baptism. He had hoped to receive it in the Jordan. Near Nicomedia he told the bishops, "The time is arrived which I have long hoped for, with an earnest desire and prayer that I might obtain the salvation of God." His body was carried to Constantinople and laid in the Church of the Apostles. Sozomen says later Christian emperors and bishops were buried there too.
