Around the year two hundred, in Alexandria, Origen's father was arrested for his faith. Origen was a boy, and the church historian Eusebius says he was seized by such a longing for martyrdom that he rushed toward danger. His mother hid his clothes to keep him home, so he wrote to his father in prison. Eusebius kept one sentence of that letter: "Take heed not to change your mind on our account."

His father died a martyr, and the state took the family's property. Origen was not yet seventeen. In his eighteenth year he took charge of teaching the faith in Alexandria, because everyone else had fled. Jerome says he was so devoted to the Scriptures that he learned Hebrew, against the habit of his time and his people. He then set the Hebrew text beside the Greek translations, in a work called the Hexapla.

He taught that Scripture, like a person, has body, soul and spirit. In On First Principles he says the plain story builds up simple believers, its soul those who have advanced, and its spiritual law the perfect. The book also taught that the Son's generation is as eternal as brightness from the sun, and that souls existed before birth. We read that book mostly in Rufinus's Latin, and Jerome said Rufinus changed it. Origen also wrote eight books in answer to Celsus, who had attacked Christians in a book called the True Discourse.

But on a journey through Palestine, the bishops of Caesarea and Jerusalem ordained Origen a priest. Jerome says this so enraged his own bishop, Demetrius, that he wrote everywhere to ruin Origen's name. In the year 231 Origen left Alexandria for Caesarea. He later wrote that he had come through a storm and been brought out of Egypt. About twenty years later, under the emperor Decius, he was stretched in the stocks. Jerome says he died at Tyre, aged sixty-nine.

Gregory of Nazianzus later sent a friend a book of passages from Origen, which he and Basil of Caesarea are thought to have gathered. The church historian Socrates, who defended Origen, says Athanasius often cited him in support of his own faith. Three centuries after his death, a council at Constantinople named him among condemned heretics, though how far it went is still argued.
