# Source sheet: Bardaisan of Edessa

Census id `bardaisan-of-edessa` ("Bardaisan of Edessa"). Told within **Bardaisan and the Bardaisanites** (short name "Bardaisanites"; informal name "The School of Bardaisan"). The city's own church is **Syriac Christianity (Edessa/Nisibis)** (short name "Syriac Edessa"). The card (`Card.existing.json`) was used as a starting list only.

Files, by short name (all under `cic/texts/`):

- **BLC** = `anf08_twelve-patriarchs-clementina-apocrypha-edessa-syriac.xml`: *The Book of the Laws of Countries*, B. P. Pratten's English (ANF 8) from Cureton's Syriac, with Pratten's and Coxe's notes. The Syriac is not held; Eusebius's Greek extract (*Preparation for the Gospel* VI.10) is not held. ## Setting

**S1. Born at Edessa, beside the Daisan.** CHE 49: "The year 465, in the month Tammuz, on the eleventh day (i.e., July 11th, 154 A.D.), Bardesanes was born." LATER (compiled about 540, from the city archives, CHE 32). EU 26584–26589 (McGiffert, citing Hort): by the *Chronicle of Edessa* "he was born July 11, 155". EDITOR. The year is 154 by Cowper's reckoning, 155 by Hort's: say "about 155", or "the 150s". The name: BLC 67892–67894 (editor): "Lit. 'Son of Daisan,' from a river so called near Edessa.—Hahn." EPH-G 11186–11188: "Bardaisan (son of Daisan), so called from his birthplace beside the river". EDITOR. BHE 4221–4225: Daisan is the river of Edessa, and he was named from it because his mother bore him on its bank. LEGEND (13th century). Widely Accepted.

**S2. Edessa and its king.** EPI 26326–26334 (Greek): "ἐκ Μεσοποταμίας μὲν τὸ γένος ἦν, τῶν κατὰ τὴν Ἔδεσσαν ... πόλιν κατοικούντων" (by race from Mesopotamia, of those living at the city of Edessa); "λόγιός τις ὢν ἐν ταῖς δυσὶ γλώσσαις, Ἑλληνικῇ τε διαλέκτῳ καὶ τῇ τῶν Σύρων φωνῇ" (learned in two languages, Greek and Syriac); close to "Abgar, the ruler of the Edessenes, a most holy and most learned man", "at first being his intimate and working with him, and sharing in his learning" (my reading of 26332–26334; the OCR garbles "Αὐγάρῳ"). LATER (about 375, some 150 years on). Documented as Epiphanius's report. Holl (EPI 26369–26372): the king meant is Abgar IX bar Manu. EDITOR. EU 9459–9461 (McGiffert): "in the latter part of the second century there was a Christian Abgar, King of Edessa, at whose court Bardesanes ... enjoyed high favor". EDITOR.

**S3. A Christian city, by the dialogue's own word.** BLC 68959–68965 (the dialogue): "In Syria and in Edessa men used to part with their manhood in honour of Tharatha; but, when King Abgar became a believer he commanded that every one that did so should have his hand cut off, and from that day until now no one does so in the country of Edessa." OWN. Documented as the dialogue's claim (the king's conversion rests on it; the dating in the note at 68960–68963 is Neander's, EDITOR). CHE 53: the flood of 201 "destroyed the temple of the church of the Christians." LATER, from the archives. Documented.

**S4. The language and tradition.** EU 26633–26637: "a most skillful disputant in the Syriac tongue". EPH-P 3017 (Burkitt's note): Ephrem's text calls him "Philosopher of the Aramaeans." Syriac: "a form of Aramaic, the language of Edessa". Census: told within "Bardaisan and the Bardaisanites".

**S5. Death.** BHE 4220–4221: "Vixit Bardesanes annos 68, et obiit anno 533" (he lived 68 years and died in the year 533, of the Greek era). LEGEND/LATER (13th century). Holl (EPI 26426–26427): 533 = 221/222. EU 26586–26588 (McGiffert, citing Hort): "he died in 223 at the age of sixty-eight". EDITOR. Say "in his late sixties, about 222". Eusebius instead set him in the reign of Marcus Aurelius (EU 26525, "In the same reign"); McGiffert explains this as "confusion of the later with the earlier Antonines" (EU 26590–26593). EDITOR.

## Events

**E1. The scene: Bardaisan finds his pupils talking.** BLC 67908–67920: "Some days since we were calling to pay a visit to our brother Shemashgram, and Bardesan came and found us there. And when he had made inquiries after his health, and ascertained that he was well, he asked us, 'What were you talking about? for I heard your voice outside as I was coming in.' For it was his habit, whenever he found us talking about anything before he came, to ask us, 'What were you saying?' that he might talk with us about it." OWN (pupil's record). Documented.

**E2. Avida's question.** BLC 67921–67926: "'Avida here,' said we to him, 'was saying to us, "If God is one, as ye say, and if He is the creator of men, and if it is His will that you should do that which you are commanded, why did He not so create men that they should not be able to do wrong, but should constantly be doing that which is right? for in this way His will would have been accomplished."'" BLC 67944–67946: Avida "was too bashful to ask thee." BLC 67961–67962: "a man who has but lately joined the disciples and is a recent inquirer". BLC 67975–67977: his brethren tell him "'Only believe, and thou wilt then be able to know everything.' But for my part, I cannot believe unless I be convinced." OWN. Documented.

**E3. Teaching by questions.** BLC 67939–67943: "For teachers are 'asked' questions, and do not themselves ask them ... For it is a good thing that a man should know how to ask questions." BLC 67983–67986: "since Avida is not willing to believe, lo! I will speak to you who do believe ... and thus he too will hear something further about it." OWN. Documented.

**E4. Freedom: the harp and the ship.** BLC 68006–68023: "'How is it that God did not so make us that we should not sin and incur condemnation?'—if man had been made so, he would not have belonged to himself, but would have been the instrument of him that moved him ... And how, in that case, would a man differ from a harp, on which another plays; or from a ship, which another guides ... But God in His benignity chose not so to make man; but by freedom He exalted him above many of His creatures, and even made him equal with the angels." BLC 68029–68036: the sun never says, "I will not rise at my appointed time"; "all these things are servants, and are subject to one decree". BLC 68066–68071: man is "guided by his own will ... so that he may justify himself or condemn. For, had he been made so as not to be able to do evil ... the good which he did would not have been his own". OWN. Documented.

**E5. Nature, fate and freedom.** BLC 68249–68252: "The nature of man is, that he should be born, and grow up ... and grow old, eating and drinking, and sleeping and waking, and that then he should die." BLC 68270–68275: "Men, on the contrary, ... in the matters pertaining to their minds they do that which they choose, as those who are free". BLC 68461–68463: "this order, under the name of Fate and γένεσις [nativity, the hour of birth]". BLC 68396–68397: "There is, then, such a thing as Fate, as the astrologers say." BLC 68430–68433: "wealth, and honours, and health, and sickness, and children ... are placed under the control of Fate, and are not in our own power". BLC 68441–68443: "And thus we men are found to be governed by Nature all alike, and by Fate variously, and by our freedom each as he chooses." BLC 68557–68560: "so can we also see the freedom of man defeating and crushing Fate itself,—not, however, in everything". OWN. Documented. **He does not deny fate or the stars:** he limits them to fortune and the body.

**E6. He had once followed the astrologers.** BLC 68337–68342: "'I too am aware, O Philip and Baryama,' said he to us, 'that there are such men: those who are called Chaldæans, and also others who are fond of this subtle knowledge, as I myself also once was.'" OWN. Documented as his admission. "Chaldaeans" = astrologers (the editor glosses it so at 68359–68360). The narrator's name: "O Philip" (68100), "my son Philip" (68879).

**E7. The laws of countries.** BLC 68605–68612: "that which the stars decree ... is not practised by all men alike ... For men have made laws for themselves in various countries, in the exercise of that freedom which was given them by God". Examples, BLC 68616–68625 (the Seres: no killing, no idols; "born at all hours and on all days"), 68629–68636 (the Brahmans of India: no killing, no meat, no wine), 68666–68670 (the Persians marry sisters and daughters), 68743–68749 (among "the Edessæans", a woman merely suspected of adultery is put to death), 68750–68754 (in Hatra a petty thief is stoned), 68793–68798 (Britons: many men one wife; Parthians: one man many wives). BLC 68846–68849: "The truth is, that in all countries, every day, and at all hours, men are born under Nativities diverse from one another, and the laws of men prevail over the decree of the stars, and they are governed by their customs." BLC 68920–68924: "it is but as yesterday since the Romans took possession of Arabia, and they abolished all the laws previously existing there, and especially the circumcision". BLC 68932–68943: the Jews circumcise on the eighth day wherever they live, "without waiting for the coming of the proper stars". OWN. Documented. Note the record's force: the laws he cites include death for suspected adultery and Abgar's order to cut off hands (S3). He cites them as facts of law, not as praise.

**E8. The Christians: one name, one law.** BLC 68966–68976: "And what shall we say of the new race of us Christians, whom Christ at His advent planted in every country and in every region? for, lo! wherever we are, we are all called after the one name of Christ—Christians. On one day, the first of the week, we assemble ourselves together, and on the days of the readings we abstain from taking sustenance." BLC 68976–68991: the brethren in Gaul, Parthia, Judaea, among the Geli, in Persia, Media, Edessa and Hatra do not keep the local customs; "but, wherever they are, and in whatever place they are found, the laws of the several countries do not hinder them from obeying the law of their Sovereign, Christ; nor does the Fate of the celestial Governors compel them to make use of things which they regard as impure." OWN. Documented. "Days of the readings": the editor says "what is meant is not clear" (68971–68975); leave the fasting out.

**E9. Avida yields, Philip is convinced.** BLC 68568–68576: "'I am convinced,' said Avida, 'by the arguments thou hast brought forward, that it is not from his nature that a man does wrong ... If thou canst further prove also that it is not from Fate and Destiny ...'" BLC 68587–68589: "'Prove me this,' said Avida, 'and I will be convinced by thee, and whatsoever thou shalt enjoin upon me I will do.'" BLC 68870–68872: "'Of these things, Father Bardesan,' said I to him, 'thou hast convinced us, and we know that they are true.'" OWN. Documented. The dialogue never records Avida's final answer.

**E10. The new world.** BLC 69023–69031: "A time is also coming when this propensity to inflict harm which still remains in them shall be brought to an end ... And at the establishment of that new world all evil commotions shall cease, and all rebellions terminate, and the foolish shall be convinced, and all deficiencies shall be filled up, and there shall be quietness and peace, through the gift of the Lord of all existing beings." BLC 69032–69033: "Here endeth the Book of the Laws of Countries." OWN. Documented.

**E11. Who wrote the dialogue.** EU 26622–26629 (McGiffert): "it is no less certain that it was not written by Bardesanes himself. As Hort remarks, 'the natural impulse to confuse the author with the chief interlocutor in an anonymous dialogue will sufficiently explain the early ascription of the Dialogue to Bardaisan himself by the Greek Fathers.' It was undoubtedly written by one of Bardesanes' disciples, probably soon after his death, and it is quite likely that it does not depart widely from the spirit of Bardesanes' teaching." BLC 67855–67857 (Pratten): "Many modern critics have come to the conclusion that it was written by a scholar of Bardesanes, but that it gives us the genuine opinions and reasonings of Bardesanes." EDITOR. Widely Accepted. The ancient writers (Eusebius, Epiphanius, Jerome) treat it as his. The narrator is addressed as Philip (E6).

**E12. His school and his debates.** EU 26633–26639: "a most able man and a most skillful disputant in the Syriac tongue, having composed dialogues against Marcion's followers and against certain others who were authors of various opinions, committed them to writing in his own language, together with many other works. His pupils, of whom he had very many (for he was a powerful defender of the faith), translated these productions from the Syriac into Greek." LATER (Eusebius, early 300s, about a century on). Documented as Eusebius's report. The Greek of "powerful defender of the faith" is not held: the wording is McGiffert's English. HIP 12054–12060: a follower of Marcion, Prepon, "has given an account of his heresy in a work inscribed to Bardesanes, an Armenian". CONTEMP (Hippolytus, Rome, about 225–235). Documented as Hippolytus's report. The *Dialogues against the followers of Marcion* are not held.

**E13. A stand in persecution (Epiphanius only).** EU 26641–26645: "other works which they say he wrote on occasion of the persecution which arose at that time." LATER. EPI 26417, 26486–26491 (Greek): "Ἀπολλωνίῳ δὲ τῷ τοῦ Ἀντωνίνου ἑταίρῳ ἀντῆρε, παραιτούμενος ἀρνήσασθαι τὸ Χριστιανὸν ἑαυτὸν λέγειν. ὁ δὲ σχεδὸν ἐν τάξει ὁμολογίας κατέστη, λόγους τε συνετοὺς ἀπεκρίνατο ὑπὲρ εὐσεβείας ἀνδρείως ἀπολογούμενος, θάνατον μὴ δεδιέναι φήσας, ὃν ἀνάγκῃ (ἔφη) ἔσεσθαι, κἂν τε τῷ βασιλεῖ μὴ ἀντείποι." My reading: He stood against Apollonius, the companion of Antoninus, refusing to deny that he called himself a Christian. He came near to the rank of a confessor; he gave wise answers for the faith, defending himself bravely, and said he did not fear death, which, he said, would come of necessity even if he did not oppose the emperor. LATER (about 150 years on). Holl (EPI 26418–26425, 26453–26458): the emperor cannot be Marcus Aurelius; perhaps Caracalla or Elagabalus. EDITOR. Contested (one late witness; the emperor uncertain). If told, in Epiphanius's voice, without a date or emperor.

**E14. Epiphanius knew the argument against fate.** EPI 26409–26416 (Greek, OCR as printed): "ος πολλὰ {προς)" ... "ABeıdav τον ἀστρονομον κατὰ εἱμαρμένης λέγων συνελογίσατο, καὶ" ... "ἄλλα δὲ χατὰ τὴν εὐσεβῆ πίστιν ἐμφέρεται αὐτοῦ συντάγματα." (my reading: he argued at length against Abeidas the astronomer, speaking against fate, and other writings of his for the godly faith are in circulation). LATER (about 375). Documented as Epiphanius's report. That Abeidas is the dialogue's Avida is Holl's and McGiffert's reading (D5): EDITOR. Tell only "a dialogue against fate".

**E15. The text's own narrator.** The dialogue is told in the first person by a pupil whom Bardaisan addresses as "O Philip" (BLC 68099–68100) and "my son Philip" (BLC 68878–68879): "said I to him" (BLC 68096, 68871). OWN. Documented: the story may say plainly that it comes to us as his pupil Philip tells it. That Philip, not Bardaisan, is its author, and that it keeps his views, are the editors' judgments (E11, D2): EDITOR, kept off the story's voice as a judgment.

## Explain it: what the named teachings were

**X1. Marcion's followers.** Justin, *First Apology* 58 (ANF1 16131–16135): Marcion of Pontus "is even now teaching men to deny that God is the maker of all things in heaven and on earth, and that the Christ predicted by the prophets is His Son, and preaches another god besides the Creator of all". CONTEMP (about 155). Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* I.27.2 (ANF1 33572–33582): Jesus "derived from that father who is above the God that made the world". LATER (about 180). Documented as their charge. Plain sense: "Marcion, who taught that the God who made the world was not the Father of Jesus".

**X2. Valentinus's followers.** Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* I.1.1 (ANF1 29919–29932, 29965): the disciples of Valentinus "maintain ... that in the invisible and ineffable heights above there exists a certain perfect, pre-existent Æon" with whom other beings came forth in pairs ("emanations by means of conjunction"). Epiphanius's own gloss on Bardaisan's fall (I1b): "bringing in many first principles and emanations". LATER, hostile. Documented as their account. Plain sense: "Valentinus, a teacher who said that a chain of divine beings came forth, pair by pair, from a hidden first Father". Avoid "aeons" and "Gnostic".

**X3. Ephrem's charge on the body.** EPH-P 1937: "BARDAISAN, lo, declares--that even without the sin of Adam--the Body would turn to its dust,--that Flesh does not cleave to Spirit". LATER, hostile. Documented as Ephrem's charge. Plain sense: Bardaisan taught that the body was always meant to return to dust, sin or no sin, and so would not rise. Epiphanius's charge agrees in substance: "denying the resurrection of the dead" (I1b).

## Impact, heaviest first

**I1. Called a heretic, though the witnesses disagree on how.** (a) EU 26652–26656: "He indeed was at first a follower of Valentinus, but afterward, having rejected his teaching and having refuted most of his fictions, he fancied that he had come over to the more correct opinion. Nevertheless he did not entirely wash off the filth of the old heresy." Greek (Holl's note, EPI 26510–26513): "ἦν δ᾽ οὗτος πρότερον τῆς κατὰ Οὐαλεντῖνον σχολῆς, καταγνοὺς δὲ ταύτης πλεῖστά τε τῆς κατὰ τοῦτον μυθοποιίας ἀπελέγξας, ἐδόκει μέν πως αὐτὸς ἑαυτῷ ἐπὶ τὴν ὀρθοτέραν γνώμην μετατεθεῖσθαι, οὐ μὴν καὶ παντελῶς γε ἀπερρίψατο τὸν τῆς παλαιᾶς αἱρέσεως ῥύπον." LATER. (b) EPI 26328–26330: "ὃς τὰ μὲν πρῶτα ἄριστός τις ἀνὴρ ἐτύγχανε ... ἐκ γὰρ τῆς ἁγίας τοῦ θεοῦ ἐκκλησίας ὡρμᾶτο" (at first he was an excellent man ... for he came from the holy church of God); EPI 26491–26501: "and so the man was finely adorned in all things, until he fell into the error of his own heresy, like a most beautiful ship laden with a cargo beyond compare, wrecked by the banks of the harbour, losing all its goods and bringing death to the other passengers. For he attached himself to the Valentinians ... bringing in many first principles and emanations, and denying the resurrection of the dead" (my reading of the Greek). LATER. (c) HIP 9656–9664: "The Orientals, on the other hand, of whom is Axionicus and Bardesianes, assert that the body of the Saviour was spiritual". CONTEMP (Hippolytus, as a branch of Valentinus's school). (d) THD 40139–40142 (Jerome): "He was at first a follower of Valentinus and afterwards his opponent and himself founded a new heresy." LATER. Documented that each says so; the order and the charge are Contested (D1).

**I2. Ephrem's long attack.** EPH-P 403: "THE SECOND (DISCOURSE) TO HYPATIUS AGAINST MANI AND MARCION AND BARDAISAN". EPH-P 428: "Bardaisan, the teacher of Mani ... said that of seven Parts the Soul was composed". EPH-P 1352: a discourse "AGAINST THE DISCOURSE WHICH IS CALLED 'OF DOMNUS,' WHICH WAS COMPOSED BY BARDAISAN AGAINST THE PLATONISTS." EPH-P 1937: "BARDAISAN, lo, declares--that even without the sin of Adam--the Body would turn to its dust". EPH-P 959: "the Teaching which comes from the party of Marcion and Valentinus and Bardaisan and he [Mani] is the last of all". EPH-G 11200–11204 (*Nisibene Hymn* 51): "I have chanced upon a book of Bardaisan, / And I was troubled for an hour's space; / It tainted my pure ears". EPH-G 11261–11270 (Ephrem, on "the Seven"): "Cursed be our trust [if it be] on the Seven; ... Anathema [be he] who says, as he said: that from them descend the rain and the dew! ... Cursed be he who says, as he said: that from them are the snow and the ice!" Only "[if it be]" is bracketed: "the Æons which Bardaisan confesses" is part of the line as Gwynn gives it (11261–11263), and his note (n. 266) glosses it "The Seven *Æons* (or Beings) of Bardesan's heresy": EDITOR. Mitchell's Ephrem has the same reading, restored in brackets: "Bardaisan said [seven Entities constitute] the world" (EPH-P 1549). McGiffert (EU 26556–26559): Ephrem accuses him "of teaching Polytheism, in effect if not in words", a charge McGiffert thinks arose "from a misunderstanding". EDITOR. In the dialogue "the Seven" are stars (BLC 68354–68355: "those stars which are called the Seven"). LATER (Ephrem, Edessa, d. 373). Documented as Ephrem's charges. EU 26574–26577 (McGiffert): Ephrem "knows him only through his followers ... and hence passes a harsher judgment". EDITOR; Ephrem's own lines say he read Bardaisan's book (EPH-G 11200) and answer the *Domnus* by name (EPH-P 1352), so McGiffert's claim does not hold as stated. Burkitt (EPH-P 2612): Ephrem "does not seem actually to misrepresent the theories of his opponents or to misquote them." EDITOR.

**I3. The songs, and Ephrem's answer** (told in `ephrem-the-syrian`: a clause here). EPH-G 11241–11247 (Ephrem, *Hymns against Heresies* 53, in Gwynn's English): "He fashioned hymns, and joined them with tunes; and composed psalms ... He blended for the simple poison with sweetness ... An hundred and fifty psalms, he likewise composed." (The census edge note quotes this; not a story text, but leave it unquoted.) SOZ 32079–32103: "Bardasanes, who devised a heresy designated by his name, and Harmonius, his son ... the first to subdue his native tongue to meters and musical laws ... When Ephraim perceived that the Syrians were charmed ... he applied himself to the understanding of the metres of Harmonius". THD 15057–15066: Harmonius "had once composed certain songs and by mixing sweetness of melody with his impiety beguiled the hearers"; Ephrem "adopted the music of the songs, but set them to piety". LATER. Documented. Who made the songs, father or son, is D3.

**I4. The argument carried into Greek and Latin.** EU 26637–26639 (pupils translated his works into Greek, E12); EU 26641–26643: "his most able dialogue On Fate". EU 26618–26621 (McGiffert): "A fragment of this work is given in Eusebius' Præp. Evang. VI. 9–10". EDITOR (the *Preparation* is not held). REC 6252–6254 (editor): "The Recognitions also contains a large extract from the work De Fato, ascribed to Bardesanes, but really written by a scholar of his"; REC 18279–18281: "Ch. 17 and ch. 19–29 are taken in an altered form from the writing ascribed to Bardesanes, De Fato." EDITOR. Widely Accepted. JER-L 53437–53442 (Jerome, 392/3, Latin): "In quibus clarissimus ille et fortissimus liber, quem M. Antonino de Fato tradidit ... Si autem tanta vis est et fulgor in interpretatione, quantam putamus in sermone proprio?" (Among them the most famous and strongest book, *On Fate*, which he presented to Marcus Antoninus ... If there is such force and brilliance in the translation, how much do we think there is in his own tongue?). THD 40142–40150 (English). LATER. Documented.

**I5. A name in the lists.** THD 35058–35063 (Theodoret, 5th century): "Valentinus, however, Basilides, Bardesanes, and Harmonius and their following, accept the conception of the Virgin and the birth; but they deny that God the Word took anything from the Virgin". THD 4224 (Theodoret's list of heresies). GN 20491 (Gregory of Nyssa): "Eunomius, the imitator of Manichæus and Bardesanes". LATER. Documented. McGiffert (EU 26575–26576): his followers "were very numerous throughout the East in the fourth century". EDITOR.

**I6. Later Syriac legend.** MS 14521–14544, 14606 (French): in the year 475 of the Greek era (about 164) his parents fled from Persia to Edessa; he was born as they crossed the river; a pagan priest at Mabbug raised him and taught him "les cantiques des païens"; at 25, passing the church, he heard bishop Hystaspes explaining the Scriptures, was baptized and made deacon, wrote against heresies, then turned to Valentinus. LEGEND (12th–13th centuries, about a thousand years on). Leave out, or one clause credited to "chronicles a thousand years later".

## Where the sources disagree or come late

**D1. Valentinian first, or last?** Eusebius: first a Valentinian, then left them, not wholly clean (I1a). Epiphanius, Barhebraeus and Michael: first an excellent churchman, then fell to the Valentinians (I1b, I6). Hippolytus: one of Valentinus's eastern school (I1c). McGiffert, with Hort (EU 26529–26565): "commonly classed among the Gnostics, but, as Hort shows, without sufficient reason"; "There is no reason to think that he taught a system of æons like the Gnostics, but he does seem to have leaned toward docetism, and also to have denied the proper resurrection of the body"; Eusebius's order "is not improbable". EDITOR. The census tradition entry calls the question open. Contested.

**D2. Who wrote the dialogue** (E11). Ancient writers: Bardaisan. Modern editors: a pupil, soon after his death. Widely Accepted that a pupil wrote it; how closely it keeps his views is Dominant Modern Reconstruction ("quite likely", McGiffert).

**D3. Whose songs.** Ephrem gives the hymns and 150 psalms to Bardaisan himself; Sozomen and Theodoret to his son Harmonius (I3). Gwynn notes this (EPH-G 11234–11239). Burkitt (EPH-P 2692–2702) doubts that the *Hymn of the Soul* is his. Contested.

**D4. Dates** (S1, S5). Born 154 or 155; died about 222 or 223; Eusebius and Jerome put him under Marcus Aurelius, which the editors call a confusion. The editors of Barhebraeus prefer Elias of Nisibis's 134 (BHE 4254–4264); Michael the Syrian gives 475 of the Greek era, about 164 (MS 14521). Jerome's Latin of Eusebius's *Chronicle* (`jerome_eusebii-chronici-canones-lat_fotheringham1923.txt` 43062–43064), entered just after Tatian: "Bardesanes alterius hereseos princeps notus efficitur" (Bardesanes becomes known as the founder of another heresy). LATER. It agrees with the *Church History*'s placing and changes no line. Widely Accepted: born in the 150s, died in the early 220s.

**D5. "Addressed to Antoninus."** Eusebius and Jerome say *On Fate* was addressed to the emperor Antoninus; Epiphanius says he argued "against Abeidas the astronomer" (EPI 26413); McGiffert (EU 26607–26614) and Holl (EPI 26440–26444) take "Antoninus" as a slip for Avida. EDITOR. Leave out.

**D6. The stand before Apollonius** (E13). Epiphanius only, the emperor confused. Contested.
