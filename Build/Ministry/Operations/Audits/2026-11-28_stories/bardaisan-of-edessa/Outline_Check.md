# Outline check: Bardaisan of Edessa (review 1 of 3)

## My own account of the heart (written before reading Outline.md)

From the census entry, the source sheet and the approved heart (Hearts.md, batch 6), for a person, shape Teacher or thinker:

- **Essence.** Bardaisan was a Christian teacher at Edessa around 200, who taught in Syriac that people are free: nature rules the body and fate (the course of the stars) deals out fortune, but what we do is our own. His proof was that nations keep different laws under the same stars, and that Christians keep one law in every land. The argument comes down as a dialogue his pupil wrote down, the Book of the Laws of Countries (Documented that it is his argument as the pupil tells it; that a pupil wrote it, Widely Accepted).
- **Also told.** The scene and Avida's question (why did God not make us unable to do wrong?); the harp and the ship; his admission that he once followed the astrologers; fate kept but limited (Documented, BLC). Eusebius: very many pupils, a powerful defender of the faith, dialogues against Marcion's followers, works put into Greek (Documented as Eusebius's report).
- **What came of him, heaviest first.** (1) Named a heretic by later writers who disagree why and in what order: Eusebius (Valentinian first, never wholly clean), Epiphanius (churchman first, then fell, denied the resurrection), Hippolytus (of Valentinus's eastern school), Jerome (founded a new heresy); Contested. (2) Ephrem's long attack in prose and verse at Edessa (body to dust, the Seven). (3) The argument on fate carried on in Greek and Latin (Eusebius's On Fate, Jerome's praise; the Recognitions' reworking, an editors' identification). (4) His followers' songs, which Ephrem answered in the same meters (father or son, Contested), as a clause.
- **Defended:** freedom against fate; the faith against Marcion's followers (Eusebius).

**Against the outline.** The outline's heart agrees with mine in substance: freedom under the stars, the dialogue as Philip tells it, praise and condemnation from the same witnesses, Eusebius on the pupils and the anti-Marcion dialogues, Ephrem's answer to the songs. No major contribution is left out of the heart itself. One later witness that carries the argument is missing from "What came of him" (finding B1).
