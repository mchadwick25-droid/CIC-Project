Around the year 200, a Christian teacher in Edessa, a city in what is now south-eastern Turkey, came in on some of his pupils as they were visiting a friend. His name was Bardaisan, and as he always did, he asked what they had been talking about. They answered for a newcomer, Avida, who was too shy to ask himself: if God is one, and made us, and wants us to do right, why did he not make us unable to do wrong?

Bardaisan's answer was that people are free, and not ruled by the stars. The conversation comes down to us as one of his pupils, Philip, tells it, in the Book of the Laws of Countries. Later writers praised the argument, and the same writers named Bardaisan among the teachers the church condemned.

Had God made people unable to do wrong, Bardaisan tells Avida, they would not belong to themselves. They would be like a harp that another plays, or a ship that another steers, where the praise and the blame belong to the player. The sun cannot refuse to rise at its hour, but a person can choose, and so the good a person does is his own.

Nature, he goes on, gives everyone the same life: birth, growth, old age and death. He admits that he once followed the astrologers himself, and he still grants them their share. Fate, the course the stars run from the hour of a person's birth, deals out wealth and honors, health and sickness, and children, and these are not in our power. "So we humans are ruled by nature all alike, by fate each in a different way, and by our freedom each as we choose."

Avida grants that wrongdoing does not come from nature, and asks for proof that it does not come from fate. Bardaisan finds it in the laws of countries. People are born at every hour in every land, yet each people keeps laws it has made for itself. In India the Brahmans keep a law that forbids them to kill or to eat meat. And laws change when rulers change them: when King Abgar of Edessa became a believer, the dialogue says, he ordered that any man who castrated himself in a pagan rite should have his hand cut off.

Last come the Christians, whom Christ planted in every land. "Wherever we are, we are all called by the one name of Christ: Christians." From Gaul to Persia they do not take up the customs around them, and neither the laws of their countries nor fate keeps them from the law of Christ.

Avida's was not the only question Bardaisan answered. Eusebius, the church historian, writing about a century later, calls him a most skillful debater in Syriac, the form of Aramaic spoken at Edessa. He wrote dialogues against the followers of Marcion, who taught that the God who made the world was not the Father of Jesus, and his very many pupils put his works into Greek. Eusebius praises him as a powerful defender of the faith and names his most able dialogue, On Fate. In another book, written against the astrologers, Eusebius himself copied long stretches of this same conversation into Greek, under Bardaisan's name.

Yet Eusebius also says that Bardaisan began among the followers of Valentinus, a teacher who said that a chain of divine beings came forth, pair by pair, from a hidden first Father. Bardaisan left them and refuted most of their tales, but he never wholly washed off the stain of that old heresy.

At Edessa, about a century and a half after Bardaisan, the people of Syria still sang songs made in his circle. Ephrem the Syrian learned the beat and pattern of their verse and answered them with hymns for the church. In prose he attacked Bardaisan's teaching that the body was always meant to return to dust, sin or no sin, and so would not rise.

Epiphanius, a bishop writing in the same years, tells it the other way round. Bardaisan, he says, was first a fine man of the church, who then fell in with the followers of Valentinus and denied that the dead will rise. He was like a richly loaded ship wrecked at the edge of the harbor, bringing death to its passengers. Jerome, writing in Latin about twenty years later, called Bardaisan's book on fate the most famous and strongest of all his works.

The conversation with Avida ends by looking ahead, to a time when the harm still done in the world will be brought to an end. "When that new world is set up, all evil turmoil will cease, and every rebellion will end."
