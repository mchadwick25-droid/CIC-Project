# Packet L01: Tertullian, rebuilt brief (after the fix pass)

## tertullian-s-voice

Tertullian

At Carthage, in Roman Africa, around the year 200, Tertullian wrote to the governors who judged Christians for Rome. If they could not look into the charges openly, from the judge's seat, he wrote: "Let the truth reach your ears at least by the hidden road of silent writing." Of the truth he added: "For now, she longs for one thing: not to be condemned unknown."

Tertullian was the foremost early writer of the faith in Latin, around the year 200. He defended the Christians to Rome's governors, claimed the Scriptures for the churches the apostles founded, and said in Latin how God is one and yet three, and the Latin writers after him read him closely. Yet later in life he joined a strict movement, the New Prophecy, and wrote against the bishops.

Those lines open his Apology, a defense of Christians on trial. The governors, he argued, condemned the very name of Christian without asking what Christians did. He ended it in defiance: the more often Rome cut them down, the more they grew, for the blood of Christians is seed.

Rival teachers used the Scriptures too, for teachings the churches rejected. Marcion, as Tertullian tells it in another book, taught that beside the Creator there was another god, who was only good. Valentinus told of a swarm of divine beings, and said the Creator came from the sin of one of them. Marcion cut the Scriptures down to suit himself, and Valentinus kept them whole but bent their meaning. In that book he answered them with a plea from the law courts, one that throws a case out before it is argued. Before anyone argued from Scripture, he said, it must be settled whose the Scriptures were. They belonged to the churches the apostles founded, such as Rome, which still kept what the apostles taught. Then he spoke as an owner speaks to trespassers: "By what right, Marcion, do you cut my timber? By whose leave, Valentinus, do you turn my springs aside?" He went on: "This property is mine. I have held it long; I held it first." And he claimed: "I am the heir of the apostles."

The New Prophecy had begun in Phrygia, in today's Turkey. Its prophets said that the Holy Spirit, the Helper Jesus promised, was speaking anew through them, and they asked a stricter life of Christians, with no second marriage after a husband or wife had died. Tertullian wrote that his acceptance and defense of the Spirit had set his side apart from the others, whom he called the unspiritual. Jerome, who wrote his life, says the envy and insults of the clergy of Rome drove him to the New Prophecy.

After his turn he wrote Against Praxeas. By Tertullian's account, Praxeas taught at Rome that the Father himself was born and suffered, so that Father and Son were one and the same. Tertullian held that Father, Son and Spirit are distinct but not divided, and he showed it with things from nature: "The root and the tree are two things, but joined. The spring and the river are two forms, but undivided. The sun and the ray are two forms, but they hold together." Of the Spirit he went on: "The Spirit is third from God and the Son, as the fruit from the tree is third from the root." In the same book he put it in two words. God is one substance, one in what God is, and Father, Son and Spirit are three persons, each distinct. The Latin word for the Trinity, trinitas, stands in this book too.

When a bishop announced that he would forgive adultery once the sinner had repented, Tertullian answered him in On Modesty. The church would forgive sins, he wrote, but it would be the church of the Spirit, through a spiritual man, and not the church that is a number of bishops. Jerome lists the book among those he wrote directly against the church.

A generation later, Cyprian, the bishop of Carthage, never let a day pass without reading him. Jerome heard it from an old man in Italy who had met Cyprian's secretary, and Cyprian, the secretary said, would often ask, "Give me the master," meaning Tertullian.

About two centuries after Tertullian, Augustine wrote that many of his works, written with great eloquence, were still read. Yet he listed the Tertullianists among the heresies, the teachings he counted outside the true faith. Tertullian became a heretic, Augustine wrote, because he went over to the prophets he had once argued against, and began to condemn second marriage as if it were sexual sin. Later he broke from the prophets too and gathered meetings of his own. His followers at Carthage dwindled. While Augustine was there, the very few who were left crossed into the Catholic church, the main body, and handed over their church building.
