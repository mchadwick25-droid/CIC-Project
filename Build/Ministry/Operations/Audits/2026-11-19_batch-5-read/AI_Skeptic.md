# The AI skeptic's read: batch 5

Read: `cft-website/about.html` and `Pieces.md`. Each piece heard once. I judged only what I heard, and checked no facts against sources. This is a drift check. It asks for no changes.

The about page tells the listener that AI helped check every claim. So a skeptical listener arrives already listening for the machine.

## Piece by piece

**Nestorius.** Tell: the held-back name ("The writer was Nestorius."), then a stock outside hedge, "Historians still argue over what he really taught." Mixed. The sources feel handled and the desert-as-mother ending is strong, but the frame is the batch template.

**Cyril of Alexandria.** Tell: the balancing clause "Whether Cyril was to blame is still argued," closing a dense paragraph of violence. Mixed. The detail is gritty and unflattering, but each hard episode is tidied off with an even-handed clause.

**Barsauma of Nisibis.** Tell: the corrective aside "The Persian church had run its own affairs since 424; what his synods set apart was its teaching and its rules." It sounds like a fix bolted on. Human. It is knotty and unresolved, with witnesses pulling against each other. That is hard to follow by ear, but it is not machine-smooth.

**Babai the Great.** Tell: the held-back name ("He was Babai") and a stacked gloss: "a monk vowed to prayer, poverty and an unmarried life, and abbot, or head". Human. The burned huts, the wronged Jacob and the odd angel exchange are specific and uneven in a way a generator smooths out.

**Timothy I of Baghdad.** Tell: the gloss chain "The book of the four Gospels, the life of Jesus, read in worship, would stand in for the missing bishop, laid on the throne, the bishop's chair in the church." Mixed. The sack of stones and the China stone opening have wit. The glossing turns mechanical in the key scene.

**Martin Luther.** Tell: the second paragraph is a textbook résumé (office, doctrine, trial, outlaw, translation, "carry his name"). Mixed. The peasant-war section is unsparing and the "Here I stand" note is a real point of view. The frame around it is the most generic in the batch.

**William Tyndale.** Tell: the summing line "Smuggled in faster than bishops could burn it, the book had reached people no one had allowed to have it." Mixed. The Cologne printers over their wine and the Wiltshire farmer are human texture. The tidy moral is the machine's habit.

**John Calvin.** Tell: "then did something more frightening" works as a stock suspense setup before the quote. Mixed. Calvin's own quotes carry the voice. The narrator's middle is an orderly list of offices.

**John Wesley.** Tell: "The class came by accident." is good, but the piece then marches year by year ("In 1739 … In 1742 … In 1784 … In 1791"). Mixed. The brickyard, the penny a week and Richard Allen's complaint are textured. The structure is a timeline.

**Augustine of Hippo.** Tell: the closing flourish "whoever reads the words aloud gives the writer his voice". It is a clever nod to the narration itself, the kind of neat bow a generator likes. Mixed. The joking beggar opening is fresh. The film-script fragment ("Milan, where the emperor then held court.") and the meta ending feel staged.

**Patrick.** Tell: "Legends grew up around him centuries later, in the work of writers who came long after." It is redundant filler. Human. It stays close to Patrick's own words, admits what no record says, and the ring ending ("Patrick the sinner, unlearned") is earned.

**Athanasius of Alexandria.** Tell: the encyclopedia line "This was Athanasius, bishop for forty-five years, who had to leave his city five times," plus a stray 1921 fact at the end. Mixed. The night raid opening is vivid. The reception paragraph reads like a list of facts.

Tally: Human 3, Mixed 9, Generated 0.

### 2. Confidently specific lines that make me doubt the sourcing

Most precise numbers come attributed ("Mari says", "Thomas counted", "by one editor's count"), and that reassures. The lines that trip the alarm are the confident ones with no named witness:

- Athanasius: "In 1921 a Church of England proposal for sharing communion with the Orthodox churches of the East took the books as Athanasius had defined them." It is obscure, exact, unattributed and out of place. This is the clearest one.
- Luther: "two centuries later the hymnbook of Halle, a Lutheran town in Germany, still printed them." Why Halle? It sounds picked to sound researched.
- Barsauma: "The Persian church had run its own affairs since 424." It is a flat narrator assertion that corrects the line before it.
- Cyril: "a chronicler of Egypt's own church, more than two centuries later, praised the deed." This is a strong claim about praising a murder, with the chronicler unnamed.
- Attributed, but numbers that a listener can't place: Cyril's "debt of 1,500 pounds of gold", Babai's "eighty-four of his works", Timothy's "9 January 823" and "ninety-five", Luther's "four of the eight hymns".

### 3. Point of view and texture

There is a real point of view, and it is the same one every time: the record is messy, saints coerced, and here is who says what. The narrator never judges in its own voice. It hands every judgment to a source ("Socrates blames him", "Thomas says, unjustly"). That is principled, but it also makes the narrator interchangeable. The voice of Egypt, Persia, Wittenberg and Methodist Bristol is one calm documentary voice. Texture comes from the quoted people and the gritty detail (the mangled hand, the burned huts, the penny a week, the gunshots), not from the narrator. Take away the quotes and the twelve could swap paragraphs.

### 4. Verdict on drift

The batch has not tipped into a generated voice. There is little filler, nothing gushing, no false uplift, and the pieces keep hard and unflattering material that a generic model softens. But it is drifting toward a template. Every piece follows the same build: a cold-open scene, the held-back name, a résumé paragraph, a chronicle of dated episodes, a shadow episode with two witnesses, a leap to reception history, and a quiet closing line. Heard one after another, the sameness itself becomes the tell, and by the project's own rule a uniform polish is a fabrication risk. The glossing habit is the most audible symptom. It is applied by rule, not by need ("Scripture, the Bible", "the resurrection, when the dead rise at the end of time"), and in key scenes it stacks into chains a listener can't parse.

Top three tells to watch in future batches:

1. **The template build.** Held-back name reveal plus résumé second paragraph plus a quiet-quote ending, in nearly every piece. Vary the openings and let some pieces start with the person.
2. **Glossing by rule.** Comma-appositive definitions for every church word, even obvious ones, stacking two or three to a sentence.
3. **Unattributed precision in the reception tail.** Exact late facts (1921, Halle, "since 424") stated flat by the narrator, plus the "historians still argue / editors say" hedge used as a stock closer.
