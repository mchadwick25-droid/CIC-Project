# Batch 5 read: the teacher

Reader: a teacher of church history or religious education, at secondary school or adult education level. I want pieces to play to a class or set as listening. They need to be trustworthy, clear and memorable.

Read: `cft-website/about.html`, then `Pieces.md`. Nothing else.

## Each piece

### Nestorius
- **Took away:** A bishop of Constantinople who would not simply call Mary "Mother of God". He was condemned at Ephesus in 431 and died in exile. He wrote his last defense under a false name, and the Church of the East kept it.
- **Verdict:** Mostly.
- **Why:** The hidden-name opening and the desert close stick, but the middle swaps "Socrates says" and "Nestorius says" too often for one hearing.

### Cyril of Alexandria
- **Took away:** Nestorius's great opponent, bishop of Alexandria. His teaching that Christ is one became the test for the Egyptian and Syrian churches. His years were violent, and whether he was to blame for Hypatia's death is still argued.
- **Verdict:** Mostly.
- **Why:** The burning bush and the Hypatia section are excellent teaching material, but the Chalcedon paragraph near the end packs three ideas and a new name into a few lines.

### Barsauma of Nisibis
- **Took away:** A border bishop whose synod his own church first left out of its book and later praised. It had something to do with married clergy and a rival who was killed.
- **Verdict:** Misses.
- **Why:** Five synods, seven dates and four hostile sources come too fast; a class could not retell it after one hearing.

### Babai the Great
- **Took away:** A monk who ran the Persian church for twenty years while the king refused it a head. He was a harsh abbot. His Book of the Union said the man Jesus suffered, not God.
- **Verdict:** Meets.
- **Why:** A clear frame (the king's oath), one plain line of theology, and an angel story at the end that a class will remember.

### Timothy I of Baghdad
- **Took away:** A patriarch in Baghdad for forty years under the caliphs. He won office with sacks of stones. He let a Gospel book stand in for a missing bishop so the church could grow toward China.
- **Verdict:** Meets.
- **Why:** Three vivid scenes carry it, though the caliph paragraph reads like a list.

### Martin Luther
- **Took away:** A friar who said forgiveness cannot be bought. He would not recant at Worms, put the Bible into German, and urged rulers to kill rebel peasants.
- **Verdict:** Meets.
- **Why:** It is honest about the Peasants' War, and it tells me "Here I stand" may not be his words, which is exactly what I want a class to hear.

### William Tyndale
- **Took away:** The man who put the Bible into English from Greek and Hebrew. He printed it abroad and smuggled it in, and was strangled and burned for it. Most of the first approved English Bible was his.
- **Verdict:** Meets.
- **Why:** The flight up the Rhine, his offer to the king and the farmer who learned it by ear make the stakes real.

### John Calvin
- **Took away:** A timid scholar cursed into staying at Geneva. He gave its church four offices that France and Scotland copied. Geneva burned Servetus with his approval.
- **Verdict:** Mostly.
- **Why:** The opening and the gunshots at the end are superb, but the middle on offices, councils and the 1555 dispute is a lecture, not a story.

### John Wesley
- **Took away:** An Anglican priest whose heart was "warmed" in 1738. He preached outdoors and organized converts into weekly classes, which became the Methodist churches.
- **Verdict:** Meets.
- **Why:** The penny-a-week origin of the class is a gift for teaching, and Richard Allen's line keeps it honest.

### Augustine of Hippo
- **Took away:** A teacher of speaking who turned to Christ in a garden in Milan. As bishop of Hippo he taught that grace comes first, and he backed force against the Donatists. Luther and Calvin built on him.
- **Verdict:** Mostly.
- **Why:** The beggar opening is lovely, but at five minutes it tells the garden twice and ends with a list of later readers.

### Patrick
- **Took away:** A Briton enslaved in Ireland who escaped and went back as a bishop. Almost all we know comes from his own two letters.
- **Verdict:** Meets.
- **Why:** Clear, moving, and honest about what we do not know.

### Athanasius of Alexandria
- **Took away:** A bishop of Alexandria who held to Nicaea's "of one being" and was driven out five times. He listed the twenty-seven books of the New Testament.
- **Verdict:** Mostly.
- **Why:** The night escape and the Easter letter are strong, but the chain of exiles and dates in the middle is too much to hold by ear.

**Counts:** Meets 6, Mostly 5, Misses 1.

## Across all twelve

### 1. What works
Each piece opens on a scene or a voice, not a date. That is how I would start a lesson. The pieces say who told each story and when, and say plainly when a story is disputed. That makes them usable for teaching how to weigh sources. They do not hide the ugly parts: Luther on the peasants, Calvin and Servetus, Cyril and Hypatia, Augustine and the Donatists.

The best:
- **Patrick.** It is short, follows one life in order, and its honesty about the evidence is itself the lesson.
- **Tyndale.** Strong stakes, a clear line from start to finish, and ordinary readers in the story, not just the great man.
- **Luther.** It is trustworthy where pupils most often get myths, and it corrects one in passing.

### 2. What doesn't work
When the pieces fail, it is for one reason: too many dated events in a row, with no scene to hang them on. Each date asks the listener to hold something.

The weakest:
- **Barsauma.** A hard subject made harder by layered, clashing sources and too many synods.
- **Athanasius.** Its middle is a run of exiles and returns.
- **Calvin, middle section.** The church-order material reads like notes, between two excellent scenes.

Many pieces also give away the ending in the second paragraph, then tell the life in order. For a class, that summary helps. But Tyndale's death and Nestorius's exile are each told twice.

### 3. What a teacher would want that the pieces don't give
- A short closing line saying why this person still matters, said plainly. Most end on a death or last words, which is moving but leaves the "so what" to me.
- A written transcript with the sources listed, so I can trust a quote and show it on screen.
- A one-line sense of which river on the tree this person belongs to. "Told within" is shown here but not heard. The about page says the tree is about links between traditions, and the pieces state those links only now and then (Augustine to Luther and Calvin; Nestorius to the Church of the East).
- A warning on the page for violent content (strangling, burning, torture, "kill and stab"). Fine for older classes, but I need to know first.
- A length closer to three minutes. Augustine and Luther run four to five.

### 4. One voice, or a template? Human, or generated?
Mostly one voice, and a human one. It sounds like a careful documentary narrator, with no chatbot hedging or filler.

But the template shows. Nearly every piece has the same reveal in paragraph two: "The friar was Martin Luther", "The scholar was John Calvin", "The teacher was Augustine", "The new patriarch was Timothy", "This was Athanasius". The same pattern follows: summary, then events in order, then death. Heard one at a time, that is fine. Played as a series in class, pupils will notice it.

The inline glosses are the other tic. "Bishop, the leader of the church" appears in almost every piece. Some glosses are helpful; some interrupt a sentence that needed to move. A few are odd, such as calling the New Testament "the second part of the Bible", or explaining "saving souls" in the middle of a sentence about Wesley's doubts.

### 5. Top three changes for future batches
1. **Cap the dated events.** Keep to about four or five dates per piece, each tied to a scene. Summarize the rest in one sentence rather than listing them.
2. **Vary the shape.** Drop the fixed "The X was Y" reveal and the summary-then-chronology pattern where the story has a better form, and don't tell the ending twice.
3. **Gloss once, lightly.** Explain a term the first time it truly matters, and trust the listener after that, especially common words like bishop or council.

## Which I would play as they are

**Would play to a class as they are:** Patrick, Tyndale, Luther, Wesley, Babai, Timothy.

**Would play with a short set-up first:** Nestorius and Cyril, as a pair, after I explain Ephesus 431 and "Mother of God". Calvin, with a word on Geneva's councils. Augustine, though I might stop it before the last paragraph.

**Would not play as they are:** Barsauma, which a class could not follow by ear. Athanasius, unless I first put the exile dates on the board.

## Do they hold to what the tree is for?

Yes, broadly. The about page promises sourced, graded, word-for-word-checked material, and plain grading of disputed claims. These pieces keep that promise: they name their witnesses, flag disputes in plain words, and invent nothing I could detect. One gap: the about page describes movements, links and gap stories, but does not mention person pages like these. The pieces rarely tie their person back to the rivers and links that are the tree's whole point. They drift toward stand-alone biographies. Good ones, but not yet clearly part of the map.
