# Batch 5 read: product fit

Reader: a product reader for the Church Family Tree. I judged whether these twelve pieces serve the site and its listeners as a collection. I read `about.html`, `Pieces.md`, and each piece's current page in `cft-website/tree/` (read-only).

At 150 words a minute the twelve run about 48 minutes in all. They range from 3 min 17 s (Babai) to 5 min (Augustine). Most current "About" texts on these pages are 250 to 450 words. So every new piece is longer than what it joins, often by half again or more.

## Piece by piece

**Nestorius** (568 words, 3:47)
- Took away: a bishop of Constantinople, removed at Ephesus in 431 over "Mother of God," who wrote his last defense under a borrowed name. A Persian church kept it but said he followed them, not they him.
- Verdict: **Mostly.**
- Why: the pen-name opening and the desert ending are strong, but the middle swings between Socrates and Nestorius too often for one hearing.

**Cyril of Alexandria** (599 words, 4:00)
- Took away: the bishop of Alexandria who brought Nestorius down and whose words became the test of faith for Egypt and Syria. Violence in his city, Hypatia's death among it, still clouds him.
- Verdict: **Mostly.**
- Why: the burning-bush opening is vivid and the dark record is told fairly, but the "one nature" paragraph is too dense to follow by ear.

**Barsauma of Nisibis** (570 words, 3:48)
- Took away: a frontier bishop behind a breakaway synod in 484, who later took it back. His church still kept his side's married clergy and its guide, Theodore. Beyond that I lost the thread.
- Verdict: **Misses.**
- Why: five synods, a run of dates and five rival chroniclers across three centuries crowd out the person, so a listener cannot hold who he was.

**Babai the Great** (492 words, 3:17)
- Took away: a monk-abbot who led the Persian church while a king refused it a head. He was harsh with his monks, and he fixed its teaching that God did not die on the cross.
- Verdict: **Meets.**
- Why: one clear frame (the king's oath), one hard human story, one plain idea, and an ending that lands, all in the shortest piece.

**Timothy I of Baghdad** (541 words, 3:36)
- Took away: the patriarch in Baghdad under five caliphs who sent monks out as bishops toward the Caspian and China. He let a Gospel book stand in for a missing bishop.
- Verdict: **Meets.**
- Why: the China stone, the sacks of stones and the Gospel book on the throne make a story a listener can retell.

**Martin Luther** (666 words, 4:26)
- Took away: the friar who said forgiveness cannot be bought, stood firm at Worms, and put the Bible into German. He urged the rulers to kill the rebel peasants in 1525.
- Verdict: **Mostly.**
- Why: it is honest and full, but the summary paragraph tells the whole life before the body tells it again, and the second telling costs time.

**William Tyndale** (639 words, 4:16)
- Took away: the scholar who printed an English New Testament abroad and offered his life for the bare text. He was strangled and burned, and his words lived on in the king's Bible.
- Verdict: **Meets.**
- Why: one through-line (the book in people's hands) runs from the press at Cologne to the farmer Maundrel, so the length is earned.

**John Calvin** (646 words, 4:18)
- Took away: a timid French scholar cursed into staying at Geneva. He gave its church a plan of offices that France and Scotland copied, and his city burned Servetus.
- Verdict: **Mostly.**
- Why: the opening and the gunshot ending are excellent, but the four offices and the long dispute over communion are dry by ear.

**John Wesley** (595 words, 3:58)
- Took away: an Anglican priest who felt his heart warmed in 1738. He preached in fields and built weekly classes that became the Methodist churches, here and in America.
- Verdict: **Meets.**
- Why: the class "by accident" over a penny a week is the best small scene in the batch, and Richard Allen's voice gives it reach and honesty.

**Augustine of Hippo** (747 words, 4:59)
- Took away: a teacher of speaking turned bishop of Hippo who taught that grace comes first. He backed force against the Donatists, and he shaped Luther, Calvin and Rome alike.
- Verdict: **Mostly.**
- Why: the garden scene works, but at five minutes the closing roll call of Orange, Luther, Calvin and Jansen feels like a list, not a story.

**Patrick** (524 words, 3:30)
- Took away: a former slave who went back to Ireland as a bishop, in his own humble words. He stood up to a warlord who killed his new converts.
- Verdict: **Meets.**
- Why: it rests on his own voice and plainly marks where record ends and legend begins, which is exactly the tree's habit.

**Athanasius of Alexandria** (623 words, 4:09)
- Took away: the bishop of Alexandria who held to Nicaea through five exiles. He named the twenty-seven books of the New Testament.
- Verdict: **Mostly.**
- Why: the night of 356 and the canon list are strong, but the run of exile years (335, 337, 339, 346, 356, 362, 365) blurs by ear.

**Counts: Meets 5, Mostly 6, Misses 1.**

## Across all twelve

### 1. What works
- An opening scene, then the name. A listener is placed in a moment before being given facts.
- Most pieces end on the person's own words: Calvin's "poor, timid scholar," Wesley's "God is with us," Tyndale's syllable he never changed, Patrick "the sinner, unlearned." These endings are the collection's best habit.
- They are honest. Hostile witnesses are named as hostile, legends are marked as later, and hard deeds (Luther on the peasants, Calvin on Servetus, Cyril and Hypatia, Augustine on force) are told, not hidden. That is what `about.html` promises.
- They show links between traditions, which is the tree's whole idea: Wesley hears Luther on Romans, Luther and Calvin read Augustine, Geneva's offices travel to France and Scotland, and Wesley's Rules cross to America.
- Best three: **Wesley**, **Tyndale** and **Babai**. Each has one clear thread and one or two scenes a listener could retell, and each spends its time on the person rather than on the record about the person.

### 2. What doesn't work
- Date runs and source runs. When a piece lists five dates or four chroniclers in a row, a listener lets go. It reads well on a page and poorly by ear.
- The summary paragraph after the reveal sometimes retells what the body will tell again. This costs most in the longest pieces.
- Legacy roll calls at the end (Augustine, Athanasius's 692 and 1921) add length without a scene.
- Weakest three: **Barsauma** (the person is lost in the record), **Augustine** (too long for what it adds after the garden), and **Athanasius** (the exile chronology swamps a strong opening). The current Athanasius page already tells the severed-hand trial at Tyre, a better scene than any in the new piece.

### 3. What the listener would want
- Where this person sits on the tree. Each piece is "told within" a tradition, but few say so in a way a listener hears. Nestorius never mentions the Antiochene preachers his page shelves him with. One line tying the person to their river would help.
- Who carries them today. Luther, Wesley and Calvin get this. The Eastern pieces do not: the Church of the East's living heirs are never named.
- A sense that the pages connect. Nestorius, Cyril, Babai, Barsauma and Timothy form one story, but a listener on one page does not know the others are a click away.

### 4. One collection or a template? Human or generated?
- It sounds like one collection with one voice: calm, fair, documentary. It is mostly human. Lines like "Some changed, and some were put out" sound written by a person.
- But the skeleton shows. Almost every piece opens on a scene, then says "The friar was," "The scholar was," "The teacher was," "This was," then gives a summary paragraph. By the fourth piece a listener hears the frame.
- The inline glosses are the main tell. "Bishop, the leader of the church" comes back in piece after piece, and some sentences stack two glosses ("put the New Testament, the gospels and letters of the early church, into German"). Each gloss is kind to a newcomer. Together they sound processed, and they slow the pace.

### 5. Top three changes for future batches
1. **Set a default of about 550 words (under four minutes).** Go longer only for a second strong scene, and cut date runs and legacy roll calls first.
2. **Drop the summary paragraph when the body retells it, and vary the reveal.** Let the opening scene lead into the life, not into "The X was Y" and a précis.
3. **Hold one clear disagreement per piece, told as "one says, another says."** More than two witnesses on one point is a reading pleasure and a listening loss, as Barsauma shows.

## Length and pace

The longer pieces earn their time when they carry more than one scene on one thread. Tyndale (4:16) and Wesley (3:58) do. Luther (4:26) and Calvin (4:18) half do: their best minutes are the opening and the end, and their middles are record, not story. Augustine (4:59) does not: the last 150 words could go with little lost. The shortest, Babai (3:17), lands the cleanest. In this batch, shorter pieces did better than longer ones.

## Drift check

These twelve hold to what the tree is for, as `about.html` sets it out. They are true to the record, they name their sources and their doubts, and they show how traditions link. Nothing reads as invented. The drift is in form, not content. The pieces are growing longer than the pages they sit on and are settling into a fixed frame. One (Barsauma) has slipped from telling a person to reporting a dispute over a person.
