# Batch 5 read: the pastor

Reader: a pastor who might share these with a congregation or a study group. I care that each person's faith comes through honestly. No tradition should be caricatured. The hard parts should not be hidden. Nothing should preach.

Read: `cft-website/about.html`, then `Pieces.md`. Nothing else.

## Piece by piece

**Nestorius.** A bishop condemned at Ephesus, who said he united Christ's worship while keeping the natures distinct, died in exile, and was honored by a Persian church that would not take his name.
**Meets.** His own voice, his enemies' charges and his harsh sermon against heretics all stand side by side, and the ending gives his hope of resurrection without comment.

**Cyril of Alexandria.** The bishop who fought Nestorius, called Mary Mother of God, and whose years saw riots, a synagogue seizure and the killing of Hypatia.
**Mostly.** It is fair and hides nothing, but the Chalcedon and "one nature" paragraph is too dense for one hearing, so his faith is clearest at the start and lost near the end.

**Barsauma of Nisibis.** A border bishop tied to a disputed synod, clergy marriage and a patriarch's death, remembered very differently by different churches.
**Misses.** After one hearing I could not say what he believed or why he matters; a run of synods, years and rival rumors about his marriage leaves only confusion.

**Babai the Great.** A monk who led the Persian church for twenty years without a head, wrote on how God and man are one in Christ, and was harsh with his monks.
**Meets.** Both his severity and his faith are told plainly, and the angel at the end is moving because it is given as Thomas's story, not as fact.

**Timothy I of Baghdad.** A patriarch under the caliphs who won office with a sack of stones, fought a bitter split, and let the Gospel book stand in for a missing bishop.
**Mostly.** The Gospel-book story is one of the best moments in the batch, but the middle (lists of writings, histories five centuries later) is filler, and his own faith is barely heard.

**Martin Luther.** A friar who said forgiveness cannot be bought, stood at Worms, gave Germans the Bible and hymns, and urged rulers to kill rebel peasants.
**Meets.** The peasants' war is told in his own words, with his later call for mercy, so the hard part is neither hidden nor used as a weapon.

**William Tyndale.** A priest who put the Bible into English, smuggled it home, offered his life for the bare text, and was strangled and burned.
**Meets.** John Maundrel, the farmer who could not read, makes the cost and the gift real to ordinary people, and nothing is overstated.

**John Calvin.** A timid scholar cursed into staying at Geneva, who built a church order others copied, taught predestination, and wanted Servetus dead.
**Mostly.** Servetus and Bolsec are told honestly, but the piece gives far more of his discipline and his severity than of what drew people to his faith, so a listener could leave with the caricature.

**John Wesley.** A priest whose heart was warmed, who preached outdoors, gathered people into weekly classes, and faced mobs.
**Meets.** Warm without gushing, honest about Richard Allen's charge of tyranny against Black members, and the last words land.

**Augustine of Hippo.** A teacher who heard "take it and read," became a bishop, taught that grace comes first, and backed laws to force the Donatists back.
**Mostly.** The garden and the forced-conversion turn are both here, but at nearly five minutes it is too much for one hearing, and the closing chain from Orange to Jansen is a list.

**Patrick.** A former slave who went back to Ireland as a bishop, called himself a sinner, and stood with converts killed and sold by a warlord's men.
**Meets.** The best of the twelve: his own humble voice, the slave women who "suffered most," and the honest line that no record says whether any captive came home.

**Athanasius of Alexandria.** A bishop exiled five times for holding to Nicaea, who wrote the Life of Antony and listed the books of Scripture.
**Mostly.** The opening night in the church is gripping, but the middle becomes a timeline of exiles and returns, and the person fades behind the dates.

**Counts: Meets 6, Mostly 5, Misses 1.**

## Across all twelve

**1. What works.** The pieces show the hard parts and let the faith stand next to them. Nobody is a villain or a plaster saint. The best are **Patrick**, **Tyndale** and **Wesley**. Each rests on the person's own words and on one or two concrete scenes. Each lets ordinary believers in: the slave women, the farmer Maundrel, the penny-a-week class. The Persian church pieces also avoid the old "Nestorian" caricature, which matters to me.

**2. What doesn't work.** When a piece loses its thread, it becomes a chain of dates, synods and later historians. The weakest are **Barsauma**, **Athanasius** and **Timothy**. In each the record's arguments crowd out the person, and his own faith is hard to hear. Source tags ("editors say," "by one editor's count," "five centuries later") pile up in these ones, and on the ear they sound like footnotes.

**3. What my listener would want.** One plain sentence of what this person loved and prayed for, in their own warmth, early and kept. Calvin, Timothy and Barsauma lack it. More of the ordinary people who lived the faith, and more women; women appear mostly as victims or housekeepers. A slower pace: some pieces ask a listener to hold six names and four dates in one paragraph.

**4. One voice or a template?** One voice, and mostly a human one. It is calm, plain and never preaches. But the shape is a template: an opening scene, then "The friar was Martin Luther," "The scholar was John Calvin," "The teacher was Augustine," then a summary, then a hostile source, then the hard part, then the death. Heard on one page, that is fine. Heard in a row, as a study group would, it shows. The inline glosses ("bishop, the leader of the church"; "Scripture, the Bible") repeat in every piece and slow the ear. They sound like a process, not a narrator. I heard no chatbot cadence.

**5. Top three changes.**
- Give every piece one early sentence on what the person believed and why it mattered to their people, and keep it in view through the hard parts.
- Cut dates, synods and legacy chains to the one or two that carry the story. Aim nearer 500 words than 700.
- Vary the "The X was Y" opening, and gloss a term once in the plainest word, or choose a plainer word and skip the gloss.

## What I would share

**As they are:** Patrick, Wesley, Tyndale, Luther, Nestorius and Babai. Each tells the truth about the person, faith and failings alike, and a listener can follow it in one hearing. Luther's peasants passage is hard, and it should be. I would want my people to hear it.

**With a study group only, with time to talk:** Calvin, Augustine, Cyril and Athanasius. They are honest, but dense. Calvin would need someone to add what drew people to his faith, or the room hears only Servetus. Cyril needs time on the Chalcedon paragraph.

**Not at all, as it stands:** Barsauma. It is not unfair; it is just impossible to follow. My people would come away knowing only rumors about a marriage. Timothy is close. I would share the Gospel-book story on its own, not the whole piece.

## Do they hold to what the tree is for?

Yes. `about.html` promises real, documented episodes, named sources and honest confidence. These twelve keep that promise. They name their sources, they mark what is argued, and they do not hide the record. Where they slip, it is not drift from the tree's purpose. It is density: too much record for one hearing. Barsauma is the one piece that fails the listener.
