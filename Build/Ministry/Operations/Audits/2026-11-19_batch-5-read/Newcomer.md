# Batch 5 read: the newcomer

Listener: an interested adult with no church history background, curious about where Christianity came from. English is my second language. I hear each story once, on my phone, while walking. I stay if it holds me, and leave if it loses me.

Read: `cft-website/about.html`, then `Pieces.md`. Nothing else.

## Verdict counts

Meets: 6 · Mostly: 5 · Misses: 1

## Piece by piece

### Nestorius — Mostly
- **Took away:** A bishop of Constantinople was thrown out in 431 over what to call Mary. He died in exile, hiding his name on his last book. A church in Persia kept his book but said he was not its founder.
- **Why:** The ending is moving, but the opening is a puzzle (a book under another man's name, a Syriac translator, Persia) before I know who anyone is.
- **Where I'd stop:** Nearly in the first paragraph. Three names and two languages arrive before the story does. If I get past it, I stay. The "natures" quote is the next weak point. I hear the words but cannot hold the idea while walking.

### Cyril of Alexandria — Mostly
- **Took away:** A powerful bishop in Egypt won the fight against Nestorius. Riots, a murdered philosopher and gold for the court surround him. Egypt's church still calls him its great teacher.
- **Why:** The burning bush opening and the riot story hold me, but the last third (Chalcedon, "one incarnate nature", Severus) turns into a theology lesson I cannot follow by ear.
- **Where I'd stop:** At the paragraph on 451. "Two natures" against "one nature" sounds like the same thing said twice, and then a new name, Severus, arrives.

### Barsauma of Nisibis — Misses
- **Took away:** A border bishop in Persia had a quarrel with his church's head, and something about married priests. I am not sure what he did or why he matters.
- **Why:** Too many names, synods and chroniclers who disagree, with no single person or scene to hold on to.
- **Where I'd stop:** Around the third paragraph. By then I have heard Beth Lapat, the catholicos, Babowai, Theodore of Mopsuestia and "the year 424". Then three chroniclers disagree about a nun. I can no longer tell which "historian" or "rival church" is which. The opening, a synod left out of a book, is a scholar's hook, not a listener's.

### Babai the Great — Meets
- **Took away:** For twenty years a Persian king refused the Christians a leader, so an abbot, not even a bishop, held the church together. He was hard on his monks. He set out its teaching on Christ, then refused the top job at the end.
- **Why:** A clear hook (a king swearing by the Sun), a clear man, a hard side shown honestly, and an angel at the end that I will remember.
- **Where I'd stop:** I would not. The 612 paragraph is dense, but the quote, "we do not say that God was crucified", is plain enough to carry me.

### Timothy I of Baghdad — Meets
- **Took away:** A church leader in Baghdad under the caliphs sent bishops as far as China. He won his post with a trick of sacks full of stones, and let a Gospel book stand in for a missing bishop.
- **Why:** Three images I can see (the stone in China, the sacks, the book on the chair), and they show why he matters.
- **Where I'd stop:** I might drift at the split with the bishop of Elam and the list of caliphs. The Gospel-book story brings me back.

### Martin Luther — Meets
- **Took away:** A German teacher said God's forgiveness cannot be bought. He refused to take it back before the emperor, put the Bible into simple German, and then told rulers to kill rebel peasants.
- **Why:** It tells me plainly why he matters, and it does not hide the ugly part.
- **Where I'd stop:** Not really, but it is long. The dates in the 1520 paragraph (June, December, January) blur together.

### William Tyndale — Meets
- **Took away:** An English scholar printed the Bible in English abroad and smuggled it home. He was strangled and burned for it, and a farmer who could not read learned it by heart.
- **Why:** Strong scenes from start to finish, and I understand exactly why he matters.
- **Where I'd stop:** I would not. The paragraph on his books about kings is the weakest, but it is short.

### John Calvin — Mostly
- **Took away:** A shy French scholar was frightened into staying at Geneva. He built a strict church there that France and Scotland copied, and the city burned a man he had wanted dead.
- **Why:** The opening and the gunshot ending are excellent, but the middle about offices, elders, councils and who could bar people from communion is a rulebook, not a story.
- **Where I'd stop:** At the paragraph that starts "His rules set pastors to preach". Four offices and a court with a new word, "consistory", come in one breath. I would skip ahead if I could.

### John Wesley — Meets
- **Took away:** An English priest felt his heart warmed at a prayer meeting and preached outdoors to thousands. He organised people into small weekly groups, and those groups became the Methodist churches.
- **Why:** The penny-a-week class story shows me how a movement actually starts, and the ending is warm without being sweet.
- **Where I'd stop:** The short paragraphs on 1744, 1784 and the American church feel like a list of facts. I would stay, but my attention would drop there.

### Augustine of Hippo — Mostly
- **Took away:** A North African teacher of speech heard a child say "take and read", became a bishop and wrote about God's grace. His ideas shaped and split the Western churches for a thousand years.
- **Why:** The garden scene is the best moment in the batch, but the piece is the longest and keeps going through Donatists, Pelagius, Orange, Luther, Calvin and Jansen.
- **Where I'd stop:** First, at the opening. "Milan, where the emperor then held court." is not a full sentence, and heard once, it sounds like I missed something. Later, I would leave around Pelagius. The story is over for me, and the piece becomes a list of later fights.

### Patrick — Meets
- **Took away:** A young Briton was enslaved in Ireland and escaped. He chose to go back as a bishop to the people who had held him, and was mistreated by kings and by his own church.
- **Why:** His own voice, a slave's story, and an honest note that the legends came later; I know who he was and why he matters.
- **Where I'd stop:** I would not.

### Athanasius of Alexandria — Mostly
- **Took away:** A bishop of Alexandria held to the creed of Nicaea that the Son is fully God. He was driven out five times, and he named the books of the New Testament we still have.
- **Why:** A gripping first scene, then a run of years and exiles I cannot keep in order by ear.
- **Where I'd stop:** At the paragraph "He came home in 337 …". It has six dates in a few sentences. The Easter letter that names the 27 books is the thing a newcomer would most want to hear, and by then I might be gone.

## Across all twelve

### 1. What works
The best pieces open on one scene I can see and then tell me who the person was. They keep one thread and end on a human moment.
- **Tyndale:** printers boasting over wine, a run up the river, a farmer who knows the book by heart. Every part serves one story: an English Bible, and its cost.
- **Patrick:** his own words, a simple life (slave, escape, return), and honesty about what we do not know.
- **Timothy:** three concrete images that each explain something about his church.

The plain glosses help me a lot. "Bishop, the leader of the church" and "communion, the shared bread and wine" mean I am never lost on a single word.

The pieces are honest about bad deeds and about who is telling the story ("a hostile historian", "the winning side"). That matches what `about.html` promises: grades and named sources, nothing contested shown as settled.

### 2. What doesn't work
- **Barsauma:** a story about sources, not about a man. Too many names, and conflicting chroniclers that I cannot hold apart by ear.
- **Athanasius and Calvin:** each has a strong frame around a middle of dates or rules that reads like notes.
- **Augustine:** too long, a fragment for an opening, and a tail of later history after the story has ended.

As a pattern, the weak moments are always the same: a paragraph that piles up dates, offices or named sources. These work on a page, where I can look back. On a walk, I cannot.

### 3. What my listener would want
- One sentence early on that places this person on the tree: which river they belong to, and why it is on the map. Luther, Wesley and Calvin do this. Barsauma and Babai assume I already know what "the Church of the East" is and why it split.
- Fewer people per story. Each new name costs me something. Two or three named people besides the subject is about my limit.
- A last line that ties back to today, as Athanasius's 27 books and Luther's German Bible do. "Why should I care now?" is my real question.

### 4. One voice, or a template?
It is one voice, and mostly a human one. It is calm, serious and specific, like a good documentary. It does not sound like a chatbot. There is no filler and no "it is important to note".

But the template shows when you hear several in a row. Each opens with a scene, then "The friar was Martin Luther" or "The scholar was John Calvin". A summary paragraph follows, then source-tagged episodes, then the death. By the fifth piece I could predict the shape. The glosses also start to sound mechanical. "Bishop" is explained again in almost every piece, each time a little differently. Some glosses sit right next to each other ("Its rites, its set services"), and by ear they sound like a list of different things.

### 5. Top three changes for future batches
1. **One thread per piece.** Pick the one story that shows why this person is on the tree. Cut paragraphs that only add dates, offices or extra named sources. Barsauma, Calvin's middle and Athanasius's exile years show the cost.
2. **Cap the names and dates a listener must hold.** As a working rule, no more than two or three people besides the subject, and no paragraph with more than two years in it.
3. **Place the person on the tree in the first half-minute.** Name the river or family they belong to and what it is, in plain words, before the detail starts. Vary the "The X was Y" reveal so the pattern does not show.

## Do they hold to what the tree is for?
Mostly, yes. `about.html` describes a map of movements and the links between them, with honest grades and named sources. These pieces fit that. They are sourced, they say what is contested, and most of them make clear which family of churches the person shaped. The drift is not in honesty. It is in reach: a few pieces are written for someone who already knows the field, not for someone hearing it once. Barsauma is the clear case of that drift. Athanasius, Calvin, Augustine and Cyril drift that way for one paragraph each.
