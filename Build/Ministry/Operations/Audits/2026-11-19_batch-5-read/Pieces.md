# The pieces

Twelve Church Family Tree stories, each heard once by narration, about 150 words a minute. Each is the text exactly as it will be narrated, with its census id and the tradition it is told within.

## Nestorius  (`nestorius`)

Told within: `antiochene-exegetical-christianity-chrysostom-ce` · 568 words

A book about Christ came down to us under the name of Heracleides, a man who lived near Damascus. Its translator into Syriac, the Aramaic language of a church in Persia, explains why: "The writer chose to give the book this man's name. He feared that his own name, which many hated, would stop them from reading it and turning to the truth." The writer was Nestorius.

From 428 he was bishop, the leader of the churches, in Constantinople, the empire's capital. He taught that Christ is God and man, the two kept distinct yet worshiped as one Christ. So he would not simply call Mary "Mother of God", the one who gave birth to God. In 431 a council at Ephesus, a meeting of bishops called to settle teaching, was led by his enemy, Cyril of Alexandria. It judged that he split Christ in two and removed him from office. He denied it, and died in exile, forced to live far away under guard. Historians still argue over what he really taught.

Socrates, a historian of the city in his lifetime, tells of the first sermon this newcomer from Antioch preached. In it he asked the emperor for "the earth purged of heretics", people the church judged to teach falsely. Later he had the emperor take churches from a rival Christian group.

Socrates also says he rejected the name "Mother of God" outright. Nestorius says he offered a name drawn from the Gospel's own words, "Mother of Christ".

In a sermon kept by his opponents, he said: "God is not separated from the one who is seen. So I do not separate the honor of the one who is not separated. I separate the natures, but I unite the worship." The natures are what Christ is as God and as man, two, yet Christians bow in worship to one Christ. Socrates, who blames his temper, says he did not deny that Christ was God, but feared the name "as though it were some terrible phantom".

In 435, four years after the council, the emperor's law ordered his writings burned. Banished the next year to southern Egypt, he was dragged to and fro by soldiers, old and sick, with "a mangled hand and side", his own letters say.

Near the end, perhaps in 451, he wrote his defense in Greek under the borrowed name. In it he offered his own name to be cut off under the church's solemn curse: "So let Nestorius be anathema. Only let them say of God what I beg them to say."

His Greek is lost. The book survives because the Church of the East, the church of Persia beyond the Roman empire, put it into Syriac, perhaps a century later. Its rites, its set services, call him "the persecuted martyr", one who suffered for the faith. Yet that church would not be called his. In 1298 its chief bishop of Nisibis wrote that Nestorius had never been its patriarch, its head. They had held the faith in two natures already, he wrote, and they refused to cut him off: "Nestorius, then, followed them, and not they him."

When and how he died is not known. In his last book he bids the desert rejoice for him. He calls the land of his exile his mother, which will guard his body until the resurrection, when the dead rise at the end of time.

## Cyril of Alexandria  (`cyril-of-alexandria`)

Told within: `cyrilline-miaphysite-egyptian-christianity` · 599 words

In the story of Moses, a bush in the desert burns but is not burned up. Cyril of Alexandria read it as a picture of Christ: "He came like fire upon the bush in the desert. The fire kept playing over the bush, and yet the bush was not burned up." God took a human life, he meant, and that life was not lost.

Cyril was bishop, the leader of the church, in Alexandria, Egypt's great port city, from 412 to 444. He taught that Christ is one: God's own Son, whom John's Gospel calls the Word, was himself born of Mary. He led the attack on Nestorius, bishop of Constantinople, which had him removed from office in 431. His words became the test of faith for the churches of Egypt, Syria and Armenia that later broke with the council of 451, a meeting of bishops.

Socrates, a historian writing in Cyril's lifetime and hostile to him, tells how Cyril took the bishop's chair amid a riot, though the army had backed a rival. At once he shut the churches of the Novatians, a strict Christian group outside his own, and seized their goods.

Socrates blames him for what followed. Cyril threatened the Jewish leaders with the harshest measures, and some Jews then killed Christians in a night ambush. Cyril took the synagogues and drove the Jews from the city, letting a crowd plunder them. A monk who had come to fight for Cyril struck the emperor's governor with a stone and died under torture. Cyril hailed him as one who died for Christ, until he let it be forgotten. In 415 a Christian mob led by a church reader, who read Scripture aloud in services, killed the philosopher Hypatia. Socrates says the shame fell on Cyril and his whole church; a chronicler of Egypt's own church, more than two centuries later, praised the deed. Whether Cyril was to blame is still argued.

In Constantinople, Nestorius would not simply call Mary the one who gave birth to God. Cyril wrote to him of Mary, the holy Virgin: "An ordinary man was not born first of the holy Virgin, with the Word coming down on him afterwards. Rather, the Word, united from the womb itself, is said to have gone through birth in the flesh." So she is Mother of God, because the one born of her was the Word himself.

In 431 a council at Ephesus approved the letter Cyril had written to Nestorius about Mary. Afterward Cyril's own archdeacon, his chief assistant, listed gifts to the emperor's court and a debt of 1,500 pounds of gold. Nestorius called it "the gold of iniquity"; a historian of the councils calls such gifts custom.

In 451 the council of Chalcedon took his letters as a guide, yet said Christ is known in two natures, fully God and fully human. The churches of Egypt and Syria heard that as Nestorius's split returning, and held to Cyril's "one nature" instead. Cyril had explained it himself as "one incarnate nature of the Word": the Word himself become a human being, body and soul, with "no commingling, no confusion". Severus, bishop of Antioch, who later hid in Egypt, settled a question from "the letter of the holy Cyril to Nestorius. Every word he spoke, one could say, is a law of the church."

Cyril died in 444. The Coptic church, Egypt's own, keeps his day in its calendar of saints. It says he fell slightly ill and died in peace after thirty-two years as bishop, his letters still in the hands of the faithful.

## Barsauma of Nisibis  (`barsauma-of-nisibis`)

Told within: `the-church-of-the-east-under-persia` · 570 words

The Church of the East gathered its synods, its councils of bishops, into one book. Its compiler, about three centuries later, left one out on purpose. Barsauma had held it at Beth Lapat, and he himself had said it was held "out of ill will and human passion."

Barsauma was bishop of Nisibis, a city on the border between the Roman and Persian empires, in the late 400s. In 484 he led that synod against the catholicos, the head of his church at the Persian capital, and later took it back in writing. Yet his church kept what his side stood for: married clergy, from bishops to assistant ministers, and the Greek Bible teacher Theodore of Mopsuestia as its sure guide. Writers of the churches that broke with it remembered him as the man who set the Persian church apart. The Persian church had run its own affairs since 424; what his synods set apart was its teaching and its rules.

In April 484 came the break. By his own account, "Satan stirred up a quarrel" between him and the head of the church, Babowai. At Beth Lapat, Barsauma and the bishops on his side drew up a written case against Babowai and made rules on clergy marriage. That year the Persian king put Babowai to death. A later historian of Barsauma's own church says Barsauma passed Babowai's letter to the king and then regretted it, while writers of a rival church say he had him killed. His own letters speak only of the quarrel.

That historian, writing in the 1100s, says Barsauma then married a nun, a woman vowed to a single life in religion, on the king's order. A chronicler of the rival Syriac church, in the 1200s, says instead that he kept a woman and called her his lawful wife. A hostile chronicle of the 1100s, built on old men's memories, says he made the marriage rule so he would not be blamed for her.

In 485, with Acacius as the new head of the church, Barsauma took back everything done at Beth Lapat, its rules included. His six letters, the nearest witness to his life, show a frontier pressed by drought, raiding tribes and a Roman army. He asked Acacius to cut the rebels in his own city off from the church's worship and fellowship, "like the doctor who amputates," and to threaten to report them to the king.

A synod in 486, which he stayed away from but accepted in advance, confessed Christ fully God and fully human, the two never mixed. And it barred bishops from stopping their clergy marrying and expected new assistant ministers to be married.

In 497 a synod let every cleric, from the head of the church down, marry one wife openly. It accepted Beth Lapat "but only on this point." Writers of the rival Syriac church drew on a letter written from old men's memories a century and a half later. They said he told the Persian king his Christians would never be loyal until their faith differed from the Romans'. With the king's soldiers, they said, he killed thousands who refused.

In 605 a synod ruled that every teacher in the church must study Theodore's writings. It quoted Beth Lapat's ruling on Theodore. It called the synod left out of the book "the divine assembly," and its leader, with the title Mar, "my lord," "the blessed Mar Barsauma."

## Babai the Great  (`babai-the-great`)

Told within: `the-church-of-the-east-under-persia` · 492 words

Thomas of Marga tells how the Persian king Khosrau swore by the Sun, his god, that while he lived, the Christians of his land would have no new head of their church. He kept his word for close to twenty years. In that time the bishops, the leaders over each city and its region, turned to a man who was not even a bishop.

He was Babai, a monk vowed to prayer, poverty and an unmarried life, and abbot, or head, of the Great Monastery on Mount Izla, near Nisibis. For the Church of the East, the church of the Persian empire, he drove out of its monasteries those the bishops judged heretics, people teaching what the church had condemned. He also wrote the Book of the Union, on how God and man are joined in the one Christ. His church kept and honored his books, and historians judge that they helped settle its teaching on Christ.

Thomas, who tells most of his story two centuries later, says some monks at Izla were found living with wives. Babai and the senior monks stripped them of the monk's robe, drove them out with their families and burned their huts. Babai then drove out a gentle monk named Jacob, unjustly, Thomas says, and condemned him in writing. The monks rebuked their abbot, and many left, and founded monasteries across the East, among them Jacob's, where Thomas would later write.

In 612, Babai says, the king told the bishops to show him first which faith was true, and then they would have a head. In Christ, they wrote, are two natures, what makes him God and what makes him human, each real and complete, in one person. But while they honored Nestorius, their teacher, a bishop of Constantinople condemned in 431, the king answered, they would have no head. Babai's Book of the Union says: "Here too, in the same way, we say that Christ was crucified, that Christ died and rose again. But we do not say that God was crucified and died." The man Jesus suffered and God did not, yet both are one Christ. That teaching set his church apart from its neighbors. Two centuries on, Thomas counted eighty-four of his works still honored in the church.

In 628, after the king was murdered, the bishops all begged Babai, Thomas says, to become head of the church. He refused, choosing to end his days in his hut. Thomas tells that an angel on a white horse, with a fiery sword, then stood before it. It had stood beside him, it said, all the years he took the place of the head of the church, and now must go to the new one. Babai answered: "If I had known you were with me, I would have taken on this great work with all care. But now go in peace, and pray for me." Babai, by then an old man, died about that time.

## Timothy I of Baghdad  (`timothy-i-of-baghdad`)

Told within: `the-church-of-the-east-on-the-silk-road` · 541 words

Early in 781, Christians in China's capital set up a great stone. On it, in Syriac, the language of their church, they named their patriarch, the head of the whole church, far away in Mesopotamia. But he was dead, and a new patriarch had been installed months before; the news, it seems, had not yet reached them.

The new patriarch was Timothy. For more than forty years he led the Church of the East, the church of the Christians of Mesopotamia and Persia. He led it from Baghdad, under five caliphs, the Muslim rulers of the empire. Historians of his church say he argued for his faith at the caliphs' court. And he sent monks out as bishops, the church's leaders for a region, and let them make more bishops where they were. His letters show a church that reached toward the Turks and China.

Timothy was already a bishop when the patriarch before him died. Mari, a historian of his church writing more than three centuries later, says Timothy showed the electors sacks he said held money for them, and won. When the electors asked for their money, he answered, "The priesthood is by no means sold for money," and showed them the sacks held only stones. Other later histories tell the trick differently.

The chief bishop of Elam, a rival for the post, refused to worship or work with him. Then the chief bishop of Elam and thirteen bishops declared Timothy cursed and cut off from the church, and he cut them off in turn. The split lasted about two years, by Timothy's own count, and in it Christians fought, killed and jailed one another.

A list of his writings, made five centuries later, names a debate with the caliph al-Mahdi. Another historian of his church, five centuries later, says the next caliph, al-Hadi, sent for him on most days to put hard questions to him about religion.

Thomas of Marga, a monk who wrote about twenty years after Timothy's death and had read his letters, tells of the bishops Timothy sent. Two brothers from one monastery, Kardagh and Yahballaha, went as chief bishops to Gilan and Dailom, south of the Caspian Sea. When many peoples there came to the faith, Yahballaha asked leave to make bishops. But to ordain a bishop, to make one, took three bishops, and the brothers were two, far from Baghdad, where a new bishop normally went to have his making confirmed.

Timothy wrote back. The book of the four Gospels, the life of Jesus, read in worship, would stand in for the missing bishop, laid on the throne, the bishop's chair in the church. "Make bishops of whomever you and the pious Kardagh choose. In place of the third bishop, let the Book of the Gospels be laid on the throne at the right hand. So, by the hand of God, ordain the first bishop." They made seven bishops. From Timothy's letters, Thomas learned of a chief bishop of Beth Sinaye, which scholars take to be China.

He died in the early 820s, under the fifth caliph; one chronicle gives 9 January 823. Mari says he was ninety-five. He was buried at Baghdad, in the monastery he had restored and made his home.

## Martin Luther  (`martin-luther`)

Told within: `lutheran-wittenberg-and-its-congregations` · 666 words

On 31 October 1517, a friar, living under religious vows, wrote to the archbishop of Mainz, a great prince of the church. Pardons, letters said to cancel punishment for sin, were being sold in the archbishop's name for the pope's new church in Rome. People believed, he warned, that a pardon made them sure of heaven.

The friar was Martin Luther, who taught at Wittenberg, a university town in eastern Germany. He taught that forgiveness is grace, God's free gift, received by faith, by trusting what God promises, so it could not be bought. Condemned by the pope, he would not take back his books before the emperor, ruler of the German lands and more, unless Scripture, the Bible, or clear reason proved him wrong. Made an outlaw, whom anyone could seize, he was hidden away and put the New Testament, the gospels and letters of the early church, into German. The Lutheran churches, grown from his teaching, carry his name.

With his letter he sent theses, short statements for a university debate. One put his teaching in a line: "Every true Christian, living or dead, has a share in all the good things of Christ and the church. God gives it to him, even without letters of pardon."

In June 1520 the pope's decree condemned his books, ordered them burned and gave him sixty days to take them back. In December 1520 he burned the popes' book of church law, and editors say the pope's decree against him burned with it. In January the pope cut him off from the church.

In April 1521, under the emperor's promise of safe passage there and back, he came to Worms. His friends printed his answer: "Unless I am convinced by the witness of the Scriptures or by clear reason … I cannot and will not take back anything." It ends, "God help me. Amen." "Here I stand, I cannot do otherwise" appears only in prints from one Wittenberg press.

In May the emperor, Charles V, made him an outlaw: no one was to house or feed him. Hidden on his friends' advice, he made that German New Testament, printed in 1522. He wrote that it must be in the simplest language, so that all could understand it. He wanted the people to sing in their own language, not only to hear trained singers. By one editor's count, four of the eight hymns in Wittenberg's first hymnbook, in 1524, were his.

In 1525 peasants across the German lands rose against their lords. Luther first warned both sides. The rulers, and above all the bishops, had only themselves to thank for the rising, he wrote, for they did nothing but rob their people. To the peasants he said that whoever takes the sword will perish by the sword. Then armed bands began robbing and burning. He urged rulers, and anyone who could, to strike them down: "So let everyone who can strike, kill and stab, in secret or in the open. Remember that nothing can be more poisonous, harmful or devilish than a rebel."

One friend wrote to him that people who favored him found it strange that he let the tyrants kill without mercy. In July he held to his call: rulers should strike quickly, guilty or innocent. But once they had won, he said, they should show mercy even to the guilty, and he condemned the "furious, raving, senseless tyrants" who could not get their fill of blood. A later, hostile chronicle of the Anabaptists, a church of believers baptized as adults, said he had leaned on worldly power.

The whole Bible followed in German by 1534, finished with his colleagues, editors say. His churches kept his songs, and two centuries later the hymnbook of Halle, a Lutheran town in Germany, still printed them.

In 1539 he looked back on putting the Bible into German. He had hoped for less writing and more reading of Scripture, so that each might "drink for himself from the fresh spring".

## William Tyndale  (`william-tyndale`)

Told within: `the-english-reformation-and-elizabethan-settleme` · 639 words

In 1525, at Cologne on the Rhine, an English New Testament, the second part of the Bible, was on the press. A German churchman, an enemy of Luther, told years later how he heard the printers boasting of it over their wine and had the city council stop the press. Two Englishmen, he wrote, fled up the river with the printed sheets.

One was William Tyndale, a priest ordained to lead worship, and a scholar. He was putting the New Testament, its gospels and letters, into English straight from the Greek they were first written in, not the church's Latin. At home that needed leave from a bishop, a senior church officer, so he printed abroad and had the books smuggled in, where the king ordered them burned. He would offer that king his life for the Bible's words alone, and in 1536 he was put to death.

Only this, he wrote, moved him to translate: people outside the church's ordained ranks could hold no truth unless the Bible lay plain before their eyes in their own tongue. At Worms in 1526 he printed the Testament in thousands.

Tyndale then wrote about kings and their power, and one book taught that the king answers to God alone. The king praised it, in the story told by John Foxe, who later collected the stories of Protestants put to death. In 1530 Tyndale printed the first five books of the Bible, put into English from the Hebrew they were written in. Another book, on the bishops' dealings with kings, angered the king most, Tyndale told the king's envoy. And he called the churchmen who fought his translation hypocrites.

In 1531 that envoy met him outside Antwerp and asked him to come home. Tyndale would not come home on any promise. He asked only for the bare text of Scripture, the Bible's words without his notes. The envoy reported his answer: "Suppose the king, of his grace, grants his people only the bare text of the Scripture … in the translation of anyone he pleases. Then I will at once make a faithful promise never to write again. … I offer my body to suffer whatever pain or torture, yes, whatever death the king wills, if only this is granted."

No answer from the king is known, and within a year the king's envoys were ordered to seize him. In 1535, as Foxe tells it, an Englishman who had won his trust pointed him out to waiting officers in Antwerp. After more than a year in the castle of Vilvoorde, near Brussels, he was condemned under the law of the emperor, whose officers ruled there. He was tied to a post and strangled, and his body was burned. Foxe says he cried out, "Lord, open the king of England's eyes."

Foxe kept the readers' stories too, of people whipped, jailed and questioned for the book. John Maundrel, a Wiltshire farmer who could not read, had others read the Testament to him until he knew most of it by heart. Some readers were later burned as heretics, for teaching the church condemned as false. Maundrel himself was burned at Salisbury in 1556, when the old church was restored under Queen Mary. Smuggled in faster than bishops could burn it, the book had reached people no one had allowed to have it.

Within a year of Tyndale's death, the archbishop of Canterbury, England's senior bishop, praised a new English Bible, and the king allowed it to be bought and read. It bore another man's name, but editors find that most of it was Tyndale's.

In 1533 he had written to a friend in prison. He called God to witness that he had never changed one syllable of God's word against his conscience, and never would, for all the pleasure, honor or riches on earth.

## John Calvin  (`john-calvin`)

Told within: `the-reformed-cities-zurich-and-geneva` · 646 words

In August 1536 a young French scholar, already the author of a book that defended his persecuted fellow believers, stopped at Geneva for one night, on his way to Strasbourg. The city's preacher, William Farel, begged him to stay, then did something more frightening. The scholar told it himself, twenty years later: "When he saw that asking got him nowhere, he went as far as a curse. He called on God to curse my quiet life if I held back from helping in so great a need. Struck by that terror, I gave up the journey I had begun."

The scholar was John Calvin. His book was the Institutes, a book of instruction in the Christian faith. He stayed, and gave Geneva a written plan for its church, with four kinds of office. Churches in France and Scotland built theirs the same way, and his name became the label for a family of churches begun at Zurich before him.

Born in France in 1509, he says he left it to hide in some quiet corner. But believers burned there were being slandered as rebels, so he wrote a short handbook to the French king to clear their name.

In 1538 Geneva's citizens voted him and Farel out for defying a ban on their preaching. In 1540 Geneva's councils wrote begging him back. He went back in 1541, but would take up his post only once the city swore to keep a catechism, a book of questions and answers on the faith. It also swore to keep a church discipline, a system for correcting members' lives.

His rules set pastors to preach, baptize and give communion, the shared bread and wine of worship. Teachers taught sound teaching, and deacons cared for the poor and sick. Twelve elders, laymen from the city's councils, watched over people's lives and met weekly with the pastors as the consistory, a church court that questioned and warned. But the councils kept the power to punish. Who could bar people from communion stayed in dispute between them until 1555. In 1547, while that dispute went on, the city beheaded a man for speaking against God, for acts against the city's government, and for threatening the pastors.

By then the much larger Institutes taught that God chooses beforehand who will be saved. In Calvin's words, "Some are ordained beforehand to eternal life, others to eternal damnation," to life with God, or to being lost forever. In 1551, as Geneva's pastors recorded it, a doctor of medicine, Jérôme Bolsec, rose at their weekly Bible meeting. He said this made God a tyrant. He was jailed, and when Geneva asked other Swiss churches, Bern's pastors wrote that some said he was not so bad a man, and hoped for agreement. Geneva still sent him away for good.

In 1553 the city council burned Michael Servetus alive for books against infant baptism and against the teaching that God is three persons in one. It had first asked the advice of four Swiss churches. Calvin had written that if Servetus came, he would not leave alive, and during the trial he hoped for a death sentence, though a less cruel one.

Others built the same offices with no friendly city behind them. In 1559, when going to Geneva could mean death, French churches met secretly to adopt a statement of belief and a rulebook with pastors, elders and deacons. In 1562 Scotland's church ruled to give baptism and communion, marry and bury as Geneva's church did.

Gravely ill in 1564, Calvin spoke to the city's pastors, and one of them wrote it down: "I have lived here amid extraordinary battles. In mockery, I was greeted in the evening outside my door with fifty or sixty gunshots. Think how that could shake a poor, timid scholar, as I am and always have been, I confess." He died that May.

## John Wesley  (`john-wesley`)

Told within: `the-methodist-revival` · 595 words

One May evening in 1738, John Wesley went very unwillingly to a society meeting, a weekly prayer group inside the church, in Aldersgate Street, London. As someone read Luther's preface to Paul's letter to the Romans, Wesley felt his heart warmed.

He wrote, "I felt that I did trust in Christ, in Christ alone, for salvation. And I was given an assurance that he had taken away my sins, even mine, and saved me from the law of sin and death." He meant a felt certainty of God's forgiveness, and freedom from sin's hold and from death, its end. That night, his brother wrote, he declared, "I believe."

He was a priest of the state Church of England, a minister formally set apart, or ordained. From 1739 he preached outdoors to thousands. He gathered his converts into societies, split into weekly classes of about twelve under a leader who asked how each lived. Mocked as Methodists, a nickname at first, they became the Methodist churches.

In 1739 George Whitefield called him to Bristol, a port city in the west of England, where Whitefield had begun preaching outdoors. Wesley could barely accept it, for he had thought saving souls, bringing people to faith and forgiveness, almost a sin outside a church. Yet on 2 April, in a brickyard by the city, he preached to about three thousand people.

By his own account, the bishop of Bristol, the senior churchman over that region, told him he had no business preaching in his area and advised him to go. Wesley stayed.

The class came by accident. In 1742 the Bristol society agreed to pay off a building debt at a penny a week from each member who could. To collect it, they divided into classes of about twelve, each with one collector. When the collectors told him some members were not living as they should, he saw in it the very thing they had long wanted. From then on each leader saw every member weekly, and advised, rebuked or comforted them. Some changed, and some were put out.

The work drew violence. At Wednesbury, in 1743, a mob surrounded the house where he was writing, crying, "Bring out the minister." That night a second mob dragged him through Walsall, many crying out to knock his brains out. That winter, one of his preachers reported, mobs in that district beat Methodists and plundered their houses.

His preachers were ordinary men who had not been ordained. At a yearly meeting with his preachers, first held in 1744, they allowed such men only in cases of necessity.

After American independence, in 1784, Wesley ordained two preachers for America himself, though only bishops normally did so.

That year the Americans formed their own Episcopal church, meaning one led by bishops. In 1791 his preachers counted more than 130,000 members in Europe and America.

An American preacher later wrote that the American Methodists kept the written Rules Wesley had drawn up for the societies, with very little alteration. Their rule book still set out classes under a leader. Richard Allen, who later founded the African Methodist Episcopal Church, chose Methodism for its good discipline. Yet he wrote that some preachers became tyrants to Black members, turning them out without a trial.

The day before he died, too weak to make himself understood, he gathered his strength and said twice, "The best of all is, God is with us." A woman who kept his house wrote it down. He died the next morning, 2 March 1791, nearly eighty-eight, with friends kneeling around the bed.

The teacher was Augustine. He taught rhetoric, the art of persuasive public speaking. In a garden in Milan, he says, a child's voice sent him to the Bible, and he turned to Christ. He became bishop, the leader of the church, in Hippo, a port town in Roman North Africa. There he wrote the Confessions, his life told as a prayer to God, and the City of God, on those who love God above themselves. He taught that grace, God's free and unearned gift, comes first: no one even begins to believe unless God gives it. The Western churches carried that teaching on, and it both shaped and divided them.

Born in Roman Africa, he taught at Carthage and Rome, then Milan; in his own words, he sold the skill of talking to win. At Milan he heard its bishop, Ambrose, preach. There, in 386, came the moment he tells in his Confessions. Torn and weeping, he threw himself down under a fig tree. Then, he writes: "And look, I hear a voice from the house next door, singing and repeating it again and again, like a boy's or a girl's voice, I do not know which: 'Take it and read, take it and read.'" He opened Paul's letters at the first lines he saw: not drunken revels or quarrels, but put on the Lord Jesus Christ. He told his mother, Monica, who rejoiced, and at Easter Ambrose baptized him, washing him in water to join the church.

Back in Africa, he became bishop of Hippo. Early in those years, a bishop of Milan sent him questions on Paul's letter to the Romans. Answering them, he changed his mind about free will, a person's own power to choose good or evil. Near the end of his life, calling Paul the apostle, he looked back: "In answering this question, I worked hard for the free choice of the human will. But God's grace won. I could only come to see that the apostle spoke the plainest truth: 'Who sets you apart? What do you have that you did not receive?'"

Beside his church stood the Donatists, a rival African church, for a century the larger one. It held that bishops who had handed over the Bible in persecution had lost the church. At first, Augustine wrote in about 408, he held that no one should be forced into the church; argument alone should win. But his fellow bishops pointed to his own town, brought back by fear of the emperors' laws. He came to back those laws to force the Donatists back, citing Jesus's parable: compel them to come in.

In the same years Pelagius, a monk at Rome, held that God had already given human nature the power to do good. Augustine answered that free will and good teaching are not enough: God's Spirit must give the love of the good. His friend Possidius, who later wrote his life to honor him, tells it from the winning side. He says meetings of African bishops moved two bishops of Rome, the leaders of the church there, to condemn Pelagius's followers. The emperor's laws then punished them as heretics, false teachers cut off from the church.

He died in 430, at seventy-six, while the Vandals, a people from beyond the empire's frontier, besieged Hippo. Possidius says he told them to keep the church's library.

Those books carried his teaching on. In 529 a meeting of bishops at Orange, in what is now France, set out rulings on grace drawn largely from his writings, as their modern editor shows. A thousand years later Martin Luther sent an opponent to his book On the Spirit and the Letter. Its letter is the law that commands; its spirit is God's Spirit, giving the love to keep it. John Calvin said he could build a whole volume on God's choice and grace from Augustine's words alone. Both stood against Rome. Yet inside the church united with Rome, the Catholic bishop Cornelius Jansen wrote a book called Augustinus, and Rome condemned it.

Near the end of his account, Possidius recalled a poet's lines: whoever reads the words aloud gives the writer his voice.

## Patrick  (`patrick-of-ireland`)

Told within: `insular-irish-monastic-christianity` · 524 words

Near the end of his life, a bishop in Ireland, a senior church leader who could appoint other ministers, began his account of himself: "I am Patrick, a sinner. I am utterly unlearned, and the least of all believers. Very many people look down on me."

He had first come to Ireland as a slave and escaped after six years. Years later, in the fifth century, he went back of his own will, as a bishop, to the people who had enslaved him. Almost all we know of him comes from two short Latin writings of his own. One is a letter against a warlord's soldiers, and the other is the Confession, his account of his life and thanks to God.

He grew up in Roman Britain, then a province of the empire. At about sixteen he was carried off to Ireland. There he tended flocks and prayed, he says, up to a hundred times a day, until a voice in his sleep told him a ship was ready.

Back in Britain, he dreamed of a letter headed The Voice of the Irish, and heard them cry: "We beg you, holy boy, come and walk among us once more." Senior churchmen judged him too unlearned, meaning no malice, he says, but he went.

He says he baptized many thousands, washing them in water to make them Christians, and had church ministers appointed everywhere. Children of Irish chiefs, he writes, became monks, men who left ordinary life for God, and women vowed to stay unmarried for God, some against their fathers' will. The slave women among them suffered most. Kings robbed him and held him in chains for fourteen days, and every day he expected to be killed or made a slave again. At one time, some of his own senior churchmen held against him a sin from his youth, something he had once confided to his dearest friend. That same friend later shamed him in public.

At another time, soldiers of a warlord named Coroticus cut down new converts with the sword the day after their baptism, still in white, the blessing oil on their foreheads. Others they sold, he writes, as if into a brothel. Patrick called the killers fellow-citizens of demons, and told Christians not to eat with them or take their gifts until they showed sorrow and freed the captives. No record says whether any captive came home.

His writings were kept, and Armagh, a church in the north of Ireland that claimed him as founder, copied the Confession. A chronicler of his own century records a bishop sent to Irish believers in 431, and never names Patrick. More than two centuries on, a hymn copied by monks called him the teacher of the Irish. Legends grew up around him centuries later, in the work of writers who came long after.

He had longed to see Britain again, but believed he was bound to stay, praying to shed his blood with his new Christians and captives. Only the gospel, the Christian message, had brought him back, he writes. He signed his Confession as written in Ireland, by Patrick the sinner, unlearned.

## Athanasius of Alexandria  (`athanasius-of-alexandria`)

Told within: `alexandria-catechetical` · 623 words

One night in 356, soldiers surrounded a church in Alexandria, in Egypt, as its people prayed. Their bishop, the leader of the city's church, later wrote: "I thought it wrong to leave the people in such an uproar, rather than face the danger for them first." He sent the people home, and then monks, men who had left ordinary life for prayer, dragged him out past the soldiers.

This was Athanasius, bishop for forty-five years, who had to leave his city five times. At Nicaea, in today's Turkey, bishops agreed a creed, a statement of faith, in 325: God's Son is "of one being" with the Father, fully God. He held to it against the party of Arius, who said the Son had a beginning. The Son became human, he taught, so that people might be made divine, joined to God and freed from death. Only a Son who was truly God could do that. He also wrote the life of a hermit, a monk living alone, and listed the books to be read as God's word.

At Nicaea he was a deacon, an officer under the bishop, says Socrates, a later historian. He became bishop in 328. When Arius was allowed home, he refused him communion, the shared bread and wine of one church, and the emperor Constantine threatened to remove him.

Most of our record comes from Athanasius or writers who used him, but a monk of the Melitians, a breakaway church in Egypt, gave the other side. In 335 he wrote that Athanasius's followers came with soldiers and beat their monks bloody. That year a council of bishops at Tyre, a meeting that judged church matters, removed him from office. Constantine sent him far away to Trier, now in Germany.

He came home in 337, and when a rival bishop came in 339 he fled to Rome, returning in 346. After that night in 356, under Constantine's son, now emperor, he hid for six years. A chronicle of his church records that searchers tortured a woman vowed to God to find him. From hiding he wrote his defenses. Yet in 359 he wrote that those who accepted all of Nicaea's statement but its one word should be met "as brothers with brothers". Those who rejected the council he still called enemies of Christ. In those years, by his editor's dating, he wrote the Life of Antony, for monks abroad who had asked how Antony lived. The emperor Julian drove him out in 362, and in 365 he hid four months near the city before he was led home.

Each year the bishop of Alexandria wrote to the churches of Egypt and Libya to set the date of Easter. In his Easter letter of 367, Athanasius set out the books passed down as Scripture, those the church reads as God's word. He named twenty-two of the Old Testament and twenty-seven of the New. He set other books apart for new believers. Still others, he said, were made up by heretics, teachers he held false, and passed off as old. Of the true books he wrote: "Let no one add to them, or take anything away from them."

A churchman of Antioch put the Life into Latin, Jerome says, and later it reached readers in Syriac, the language of the churches of Syria and Mesopotamia. In 692 a council made his rulings church law, and Greek copies of that law carry his list. In 1921 a Church of England proposal for sharing communion with the Orthodox churches of the East took the books as Athanasius had defined them.

He died in his own city in 373. Gregory of Nazianzus, preaching a few years later, said that many tears were shed for him.
