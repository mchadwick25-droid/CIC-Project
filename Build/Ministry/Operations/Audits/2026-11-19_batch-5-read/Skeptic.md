# The skeptic's read: batch 5

Read: `cft-website/about.html`, Part 1 of the brief kit, and the twelve pieces in `Pieces.md`. Nothing else. Each piece judged as heard once. No fact was checked against sources. This is a drift check, not a call for rewrites.

Counts: 9 Holds, 3 Drifting, 0 Off.

## Each piece

**Nestorius.**
Objection: the essence states his teaching in his own terms ("kept distinct yet worshiped as one"), and the ending is tender. A listener may leave on his side. "Historians still argue over what he really taught" is a stock hedge line.
Holds. The persecuting sermon and the church seizures are told, the council's charge is stated, and every view is credited.

**Cyril of Alexandria.**
Objection: after the riot, the expulsion of the Jews and Hypatia, the last word goes to the Coptic calendar's "died in peace". The church's own warm memory closes the story.
Holds. The violence is told with force, Socrates is named as hostile, and the praise and the blame are both credited.

**Barsauma of Nisibis.**
Objection: one listener cannot hold five witnesses (a later historian, a rival church's writers, a 1200s chronicler, a hostile 1100s chronicle, a letter from old men's memories). It becomes a report about the record.
Drifting. Fairness holds, but the middle stacks witnesses, and the massacre claim sits out of order inside the 497 paragraph.

**Babai the Great.**
Objection: the angel story ends in Babai's direct reply. That reply is still inside Thomas's tale, but it is heard as a scene, not a report.
Holds. Thomas is credited throughout, the burned huts and the unjust expulsion of Jacob are told, and the angel stays in Thomas's voice.

**Timothy I of Baghdad.**
Objection: "the news, it seems, had not yet reached them" is the narrator's guess. The court debate, his best-known act, is only a line in a list.
Holds. The sack trick is credited to Mari and flagged as told differently, and the two-year split with its killings is told plainly.

**Martin Luther.**
Objection: the "strike, kill and stab" quote is framed by mitigation on both sides. "Then armed bands began robbing and burning" gives his turn a cause in the narrator's voice. His writings against the Jews are not heard, while Cyril's actions against the Jews are.
Drifting. The hardest episode is present but cushioned. Whether the outline held the Jews material was not checked, but the listener hears the gap.

**William Tyndale.**
Objection: the opponents have no faces or reasons. Only bishops who burn, a churchman who stops a press, and "the old church restored" are heard. "Smuggled in faster than bishops could burn it, the book had reached people no one had allowed to have it" is the narrator cheering.
Drifting. Foxe is credited, but the shape is a martyr story. The ending is his own oath of integrity, so no other side is heard at the end.

**John Calvin.**
Objection: after Servetus, the story ends on his self-portrait as "a poor, timid scholar".
Holds. Servetus, the 1547 beheading, Bolsec and Bern's dissent are all told with their force. The closing words are his own and credited.

**John Wesley.**
Objection: the opposition is mostly mobs and one bishop. The 1784 ordinations, a real break with his own church, pass as one neutral clause. The deathbed with kneeling friends is a pious trope, though it is credited.
Holds. Richard Allen's charge that preachers became tyrants to Black members is told plainly, and the warmed heart stays in Wesley's own words.

**Augustine of Hippo.**
Objection: the garden conversion is told twice, once in the essence and again in full, which breaks "never the same event twice". The opening is a fragment.
Holds. He backs forcing the Donatists, and the narrator flags that Possidius "tells it from the winning side". Pelagius's view is stated fairly.

**Patrick.**
Objection: his Confession is a self-defense against accusers. The narrator passes on its humility and suffering mostly at face value. "Centuries later, in the work of writers who came long after" says the same thing twice.
Holds. The one-witness problem is said once, where it matters. The silent 431 chronicle is told, and the legends are kept outside the story.

**Athanasius of Alexandria.**
Objection: the exile paragraph becomes a list of dates. Arius gets one clause while Athanasius gets the theology.
Holds. The Melitian monk's charge of beatings is heard, the record's one-sidedness is named where it bites, and his "enemies of Christ" line is kept.

## Across all twelve

**1. Witness, not judge.**
Mostly yes. The narrator credits praise and blame, and names hostile witnesses. The slips are in narrator lines that carry a side forward:
- Tyndale: "Smuggled in faster than bishops could burn it…" and "the old church was restored".
- Luther: the unattributed causal line before "strike, kill and stab", and mitigation on both sides of it.
- Nestorius, more lightly: his teaching is stated in his own sympathetic terms in the essence.
Force is not softened in Cyril, Calvin, Babai, Barsauma or Athanasius. In Luther it is cushioned. In Wesley the 1784 conflict is flattened to a neutral fact.

**2. Legend and wonders.**
Held. Babai's angel is in Thomas's voice. Augustine's voice in the garden and Patrick's dreams are in their own words. Timothy's sack trick is credited and flagged as disputed. Patrick's later legends are kept out. "Here I stand" is marked as one press's version. The weakest spot is Babai's reply to the angel, heard as a scene inside the tale.

**3. Balance across traditions.**
The Church of the East pieces (Nestorius, Barsauma, Babai, Timothy) and the Coptic pieces (Cyril, Athanasius) face named hostile witnesses and get their violence told in full. Of the Protestants, Calvin gets the same treatment. Luther, Tyndale and Wesley face mostly faceless opposition, so they come out as heroes more easily. The clearest gap: Cyril's acts against the Jews are told, while Luther's writings against them are not heard. The weight is near even. The fairness is slightly better for the older Eastern and Coptic figures than for the Protestants.

**4. Voice.**
It is mostly one narrator, but a template shows through. Tells:
- The reveal hinge. "The writer was Nestorius." "The friar was Martin Luther." "The scholar was John Calvin." "The teacher was Augustine." "The new patriarch was Timothy." "He was Babai." "This was Athanasius." "One was William Tyndale." That makes eight of twelve. The kit's open-then-essence shape is producing one stock sentence.
- The appositive gloss, "X, the Y", several times a paragraph in every piece. Heard in a row, it is a machine rhythm.
- "Still argued" lines: Nestorius ("Historians still argue…") and Cyril ("Whether Cyril was to blame is still argued").
- "Editors say" or "editors find" turns up again and again (three times in Luther, once in Tyndale).
- The same word is glossed differently from piece to piece. "Bishop" is "the leader of the churches", "a senior church officer", "the leaders over each city and its region", or "a senior church leader who could appoint other ministers". Each piece glosses alone, with no single voice behind it.

**5. Plainness.**
The glosses help with real puzzles: catholicos, anathema, consistory, the Novatians, Beth Sinaye. They start to talk down on words a grown listener knows: "Scripture, the Bible"; "the gospel, the Christian message"; "the New Testament, the second part of the Bible"; "baptized many thousands, washing them in water"; "saving souls, bringing people to faith and forgiveness"; "resurrection, when the dead rise at the end of time"; "bishop" glossed in nearly every piece. The kit says to use the precise words a grown listener knows without stopping. This is the clearest drift in the batch.

**6. Verdict on drift.**
The batch mostly holds. The core rigor rules hold: wonders stay in their witnesses' voices, hostile views are credited, and doubt is said plainly. The drift is at the edges, in three places. First, the gloss habit has hardened into a reflex that talks down, and it gives the voice a template rhythm. Second, the reveal hinge repeats so often that the opening shape is now a formula. Third, the Protestant heroes (Tyndale most, then Luther and Wesley) get softer handling of their opponents than the Eastern and Coptic figures get. Barsauma shows a different risk: fairness pursued until the story turns into a list of witnesses.

**Top three risks for future batches.**
1. Glosses on common church words: talking down, and a template rhythm.
2. Uneven hostile witness: heroes whose opponents are faceless, and narrator lines that cheer or justify.
3. Stock structure: the "The X was Y" hinge and "still argued" endings, plus witness-stacking that turns a story into a report.
