# Source sheet: Bernard of Clairvaux

Researcher's sheet for this batch ([date]). The job (Mark's, [date]): the young noble who brought his brothers into Cîteaux in 1113 and, as abbot of Clairvaux, became the most listened-to churchman in Europe: his sermons on the Song of Songs, his letters to popes, the quarrel with Abelard, and his preaching of the Second Crusade, told with its failure. ## Setting

S1. **Birth and family.** Mabillon's chronology: "1091 … Bernard was born in the castle called Fontaines, near Dijon, in Burgundy. His father was Tescelin Sorus, lord of Fontaines; his mother Alith, daughter of Bernard, lord of Montbar" (E1-ed 4360–4366). William: "Pater ejus Tecelinus [al. Tesselinus], vir antiquae et legitimae militiae fuit … Mater Aleth, ex castro cui nomen Mons-Barrus … Septem quippe liberos genuit … sex mares, feminam unam: mares omnes monachos futuros, feminam sanctimonialem" (VP 15616–15631; "his father Tescelin was a man of old and lawful knighthood … his mother Aleth, from the castle called Montbard … she bore seven children, six sons and one daughter: the sons all to be monks, the daughter a nun"). Witness: William (contemporary) for the family; the year is the editor's. William's own reckoning (S3) puts the birth about 1090. Confidence: noble knightly family, Documented (William); year 1090 or 1091, Widely Accepted (editor; say "around 1090").

S2. **Cîteaux before him.** Mabillon: "1098. B. Robert, Abbot of Molêsmes, taking with him twenty-one monks of the same house, withdrew into the desert of Citeaux" (E1-ed 4368–4371). William: the little flock of Cîteaux under abbot Stephen was weary of its smallness, "and all hope of posterity" was failing, since all admired its holiness but fled its austerity: "cum jam graviter ei taedio esse inciperet paucitas sua, et omnis spes posteritatis deficeret … venerantibus omnibus in eis vitae sanctitatem, sed refugientibus austeritatem" (VP 16264–16280, two columns run together; damaged). CEN `cistercian-monasticism` gives 1098, Molesme, the plain Rule, and Stephen Harding as third abbot. Confidence: Documented (William for the house's straits; editor and tree for 1098).

S3. **The year 1113.** William: "Anno ab incarnatione Domini millesimo centesimo decimo tertio, a constitutione domus Cisterciensis quindecimo, servus Dei Bernardus annos natus circiter tres et viginti [al. duo et viginti], Cistercium ingressus, cum sociis amplius quam triginta, sub abbate Stephano, suavi jugo Christi collum submisit" (VP 16326–16332; "In the year 1113, the fifteenth from the founding of the house of Cîteaux, God's servant Bernard, about twenty-three years old [another reading: twenty-two], entered Cîteaux with more than thirty companions, under abbot Stephen, and bent his neck to the sweet yoke of Christ"). Mabillon: "1113 … He entered the community … then under Abbot Stephen, with thirty companions" (E1-ed 4425–4429; the OCR garbles the house's name). Witness: William, contemporary. Confidence: Documented. The number differs (D1).

S4. **Clairvaux.** William: abbot Stephen sent Bernard's brothers to build the house of Clairvaux and set Bernard over them as abbot, to the wonder of men older and abler, who feared for him "pro tenerioris aetate juventutis, tum pro corporis infirmitate" (because of his youth and his weak body). "Erat autem Clara-Vallis locus in territorio Lingonensi, non longe a fluvio Alba, antiqua spelunca latronum, quae antiquitus dicebatur Vallis absinthialis" (VP 16572–16582; "Clairvaux was a place in the territory of Langres, not far from the river Aube, an old den of robbers, called of old the Valley of Wormwood"). They lived "in fame et siti, in frigore et nuditate"; their stews were often of beech leaves, their bread of barley, millet and vetch (VP 16586–16591). Year: Mabillon, "1115. In this year were founded Clairvaux … Bernard was made Abbot of it, being then in his twenty-fourth year" (E1-ed 4446–4452). Witness: William; the year is the editor's, from the Cistercian *Exordium* and Bernard's tomb tablet (E1-ed 13613–13616). Confidence: Documented (place, hardship); 1115, Widely Accepted.

S5. **Death.** Mabillon: died 1153, "in his sixty-third year, in the fortieth year of his monastic profession, and in the thirty-eighth year of office as Abbot" (E1-ed 5224–5234). Geoffrey: on the day he died he left at Clairvaux "septingentas ferme animas" serving the Lord, besides those "in centum sexaginta monasteriis" brought to penitence under his care (VP 24345–24355; "nearly seven hundred souls … in a hundred and sixty monasteries"). Confidence: 1153, Documented (editor and the Life); 700 and 160, Documented as Geoffrey's figures.

S6. **Tradition.** CEN `cistercian-monasticism`: name "Cistercian Monasticism", informalName "The Cistercians", 1098–1300, "Burgundy, then Europe". Its `voices` line: "Bernard of Clairvaux — the order's most famous voice, whose sermons on the Song of Songs and vast correspondence made him the most listened-to churchman in Europe." That phrase is the tree's own judgment.

## Events

E1. **His brothers come with him: Nivard.** William, VP I.iii.17 (VP 16242–16256, right-hand column, damaged scan): "Videns autem Guido primogenitus fratrum suorum Nivardum fratrem suum minimum, puerum cum pueris aliis in platea: 'Eia,' inquit, 'frater Nivarde, ad te solum respicit omnis terra possessionis nostrae.' Ad quod puer non pueriliter motus: 'Vobis ergo,' inquit, 'coelum, et mihi terra? Non ex aequo divisio haec facta est.' Quo dicto abeuntibus illis, tunc quidem domi cum patre remansit, sed modico post evoluto tempore fratres secutus, nec a patre, nec a propinquis seu amicis potuit retineri." ("Guy, the eldest of the brothers, seeing Nivard, his youngest brother, a boy among other boys in the street, said: 'Well, brother Nivard, all the land we own now looks to you alone.' The boy, moved not like a boy, said: 'So heaven for you, and earth for me? This division is not fairly made.' When they had gone, he stayed at home with his father; but a little later he followed his brothers, and neither father, kin nor friends could hold him.") OCR mends: "rxltiui" read as "coelum"; "a:qiio" as "aequo"; "! xc" as "haec". The heaven-and-earth reading is confirmed in sense by Alan's *Vita secunda* (VP 34739–34743, cleaner): Guy: "Ecce, inquit, Nivarde, secure potestis ludere, quoniam terram multam habiturus estis." Nivard: "Cum maledictione, inquit, fiat haec distributio, ut vos coelum habeatis, ego terram." Witness: William, contemporary; Alan, later. Confidence: Documented as William's story; wording Contested between the two Lives (D2). **Damaged scan**: both readings stand in one damaged scan; no other held print of either Life.

E1a. **He drew others.** William: as he preached in public and private, "matres filios abscondebant, uxores detinebant maritos, amici amicos avertebant" (VP 16236–16240, damaged: "in.ities … Alios abscondebant, uxores delinebant maritos, … amici amicos avertebant"; "mothers hid their sons, wives held back their husbands, friends turned their friends away"), because the Spirit gave his voice such power. The first words are damaged; only "wives held back their husbands, friends turned friends away" is safe. Confidence: Documented as William's words; the first clause Inferential-Thin as read.

E1b. **"Bernard, Bernard, why have you come?"** William: he always had in his heart and often on his lips: "Bernarde, Bernarde, ad quid venisti?" (VP 16298–16300). Confidence: Documented as William's report. Card material.

E2. **The Song of Songs sermons.** Mabillon (E4-ed 1959–1963): the work was begun in Advent 1135, as the second sermon shows. There are 86 sermons; the last "was evidently left unfinished … Thus far had the holy Bernard written, when death arrested his proceeding farther with the work" (E4-ed 33259–33262). Sermon 83.4 (PL183 105303–105306): "Ipse meritum, ipse praemium est sibi. Amor praeter se non requirit causam, non fructum. Fructus ejus, usus ejus. Amo quia amo; amo, ut amem." Eales (E4 32160–32164): "It is itself a merit, and itself its own recompense. It seeks neither cause, nor consequence, beyond itself. It is its own fruit, and its own object and usefulness. I love, because I love ; I love, that I may love." The sermon's title in Eales: the soul "by a chaste and holy love" recovers "its resemblance to the Bridegroom, that is, Christ" (E4 32031–32036). Geoffrey, quoted by Mabillon: "In the sermons upon the Song of Songs, he shows himself both a magnificent investigator of the mystical sense, and expounds the moral to edification" (E4-ed 1955–1958). Confidence: Documented (his own words; 1135 and "unfinished" the editor's).

E3. **The papal schism, 1130.** Mabillon: "1130 … schism in the Church, caused by an election to the Papacy disputed between Gregory (Innocent) and Peter Leonis (Anacletus). Bernard energetically supported the cause of Innocent for eight years" (E1-ed 4620–4624). Bernard to Innocent in 1140: "I was but just now foolishly promising myself some rest, when the schism of Leo was healed" (E2 4742–4744). Confidence: Documented (his letter; the editor's summary). Card or one clause.

E4. **Against Abelard, 1140.** Bernard to the cardinals (Letter 188, E2 4621–4630): "The faith of simple folk is scoffed at, the hidden things of God are exposed … so human reason usurps for itself everything, and leaves nothing for faith." He names Abelard's books on theology and *Know Thyself* (E2 4635–4663). Bernard to Innocent II (Letter 189, E2 4771–4772, 4817–4881): "We have escaped the lion, Leo, to fall on the dragon Peter Abaelard"; Abelard "challenges me, the weakest of all, to single combat"; the archbishop of Sens fixed a day; "I refused … because I thought it unfitting that the grounds of the faith should be handed over to human reasonings for discussion"; he went at last "much against my will, and with tears"; "in the presence of all, my adversary standing opposite, I produced certain articles taken from his books. And when I began to read them he departed, unwilling to listen, and appealed from the judges that he had himself chosen." The king, bishops, abbots and masters were present (E2 4867–4873). Witness: Bernard, his own account to the pope. Confidence: Documented as his account.

E4a. **The other side at Sens.** Berengar of Poitiers, Abelard's pupil, mocks the bishops: after dinner and wine they dozed; when the reader came to a thorny point in Peter's book he cried "Damnatis?"; some, half-awake, answered "Damnamus"; others, catching only the end, "…namus" ("we swim") (ABL 159272–159287). Hostile satire by a partisan. Confidence: Documented as Berengar's satire; as a report of the session, Contested (D3).

E4b. **Abelard's own voice.** Abelard to Heloise, his confession of faith, kept by Berengar (heading ABL 27869–27874): "odiosum me mundo reddidit logica … Nolo sic esse philosophus, ut recalcitrem Paulo. Non sic esse Aristoteles, ut secludar a Christo" (ABL 27882–27901; "logic has made me hateful to the world … I do not wish to be a philosopher in a way that kicks against Paul, nor an Aristotle in a way that shuts me off from Christ"). OCR mends: "mc" read "me", "recalcitrcra" "recalcitrem", "secludat" read "secludar". Confidence: Documented. Card material (four-quote limit).

E4c. **The sentence.** Innocent II, as Mabillon prints it: "we command you … to cause Peter Abaelard and Arnold of Brescia, perverse manufacturers of dogma, and impugners of the Catholic faith, to be imprisoned separately in houses of religion … and to cause their books to be burnt wherever they may be found" (E2-ed 4405–4419; Latin PL182-ed 36641–36648: "separatim faciatis includi, et libros erroris eorum … igne comburi"). Mabillon: Abelard then gave up his appeal on the advice of Peter, abbot of Cluny, "by whom he was kindly received"; was reconciled with Bernard; "he died, a.d. 1142" at Saint-Marcel near Chalon (E2-ed 4419–4430). CEN `cluniac-reform-monasticism`: Peter the Venerable "took in Peter Abelard". Confidence: the sentence Documented (papal text via editor); the reconciliation and death Widely Accepted (editor, citing Peter's letters, not held).

E5. **His monk becomes pope, 1145.** Bernard to the cardinals who elected the abbot of Saint Anastasius as Eugenius III (Letter 237, E2 12601–12640): "May God forgive you what you have done! You have recalled to the world a man who was buried … you have chosen him to be lord of all, who had chosen to be a door-keeper in the house of his God." To Eugenius (Letter 238, E2 12792–12795): "I dare not call you any longer my son, because the son has been changed into father and the father into son." Editor: Eugenius "had been a disciple of S. Bernard, and to him S. Bernard addressed his book De Consideratione" (E2-ed 12609–12614). Confidence: Documented.

E6. **Called to preach the crusade.** Geoffrey (VP III.iv.9, VP 20837–20862): the preaching brought a grave scandal against him, "cum tristior sequeretur effectus"; asked by the king of the Franks "semel et iterum", and urged by papal letters, he would not speak or advise, "donec per ipsius tandem summi Pontificis generalem epistolam jussus ab eo est tanquam Romanae Ecclesiae lingua exponere populis atque principibus" ("until at last, by the pope's own general letter, he was ordered to set it out to peoples and princes, as the tongue of the Roman Church"). Mabillon: 1145, "Bernard was consulted by King Louis respecting a Crusade, and devolved the decision upon the Pope"; 1146, at Chartres, "Bernard was, by the direction of Eugenius, chosen as chief advocate of this warfare. He exhorted the peoples of Germany, of Eastern France, the Bavarians, the English, etc., both by letters and by preaching, to take the Cross" (E1-ed 4994–5012). Editor's note: at Chartres he was chosen "general in chief of the expedition, a title which he refused as being incompatible with his monastic profession" (E2-ed 24198–24200). William of Tyre: Eugenius sent preachers through the West; among them Bernard "praecipuus eligitur … licet corporis esset invalidi, tum propter senium imminens, tum propter jejunia pene continua", went round kingdoms and promised the eternal reward (WT 2852–2870; "was chosen first … though weak in body, from coming age and almost constant fasting"). King Louis took the Cross "on Palm Sunday" 1146 (E1-ed 5037–5042). Confidence: Documented (Geoffrey, William of Tyre, the editor agree that the pope charged him); that he refused command, Widely Accepted (editor, citing Letter 256, not read).

E7. **What he preached.** Letter 363 (1146), to the clergy and people of Eastern France and Bavaria (E2 23873–24104); the same letter went to the English (E2-ed 23938–23942). "Take the sign of the Cross, and you shall gain pardon for every sin that you confess with contrite heart" (E2 24034–24036). "Now, O brave Knight … you have a battle you may fight without danger : where it is glory to conquer, and gain to die" (E2 24029–24032). Confidence: Documented.

E8. **The Jews of the Rhineland, 1146.** Letter 363 §6 (E2 24062–24075): "The Jews must not be persecuted, slaughtered, nor even driven out … They are living signs to us, representing the Lord's Passion. For this reason they are dispersed into all regions, that now they may pay the just penalty of so great a crime … They undergo a hard captivity under Christian princes." Letter 365 to Henry, archbishop of Mainz (E2 24226–24300): a monk named Ralph "was instigating the faithful to the massacre of the Jews"; Bernard blames "his usurpation of the right to preach, his contempt of the authority of the Bishop, and finally his inciting to murder." Editor (from Otto of Freising, not held): Bernard brought Ralph "by virtue of his vow of obedience, to retire to his monastery, much to the indignation of the people" (E2-ed 24253–24310). Joseph ha-Kohen (1575; JHK 3741–3762): God "sent … Saint Bernard of Clairvaux" who said "les toucher, c'est toucher à la prunelle de l'œil de Jésus" ("to touch them is to touch the apple of Jesus's eye"), and that Ralph "n'a pas parlé justement"; "if God's mercy had not sent this priest, not one would have been left who escaped" ("si la miséricorde de Dieu n'avait pas envoyé ce prêtre, il n'en serait pas resté un seul qui se fût échappé"). Confidence: his letters Documented; that he stopped the killing, Documented as the Jewish chronicle's view and the editor's (Otto); his view of the Jews both protective and contemptuous in the same letter, Documented.

E9. **The failure, 1148.** William of Tyre (WT 4938–4946, year A. 1148 at 4950): before Damascus, short of food, the kings and princes, "quantos a seculis non legimus convenisse, confusione induti … peccatis nostris exigentibus, infecto negotio redire compulsi" ("such kings and princes as we have never read of gathering … clothed in shame, our sins requiring it, were forced to go back with nothing done"). On the cause he gives rival rumours (local barons' jealousy; the prince of Antioch; bribes) and says "mihi pro certo compertum non est" ("I have not found it out for certain") (WT 5055–5073). Mabillon: 1149, King Louis "returned to France after the unsuccessful issue of the Crusade" (E1-ed 5149–5151). Bernard, *De consideratione* II.1 (PL182 75226–75229, chapter heading: "Apologiam instituit, ob infelicem successum expeditionis in Terram-Sanctam susceptae"): the crusaders "strati sunt in deserto, aut interfecti gladio, aut fame consumpti. Effusa est contentio super principes" (PL182 75372–75374; "were laid low in the desert, killed by the sword or wasted by hunger. Quarrelling was poured out on the princes"); "Diximus, Pax, et non est pax: promisimus bona, et ecce turbatio" (PL182 75379–75380; "We said, Peace, and there is no peace; we promised good things, and see, confusion"). OCR mend: "el" read "et". Confidence: Documented.

E10. **His answer: the shield.** *De consideratione* II.1.4 (PL182 75604–75611): "Etsi necesse sit unum fieri e duobus, malo in nos murmur hominum, quam in Deum esse. Bonum mihi, si dignetur me uti pro clypeo. Libens excipio in me detrahentium linguas maledicas, et venenata spicula blasphemorum, ut non ad ipsum perveniant. Non recuso inglorius fieri, ut non irruatur in Dei gloriam." The same passage, quoted by Geoffrey (VP 20922–20928): "Si necesse sit unum fieri e duobus, malo in nos murmur hominum, quam in Deum esse. Bonum mihi, si dignetur me uti pro clypeo. Libens excipio in me detrahentium linguas maledicas, et venenata spicula blasphemorum, ut non ad ipsum perveniant." OCR mends (PL182): "dignemr" read "dignetur", "detranent'mm" "detrahentium", "blaspüemorum" "blasphemorum". The two prints agree word for word. He blames the crusaders: as the Israelites fell in the desert for their sins, "miramur istos eadem facientes, eadem passos?" (PL182 75462–75464; "do we wonder that these, doing the same things, suffered the same?"). Confidence: Documented, his own words; the cause he gives is his view (D4).

E11. **Geoffrey's view of the failure.** VP III.iv.10 (VP 20909–20913): "Nec tamen ex illa profectione orientalis Ecclesia liberari, sed coelestis meruit impleri et laetari" ("from that expedition the Eastern church did not earn its freedom, but the heavenly church earned to be filled and to rejoice"). Then Geoffrey tells a wonder: when news of the army's ruin came, a father brought his blind son and Bernard prayed that, if his preaching had been from God, God would show it; the boy said "video" (VP 20929–20950). A wonder, in Geoffrey's voice only. Confidence: Documented as Geoffrey's view; the wonder stays his.

E12. **The book to Eugenius.** *De consideratione* IV (PL182 78506–78517): "Pastorem te populo huic certe aut nega, aut exhibe … Petrus hic est, qui nescitur processisse aliquando vel gemmis ornatus, vel sericis; non tectus auro, non vectus equo albo, nec stipatus milite … In his successisti, non Petro, sed Constantino" ("Either deny that you are this people's shepherd, or show it … This is Peter, who is not known ever to have gone in procession adorned with gems or silks, not covered with gold, not riding a white horse, nor ringed by soldiers … In these things you have succeeded not Peter but Constantine"). Schaff's English of Hus's citation (HUS 6142–6157) agrees. Confidence: Documented.

E13. **The Templars.** *Liber ad milites Templi*, prologue addressed to Hugh, master of the knighthood (PL182-ed 91441–91455): "cum occidit malefactorem, non homicida, sed, ut ita dixerim, malicida" (PL182 91845–91846; "when he kills an evildoer, he is not a homicide but, if I may say so, a malicide"). CEN `military-religious-orders-templars-hospitallers` already tells this, with "malicide". Confidence: Documented. The Library sets no year for the treatise; give none.

## Impact, heaviest first

I1. **The order he spread.** Geoffrey's figures (S5): nearly 700 at Clairvaux, 160 monasteries under his care. Mabillon's chronology lists the houses Clairvaux founded each year, from Ireland to Portugal and Sweden (E1-ed 4866–5150: Mellifont 1141, Alvastra 1143, Alcobaça 1147). CEN: "After Bernard of Clairvaux arrived in 1113, the order spread across Europe at extraordinary speed" (`longDescription`; `why`). Confidence: Documented (Geoffrey; editor) and the tree's own entry.

I2. **The book to Eugenius, carried on by Hus.** Hus, *De Ecclesia* (1413): "Therefore, St. Bernard, de consideratione, IV, 1 writes as follows … 'In things like these thou hast followed not Peter, but Constantine.' Thus far Bernard. That holy man knew that Pope Eugenius ought to be a vicar in poverty and humility" (HUS 6135–6164). Schaff: "The most famous book ever written on the papal office … much quoted by Huss, also in other writings" (HUS-ed 6137–6172). Hus also names "the blessed Bernard in his book addressed to Pope Eugenius" on monks' poverty (HUSL 3094–3096; Workman notes the reference is "vague", HUSL-ed 3114–3118). Witness: Hus, about 260 years later. Confidence: Documented. What passed: Bernard's warning to a pope he loved, that a pope in gold and on a white horse follows Constantine, became, in Hus's hands, a test of whether a pope is Peter's true vicar at all.

I3. **The Song sermons, read on.** Calvin quotes Sermon 61 on the Song: "where have the infirm firm security and safe rest, but in the wounds of the Saviour? … My merit, therefore, is the compassion of the Lord" (CAL 15605–15619). Hus cites Sermon 12 on the Canticles (HUS 2951). CEN `cistercian-monasticism` `legacy`: Bernard's Song sermons "fed a warm, loving strand of Western prayer", and Rolle and Hilton "still quoted Bernard by name". Luther, as his editor quotes him: "If there ever lived on earth a God-fearing and holy monk, it was Saint Bernard, of Clairvaux" (LUT-ed 10535–10538; editor's citation, Erl. 36, 8, not held). Confidence: Documented (Calvin, Hus); Luther's line, the editor's report.

I4. **Honour.** Mabillon: Alexander III, "in the very Mass of his canonization", read the Gospel kept for the holy Doctors (E1-ed 1668–1672). No held text gives the year. Confidence: Widely Accepted (editor). Card.

I5. **The crusade's memory.** The scandal against him (Geoffrey, E6; Bernard, E10) is itself part of the record; the failure is Documented, its cause disputed (D4).

## Where sources disagree or come late

D1. **How many came with him.** William: "more than thirty" (VP 16331). Mabillon: "thirty" (E1-ed 4427). Age: William "about twenty-three", with a variant "twenty-two" (VP 16329). Tell "more than thirty", as William's.
D2. **Nivard's words.** William: "So heaven for you, and earth for me? This division is not fairly made" (E1). Alan: Guy says "you can play safely, for you will have much land"; Nivard: "Let this division be cursed, that you have heaven and I earth" (VP 34739–34743). Same sense, different words. Use William's (the earlier); the card names Alan's.
D3. **Sens.** Bernard: Abelard walked out when the articles were read, and appealed (E4). Berengar: the bishops condemned him drunk and half asleep (E4a). Mabillon reports two more, not held: Geoffrey (Abelard "became confused, and unable to speak") and Otto ("afraid of popular violence") (E2-ed 4390–4394). Tell Bernard's account as his, Berengar's as a hostile pupil's.
D4. **Why the crusade failed.** Bernard: the crusaders' sins, God's judgment beyond understanding (E9, E10). Geoffrey: the East was not freed, but heaven was filled (E11). William of Tyre: treachery before Damascus, by rumour, and he does not know who (E9). Mabillon also cites William of Newburgh (vice in the camp; PL182-ed 75343–75400, 75533–75542) and Otto (the expedition good for souls though joined with great slaughter; PL182-ed 75246–75258), neither held. Tell each as its witness's.
D5. **Who started the crusade.** Geoffrey: he would not speak until the pope ordered him (E6). Mabillon: Louis consulted him and he passed the question to the pope. William of Tyre: he was the first of the preachers Eugenius chose. All agree he preached it at the pope's order; none says he began it.
D6. **The Jews.** His letter forbids killing them and calls their scattering a just penalty (E8). Joseph ha-Kohen, four centuries on, calls him the priest God sent to save them (E8). Both stand.
D7. **Late material.** The founding narratives of Cîteaux are disputed as later constructions (CEN `cistercian-monasticism` sources note on Berman). The miracles of the crusade preaching (Geoffrey) are wonders in his voice.
