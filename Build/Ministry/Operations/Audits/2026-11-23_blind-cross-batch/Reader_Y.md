# Reader Y: blind scoring

Order read: S02, S06, R1, S08, S03, R2. Each story was read once, then checked against its outline, source sheet and the cited files in `cic/texts/`, and scored with `python -m engine.m1.profile_readability`.

## S02

**Three lines.**
1. Catherine of Siena, a dyer's daughter and laywoman of the Dominican Sisters of Penance, who dictated letters to popes and cities.
2. In 1376 she went to Avignon and pressed Gregory XI to take the papacy back to Rome, and he left that September. She later dictated the Dialogue, and she died in Rome in 1380 with the split between two popes unhealed.
3. Her circle carried her voice and her book on, and how far she moved the pope is disputed: Raymond credits her, while her own letters show he had already decided.

**Scores.**
- Voice 4: an adult documentary register, with a vivid audience scene and a strong closing death scene. The What-came paragraph slips into a list.
- Craft 4: the dispute is told as a scene with four witnesses and no verdict. The hook runs about six sentences with the quote, and the essence lands at about 162 words. "That September he left" is said in the essence and again in the life.
- Rigor 5: every sentence traces to the outline, and the Do not say list is kept ("said nothing", "lost to her senses", "at the end of April"). Q1 was checked against Mirbt l.13887-13891, Q2 against Scudder l.5221-5222, Q3 against Fiorilli l.14405-14407, and the short phrases at l.5039, l.7063-7064 and RAY l.214145. All match.
- Accessibility 4: grade 7.0, reading ease 78, with the hard words worked in. Two sentences run 32 words, and the opening has the pope ask about "the ways of his court" without her complaint that comes before it.

**BLOCKING:** none.

## S06

**Three lines.**
1. Tertullian of Carthage, a convert trained in Roman law (Eusebius says) and the foremost early writer of the faith in Latin, around 200.
2. He defended the Christians before Rome's governors, claimed the Scriptures for the apostles' churches, and put to work the Latin words for one substance in three persons. Then he turned to the New Prophecy.
3. Cyprian, Novatian and Lactantius drew on him, and the Latin church kept his words for God, but his later followers were counted heretics.

**Scores.**
- Voice 4: his own lines carry the story in a grown-up register. One stock how-we-know sentence stands alone: "Most of Tertullian's life rests on his own books and on Jerome."
- Craft 3: it moves book by book, close to the list-of-books trap, although each book gets one of his own lines. There are ten names, and the Praxeas paragraph is heavy for one hearing. Augustine's verdict is told in the essence and again near the end.
- Rigor 5: all three quotes check against Oehler l.11253-11261, Oehler l.2280-2290 with ANF3 l.21742-21751, and Kroymann l.12556-12562. The short phrases check against ANF3 l.4661, ANF4 l.9544-9545 and Jerome l.40504. The motives are credited to both Jerome and Augustine, and "is said" is kept at the end.
- Accessibility 3: grade 8.1 and reading ease 75 pass, but the idea load is high. The "she" in the opening quote (the truth) is unclear once the middle of the quote is cut.

**BLOCKING:** none.

## R1

**Three lines.**
1. Basil of Caesarea, a teacher of rhetoric who turned to the monk's life and became bishop of Caesarea from 370.
2. He wrote rules for monks living together, built a great lodging for the poor and sick, faced down the emperor's official, and defended the Holy Spirit's place beside the Father and the Son.
3. His Rule passed to the West through Rufinus and Benedict and was kept by the council of 787, and the council of 381 confessed the Spirit's place after his death.

**Scores.**
- Voice 4: a clear adult narration that draws on his own letters and the Rule. The one-line ending is abrupt.
- Craft 3: two Gregories are named in consecutive sentences, and after that every "Gregory says" is ambiguous, which is where I got lost. "Gregory's speech in Basil's memory is praise" is a stand-alone sentence about the record. He "came back" to serve the bishop, but his leaving is never told. The rival bishop arrives with no setting.
- Rigor 4: all claims are held. Q1 checks against Jackson l.39133-39135, Q2 against PG31 l.72547-72549 (confirmed by Clarke l.13460-13464), and Q3 against Jackson l.33483-33485. The opponents' view, "The Spirit, they held, could not be ranked", is Basil's report but is not credited to him. The Gregory ambiguity leaves witness credits unclear.
- Accessibility 4: grade 7.4 and reading ease 77, with short, plain sentences. "Rhetoric" is glossed only on its second use, not its first.

**BLOCKING:** none.

## S08

**Three lines.**
1. Eutyches, an aged abbot in Constantinople who fought Nestorius's divided Christ by holding to one nature after the union.
2. He was condemned in 448, restored at Ephesus in 449, and condemned again at Chalcedon in 451.
3. Both sides of the split cursed his name: Chalcedon's side called its opponents "Eutychians", and the churches that refused Chalcedon cursed him too.

**Scores.**
- Voice 4: a serious documentary voice, and the irony of the ending is earned. Some paragraphs chain "he wrote ... he wrote".
- Craft 4: the spine of three verdicts is clear, the doubt is told as a scene (the judge who "did not remember"), and the connection is told through Timothy's curse. The Ephesus paragraph is dense with procedure.
- Rigor 5: Q1 checks against Evagrius (Greek) l.2284-2289 and Perry l.23843-23845, Q2 against Perry l.23494-23504, and Q3 against Feltoe l.5028. Each side is credited, the minutes are named as the restoring side's, the year "about 475" is in the narrator's voice, and the chronicle is credited for the scene.
- Accessibility 4: grade 8.0 and reading ease 72, with nature and union glossed at the hook. "The coming split" is not explained when first heard, and nine names is many.

**BLOCKING:** none.

## S03

**Three lines.**
1. Eunomius of Cyzicus, the fourth-century teacher who held that "unbegotten" is God's very being.
2. He argued that the Son, being begotten, cannot share the Father's being. He was briefly bishop of Cyzicus, was exiled twice under Valens, and was banished by Theodosius after the creeds of 383.
3. His Apology made Basil, and then Gregory of Nyssa, say exactly what "unbegotten" means, and his followers were suppressed by law.

**Scores.**
- Voice 3: Philostorgius is named three sentences in a row, and long stretches read as a report about the record ("X says") rather than a story.
- Craft 3: the hook and the echo ending work well, and the Cyzicus doubt is a clean three-way scene. But the witness chains crowd out events, and the central idea is told twice at length (in the essence and in the Apology paragraph).
- Rigor 4: Q1 checks against PG29 l.99678-99681 and Whiston l.38, the Basil answer against PG29 l.101276-101277, and Q3 against Walford l.328. Socrates and Sozomen are never identified as later, hostile historians, which the outline required. "Anomoean" is glossed as "unlike" without saying unlike whom.
- Accessibility 3: grade 7.4, but the concepts are abstract ("unbegotten being"). A bare "Socrates" will mislead a listener, and "Valens" is not glossed as an emperor.

**BLOCKING:** none.

## R2

**Three lines.**
1. Epiphanius, a monk from Palestine who became bishop of Salamis in Cyprus and wrote the Panarion, a "medicine chest" against eighty heresies.
2. He named each heresy and set a cure beside it. In old age he campaigned against Origen's followers at Jerusalem and Constantinople, using force, and died at sea on the way home.
3. Later writers, from Augustine to both sides of the image quarrel at Nicaea in 787, argued from his book and his letter.

**Scores.**
- Voice 4: the medicine-chest image runs through the story and returns at the end, and the river line is vivid. The register is adult throughout.
- Craft 4: the life is told once, in order, and the force is told plainly. It runs 714 words, past the outline's 675 at most. "The same letter defends two acts of force" is followed by no defense.
- Rigor 5: Q1 checks against Holl l.12387-12391 and Q2 against Fremantle l.10920-10922. Theophilus's motive is credited both ways (Socrates and Sozomen), the Long Brother's question to Sozomen, the curtain as "Christ or a saint", and the 787 ruling as the council's.
- Accessibility 4: grade 8.1 and reading ease 76, with heresy, Panarion and Origen glossed where first heard. The story is long, and "near his monastery" is unclear about whose monastery.

**BLOCKING:** none.
