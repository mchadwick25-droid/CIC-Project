## Q1: John Chrysostom

**Heart: partial; not told in the first 150 words.** The opening uses the Eutropius scene, which is well sourced and does show bold preaching and the church's mercy. But the core the sources give is the preacher who pressed the rich toward the poor (Palladius, the Matthew homily), whose free speech to the powerful set the tide against him (Socrates VI.5), and his two depositions and death in exile. That core only starts after word 150, with the hospitals and the sermon on Matthew.

- **S2. MODERN ONLY.** "He came from Antiochene Christianity, with its plain way of reading Scripture." The old source says only that Diodorus "limited his attention to the literal sense of scripture" (Socrates VI.3). Talk of an "Antiochian system of interpretation" is the modern editor's note in the same place: "became the corner-stone of the Antiochian system of interpretation" (NPNF note, npnf202). A named "Antiochene Christianity" is a modern category. S8 states the sourced fact correctly.
- **S3. ASSUMPTION ADDED (date).** "That year [399] ... Eutropius had a law passed ..." Socrates gives no year for the law. He says only that "scarcely had the law been promulgated, before Eutropius himself ... fled" (VI.5). The 399 in the NPNF margin belongs to Eutropius's consulship and fall, not to the law. Giving the law a year goes beyond the sources.
- **S7. SUMMARY HOLDS.** John: "from a desire to soften your minds towards him" and "she alone like an affectionate mother has concealed him under her cloak" (On Eutropius 3, npnf109). The two halves come from neighbouring passages of the same argument, so the "for" holds.
- **S10. SUMMARY HOLDS.** "found extraordinary extravagance, and ordered the large sums so spent to be transferred to the hospital ... he erected other hospitals" (Dialogue, Moore). On "Palladius of Helenopolis": the census flags the Dialogue's author as Moore's identification. But an old attribution does exist: George of Alexandria, in Photius cod. 96, cites "bishop Palladius, who has written an admirable and careful life of Chrysostom in the form of a dialogue". "Who stood by John to the end" is a fair reading of the Dialogue, where Palladius is a partisan held under guard.
- **S17. SUMMARY HOLDS.** "incited the people to a most alarming sedition" (VI.15); VI.16 is headed "He is recalled."
- **S18. SUMMARY HOLDS.** The statue quarrel and second synod; "Chrysostom was silenced, and went no more to the church" (VI.18). "A clash" is a fair compression of Socrates's account.
- **S19. SUMMARY HOLDS, slightly broadened.** "They said that such were their instructions; if he died by the roadside, the higher the promotion they would earn. One of them ... showed him by stealth a certain amount of kindness" (Dialogue). The piece gives the hope of his death to all the soldiers, while Palladius exempts one.

All other Q1 sentences are HELD: Socrates VI.2 (Eutropius forcing Theophilus to ordain John, 26 February 398), VI.3, VI.5 ("added insult to cruelty", the tide setting against him, Isidore), VI.15 (the Oak; deposed for refusing to appear), VI.18 (404), VII.45 (Proclus, 438); Palladius ("Glory to God for all things"); Matthew Homily 50 ("Neglect Him not when naked").

## Q2: Epiphanius

**Heart: right; told early.** The medicine chest of eighty poisons, each with its antidote, and the plain-sense war on Origen are the Panarion's own image and the letters' own concern. Both are set out within the first 150 words.

- **S6. SUMMARY HOLDS.** Panarion Proem II 2.3: "αἱρέσεών τε καὶ κακοδόξου γνώμης ἀνθρωπείας ὑπὸ πεπλανημένων ἀνδρῶν ἐν κόσμῳ ἐσπαρμένης, ἐξότε ἄνθρωπος ἐπὶ γῆς πέπλασται". That is, heresies and wrong opinion sown by men led astray, since man was formed on earth. "Since Adam" is a fair rendering.
- **S8. SUMMARY HOLDS.** "αἱρέσεις ὀγδοήκοντα, αἵτινές εἰσι θηρίων εἴτ᾽ οὖν ἑρπετῶν αἰνίγματα", with "ἀντιδότους ἐφαρμόσαι" (Proem I).
- **S9. SUMMARY HOLDS.** Augustine: "non modum, sed ordinem sum secutus" (De haeresibus). Nicaea II: "having made a treatise of eighty chapters ... he passes by none" (Mendham, Session 6). Nicaea used the list as a complete catalogue rather than copying it, so "took his book as their list" is a little loose but faithful.
- **S10. SUMMARY HOLDS.** "Same fight" holds, since Origen is among the Panarion's heresies, and Socrates VI.10 says Origen "had been dead nearly two hundred years". "Old age": John's party called him the "silly old man" (Jerome, Against John 11).
- **S12. SUMMARY HOLDS.** Twenty before and sixty after: "separatis viginti, quas ante Domini adventum ... reliquas post Domini ascensum natas sexaginta" (Augustine). "Answers it" holds for the Panarion itself, for example the refutation after the Letter to Flora. Augustine, who knew the summary, says Epiphanius wrote "nulla disputatione adversus falsitatem". That is not a contradiction of the Panarion, but it is worth knowing.
- **S16. SUMMARY HOLDS.** Letter 51.3–5 sets out his reasons against Origen.
- **S18. SUMMARY HOLDS.** "I have drunk also from the great river Euphrates, not spiritual but actual water" (Letter 51.5). The letter also complains that Origen "tampers with the true meaning of the narrative by a false use of allegory".
- **S24. ASSUMPTION ADDED.** "... and he feared they would drift from the church." The letter says only: "For a year before I had heard many of them complain that they had no one to celebrate for them the sacraments of the Lord", and it names "the fear of God" and "provision for the wants of the Church" as his motive (Letter 51.1). No fear that the monks would drift away is stated. A smaller point: the monks who complained were those of Jerome's monastery, while "the monks there" reads as Epiphanius's own.
- **S27. SUMMARY HOLDS.** Letter 90: "it is your present duty to strengthen the hands of those who are in the field and to gather together ... the bishops of your entire island".
- **S28. SUMMARY HOLDS, loosely.** Letter 90 says the church "has with the sword of the gospel cut down the Origenist serpents crawling out of their caves, and has delivered ... the monks of Nitria", and that Ammonius, Eusebius and Euthymius "have taken ship for Constantinople". The letter never says "driven out of Egypt" in so many words. The expulsion itself is told by Socrates and Sozomen.

All other Q2 sentences are HELD:
- the title and the preface quotation ("Πανάριον εἴτ᾽ οὖν κιβώτιον ἰατρικόν ... ἀλεξητήρια τῶν δεδηγμένων καὶ προκαταληπτικὰ τῶν μελλόντων");
- the date "about 375", from the eleventh year of Valens (Holl's note: "das Jahr 375");
- books, report, and "ἰδίοις ὠσὶ καὶ ὀφθαλμοῖς";
- the Letter to Flora, given "ἵνα μή τις νομίσειεν ἡμᾶς ἐξ ἀκοῆς μόνον ... ἐλέγχειν";
- Jerusalem (Against John 11);
- Origen's teachings, Paulinian and the curtain at Anablatha (Letter 51);
- Salamis as "metropolis" and "seaport" (Sozomen VI.32);
- Cyprus forbidding Origen's books, revenge on Dioscorus and his brethren, and "a man of simple mind and manners" (Socrates VI.10);
- Sozomen VIII.14, the ordination "contrary to the ecclesiastical canon" (Socrates VI.14), Ammonius's question and judging by report (Sozomen VIII.15), and death at sea;
- Nicaea II's spurious-passage argument and the Caroline Books' reply quoting the curtain letter (Mendham, Session 6 note);
- the church as "Χριστοῦ νύμφη ἁγία ... οὖσα μὲν ἀπ᾽ αἰῶνος" (Proem I).

## Q3: John Chrysostom

**Heart: right; told early.** S7 ("He preached plainly about the poor, and his blunt words to the powerful led to his exile") ends at about word 145.

- **S4. SUMMARY HOLDS.** Socrates: "he seemed to create greater displeasure in some, as he not only denied compassion to the unfortunate, but added insult to cruelty" (VI.5). The piece does not say that Socrates is the source.
- **S5. SOFTENED OR CHANGED (within a quotation).** The piece has "she alone, like a loving mother, hid him behind her curtains". The source has "she alone like an affectionate mother has concealed him under her cloak", with the translator's footnote "Possibly an allusion to the curtain ... drawn in front of the altar" (On Eutropius 3, npnf109). The rendering puts a modern note's guess into John's mouth. The Greek of In Eutropium is not in `cic/texts/` (the PG52 volume there holds the letters), so the original word could not be checked.
- **S7. SUMMARY HOLDS.** "when he proceeded to rebuke many of those in public office also ... the tide of unpopularity began to set against him ... he created many powerful adversaries" (Socrates VI.5). The exiles follow from the empress's offence (VI.15, VI.18). The causal compression is fair.
- **S8. ASSUMPTION ADDED, with a modern-only element.** (a) "His sermons were read for centuries": no old source in the library says this. Socrates says only that readers "must read for themselves" (VI.4). The claim is a legacy judgment. (b) "Came back only when his body came home": Socrates says Proclus "brought back to the Church those who had separated" by bringing the body home (VII.45). But Socrates VII.25 records an earlier step: Atticus "ordered that mention of John should be made in the prayers ... by which means he trusted that many would be induced to return". "Only" is an inference the sources do not make.
- **S9. SUMMARY HOLDS.** "John was a native of Antioch" (Socrates VI.3); "For twelve years he was a shining light in the Church of Antioch" (Dialogue). The Antioch setting of the Matthew homilies rests on editorial dating, but it is widely accepted.
- **S10. SUMMARY HOLDS.** "His table indeed is full of golden cups ... Dost thou furnish His table with cloths bespangled with gold" and "silken garments" (Matthew Hom. 50).

All other Q3 sentences are HELD:
- the sanctuary law, the fall and the pulpit (Socrates VI.5), 399;
- the quotation's second half, "opposing both the wrath of the king, and the rage of the people, and their overwhelming hatred";
- published sermons and shorthand writers (VI.4), and 398 with Eutropius forcing Theophilus (VI.2);
- the hospital (Dialogue), and correcting and expelling clergy (VI.4);
- Gainas (Socrates VI.5 and Theodoret V.32, where John argues before the emperor);
- Isidore and the plot (VI.5);
- the Oak, the refusal and the call for a "general council" (VI.15);
- Photius cod. 59: the charges of selling much valuable church property, reviling the clergy, striking Memnon and eating "gluttonously alone, living like a Cyclops", and "forty-five in all ... unanimously decided";
- surrender and recall (VI.15–16);
- the statue "one-half the breadth of the street" away, the canon about resuming the see, "two Synods had condemned him", Easter in the baths of Constantianae, and "led into exile by force" in 404 (VI.18);
- Cucusus in Armenia (Sozomen VIII.22);
- the Syrian bishops and "the Church of Antioch migrating to the Church of Armenia", Pityus, promotion, white clothes and "Glory to God for all things" (Dialogue);
- 14 September 407 (VI.21), and 438 with the Church of the Apostles (VII.45).

## Q4: Basil of Caesarea

**Heart: right; told early.** The conversion, the common life and service of the monks' rules, the lodging for the poor and the sick, and the defence of the Spirit are all set out by about word 150.

- **S4. SUMMARY HOLDS.** Letter 223.2 runs from the waking to the gospel's call to sell one's goods and the search for "some one of the brethren who had chosen this way of life". "Set the course of his life" is Basil's own framing, put briefly.
- **S5. SUMMARY HOLDS.** Rufinus HE XI.9: "ad profitendam rhetoricam rogabantur. quod opus magnifice quidem implebat Basilius". Macrina drew him to "this busy life where one toils with one's hands" (Life of Macrina).
- **S13. SUMMARY HOLDS.** Longer Rule 7: "love ... seeketh not its own"; "He girded Himself and washed the feet of the disciples".
- **S18. SUMMARY HOLDS.** Or. 43.30–31 on Valens: "dishonouring His offspring with his unequal degrees of Godhead"; "He immediately returned with me from Pontus ... devoted himself to the service of his mother, the Church."
- **S24. SOFTENED OR CHANGED.** The piece has "His accusers had won over the governor". The source has "I should like those who are besieging your impartial ears to be asked what harm the government suffers from me?" (Letter 94, to Elias). Basil calls the governor's ears "impartial" and says the accusers are besieging them. "Won over" claims a result the letter does not grant. The rest of the sentence holds: "a place of entertainment for strangers, both for those who are on a journey and for those who require medical treatment ... physicians, doctors, means of conveyance".
- **S28. SUMMARY HOLDS.** On the Holy Spirit 1.3 and 10.24. The opponents say the Spirit may not "be ranked with the Father and Son". Basil's reading of "with ... together with" as ranking the Spirit with them follows the treatise.

All other Q4 sentences are HELD:
- Letter 223.2 ("like a man roused from deep sleep, I turned my eyes to the marvellous light of the truth of the Gospel"), with the slanderers in 223.1;
- Macrina (Life of Macrina) and Gregory pulling Basil from the teacher's chair (Rufinus XI.9);
- Pontus: "presided over the abodes of contemplation there. He himself too founded one" (Or. 43.29);
- the rules as answers to questions, and "Whose feet then wilt thou wash? ... In comparison with whom wilt thou be last" (Longer Rule 7);
- the quarrel with Eusebius (Or. 43.28);
- "not without ... the envious struggles of the prelates" (43.37);
- Modestus, "refusing to respect the religion of your Sovereign", and "tattered rags, and the few books" (43.48–49);
- Anthimus, the "robber band" and "the multiplication of the Bishops" (43.58–59);
- the "new city" filled "in consequence of his exhortations", and lepers "driven away from their cities" whom he "saluted ... as brethren" (43.63);
- the two doxologies attacked as "novel and at the same time mutually contradictory" (On the Holy Spirit 1.3), and baptism in the three names (10.26);
- "As we were baptized, so we profess our belief. As we profess our belief, so also we offer praise" (Basil's letters, npnf208);
- the "economy" and "often having publicly preached this truth" (Or. 43.68–69);
- "Around him poured the whole city" (43.79);
- the 381 creed (npnf214);
- Rufinus translating "the works of Basil" (Gennadius 17, with Rufinus's own preface to Urseius quoted in Clarke);
- "the Rule of our Holy Father St. Basil" (Benedict, Rule 73).
