## Q5 (John Chrysostom)

All sentences not listed below are HELD. Key anchors, for the record:
- Eutropius, the law, his flight, trembling under the altar, "added insult to cruelty": SOC VI.5, "while Eutropius trembling with fear lay under the table of the altar, mounting the pulpit ... uttered an invective against him ... he not only denied compassion to the unfortunate, but added insult to cruelty." The year 399 comes from the consulate the source gives.
- Mother hiding her enemy under her cloak: CHR9, Homily on Eutropius 3, "from a desire to soften your minds towards him ... she alone like an affectionate mother has concealed him under her cloak."
- Diodore and the literal sense: SOC VI.3, "limited his attention to the literal sense of scripture, avoiding that which was mystical"; SOZ VIII.2, "avoided allegory."
- Matthew sermon: MATT Hom. 50, "Wouldest thou do honor to Christ's body? Neglect Him not when naked."
- 398 ordination under pressure: SOC VI.2, "bidding him 'choose between ordaining John, and undergoing a trial'."
- Hospital: PALL ch. 5, "extraordinary extravagance, and ordered the large sums so spent to be transferred to the hospital."
- Gainas, Theophilus's grudge over Isidore, the false report about the Eucharist: SOC VI.5 and VI.9.
- The Oak, cited, deposed for refusing, the people rising: SOC VI.15–16.
- The charges (church property, insults to the clergy, striking Memnon, eating "gluttonously alone, living like a Cyclops"): PHOT cod. 59, the acts of the synod.
- The statue, the games, the empress, the second council over the canon, exile in 404: SOC VI.18.
- Last words: PALL ch. 11, "using his customary formula, 'Glory to God for all things'." The year 407 comes from SOC VI.21's consulate.
- Proclus in 438: SOC VII.45.

**Sentence 7. "He preached plainly about the poor, and his blunt words to the powerful led to his exile." SUMMARY HOLDS.**
SOC VI.5: "when he proceeded to rebuke many of those in public office also with immoderate vehemence, the tide of unpopularity began to set against him." SOC VI.18 traces the line from the statue speech to the empress, to the second council, to exile. Socrates himself makes the causal link, so the "led to" is not added.

**Sentence 25. "Palladius says soldiers hurried him toward Pityus, hoping he would die." SUMMARY HOLDS.**
PALL ch. 11: "hurried him over the ground at forced speed. They said that such were their instructions; if he died by the roadside, the higher the promotion they would earn." Palladius also says one soldier "showed him by stealth a certain amount of kindness." The summary leaves out that one exception. The force is intact.

Note on sentence 11 ("Palladius, who wrote John's life and took his side"): HELD. The Dialogue's manuscript heading names Palladius, and Photius (PHOT, citing George of Alexandria) has "bishop Palladius, who has written an admirable and careful life of Chrysostom in the form of a dialogue." The piece does not say which Palladius, so it avoids Moore's identification with the bishop of Helenopolis.

Heart: right. The piece opens on the sanctuary scene and gives the plain-speech-to-power-and-exile line at about word 120. The card's "why" is the same: plain speech about money and the poor, aimed at the court, ending in exile. The poor side rests on one Matthew sentence plus the hospital, so it is thinner than the power side.

## Q6 (Basil of Caesarea)

Anchors for HELD sentences:
- Letter 223 (BAS8): "had wasted nearly all my youth in the vain labour ... acquiring the wisdom made foolish by God. Then once upon a time, like a man roused from deep sleep, I turned my eyes to the marvellous light of the truth of the Gospel." It is a self-defence ("to confute my opponents"). The same letter covers reading the gospel on "selling of one's goods, the sharing them with the poor" and looking for brethren.
- Rufinus (RUF XI.9): "ad profitendam rhetoricam rogabantur. quod opus magnifice quidem implebat Basilius"; Gregory "sedentem Basilium de doctoris cathedra deponeret."
- Macrina (MACR): "Macrina took him in hand ... deserted it for this busy life where one toils with one's hands."
- Pontus community (OR43 §29): "he set out ... with me into Pontus, and presided over the abodes of contemplation there. He himself too founded one."
- Longer Rule 7 (CLARKE): "love ... 'seeketh not its own'"; "Whose feet then wilt thou wash? Whom wilt thou care for? In comparison with whom wilt thou be last if thou livest by thyself?"
- Valens and the return (OR43 §30–31): "He immediately returned with me from Pontus ... devoted himself to the service of his mother, the Church."
- The election against the bishops (OR43 §37): "not without ... the envious struggles of the prelates of his native land."
- Modestus (OR43 §48–49): "In refusing to respect the religion of your Sovereign"; "Confiscation, banishment, torture, death"; "my tattered rags, and the few books"; "Death ... will send me the sooner to God."
- The hurt time had not healed (OR43 §59): "a cause of pain which even time has not obliterated."
- Letter 94 (BAS8), to the governor, against "my accusers": "to whom do we do any harm by building a place of entertainment for strangers, both for those who are on a journey and for those who require medical treatment ... physicians, doctors, means of conveyance."
- The new city, the rich and the lepers (OR43 §63): "Go forth a little way from the city, and behold the new city ... the common treasury of the wealthy ... in consequence of his exhortations"; lepers "driven away from their cities"; "saluted them as brethren ... taking the lead in approaching to tend them."
- The doxologies (On the Holy Spirit 1.3, BAS8): "'with the Son together with the Holy Ghost,' and at another 'through the Son in the Holy Ghost' ... introducing novel and at the same time mutually contradictory terms."
- Letter quote (BAS8, line c. 33483): "As we were baptized, so we profess our belief. As we profess our belief, so also we offer praise."
- 381 creed (COUNC): "who with the Father and the Son together is worshipped and glorified."
- Benedict, RB 73 (BEN): "the Rule of our Holy Father St. Basil, but examples of the virtues, of the good living."
- Death (OR43 §79): "Around him poured the whole city."
- Dates: the episcopate from 370 is Widely Accepted. Gregory of Nyssa gives "eight years" in office (MACR). "378 or 379" is told as roughly as the sources allow.

**Sentence 4. "That waking set the course of his life in Caesarea, an inland city in Asia Minor." SUMMARY HOLDS.**
Letter 223 itself runs from the waking straight on to renunciation and the search for brethren, so Basil makes the link. The words "in Caesarea" stretch it a little: the turn the letter describes leads to Egypt, Syria and his own country, not to Caesarea as such.

**Sentence 5. "Basil gave up teaching rhetoric for a life of prayer and shared work." SUMMARY HOLDS.**
RUF XI.9 (rhetoric, then the monastery, "psalmis et hymnis et orationibus ... vacare"). MACR: "this busy life where one toils with one's hands."

**Sentence 19. "A man alone has no one to serve." SUMMARY HOLDS.**
Longer Rule 7: "the solitary life has one aim, the service of the needs of the individual"; "Wherewith shall he show compassion, when he is cut off from the communion of the many?"

**Sentence 20. "The bishop of Caesarea made him a priest, though they quarreled." SUMMARY HOLDS.**
OR43 §27–28: "proceeded to praise the Lord in the seat of the Presbyters ... There arose a disagreement between him and his predecessor." Gregory does not say in so many words that Eusebius ordained him. That Eusebius did is implied: Basil rose through the grades in the church of Caesarea.

**Sentence 26. "A rival bishop once blocked his road with armed men." SUMMARY HOLDS.**
OR43 §58: "to seize his mules by the bridle and bar the passage with a robber band." "Armed men" stands in for "robber band." The meaning holds.

**Sentence 27. "Basil answered by making his brother bishop of Nyssa and his friend bishop of Sasima, a hurt, Gregory said, that time had not healed." MODERN ONLY (the Nyssa link).**
OR43 §59 gives Basil's answer as "the multiplication of the Bishops of the country ... I myself was treated as an appendage to this scheme." Gregory names neither Nyssa nor his brother here. Sasima as Gregory's see is old: Gregory's own letter in OR43 has "not accept your Sasima." The step that puts Gregory of Nyssa's appointment inside the answer to Anthimus comes from modern editors. Clarke writes (MACR intro): "In 372 ... he forced his friend Gregory to accept the bishopric of Sasima, and his brother that of Nyssa." Jackson's prolegomena (BAS8) also have "the brother, Nyssa was selected." The second half ("a hurt ... time had not healed") is HELD.

**Sentence 33. "The Spirit, they held, could not be ranked with the Father and the Son." SUMMARY HOLDS.**
This is the position On the Holy Spirit answers throughout: the opponents object to "with ... together with" because it numbers the Spirit with the Father and Son. Gregory, OR43 §68, has the enemy "on the watch for the unqualified statement 'the Spirit is God'."

**Sentence 34. "He answered from what the church already did, and in a letter put it this way: ..." SUMMARY HOLDS.**
The letter quote is exact. "From what the church already did" fairly sums up the appeal to baptism and the doxology in On the Holy Spirit. Note that the quoted letter (Letter 159 in Jackson's numbering) is not the reply to the attackers at the prayers. The piece keeps the two apart ("and in a letter").

**Sentence 35. "Gregory admits that in writing Basil held back the bare words that the Spirit is God, from caution, though he often preached it." SUMMARY HOLDS.**
OR43 §68–69: "He postponed for the time the use of the exact term ... at a crisis when religion was in peril"; "his often having publicly preached this truth." "Admits" is a little stronger than Gregory's stance. Gregory states the fact but praises it as wise "economy."

**Sentence 37. "Rufinus put his Rule into Latin in 397 or 398." MODERN ONLY (the date).**
That Rufinus translated the Rule is old: RUF XI.9, "Basilii praeterea instituta monachorum" among his translations. The year rests on the editor's reckoning, from Rufinus's return to Italy. CLARKE intro: "Rufinus returned to Italy from the East in 397, and translated hoc opus of Basil into Latin for Urseius." Rufinus's prefatory letter to Urseius, which Clarke quotes, gives no year. Under the date rule, this should be told roughly ("about 400", "a generation after his death") or left out.

**Sentence 39. "In 787 a council told the houses still standing where men and women lived as monks, side by side but apart, to keep his rule." SUMMARY HOLDS.**
COUNC, Nicaea II canon 20: "The double monasteries which are already in existence, shall observe the rule of our holy Father Basil ... monks and nuns shall not dwell together in the same monastery." "Side by side but apart" glosses the canon's own separation rules. It does not overstate them.

Heart: right. Within the first 150 words it gives the waking, the monastic rule of mutual service, the hospital, and the defence of the Spirit. These match the card's "why" and teaser (the Spirit, the rule that a council and Benedict named).

## Q7 (John Chrysostom)

Q7 matches Q5 except for the sentences below. Everything else is HELD on the anchors listed under Q5.

**Sentence 7. "He preached plainly about the poor, and his blunt words to the powerful led to his exile." SUMMARY HOLDS** (same evidence as Q5).

**Sentence 8. "At Antioch John had trained under Diodore, who kept to the literal sense of Scripture, in the Antiochene way." MODERN ONLY (the clause "in the Antiochene way").**
Socrates and Sozomen say only that Diodore kept to the literal sense (SOC VI.3; SOZ VIII.2, "avoided allegory"). "The Antiochene way," a school or method named for the city, is a modern category. In this library it appears only in the translator's notes: SOC VI.3 note, "became the corner-stone of the Antiochian system of interpretation"; MATT preface, "Diodorus is reckoned the leader of the so-called Antiochian school." The rest of the sentence is HELD.

Sentence 9 ("There, in a sermon on Matthew ..."): HELD. Saying the Matthew homilies were preached at Antioch rests on the text itself. MATT Hom. 7: "our city first crowned itself with the name of Christian" (Acts 11:26). Hom. 66 counts the congregations at about "one hundred thousand," which the translator glosses as Antioch.

**Sentence 11. "Palladius, who stood by John to the end, says he moved the bishop's extravagant spending to the hospital." SUMMARY HOLDS.**
The Dialogue defends John throughout and was written after his death, so "stood by John" matches the work's stance. "To the end" leans toward the life of Palladius of Helenopolis, who is listed among those charged at the Oak (PHOT cod. 59). But PALL's own introduction calls the identification of author with that bishop Moore's argument ("The author's identity is Moore's argued identification, not settled here"). As worded, the sentence does not commit to the identification. Q5's wording ("who wrote John's life and took his side") is safer.

Sentence 18 ("In 403 he and his bishops, meeting at the Oak ..."): HELD under the date rule as Widely Accepted. Socrates gives no consulate for the Oak. The year follows from SOC VI.18 (the second council, then exile in 404) and the chronicle date for the statue (403) that SOC VI.18's note cites from Prosper and Marcellinus.

**Sentence 24. "A second council silenced him for taking back his see without a council's leave, and in 404 the emperor sent him into exile." SOFTENED OR CHANGED.**
SOC VI.18: "the bishops would not listen to his defence, but immediately condemned him ... the emperor therefore sent to tell John that he could not go to the church, because two Synods had condemned him. Accordingly Chrysostom was silenced." In the source the council condemned him, and the silence followed from the emperor's message. "Silenced" turns a condemnation into the result that came after it. Q5's "condemned" is right.

**Sentence 25. "In 407, Palladius says, soldiers hurried him toward Pityus, hoping he would die." SUMMARY HOLDS.**
Same Palladius evidence as Q5. The order of the sentence makes the year sound like part of what Palladius says, but Palladius gives no year. 407 comes from SOC VI.21, which dates the death to the consulate of 407, and Palladius says the march lasted three months before it. The year is correct but rests on Socrates.

Heart: right; early yes (as Q5).

## Q8 (Epiphanius of Salamis)

Anchors for HELD sentences:
- Preface (HOLL1, Proem I): "ἐπειδὴ γὰρ μέλλομεν ὑμῖν τά τε ὀνόματα τῶν αἱρέσεων δηλοῦν τάς τε παρ᾽ αὐτοῖς ἀθεμίτους πράξεις ἀνακαλύπτειν, ὥσπερ ἰοὺς καὶ δηλητήρια ὄντα, σὺν αὐτοῖς δὲ ἅμα καὶ ἀντιδότους ἐφαρμόσαι, ἀλεξητήρια τῶν δεδηγμένων καὶ προκαταληπτικὰ τῶν μελλόντων εἰς τοῦτο ἐμπίπτειν ... Πανάριον εἴτ᾽ οὖν κιβώτιον ἰατρικὸν ... αἱρέσεις ὀγδοήκοντα." The rendering is faithful.
- Date and ways of knowing (HOLL1, Proem II 2.3–4): "Οὐαλεντινιανοῦ καὶ Οὐάλεντος ἑνδεκάτου ἔτους καὶ Γρατιανοῦ ἑβδόμου" (Holl's note: "gemeint ist das Jahr 375"); "τὰ μὲν ἐκ φιλομαθίας ἴσμεν, τὰ δὲ ἐξ ἀκοῆς κατειλήφαμεν, τοῖς δέ τισιν ἰδίοις ὠσὶ καὶ ὀφθαλμοῖς παρετύχομεν."
- Ptolemy's letter to Flora copied whole (HOLL1, Pan. 33.2): "ἵνα μή τις νομίσειεν ἡμᾶς ἐξ ἀκοῆς μόνον τὸν ἀπατεῶνα ἐλέγχειν."
- Apollinaris (PG42, Pan. 77.2): "Ὁ πρεσβύτης καὶ σεμνοπρεπής, ὁ ἀεὶ ἡμῖν ἀγαπητός ... Ἀπολλινάριος ὁ ἀπὸ Λαοδικείας."
- Birth and see (SOZ VI.32): "near the village of Besauduc, which was his birthplace, in the government of Eleutheropolis ... chosen by the inhabitants of Cyprus to act as bishop of the metropolis of their island ... in the concourse of a large city and in a seaport." This covers "monk from Palestine" and "chief city and port."
- Jerusalem (JER, Against John of Jerusalem 11): "We were present ... when the bishop Epiphanius spoke against Origen in your church ... talked of the 'silly old man' ... kissing his feet." The 390s are told roughly, as the sources allow.
- Letter 51 (JER): souls "were once angels in heaven, and that having sinned ... have been confined in bodies"; "he denies the resurrection of this flesh"; "explains allegorically all the trees"; "I have drunk also from the great river Euphrates, not spiritual but actual water, such as you can touch with your hand and imbibe with your mouth"; Paulinian "had several times fled from you"; "I gave orders to a number of deacons to seize him and to stop his mouth ... crying out that he was unworthy"; the curtain at Anablatha, "an image either of Christ or of one of the saints ... I tore it asunder ... that curtains of the other sort ... shall not be hung up in any church of Christ."
- Theophilus and Epiphanius (SOC VI.10): "Theophilus accused Epiphanius of entertaining low thoughts of God, by supposing him to have a human form ... a man of simple mind and manners ... caused a prohibition to be therein made of the reading of Origen's works"; Theophilus's "purpose of revenge on Dioscorus and his brethren."
- Sozomen (SOZ VIII.14): "Epiphanius had long regarded the writings of Origen with peculiar aversion ... Theophilus, perceiving that there could be no danger in following the example of Epiphanius, who was the object of popular praise."
- Constantinople (SOC VI.12, 14): "at the suggestion of Theophilus ... having ordained a deacon"; "You do many things contrary to the canons"; "he would neither stay nor pray with him, unless he would expel Dioscorus and his brethren ... and ... subscribe the condemnation of Origen's books"; "died on board the ship during his voyage."
- The Long Brothers (SOZ VIII.15): "'whether you have read any of our works' ... On Epiphanius replying that he had not seen them ... he had formed his judgment by the reports he had heard."
- Augustine (AUG, De haeresibus, preface and epilogue): "de octoginta haeresibus, separatis viginti, quas ante Domini adventum ... reliquas post Domini ascensum natas sexaginta"; "non modum, sed ordinem sum secutus."
- Nicaea II (MEND, Session 6): the iconoclast extract "not to introduce images into the churches"; "a passage attributed to Saint Epiphanius which is none of his: for the same divine father, having made a treatise of eighty chapters ... had he therefore considered the making of images to have been against the mind of Christ, he no doubt would have placed that also in his catalogue."
- Caroline Books (MEND note, lib. IV c. 25): "in an epistle which was sent by him to John Bishop of Jerusalem ... I tore it."
- The church as bride (HOLL1, Proem I): "μία δὲ μετὰ τὰς ὀγδοήκοντα ... Χριστοῦ νύμφη ἁγία ἐκκλησία, οὖσα μὲν ἀπ᾽ αἰῶνος."

**Sentence 4. "A heresy, in his book, is a teaching the church judged false." SOFTENED OR CHANGED.**
Epiphanius's own scope is wider and is not a matter of church judgment. Proem II 2.3: heresies are "κακοδόξου γνώμης ἀνθρωπείας ὑπὸ πεπλανημένων ἀνδρῶν ἐν κόσμῳ ἐσπαρμένης, ἐξότε ἄνθρωπος ἐπὶ γῆς πέπλασται" ("wrong human opinion, sown in the world by men gone astray, ever since man was formed on the earth"). Twenty of the eighty come before Christ (AUG: "separatis viginti, quas ante Domini adventum"). These include Hellenism, Judaism and the philosophical schools, which no church ever judged. Several Christian entries (the Apollinarians, the Antidicomarians) are his own judgment, made before or apart from any synod. The gloss turns his "every error since Adam" into "what the church condemned."

**Sentence 7. "Later writers, from Augustine to a council at Nicaea four centuries on, took his book as their list." SUMMARY HOLDS.**
Augustine followed his order (AUG). Nicaea II treats the eighty chapters as the complete catalogue of heresies (MEND, Session 6). "Took his book as their list" is fair for both.

**Sentence 8. "In old age he carried the same fight to Jerusalem and Constantinople, against the followers of Origen, a Christian teacher who had died long before." SUMMARY HOLDS.**
Old age: JER, "silly old man," "vainglorious old man." Jerusalem: JER, Against John 11. Constantinople: SOC VI.12, 14. Origen dead long before: SOC VI.10, "who had been dead nearly two hundred years." "The same fight" links back to the Panarion, which has its own entry on Origen. The link fits his own words in Letter 51, where he puts Origen beside "the votaries of the other eighty heresies."

**Sentence 11. "Each chapter names a heresy, tells its teaching and answers it." SUMMARY HOLDS.**
Proem I: names, unholy deeds, antidotes. Augustine disagrees ("nulla disputatione adversus falsitatem pro veritate decertans"), but he was working from the short summary, not the Panarion itself. Epiphanius's own text holds.

**Sentence 18. "In a letter to that bishop Epiphanius says why." SUMMARY HOLDS.**
Letter 51 §3–4 refers back to his preaching ("when I appealed to you ... you traversed my words, and reduced me to tears") and then sets out his case against Origen. The letter does give his reasons. "Says why" ties the letter to the sermon a little more neatly than the letter does.

**Sentence 20. "Epiphanius held to the plain sense." SUMMARY HOLDS.**
Letter 51 §5: "Adam speaks not in an allegory, but literally"; "the true drift of the passage does not admit."

**Sentence 22. "If the rivers were real, so was Eden." SUMMARY HOLDS.**
Letter 51 §5: "if the water is visible and real, then the fig-tree and the rest of the timber must be real also, and Adam and Eve must have been originally formed with real ... bodies."

**Sentence 23. "The same letter defends two acts of force." SUMMARY HOLDS.**
Letter 51 §1–2 is a self-defence of the forced ordination ("You should, I think, have been grateful to me"). §9 answers those with "this grievance against me" over the curtain. "Acts of force" is the narration's label, but the seizing, the stopped mouth and the torn curtain bear it out.

Heart: right. The medicine chest, the eighty heresies with their cures, and the old man's campaign against Origen in Jerusalem and Constantinople all come in the first 150 words. The census teaser says the same: author of the Panarion and a leading accuser of Origen.
