This was the Catholic Church at the height of its establishment and on the edge of its worst crisis: enormously wealthy, woven into the monarchies of Europe, and increasingly unable to control them. Rome's condemnation of Jansenism in the bull Unigenitus (1713) turned into a fifty-year political crisis in France. Then the kings of Portugal, France, Spain and Sicily drove out the Jesuits. In 1773 Clement XIV abolished the Society. His bull names quarrels, riots, charges against Jesuit teaching, and greed for property. The kings, it says, wished to stop Christians rising against one another. Then came the Revolution: church property nationalized, thousands killed or driven abroad, and Pope Pius VI carried off to die a French prisoner in 1799. His successor signed a concordat with Napoleon in 1801. In 1809 Napoleon annexed the Papal States, saying that spiritual power joined to rule over land had bred quarrels. He kept the pope captive until 1814. The pope outlived him and restored the Jesuits that year.

Rome ran its missions through Propaganda, the congregation for spreading the faith. Its collected decrees show three acts in these years.

In 1656 the Holy Office answered questions sent from China in 1645. Christian scholars could join the ceremony in the hall of Confucius when they took a degree. That cult, it said, seemed merely civil and political. Converts could also keep rites for their dead, once anything superstitious was removed.

In 1659 Propaganda sent orders to its vicars apostolic. They were not to urge any people to change its rites, customs and ways. What is more absurd, it asked, than to carry France, Spain or Italy into China (in a plain English rendering)?

In 1726 Propaganda ruled that native priests of China, Tonkin and Cochinchina must swear an oath. It was the one set by the constitution Ex illa die. That text seemed to name only Europeans. The oath bound a priest to obey the pope's order on the Chinese rites, without evasion.
