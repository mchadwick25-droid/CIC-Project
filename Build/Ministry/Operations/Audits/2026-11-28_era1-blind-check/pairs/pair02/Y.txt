At Alexandria, in Egypt, Origen's father taught him Scripture every day. Eusebius of Caesarea, who tells most of Origen's life about two generations after his death, from letters and from people who knew him, says the boy was not content with the plain, obvious meaning. He kept asking what the inspired words really meant, until his father scolded him to his face, for appearance's sake, not to search past his age or past the plain meaning. Yet in private the father thanked God for such a son, and they say he often stood by the sleeping boy, uncovered his chest and kissed it.

That question, what Scripture means beneath its plain words, became Origen's life's work. He taught the faith at Alexandria and then at Caesarea, on the coast of Palestine. He set the Hebrew text beside the Greek translations, and bishops and scholars after him read Scripture through his books and fought over them for centuries.

Demetrius, the bishop of Alexandria, entrusted to him alone the teaching of the faith to those who came to learn it. Eusebius says Origen took too literally Jesus' saying that some men have made themselves eunuchs for the sake of the kingdom of heaven, and castrated himself. He did it to keep the saying, and to give unbelievers no ground for scandal, since he taught women as well as men. Learning of it, Demetrius admired his daring and urged him to keep teaching.

Origen learned Hebrew, got the Hebrew Scriptures from the Jewish people, and searched out other Greek translations besides the Seventy, the old Greek translation which the churches read. He set them side by side in columns with the Hebrew. He kept the churches' Greek, he explained in a letter to a fellow scholar, so that he would not be found passing a forgery to the churches. Yet he learned the Jewish readings, so that in arguments with Jewish people he would not quote what their copies lacked.

In a book called On First Principles, he wrote of why readers go wrong. The unbelieving Jewish people, he said, followed the bare letter of the prophecies and did not receive Jesus. Heretics, teachers he judged false, reading of God's anger and jealousy, took the God of the Jewish Scriptures for an imperfect, lesser maker of the world, and said Jesus had proclaimed a more perfect God above him. Simple believers imagined things of God they would not believe of the most savage and unjust man. "All these I have spoken of hold false and impious opinions about God, or ignorant ones. The only reason, it seems, is that Scripture is not understood on its spiritual side, but is taken in its bare, literal sense."

As a human being is body, soul and spirit, he taught, so is Scripture: its body, the plain sense, helps simple believers, its soul those further on, and its spirit the perfect. Yet the Word of God, he wrote, had set stumbling blocks in it, things impossible if read by the letter, so that readers would look for a meaning worthy of God.

Porphyry, a pagan philosopher who had met Origen when Porphyry was young and later wrote against the Christians, attacked this way of reading. He tied it to the secret rites of Greek religion. Origen, Porphyry wrote, used the books of Chaeremon, a Stoic philosopher, and of Cornutus. "From these writers he learned the figurative way of explaining the Greek mystery rites, and he applied it to the Jewish scriptures."

Years later, on a church errand to Greece, Origen passed through Palestine and was ordained at Caesarea as a presbyter, an elder, a priest of the church. Here the old sources disagree. Eusebius says Demetrius, seeing him grow famous, had already written to bishops everywhere that his castration as a youth had been most foolish. After the ordination, with nothing else to say against him, Demetrius accused him bitterly, and those who had ordained him too. Jerome, writing over a century later, says the ordination itself so enraged Demetrius that he wrote everywhere against him.

Origen left Alexandria for Caesarea. In his book on the Gospel of John, he wrote: "We came out of the storm; we were brought out of Egypt. The God who led his people out from there set us free."

At Caesarea two brothers came to study with him, and he found them keen on Greek and Roman learning. He gave them a love of philosophy and turned them to the study of God, and after five years both became bishops in Pontus.

The emperor Decius, Eusebius says, persecuted the churches out of hatred for Philip, the emperor before him. For the word of Christ, Origen endured chains, tortures of the body, the iron collar and the dungeon. For many days his feet were stretched four spaces in the stocks, a wooden frame that held a prisoner's feet apart, and he was threatened with fire. His judge did all he could not to let him die. Origen died in the time of the emperor Gallus, at sixty-nine.

Pamphilus of Caesarea wrote a Defense of him, answering from Origen's own words those who said his books differed from the church's teaching. In the century after Origen's death, the church historian Socrates says, Basil of Caesarea and Gregory of Nazianzus got his books and drew from them the right reading of Scripture. When the Arians, who taught that the Son of God had a beginning, quoted Origen, he adds, the two showed that the Arians misunderstood him.

Gregory also sent a friend the Philocalia of Origen, a book of passages chosen from his writings for students. Its first chapter is on how Scripture is to be read and understood, and why, taken literally, it sometimes seems impossible.

About three centuries after his death, a council of bishops at Constantinople named him, with his writings, among the heretics.
