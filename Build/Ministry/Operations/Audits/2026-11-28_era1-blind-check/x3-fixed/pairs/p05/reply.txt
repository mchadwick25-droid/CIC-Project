Verdict: Y (Josephus) is somewhat better than X (Epiphanius).

Why Y is ahead
1. The opening. Y opens on a scene with a live question, deserter or "servant sent by you", and carries that question through the cave. X opens on a book's preface, which is a reading rather than a moment.
2. Credit where it matters. Y keeps the doubt in the witness's voice: "by chance or by God's providence, he writes." It also signals the dispute early: "the Christian writers who quote it give in different words."
3. Room for the connection. Y lets its link to the church come through what later readers did: Origen, Eusebius, and Jerome's Latin, "believed to be the Christ."

X's best rigor is still very good: Socrates and Sozomen are each credited with their differing motives.

Faults in X
- Paragraph 2 gives the ending and then goes back: "Later writers, from Augustine to a council at Nicaea... In old age he carried the same fight..."
- Augustine and Nicaea are told twice, in paragraph 2 and again near the end.
- The definition of heresy is awkward: "people led astray ever since Adam."
- One motive may be the narrator's guess: "so that no one could say he answered from report alone."
- It names about ten people. Adam and Socrates/Sozomen could be trimmed or given by role.
- One reference is vague: "A book written to answer the council's argument."

Faults in Y
- The surrender is told three times (the prayer, "In 67 he gave himself up," the cave). "Back in the cave" is a flashback after the Rome years.
- One sentence leans to one side: "The passage itself says he was the Christ."
- A gloss is tacked on after the quote: "The Greeks are those who were not Jewish."
- "about a hundred words" is the narrator's own count.
- "the books that tell nearly everything known of his life" comes close to a stock how-we-know sentence.

Grades: X: C+. Y: B.
