Failures: confirmed faults and the smallest fix

A confirmed fault is one named by 2 or more of the story's 3 judges. Faults named by one judge only are notes, and they are in the replies in x3/pairs/. Each fix is a direction for the rework thread, not a rewrite. Where a fix needs a fact, it comes from the source or the line is cut. Nothing is invented.

Pamphilus of Caesarea

  1. The Defense, the torture and the death are each told twice: once in paragraph 2, then again later. 3/3 judges

    Fix: Cut the paragraph-2 preview. Tell the Defense, the torture and the death once each, in the order they happened.

  2. Unclear pronoun: “He refused and ignored his threats.” 3/3 judges

    Fix: Name both people: who refused, and whose threats.

  3. “Eusebius took Pamphilus's name as a surname” is never explained. 3/3 judges

    Fix: Give the reason only if the source states it. If it doesn't, cut the line.

  4. It ends on Jerome, a later writer, and the Jerome material runs out of order (“Some ninety years after,” then “Jerome had also found”). 3/3 judges

    Fix: Put the Jerome material in its place in time, and end on Pamphilus.

  5. The money-changer quote (“the command to become approved money-changers is wholly shut out”) is opaque when heard once. 2/3 judges

    Fix: Swap it for a plainer line from the same source, or set it up in one short sentence first.

Clement of Alexandria

  1. Clumsy, nested aside: “who had taught under the emperor Hadrian, by Clement's reckoning.” 3/3 judges

    Fix: Split it into its own short sentence, or cut it.

  2. Out of order: the Valentinus passage breaks into the philosophy thread, and it goes back in time (“trained … under his teacher”). 2/3 judges

    Fix: Tell his life in order, and move Valentinus to where it bears on Clement, or cut it.

  3. The fear of philosophy is told several times (paragraphs 2, 7 and 9; “Many believers, he wrote, feared philosophy”). 2/3 judges

    Fix: State it once, where the story needs it.

  4. “In Eusebius's account, which holds most of what is told of his life” is a stock how-we-know sentence, and it comes late. 2/3 judges

    Fix: Fold the witness into the first claim that rests on him, early.

  5. “Christian and philosophers' views” is ungrammatical. 2/3 judges

    Fix: Rewrite it, for example as “Christian views and the philosophers' views.”

  6. “Alexander, a bishop” and “Severus” are never placed. 2/3 judges

    Fix: Give each a few words: where Alexander was bishop, and that Severus was emperor.

  7. It ends on a later writer (Jerome: “What in them is unlearned?”), not on Clement. 2/3 judges

    Fix: End on Clement's own act or words.

Hippolytus of Rome

  1. The Sardinia exile is told twice: “In 235 he was sent to Sardinia” and “In 235 … were deported to Sardinia.” The martyr's day is told twice too (“Rome kept his day among its martyrs” / “kept them both on 13 August”). 3/3 judges

    Fix: Tell the exile once and the feast day once, each in its place.

  2. “The split of Novatus” against “Novatian's party” will confuse a listener. 2/3 judges

    Fix: Use one name throughout, as the source names him, and say once who he was.

  3. The narrator corrects the source at the close (“Yet that split came later”), which leaves the Damasus ending tangled. 2/3 judges

    Fix: Credit the dating to a named witness, or drop the correction.

  4. “The high priesthood meant …” is a gloss tacked on after the quote. 2/3 judges

    Fix: Explain the term just before the quote.

  5. “A later bishop there” is vague. 2/3 judges

    Fix: Name the bishop, or say “a later bishop of Rome.”

  6. It ends on a later writer (Damasus). 2/3 judges

    Fix: End on Hippolytus.

Tatian

  1. The essence paragraph gives away the break and the ending (“left the church after Justin died,” “put away the copies”), and both are told again later. 2/3 judges

    Fix: Make the essence say what he stood for, not what happened. Tell the events once, in order.

  2. Narrator's guess: “so he meant the writings of Moses and the prophets.” 2/3 judges

    Fix: Cut it, or credit it to a named source if one says so.

  3. “Eusebius … did not know how Tatian combined the Gospels” only says what is missing. 2/3 judges

    Fix: Cut it, or say plainly what the Diatessaron was.

  4. “A later Roman teacher gives Tatian's reason”: the witness is never named. 2/3 judges

    Fix: Name the witness.

  5. Too many names. Rhodon, Ephrem and Homer do no work. 2/3 judges

    Fix: Cut the names that do nothing in the story.

  6. The long quote (“I was won over …” three times) runs as a chain of lists and does the narrator's work. 2/3 judges

    Fix: Keep the quote's first clause or two, and say the rest in the narrator's own words.

Tacitus

  1. The paragraph-2 preview gives away Christus, Tertullian and Sulpicius, then tells each again later. 3/3 judges

    Fix: Cut the preview. Tell each once, in order.

  2. Three absence-only asides: “naming no source for it,” “he names none of them,” “though he names no source.” 3/3 judges

    Fix: Keep one at most, where it matters, and cut the rest.

  3. “The Jewish people” is repeated clumsily (four times in two sentences). 3/3 judges

    Fix: Say it once, then use a pronoun or recast.

  4. Stock how-we-know lines: “the details of these deaths rest on him alone”; “Later, one Christian … and another.” 2/3 judges

    Fix: Name the later writers and say what each did, as part of the story.

Suetonius

  1. Absence-only: “He names no charge, number or kind of punishment.” 3/3 judges

    Fix: Cut it, or turn it into what he does say.

  2. It ends on Bede, a later writer. 3/3 judges

    Fix: End on Suetonius's own line.

  3. The Tertullian passage is muddled and retold (“names him on other matters … without naming Suetonius”). 2/3 judges

    Fix: Tell Tertullian once, plainly, or cut him.

  4. “He has one more line like it, under Nero” comes before the Nero passage and confuses the order. 2/3 judges

    Fix: Bring in the Nero line only where the story reaches Nero.

  5. “Chrestus … was also a real name” is left hanging and sounds like a modern note. 2/3 judges

    Fix: Say why it matters, credited to a source, or cut it.

Lactantius

  1. The main idea is told twice: “religion cannot be forced, since it lives only in the will” and “Religion cannot be compelled.” 3/3 judges

    Fix: Keep it once, at the turn of the story.

  2. Meta line: “He is the last on the tree of the Latin Apologists.” 3/3 judges

    Fix: Cut it. The site shows the tree.

  3. It ends off the subject. The Lucifer paragraph is long and about a later writer, and the close rests on Jerome. 3/3 judges

    Fix: Cut or shrink the Lucifer paragraph, and end on Lactantius.

  4. “He called his Divine, a full course on …” is awkward. 2/3 judges

    Fix: Give the book's full title, then what it is, in two sentences.

  5. Claims not credited: “In extreme old age” is Jerome's claim, told as fact, and the Constantine dream has no witness. 2/3 judges

    Fix: Put Jerome's name on the old-age claim, and name the witness for the dream.

Valentinus

  1. Things are told twice: the two branches (“carried the school on in two branches” / “split into an Italian branch”), the doctrine (paragraphs 2 and 9) and the break (“Tertullian alone says he broke with the church,” then again in full). 3/3 judges

    Fix: Cut the summary paragraph's previews, and tell each once.

  2. “Under one bishop and stayed until the time of a third” is vague to a listener. 2/3 judges

    Fix: Name the bishops, or give the years.

  3. The 1945 find in Egypt gives the ending early, then the story goes back. 2/3 judges

    Fix: Move the 1945 find to the end, where it falls in time.

  4. “Almost everything known of Valentinus comes from men who wrote against him” comes late and reads as a how-we-know sentence. 2/3 judges

    Fix: Say it early, in one short line, as the frame for what follows.

  5. Paragraphs 13–15 are a list of books. 2/3 judges

    Fix: Keep the one or two that carry the story, and cut the list.

Origen

  1. Told twice: “He set the Hebrew text beside the Greek translations,” then the columns again later. 3/3 judges

    Fix: Tell the Hexapla once, where it falls.

  2. “Fought over them for centuries” is a summing-up given early. 2/3 judges

    Fix: Cut it, or save the reception for the close.

  3. Too many names: “and of Cornutus” (and Chaeremon) are never explained. 2/3 judges

    Fix: Cut them.

  4. It ends on a later council, piling reception onto reception, not on Origen. 2/3 judges

    Fix: End on Origen.

Anatolius of Alexandria and Laodicea

  1. “Whose bishop, the same deacon, had died” is knotted, and it states a fact the story never set up. 3/3 judges

    Fix: Untangle it into two sentences, and set up the deacon first.

  2. The siege is never set up: who, why, and which quarter (“besieged quarter” / “palace quarter” / “free part”). The cause is muddled too (“urged peace with Rome, but they grew angry”). 3/3 judges

    Fix: Before the deed, say in one or two sentences who was besieged, by whom and why.

  3. Too many names: Paul of Samosata, Hild, Colman, Gregory. 3/3 judges

    Fix: Cut the later English names unless the Easter thread needs one.

  4. The outcome is given before the siege is told (“used the council's vote to slip out almost all the besieged”), so the siege is told twice. 2/3 judges

    Fix: Tell the siege once, in order.

  5. “Of the siege, Eusebius tells one of his deeds, as others told it to him” is a stock how-we-know line. 2/3 judges

    Fix: Fold the credit into the deed: “Eusebius heard that …”

  6. The Easter rule is too dense to follow by ear (“must not be counted from a new moon before the spring equinox”). 2/3 judges

    Fix: Give the rule in two plain sentences, with one example.
