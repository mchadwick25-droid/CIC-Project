## tree/josephus

Josephus

When the Roman general Vespasian took Jotapata, a walled town in Galilee, in the north of the country, Josephus hid in a cave with forty leading men. When the Romans found him on the third day and offered him his life, he prayed in secret, telling God he would surrender. "I give them my hands willingly, and I am content to live. And I declare openly that I do not go over to the Romans as a deserter from the Jewish people, but as a servant sent by you."

Josephus was a priest of the Temple in Jerusalem, from a priestly family, who commanded Jewish forces in Galilee when the Jewish people rose against Rome in 66. In 67 he gave himself up to Vespasian, and lived on at Rome under the emperors' favor. In his people's history, finished there about 93, a few lines tell of John the Baptist, of James, the brother of Jesus, and of Jesus himself. Christians copied those lines for centuries as the word of a Jew outside their faith, and argued over the one on Jesus, which the Christian writers who quote it give in different words.

Back in the cave, after his prayer, the men with him held out a sword. He could die as general of the Jewish people, they said, or as a traitor to them, and they rushed at him and called him a coward. He proposed that they draw lots, each to be killed in turn by the next. He was left last with one other man, by chance or by God's providence, he writes, and persuaded him to live. To the forty, surrender was treason and death was honor; to Josephus, in his telling, taking his own life was wrong.

At Rome he wrote the books that tell nearly everything known of his life. He wrote a history of the war in Greek, because others, he says, had written falsely of it, out of flattery to Rome or hatred of the Jewish people. Then came the Jewish Antiquities, his people's history in twenty books, to set their story before Greek-speaking readers. In his Life, a book on his own life, he defended himself against a rival historian who, he says, had written falsely of him.

In the Antiquities, Josephus tells of John, called the Baptist, a good man, he writes, who urged the Jewish people to deal justly with one another and to honor God. The ruler Herod, he says, feared that John's hold on the crowds might lead to a rising, so he had him imprisoned and killed.

In the same book, between a riot under Pilate, the Roman governor of Judea, and another disaster, stands a passage of about a hundred words on Jesus. Its key word, the Christ, means the Messiah, the anointed one the Jewish people awaited. "About this time there lived Jesus, a wise man, if one ought to call him a man. For he did astonishing deeds, and taught people who gladly receive the truth. He won over many of the Jewish people, and many of the Greeks as well. This man was the Christ." The Greeks are those who were not Jewish. The passage goes on: Pilate, at the urging of the leading men, had him crucified; he appeared alive on the third day; and the Christians, named after him, have not died out.

Further on, the high priest, the chief priest of the Temple, called a council of judges. He brought before them "the brother of Jesus who was called Christ, James by name", with some others, charged them with breaking the law, and had them stoned. The fairest-minded citizens protested that he had no right to call the court.

Origen, a Christian teacher of the third century, knew the lines on John and the phrase on James, and wrote that Josephus did not accept Jesus as the Christ. Early in the fourth century, Eusebius of Caesarea copied all three passages into his history of the church. He set the lines on John and Jesus, a Jew's own word, against forged records of Pilate's dealings with Jesus. Those records, he says, were written against Jesus and sent out in his own day with the emperor's approval.

Late in that century, another Christian writer, Jerome, quoted the passage in Latin, and his Latin says only that Jesus was believed to be the Christ. The passage itself says he was the Christ, and Origen wrote that Josephus did not accept him as the Christ, so how much of it Josephus wrote is uncertain.

At the close of his Life, Josephus called it the account of his whole life, and left others to judge his character by it, as they pleased.

## tree/tacitus

Tacitus

When daylight failed in the emperor Nero's gardens in Rome, Christians were set alight to light the dark. Some, covered in the skins of animals, were torn apart by dogs, and many were nailed to crosses. The Roman who tells it, the historian Tacitus, thought them guilty and despised them.

Writing a generation or more after the great fire of Rome in 64, Tacitus gave the fullest account from outside the church of how Nero punished the Christians. In it he named Christus, that is, Christ, from whom their name came, put to death under Pontius Pilate. His account runs to a few sentences, about 140 words of Latin, and the details of these deaths rest on him alone. Later, one Christian, Tertullian, answered him by name on another matter, and another, Sulpicius Severus, retold his scene in nearly his own words and called the dead innocent.

In the Annals, his year-by-year history of Rome, Tacitus tells how Rome turned after the fire to prayers and offerings to the gods. But nothing, not human effort, not the emperor's gifts, not the offerings, ended the shameful talk that someone had ordered the fire.

So, to kill the rumor, Nero put forward others as the guilty: people hated for their shameful acts, whom the common crowd called Christians. He punished them, Tacitus writes, with the most refined cruelty. Then Tacitus stops to explain the name, naming no source for it: "Christus, from whom that name comes, had been put to death in the reign of Tiberius by the governor Pontius Pilate." Their religion was a deadly superstition, he says with contempt, and though it was held back for a moment, it broke out again, in Judaea, where the evil began, and in Rome itself.

First those who confessed were seized. Then, on their information, a huge number were convicted, and he names none of them. They were convicted, he says, less for setting the fire than for hatred of the human race. Mockery was added to their deaths, the deaths told at the start, and Nero offered his own gardens for the show. He also put on chariot races, mixing with the crowd dressed as a charioteer, or standing in a chariot himself.

About 200, in Carthage, the Christian Tertullian answered Tacitus by name in his Apology, his defense of the Christians. His quarrel was with another of Tacitus's histories, which told where the Jewish people came from. That account of the Jewish people, Tertullian says, started the tale that the Christians, close to the Jewish people in religion, worshiped a donkey's head. Tertullian called Tacitus a liar, and a wordy one, and pointed to his own words against him: the same Tacitus tells that the Roman general who took Jerusalem went into the temple and found no image there. If the Jewish people worshiped an image, Tertullian says, the temple was the very place it would be shown.

About 400, Sulpicius Severus, a Christian writing a short history of the world, told the fire and the blame, and gave the same deaths in nearly Tacitus's own Latin words, though he names no source. Where Tacitus called the dead guilty, Sulpicius wrote: "So he turned the blame onto the Christians. And the cruellest tortures were used on the innocent."

Tacitus closed his own account this way: "So pity began to rise, even toward the guilty, who had earned the harshest punishment as a warning. It was as if they were being destroyed not for the public good, but for one man's cruelty."

## tree/ignatius-of-antioch

Ignatius of Antioch

Ignatius, bishop of the church at Antioch in Syria, was a prisoner bound for death in the arena at Rome when he wrote: "From Syria all the way to Rome I fight with beasts, by land and by sea, by night and by day. I am bound to ten leopards, I mean a band of soldiers. Even when they are treated well, they grow all the worse."

On that road he wrote seven letters, six to churches and one to a fellow bishop, and they tell nearly all we know of him. Three messages run through them: keep together around your bishop, hold that Christ truly suffered, and, to Rome, do not save me. Polycarp of Smyrna gathered the letters he held and sent them on, and later bishops quoted them for centuries.

He was taken in chains as a Christian, for the name of Christ. It was the reign of the emperor Trajan, as the church historian Eusebius wrote two centuries later. Across Asia, the Roman province that is now western Turkey, churches came out city by city to greet him, even those off his route. At Smyrna he met its bishop, Polycarp. The churches of Ephesus, Magnesia and Tralles sent their bishops to him there, and he wrote to those three churches and to Rome.

His first message was unity: each church should act as one with its bishop, its elders and its deacons. At Ephesus, he wrote, the elders, the council who led a church with its bishop, were fitted to him like strings to a harp, and he urged the whole church to sing with one voice. Nothing should be done without the bishop: one thanksgiving meal of one bread and one cup, at one altar, as there is one bishop. He warned that God does not live where there is division and anger.

His second message was aimed at teachers who said Christ only seemed to have a body and to suffer: "Some who are without God, that is, unbelievers, say that he only seemed to suffer. They themselves only seem to exist. If that is so, why am I in chains? Why do I long to fight the wild beasts? Do I die for nothing, then?" They shunned the thanksgiving meal, he wrote, because they would not say it was Christ's flesh. They thought the same of his own sufferings and those of others like him, he wrote, and he charged that they cared nothing for the widow, the orphan or the hungry.

His third message went to Rome. He was afraid, he wrote, that the love of the church there would try to spare him. If they spared him, it would be hard for him to reach God; if they said nothing on his behalf, he would become God's. "I am God's wheat. I am ground by the teeth of wild beasts, so that I may be found pure bread." The bread was an offering: by the teeth of the beasts, he wrote, he would be found a sacrifice to God.

At Troas, a port on the coast, he wrote to the churches of Philadelphia and Smyrna, and to Polycarp. News had come that the church at Antioch, which he had left in chains, was at peace and whole again, and he asked them to send a messenger to Syria to rejoice with it.

Later Polycarp wrote to the church in the city of Philippi, which had seen Ignatius pass, and counted him among those whose endurance they had witnessed. At their request he sent on the letters of Ignatius he held, because they spoke of faith and patience, and asked for surer news of him and those with him. Later still, Irenaeus, writing at Lyon in Gaul, quoted the wheat line, without naming him, as the words of a fellow believer condemned to the beasts.

Eusebius also wrote that, by report, Ignatius was sent from Syria to Rome and became food for wild beasts, for his testimony to Christ.

## tree/suetonius

Suetonius

In his life of the emperor Claudius, who ruled in the first century, the Roman writer Suetonius sets down the emperor's acts one after another. Claudius freed the people of old Troy from tax forever, because, he says, they founded the Roman race, and he let German envoys sit with the senators at the theater. Between those two comes one sentence: "He drove out of Rome the Jewish people who kept rioting at the urging of Chrestus."

Suetonius was a Roman scholar who wrote the lives of Rome's rulers from Julius Caesar to Domitian. This line, and one more under Nero, is all he says of the first Christians, if this one is about them at all. Each is one item in a list of an emperor's acts, and only the second names the Christians. Later, Christians complained, without naming him, that outsiders got their name wrong, calling them "Chrestians" and Christ "Chrestus", the name in his first line. A Christian chronicle set that line down almost in his words and joined it to a story in the New Testament.

His father, in his own telling, was an army officer, and Suetonius remembers being a young man under the emperor Domitian. His lives end with that emperor, so he wrote after Domitian's death, a generation or more after both events. He later ran the emperor Hadrian's letters.

He tells the expulsion in that one sentence, with no year, number or source. The Acts of the Apostles, in the New Testament, also tells of Claudius ordering the Jewish people out of Rome. At Corinth, the apostle Paul met a Jewish man and his wife lately come from Italy because of that order. Suetonius names Chrestus without saying who he was or where. The name is close in sound to Christus, the Latin form of Christ, and it was also a real name.

In his life of Nero, Suetonius lists measures by which, he says, abuses were severely punished and put down, and new rules made. Spending was limited, and taverns were barred from selling cooked food except beans and greens. Then comes the second line: "Punishments were laid on the Christians, a kind of people holding a new and harmful superstition." Superstition was his word for a religion he despised. The list runs straight on to the chariot drivers, who were stopped from roaming the streets and cheating people for fun.

He names no charge, number or kind of punishment, and he files these among Nero's acts that deserve no blame, some even praise, before he turns to Nero's crimes. In another chapter he says Nero himself set Rome on fire, and there he names no Christians. Another Roman, Tacitus, tells at length of Christians put to death under Nero.

About 200, at Carthage in North Africa, the Christian writer Tertullian had read Suetonius and names him on other matters. In his appeal to the pagans, without naming Suetonius, he says the name "Christian" comes from anointing; in plain words, Christ means "the anointed one", for the Jewish people anointed their priests and kings with holy oil. Then he turns to them: "You say it wrong, and call us 'Chrestians', for you are not even clearly sure of the name itself. Even so, it rings of sweetness or goodness." In Greek, chrestos means good or kind.

About 725, Bede, in his chronicle of the world, set down the Claudius line almost in Suetonius's words, without Chrestus. He put it in Claudius's ninth year and added that Luke also reports it, joining it to the Acts.

That is all Suetonius set down: seven Latin words between old Troy's tax and the German envoys' seats at the theater, and nine between the taverns' beans and greens and the chariot drivers.

## tree/valentinus

Valentinus

In a letter, Valentinus, who taught at Rome in the middle of the second century, compared the heart to an inn for travelers. It has holes and ruts and is often full of dung, and the men who stay there live filthily and take no care of a place that belongs to others.

At Rome he founded a school, a circle of students around a teacher. By his opponents' accounts, it taught that heavenly powers came forth in pairs from a first pair beyond any name, and that the world came from the fall of one of them. Of the writers near his time, Tertullian alone says he broke with the church. His students carried the school on in two branches, while the church's writers answered it with the faith in one God who made the world. For eighteen centuries his school was known almost only through their books, until writings from these circles were found in Egypt in 1945.

In the same letter he went on: "So it is with the heart, while no one gives it any thought. It is unclean, a home for many demons. But when the Father, who alone is good, visits it, it is made holy, and it shines with light." Those words survive because Clement of Alexandria quoted them to argue against him, and almost everything known of Valentinus comes from men who wrote against him.

Irenaeus says Valentinus came to Rome under one bishop and stayed until the time of a third. Tertullian, writing at Carthage early in the next century, tells why he left. "Valentinus had hoped to be made a bishop, because he had the talent and the gift of speech for it. But another man won the place, by the claim his suffering for the faith gave him. Angry at this, he broke away from the church of the true rule." By the true rule Tertullian meant that faith in one God, the maker of the world. He puts the break down to ambition, and to the wish for revenge that men who want first place feel. In another book he gives a second reason, a restless curiosity that spread to other believers, for which Valentinus was put out of the church at Rome more than once.

In Irenaeus's account, a pair beyond any name, Unspeakable and Silence, gave rise to Father and Truth, then to Word and Life, and Man and Church, and to more powers after them. One of the last broke away and fell, and from its fall came the world below and a lesser maker who made it. Tertullian says Valentinus held these powers inside God, as his thoughts, feelings and movements.

In a sermon Clement also keeps, Valentinus told his hearers they had come into death only to use it up and end it. "You are immortal from the beginning, and children of eternal life. You wanted death shared out to you, to spend it and squander it, so that death might die in you and through you." Clement answers that this makes some people saved by nature, by what they were born as, a different race come down from above. Then the commandments would be useless, he says, and salvation would come without the Savior.

Tertullian says Valentinus's follower Ptolemy made the powers into beings outside God. Hippolytus says the school split into an Italian branch, Ptolemy's, and an Eastern one.

Irenaeus wrote five books against them, aimed above all at the school of Ptolemy, which he calls a bud of Valentinus's. Tertullian wrote a book against the Valentinians, and Hippolytus a long work answering their teaching.

So far had his students moved from him, Tertullian says, that Valentinus himself was now nowhere. Yet there were still Valentinians, named after him, and only one man, Axionicus at Antioch, kept his teaching whole.

## tree/tatian

Tatian

Tatian says he had been initiated into the mysteries, the secret rites of the gods, and that at Rome he found Jupiter honored with human blood. He turned inward and asked how he might find the truth.

The answer he found made him a Christian in the middle of the second century. In his Address to the Greeks, a book written as a speech, he scorned their learning as younger and worse than the old writings of Moses. Irenaeus, his opponent, says he heard Justin teach and left the church after Justin died. He wove the four Gospels into one book, read in worship by the Syriac-speaking churches of the East. In the 400s one bishop put away the copies in his own churches.

Born, he says, in the land of the Assyrians, he was trained in Greek learning and followed rhetoric, the art of public speaking. The Greeks called every other people barbarians, and Tatian claimed the word for his own faith. "While I was thinking hard about what mattered, I came upon some barbarian writings. They were older than the teachings of the Greeks, and more divine than their error. I was won over by their plain words, and by the artlessness of those who spoke them. I was won over by their clear account of how all things were made, and by their foreknowledge of what was to come. I was won over by their excellent commands, and by the rule of one over all things." He calls Moses the founder of all barbarian wisdom, so he meant the writings of Moses and the prophets.

Every art the Greeks boast of, his Address says, came from barbarians: astronomy from Babylon, geometry from Egypt, letters from Phoenicia. Moses is older than Homer, he argues, citing Greek, Chaldean, Phoenician and Egyptian writers rather than Christian ones.

Tatian charges that Crescens, a philosopher at Rome who claimed to despise death, so feared it that he worked to bring death on Justin, and on Tatian too. Justin, he says, had shown the philosophers to be gluttons and cheats. At Rome Tatian taught Rhodon, a pupil from Asia.

Irenaeus, writing not long after, tells what Tatian taught after Justin's death. "As long as he was with him, he let nothing of this kind show. But after his martyrdom he left the church. Lifted up by the conceit of being a teacher, and puffed up as if he were better than the rest, he set up a school with a character all its own."

Irenaeus says Tatian invented aeons, invisible heavenly powers, like the followers of Valentinus, a teacher at Rome. Like Marcion, another Roman teacher, he called marriage nothing else than corruption and fornication. On his own he taught that Adam, the first man, was not saved. A later Roman teacher gives Tatian's reason: disobedience began with Adam. Irenaeus puts him among the Encratites, the self-controlled, who refused marriage, and some of them meat, a group he says sprang from earlier teachers. Eusebius, a bishop, makes Tatian the source of the Encratites, and places this about 172. Some two centuries later Epiphanius, a bishop in Cyprus, says Tatian crossed to the East and set up his school in Mesopotamia.

Eusebius, about 150 years after the break, did not know how Tatian combined the Gospels into one. He says Tatian called it the Diatessaron, Greek for "through four": one gospel story made from the four. Tatian's most used book, he says, was the Address. Later writers of the Church of the East say Ephrem, a deacon of Nisibis, commented on the Diatessaron.

Theodoret, bishop of Cyrrhus under Antioch, found more than two hundred copies held in honor in his churches. Tatian, he says, had cut out the lists of Jesus's ancestors and other passages showing the Lord born, as a man, of King David's family. Even believers true to the apostles' teaching, he writes, used it simply as a short book, not seeing the harm. He gathered them all, put them away, and brought in the four Gospels.

## tree/clement-of-alexandria

Clement of Alexandria

Clement of Alexandria went looking for teachers, he wrote, and he found them in Greece, in southern Italy and in the East. The last of them was the first of all in ability. "I came upon the last, who was the first in power. I tracked him down where he was hidden in Egypt, and I found rest."

Alexandria in Egypt became Clement's own city, late in the second century and into the third. There he taught, and he served as a presbyter, an elder of the church. He answered Christians who, he wrote, feared Greek philosophy, and his answer was that it might have been God's gift to lead the Greeks to Christ. Eusebius of Caesarea, writing about a century later, says Origen was among his pupils. About two centuries after Clement, Jerome, charged with defiling the church by quoting pagan writers, pointed to Clement's books.

In Eusebius's account, which holds most of what is told of his life, Clement was trained in the Scriptures at Alexandria under his teacher and then took over from him the teaching of the faith to learners there.

Many believers, he wrote, feared philosophy as children fear masks, afraid it would lead them astray. Clement also wrote against the followers of Valentinus, a teacher he opposed, who had taught under the emperor Hadrian, by Clement's reckoning. They left faith, Clement wrote, to ordinary believers like himself, "us, the simple," and claimed knowledge for themselves as men saved by their very nature. He copied words from Valentinus's own letters into his book in order to answer them.

His answer to the believers who feared philosophy took the word Paul uses in his letter to the Galatians for the guide who leads a child, and gave it to philosophy. He set philosophy beside the law that God gave the Hebrews through Moses. "Perhaps philosophy too was given to the Greeks directly and in the first place, until the Lord should call the Greeks. For it was a guide to bring the Greek mind to Christ, as the Law brought the Hebrews." By philosophy he meant whatever each Greek school had said well. But he would never call divine the human reasoning that men had mangled and falsified. He called his book the Miscellanies: notes, he said, patched together, passing from one thing to another.

Eusebius writes that under the emperor Severus the churches were persecuted. Every day, Clement wrote, he and his fellow believers saw martyrs burned, impaled and beheaded before their own eyes. Some asked, he wrote, why Christians were persecuted and put to death if God cared for them. He answered that Christ had foretold it, and that Christians suffered unjustly, for doing right. Christ's word to flee when persecuted, he taught, was meant to keep believers from bringing harm on themselves or on the persecutor, and anyone who gave himself up to the judge shared the guilt of his own death.

Alexander, a bishop, wrote from prison to the church at Antioch and sent the letter by Clement, "the blessed presbyter." Later, writing to Origen, Alexander counted two blessed men among those who had gone on ahead, one of them "the holy Clement," his master and helper. Through them, he wrote, he had come to know Origen.

Jerome's critic had asked him why he stained the whiteness of the church with the filth of the pagans by quoting their books. Jerome answered with a roll of Christian writers who had done the same. Among them he named Clement, in his judgment the most learned of all, and he added that Origen imitated him in ten books of Miscellanies that set Christian and philosophers' views side by side. Of Clement's books Jerome asked, "What in them is unlearned? Or rather, what in them does not come from the heart of philosophy?"

## tree/origen

Origen

At Alexandria, in Egypt, Origen's father taught him Scripture every day. Eusebius of Caesarea, who tells most of Origen's life about two generations after his death, from letters and from people who knew him, says the boy was not content with the plain, obvious meaning. He kept asking what the inspired words really meant, until his father scolded him to his face, for appearance's sake, not to search past his age or past the plain meaning. Yet in private the father thanked God for such a son, and they say he often stood by the sleeping boy, uncovered his chest and kissed it.

That question, what Scripture means beneath its plain words, became Origen's life's work. He taught the faith at Alexandria and then at Caesarea, on the coast of Palestine. He set the Hebrew text beside the Greek translations, and bishops and scholars after him read Scripture through his books and fought over them for centuries.

Demetrius, the bishop of Alexandria, entrusted to him alone the teaching of the faith to those who came to learn it. Eusebius says Origen took too literally Jesus' saying that some men have made themselves eunuchs for the sake of the kingdom of heaven, and castrated himself. He did it to keep the saying, and to give unbelievers no ground for scandal, since he taught women as well as men. Learning of it, Demetrius admired his daring and urged him to keep teaching.

Origen learned Hebrew, got the Hebrew Scriptures from the Jewish people, and searched out other Greek translations besides the Seventy, the old Greek translation which the churches read. He set them side by side in columns with the Hebrew. He kept the churches' Greek, he explained in a letter to a fellow scholar, so that he would not be found passing a forgery to the churches. Yet he learned the Jewish readings, so that in arguments with Jewish people he would not quote what their copies lacked.

In a book called On First Principles, he wrote of why readers go wrong. The unbelieving Jewish people, he said, followed the bare letter of the prophecies and did not receive Jesus. Heretics, teachers he judged false, reading of God's anger and jealousy, took the God of the Jewish Scriptures for an imperfect, lesser maker of the world, and said Jesus had proclaimed a more perfect God above him. Simple believers imagined things of God they would not believe of the most savage and unjust man. "All these I have spoken of hold false and impious opinions about God, or ignorant ones. The only reason, it seems, is that Scripture is not understood on its spiritual side, but is taken in its bare, literal sense."

As a human being is body, soul and spirit, he taught, so is Scripture: its body, the plain sense, helps simple believers, its soul those further on, and its spirit the perfect. Yet the Word of God, he wrote, had set stumbling blocks in it, things impossible if read by the letter, so that readers would look for a meaning worthy of God.

Porphyry, a pagan philosopher who had met Origen when Porphyry was young and later wrote against the Christians, attacked this way of reading. He tied it to the secret rites of Greek religion. Origen, Porphyry wrote, used the books of Chaeremon, a Stoic philosopher, and of Cornutus. "From these writers he learned the figurative way of explaining the Greek mystery rites, and he applied it to the Jewish scriptures."

Years later, on a church errand to Greece, Origen passed through Palestine and was ordained at Caesarea as a presbyter, an elder, a priest of the church. Here the old sources disagree. Eusebius says Demetrius, seeing him grow famous, had already written to bishops everywhere that his castration as a youth had been most foolish. After the ordination, with nothing else to say against him, Demetrius accused him bitterly, and those who had ordained him too. Jerome, writing over a century later, says the ordination itself so enraged Demetrius that he wrote everywhere against him.

Origen left Alexandria for Caesarea. In his book on the Gospel of John, he wrote: "We came out of the storm; we were brought out of Egypt. The God who led his people out from there set us free."

At Caesarea two brothers came to study with him, and he found them keen on Greek and Roman learning. He gave them a love of philosophy and turned them to the study of God, and after five years both became bishops in Pontus.

The emperor Decius, Eusebius says, persecuted the churches out of hatred for Philip, the emperor before him. For the word of Christ, Origen endured chains, tortures of the body, the iron collar and the dungeon. For many days his feet were stretched four spaces in the stocks, a wooden frame that held a prisoner's feet apart, and he was threatened with fire. His judge did all he could not to let him die. Origen died in the time of the emperor Gallus, at sixty-nine.

Pamphilus of Caesarea wrote a Defense of him, answering from Origen's own words those who said his books differed from the church's teaching. In the century after Origen's death, the church historian Socrates says, Basil of Caesarea and Gregory of Nazianzus got his books and drew from them the right reading of Scripture. When the Arians, who taught that the Son of God had a beginning, quoted Origen, he adds, the two showed that the Arians misunderstood him.

Gregory also sent a friend the Philocalia of Origen, a book of passages chosen from his writings for students. Its first chapter is on how Scripture is to be read and understood, and why, taken literally, it sometimes seems impossible.

About three centuries after his death, a council of bishops at Constantinople named him, with his writings, among the heretics.

## tree/hippolytus-of-rome

Hippolytus of Rome

A teacher from Smyrna named Noetus said that Christ was the Father himself, so that the Father was born, suffered and died. Twice the elders, the church's priests, called him before the church, and they put him out.

Hippolytus, who tells that story, taught in the church at Rome in the early 200s and wrote in Greek. Against Noetus's teaching he held that the Son was another beside the Father, yet one God with him. He would not give way to two bishops of Rome, Zephyrinus and Callistus, on this, or on how far the church could forgive. He set the quarrel down in a book against every teaching he held false. In 235 he was sent to Sardinia with Rome's bishop, and Rome kept his day among its martyrs.

In Hippolytus's telling, the only account of the quarrel that survives, Zephyrinus let Noetus's teaching spread at Rome, with Callistus as his adviser. Hippolytus and his side stood against them for the truth.

He called the Son the Word, a name from John's gospel for the Son as he was with the Father before he became man: "When I say 'another,' I do not mean two Gods. I mean it as light from light, or water from a spring, or a ray from the sun. For there is one power, which comes from the whole. The whole is the Father, and from him comes the power, the Word." He imagined someone asking whether he spoke of two Gods, and answered that he spoke of one God, in two persons, with the grace of the Holy Spirit as a third.

While Zephyrinus still lived, Hippolytus says, Callistus called him and his side worshippers of two gods, because they would not go along with Callistus.

When Zephyrinus died, Callistus took his place. Hippolytus charges that Callistus forgave every sin of the body to anyone who came over to him, and held that a bishop should not be removed even for the gravest sin. Some whom Hippolytus's own side had judged and put out of the church went over to Callistus.

His book, the Refutation of All Heresies, traced the teachings he held false back to Greek philosophers, mystery cults and astrologers. Its last part turned to the teachings of his own day, Callistus's among them. In its opening he wrote that the apostles had received the Holy Spirit and handed it on to those who believed rightly: "We are their successors. We share in the same grace, in the high priesthood and in the teaching, and we are counted guardians of the church. We do not let our eyes droop, and we do not keep the right word silent." The high priesthood meant the church's chief priestly office.

He drew up a table, on a cycle of sixteen years, for finding the day of Easter, a day over which Christians differed, some keeping the moon's fourteenth day. Jerome says it gave Eusebius the occasion for his own nineteen-year cycle.

In 235, Rome's list of bishops records, Pontianus, the bishop, and Hippolytus, whom it calls an elder, were deported to Sardinia. Rome's calendar of martyrs kept them both on 13 August. In the East, Eusebius wrote that Hippolytus had led another church, without saying which, and a later bishop there quoted him for the faith and called him bishop and martyr.

More than a century later, Damasus, bishop of Rome, set a verse on his tomb on the Tiburtine Road, telling what he had heard. Hippolytus, an elder, it was said, had always stayed in the split of Novatus while a tyrant's orders pressed. This was the split that Novatian's party made from the church at Rome in 251, sixteen years after the exile, refusing to take back those who had given way in persecution. As Hippolytus went to the kingdom of the faithful, the people asked where to go, and he told them all to follow the catholic faith, the faith of the whole church. So, the verse says, he earned his place as a martyr.

## tree/pontius-the-deacon

Pontius the Deacon

In 257 the governor at Carthage sent the city's bishop, Cyprian, into exile at Curubis, a small town on the coast, because he would not take part in the ceremonies of Rome's gods. One of the men who went with him wrote later that Cyprian had chosen him from among the men of his household as "a willing exile." In the same breath he wished he could have shared Cyprian's martyrdom too.

His book never gives his name, but Jerome, more than a century later, calls him Pontius, one of Cyprian's deacons. Beyond that, almost all we know of him is his own book, the work of a man who loved and praised his bishop. Pontius went into exile by choice, heard Cyprian's dream there, and was with him on his last night. After Cyprian's death, Pontius wrote The Life and Passion of Cyprian, the story of his life and of his suffering and death as a martyr, so that later readers would have his example.

Cyprian told them of a dream he had on their first day at Curubis. Before he fell asleep, he said, a young man of more than human size seemed to lead him to the governor's court, where the governor wrote his judgment on his tablet. The young man standing behind the governor read it and showed it by a sign. In Cyprian's words, as Pontius gives them: "For he held his hand open and flat, like a sword blade, and copied the stroke of the usual execution. What he wanted understood, he showed as clearly as plain speech. I understood the sentence of martyrdom that was to come."

Cyprian begged a delay of one day, and the young man signaled that it was granted. Pontius read the one day as a year more of life.

Back from Curubis, Cyprian stayed at his gardens. Pontius says he turned down friends' offers of places to go. But Cyprian's own last letter says that when he heard officers had been sent to take him to another city, his dearest friends persuaded him to leave for a while. He agreed, he wrote, because a bishop should confess his faith in the city where he leads the church. Arrested, he spent his last night in an officer's house, and Pontius and others who shared Cyprian's table were with him. Next day the governor sentenced him to the sword as an enemy of the Roman gods. He died on 14 September 258, on the very day of the year, Pontius says, on which his sentence had been shown him.

The church's forebears, Pontius writes, had honored ordinary believers who died as martyrs and written down their sufferings. It would be hard if the death of so great a bishop were passed over, and his deeds in life stayed hidden. "I have decided to write down a few things, briefly. It is not that the life of so great a man is hidden from anyone, even among the pagans. It is so that this great and matchless example may be passed on to those after us too, into undying memory."

He began where Cyprian's faith began, because the deeds of a man of God, he wrote, should be counted only from the time he was born of God. Of Cyprian's earlier studies, he says, "these things I pass over." For what Cyprian told the governor at the hearing that sent him into exile, he sends readers to the court record.

The book was copied with Cyprian's own writings. In his catalog of Christian writers, Jerome called it "a notable volume."

His book ends in grief. "Shall I grieve that I was not his companion? But his victory must be celebrated. Shall I celebrate the victory? But I grieve that I am not his companion. Yet I must confess it to you plainly, and you know it too: this was how I felt. I rejoice much, far too much, in his glory. Yet I grieve more that I remained behind."

## tree/anatolius-of-alexandria-and-laodicea

Anatolius of Alexandria and Laodicea

In a besieged quarter of Alexandria, the bread had run out, and hunger was harder to bear than the enemy outside. Anatolius, a learned Christian, stood before the city council. "I advise sending out of the gates those we have too many of, who are of no use to us at all: old women, infants and old men."

Anatolius used the council's vote to slip out almost all the besieged. He later became bishop of Laodicea in Syria. He also wrote a rule for finding Easter, and three and four centuries on, Irish churchmen and the defenders of Rome's Easter both claimed him.

Born in Alexandria, he lived in the later 200s. Eusebius, the church historian, ranked him first among the men of his time in arithmetic, geometry, astronomy, logic, physics and rhetoric, and reports that the citizens asked him to found a school of Aristotle's philosophy. Of the siege, Eusebius tells one of his deeds, as others told it to him.

The palace quarter held out against a Roman army, while the rest of the city sided with Rome. Anatolius sent word to a deacon of the church in the free part of the city. The deacon, known to the Roman general, won from him safety for any who deserted the besieged side.

Anatolius called the council and urged peace with Rome, but they grew angry. He argued instead that the city should feed only men and youths. Rising first, he voted that all not needed for the fighting, men or women, should leave, since staying meant starving, and the council agreed.

Under cover of that decree he saved almost all the besieged: the church's people first, and with them many more beyond it, men among them secretly dressed in women's clothes. By night they went out to the Roman camp, where the deacon took them in "like a father and a doctor" and nursed them.

After the war, the bishop of Caesarea in Palestine made him a second bishop there, to take over at his death. Then bishops meeting at Antioch called him there, to judge its own bishop, Paul of Samosata, who, Eusebius says, taught that Christ was by nature an ordinary man. On the way, at Laodicea, whose bishop, the same deacon, had died, the believers kept him as bishop.

Eusebius kept part of his Greek book on Easter. Easter's date was tied to the Jewish Passover full moon, the fourteenth day of the first month of spring. That month, his rule ran, must not be counted from a new moon before the spring equinox, in March, when day and night are equal. Those who begin it earlier, he wrote, "make no small mistake, and no careless one." He set his count on a nineteen-year table of the spring new moons, which then begins again. The rule was not his own, he said, since the Jewish people kept it before Christ.

About 600, Columbanus, an Irish abbot, wrote to Pope Gregory the Great to defend his homeland's Easter against an Easter past the moon's twentieth day. He drew on a Latin Easter book under Anatolius's name. Such a moon, he argued, rises after midnight, so darkness rules most of the night, but the feast of the resurrection is a feast of light.

In 664 the quarrel came before a church meeting at Whitby, in Abbess Hild's monastery in northern England, as Bede, writing in 731, tells it. Colman, for the Irish side, said Anatolius, a holy man, had written that Easter could fall any day from the moon's fourteenth to its twentieth. Wilfrid, for Rome's side, claimed him for a different rule: "It is agreed that Anatolius was a most holy man, most learned and most worthy of praise." Anatolius, he said, had set out a cycle of nineteen years, which the whole church of Christ kept.

Bede doubted the Irish claim. The northern Irish and Picts before Whitby, he wrote, believed they followed the writings of the holy and praiseworthy Father Anatolius, and anyone skilled could very easily tell whether that was true.

## tree/pamphilus-of-caesarea

Pamphilus of Caesarea

Eusebius of Caesarea wrote of how his friend Pamphilus gave what he could to poor book lovers: "He handed out the holy Scriptures as well, and most readily, to keep and not only to read. He gave them to men, and to women too, when he saw they were devoted to reading. So he kept many books prepared, to give freely to anyone who wished whenever need called for it."

Pamphilus of Caesarea was a presbyter, an elder and priest of the church, at Caesarea, a port on the coast of Roman Palestine. He built a library for his church and, Jerome says, copied most of Origen's works with his own hand. Origen, the Bible scholar who had taught at Alexandria and then at Caesarea, had died in the century before. In the persecution Pamphilus was tortured, held two years in prison and put to death about 310. Eusebius says the two friends wrote a Defense of Origen together, and the books Pamphilus kept were read for generations after him.

Eusebius, whose pages hold nearly all of his life, says he was devoted above all to the Scriptures. Jerome says he searched the world for books, buying all of Origen's at great cost.

Then the emperors' orders reached Palestine: tear down the churches, burn the Scriptures, then make everyone sacrifice to the Roman gods. In the persecution's fifth year, the governor tested Pamphilus's learning in public speaking and philosophy, then tried to force him to sacrifice. He refused and ignored his threats. In anger the governor had his sides scraped in long torture and put him in prison with the confessors, Christians who had held to the faith under trial.

Eusebius says he and Pamphilus wrote the Defense with care, because of those who looked for faults. Photius of Constantinople, a scholar five centuries later, says Pamphilus wrote most of it in prison. It answered charges that Origen taught that the soul lived before the body, and that he made Christ either a mere man or one whose deeds only seemed to happen. The Defense set out Origen's own words with replies between. It was written to confessors condemned to forced labor in the mines of Palestine. The Defense says that readers of pagan books, and even of heretics' books, whose teaching the church condemns, are usually pardoned. But Origen's accusers think that they alone have the skill to test what they read, keeping the good like a money-changer who tests each coin and throws out the false. "They think the command to become approved money-changers is wholly shut out for those who read Origen's books. Such money-changers know how to keep what is good and hold back from every kind of evil. If anyone is only seen reading his books, at once they drench him in the shame of the heretics."

After two whole years in prison, Pamphilus and his companions came before a new governor. He knew they had held firm under earlier tortures, and asked only whether they would now obey. Each gave the same answer, and he sentenced them to death, as he had others before them. Pamphilus died with eleven others, the only presbyter among them. Eusebius took Pamphilus's name as a surname.

Eusebius sent readers to the library's catalog to learn which of Origen's works survived, and Jerome used the library in his day. Rufinus of Aquileia put the Defense's first book, the only one that survives, into Latin under Pamphilus's name. Some ninety years after Pamphilus died, Jerome complained that readers ignored bishops who had since condemned Origen as a heretic, because they thought a martyr had praised him.

Jerome had also found twenty-five volumes of Origen's commentaries on the twelve prophets, copied in Pamphilus's hand, guarding them, he says, like a king's riches. "If it is a joy to own one letter from a martyr, how much greater to own so many thousand lines! It seems to me he marked them with the traces of his own blood."

## tree/peter-of-alexandria

Peter of Alexandria

At the fourth Easter of the persecution, Peter, the bishop of Alexandria, wrote about Christians who had been jailed and tortured until they gave way and offered sacrifice to the gods. Some were already in a third year of mourning their fall. "They did not come to this by deliberate choice. They were betrayed by the weakness of the flesh, since they show the marks of Jesus in their bodies." The marks were the scars of their torture.

Peter led the churches of all Egypt and Libya through the persecution that began in 303. He set out how the church should take back the fallen, weighing what each had suffered and chosen. In those years one of his own bishops, Melitius, broke from him, and in 311 Peter was beheaded.

He had become bishop about 300, less than three years before the emperors ordered the churches pulled down and the Scriptures burned, and bishops of Egypt were thrown into prison.

Those who had given way asked to share communion again: a place in the prayers and in the body and blood of Christ. In that Easter writing, a sermon on repentance, Peter gave each of them a time of fasting and prayer, and its rules are known as his canons. The tortured would fast forty more days, the days Christ fasted, and those jailed without torture would wait a year more. For those who fell from fear alone, he pointed to the parable of the fig tree, spared one more year to bear fruit. Those who paid to be left alone, or left everything and fled, were not to be blamed. Clergy who gave themselves up to the persecutors of their own accord, fell, and then took up the struggle again could share communion, but could no longer serve in their office.

Four bishops of Egypt, writing from prison, rebuked Melitius, a bishop of Upper Egypt, in the south, for making priests and deacons in churches that were not his. By the law of their fathers, they wrote, no bishop could ordain outside his own church, so that those ordained were known and confusion avoided. He should have waited for the judgment of Peter, whom they called their great bishop and father.

An account kept with that letter says Melitius did not answer. After the four bishops were martyred, he went to Alexandria. The priests Peter had appointed to visit the churches were then in hiding, and Melitius set them aside and put men of his own in their place. Peter wrote to his people that Melitius was invading his church and trying to pull its priests from his authority. "Take heed of this. Do not share communion with him until I meet him with some wise and sensible men, and see what he has in mind."

Later writers tell the break otherwise. Athanasius, a later bishop of Alexandria and an enemy of the Melitians, says a council of bishops under Peter removed Melitius for many crimes, among them sacrificing to the gods. Epiphanius of Salamis, writing some sixty years later, says the two were in prison together and split over the fallen. Peter, he says, pleaded to receive them as they repented, while Melitius would wait until the persecution ended and would not restore fallen clergy to their rank.

His end came after an order from the emperors had been posted letting Christians worship again, which the eastern emperor Maximinus would not accept. Eusebius of Caesarea, who wrote at the time, gave it in these words: "He was seized for no cause at all, without warning, and suddenly and without reason, as if Maximinus had ordered it, he was beheaded." It was 311, the ninth year of the persecution, and many other bishops of Egypt died with him.

The split outlived him, and in 325 the bishops gathered at the Council of Nicaea let Melitius keep his city but took away his power to ordain. Athanasius remembered Peter as bishop and martyr. His rules were copied into the collections of church law, and in 692 a council set its seal on the canons of the fathers, naming among them those of Peter, archbishop of Alexandria and martyr.

## tree/lactantius

Lactantius

"I myself saw in Bithynia a governor astonishingly elated with joy, as if he had conquered some nation of barbarians. It was because one man, who had held out for two years with great courage, seemed at last to give way." Lactantius wrote this during the persecution of the Christians, among governors who boasted that they had shed no blood. The judges, he said, knew it was a contest, and tortured to win.

Lactantius was teaching Latin public speaking at Nicomedia in Bithynia, in what is now north-west Turkey, the city where the emperor Diocletian kept his palace, when the Great Persecution began in 303. He answered with the Divine Institutes, seven books arguing the faith to educated pagans from their own philosophers and historians. The books hold that religion cannot be forced, since it lives only in the will. He is the last on the tree of the Latin Apologists, who made the case for the faith in Latin.

Most of his life outside his books rests on Jerome, a Christian writer later that century, who says he came from Africa. He was called to Bithynia to teach, he says, and was teaching when "the temple of God was overthrown." The imperial order leveled churches, burned Scriptures, stripped high-ranking Christians of their honors, and took freedom from Christian servants who would not give up the faith. Its aim, Lactantius writes, was to make Christians worship the gods "by force and tortures."

Then two men published books against the Christians "in my presence, and to my grief," he says. One, as he tells it, a philosopher who praised poverty, wrote three books saying a philosopher's duty was to call people back to the gods. The other, a judge who had pressed for the persecution, wrote two books to the Christians, to prove their Scripture contradicted itself.

He set out to answer them and all like them. Of the Christian writers before him, he found Tertullian learned in every kind of literature, but rough and very obscure. Cyprian, the most renowned, had answered a pagan with Scripture the man thought false. A beginner needs milk before solid food, Lactantius says, and must be refuted by his own authorities.

He took his title from the lawyers' handbooks of the civil law, called Institutes, written to settle quarrels. He called his Divine, a full course on hope, life, salvation, immortality and God. The pagan gods, he argued, had once been men. The philosophers refuted one another, and there could be no religion without wisdom, nor wisdom without religion.

Religion cannot be compelled, he wrote, so it must be argued with words rather than blows. The pagans said their rites must be defended. He agreed religion must be defended with all one's strength: "For religion must be defended not by killing but by dying, not by cruelty but by endurance, not by crime but by faithfulness."

A book on how the persecutors died survives in one copy under the name Lucius Caecilius, and whether it is Lactantius's is disputed. It tells how a dream directed the emperor Constantine to mark his soldiers' shields with the sign of Christ. In extreme old age, Lactantius taught Constantine's son Crispus in Gaul.

Jerome wished Lactantius could have built up the faith as easily as he tore down other people's. Yet he asked who could stop him, Jerome, from reading the Institutes, written against the pagans with much ability.

Lucifer of Cagliari, a bishop, wrote to Constantine's son, the emperor Constantius, who backed the Arian teaching that there was a time when the Son of God did not exist. You say this teaching must be defended, Lucifer told him, and you defend it with the sword, but we know religion is defended not by killing but by dying for God. Lucifer had taken over Lactantius's sentences without naming him, recast to address the emperor.

Jerome called Lactantius the most eloquent man of his time, yet so poor in this life that he often lacked even what he needed.

## tree/methodius-of-olympus

Methodius of Olympus

In the Banquet, a book by Methodius of Olympus, a young woman and two friends climb a rough, steep path to a garden where ten guests will meet. A tall woman in a robe white as snow embraces them like a mother, and her name means Virtue. They feast under a wide, shady tree, and she asks each guest to praise the virgin life.

Methodius was bishop of Olympus, a city in Lycia, in the south of Asia Minor. In the Banquet, ten young women each praise chastity, the unmarried life given to God. In a dialogue, On the Resurrection, he argued against Origen, the great teacher of Alexandria and Caesarea, that the body is no chain on the soul and that the same body will rise. A later bishop copied his words into his own case against Origen, and Jerome says he died a martyr.

The Banquet is a story within a story, in which one friend begs another to repeat the speeches. One guest says praising virginity does not abolish marriage, which is still God's work. "The moon may be greater than the stars, but its light does not put out the light of the other stars." Virtue, speaking last, says it is no chastity to keep the body pure but not the tongue, the eyes, the hands or the mind.

The dialogue, as a later reader summed it up centuries on, asks what the body is to the soul. Origen, Methodius wrote, held that the soul lived without a body before the fall, the first sin, and was given the body afterwards as a chain. At the resurrection, Origen held, the same flesh does not return, only each person's shape, stamped on a different, spiritual body. Methodius could not bear, he says, those who turn bones and flesh into symbols, so that the dead rise without flesh.

If the body were a chain to hold back evil, he answered, it had failed, since Cain committed murder in this body. A human being, he held, is soul and body joined in one. In his telling, a craftsman finds that an envious man has spoiled a beautiful statue he made, so he melts it down and casts it again. "For the statue cannot be lost while it is in the same craftsman's hands, even if it is melted down into its material again. It can be restored. But the blemishes and the damage can be lost, for they melt away. They cannot be restored." So, he wrote, God, seeing his finest work spoiled by envy, dissolves the body in death and remakes it in the resurrection.

Eusebius of Caesarea, in a book he wrote in Origen's defense, asked how Methodius dared to write against Origen now. Jerome, who reports the question, says Methodius was crowned with martyrdom at Chalcis in Greece, at the end of the last persecution, though others, he adds, placed it under earlier emperors, Decius and Valerian.

Later in the fourth century, Epiphanius of Salamis wrote against Origen and chose to rest his case on On the Resurrection, setting out its words on the rising of the same body word for word.

More than a century after Methodius, the church historian Socrates, who defended Origen, put him first among four men who attacked Origen. "Worthless people, who cannot be noticed on their own, want to be seen by finding fault with their betters. Methodius was the first to fall into this." Socrates adds that Methodius, after railing against Origen in many places, took it all back, as it were, and praised him in a dialogue he called the Xenon. Socrates alone tells of this change of heart.

At the Banquet's close, Virtue tells the guests to rise and stand under the tree. One guest begins a hymn, and the others answer her, each keeping herself for Christ, the Bridegroom.
