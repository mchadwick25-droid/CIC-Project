# Outline check: Bardaisan of Edessa (`bardaisan-of-edessa`)

## My own account of the heart (written before reading `Outline.md`)

From the census entry (teaser, card), the source sheet and what he is known for:

- **The essence:** Bardaisan was a Syriac-speaking teacher at Edessa around the year 200. In a dialogue one of his pupils wrote down, he answers a newcomer's question and argues that God made people free: the stars and fate shape our birth, fortune, health and death, but not what we choose to do, and the different laws of every country, and the Christians who keep one law everywhere, prove it. Later church writers called him a heretic, and Ephrem the Syrian answered him in his own city in prose and in song.
- **Also told:** what the book says (the harp image: a person made unable to do wrong would be an instrument, not his own; Nature, Fate and freedom, each with its own field; the laws of countries; the Christians one name, one law; he had once followed the astrologers himself); that he was learned in Greek and Syriac and close to Edessa's ruler (Epiphanius); that he wrote dialogues against Marcion's followers and had many pupils who put his works into Greek (Eusebius, who calls him a defender of the faith); Epiphanius's report of his refusal to deny he was a Christian (Contested, credited).
- **What came of him:** (1) Ephrem's long answer in prose and hymns, at Edessa, charging him with the Seven, the Entities and mortality without Adam's sin, and by Sozomen's account taking up the meters of his son Harmonius (Ephrem says Bardaisan's own hymns); (2) the charge of heresy, told by witnesses who differ (Eusebius: Valentinian first, then left them, never wholly clean; Epiphanius: an excellent churchman who later fell to the Valentinians; Hippolytus and later writers on Christ's body); (3) the argument against fate carried into Greek and Latin (Eusebius's *Preparation*, Jerome's praise, the *Recognitions*' parallel list); (4) his name in the heresy lists, a following named after him. **Defended:** human freedom against the astrologers' fate; the faith against Marcion's followers.

### MINOR

3. **Beat 4's force line names something the beat doesn't carry.** The line reads "stoning and death on suspicion are in his list", but the beat lists no stoning (Hatra, BLC 68750–68754). Replace it with: "Keep the force: death on suspicion of adultery and Abgar's order to cut off hands are in his list, given as facts of law, not praised."

4. **Beat 5, Epiphanius's ship.** "its passengers lost" goes past the Greek, which says the ship brought death to the other passengers ("ἑτέροις τοῖς ἐπιβάταις θάνατον ἐμποιησάσης", EPI 26495–26497). Replace it with "like a fine ship broken up at the harbour's edge, its cargo lost, bringing death to the others aboard". Add to Do not say: "its passengers drowned" or "he alone was lost" → "bringing death to the others aboard" (Epiphanius).

5. **The Entities gloss.** "beings he said existed beside God" follows Burkitt's wording (EPH-P 2696, "set by Bardaisan alongside of God", MODERN). Ephrem's own lines give scattered Entities that existed before the Maker set them in order (EPH-P 2476–2482). Replace the gloss with: "beings Ephrem says Bardaisan taught were there, scattered, before the Maker set the world in order, among them Light, Wind, Fire and Water, with Darkness below (EPH-P 1003, 2474–2482; Ephrem's account)". This holds wherever the item lands after finding 1.

6. **Who tells it: whose hymns.** Gwynn's quoted lines from *Hymn* 53 do not name their subject. Gwynn says Ephrem attributes them to Bardesan (EPH-G 11234–11240). Add under Who tells it: "That Ephrem's '150 psalms' lines mean Bardaisan is Gwynn's report of the hymn (MODERN, clarifies an old text); the Syriac is not held in a readable print."

7. **Who tells it: Hippolytus's name form.** The manuscript does not read "Bardesianes" at VI.35. The name is Bunsen's correction (HIP-G 12605: "Βαρδησιάνης Bunsen"; the ANF note at HIP 9656ff gives Miller's "Ardesianes"). Add: "Hippolytus's 'Bardesianes' is an editor's correction of the manuscript (MODERN, clarifies the edition); card." The Prepon line (HIP 12054–12060) names him without a correction.

8. **Time order in the reception beats.** Beat 7 goes back to Eusebius's *Preparation* (early 300s) after Ephrem (died 373) and Epiphanius (about 375). Move Beat 7 ("The argument kept") to stand before Beat 5. Jerome's praise then reaches ahead in one clause, and Beats 5 and 6 run in their own order through to Ephrem and Sozomen. The ending still returns to the dialogue, as the opening image again.

9. **Add to Do not say:**
   - "Ephrem began writing hymns because of Bardaisan", or "Ephrem invented church hymns" → "Sozomen says Ephrem learned the meters of Harmonius's songs and wrote hymns of his own in them" (SOZ 32093–32101). Gwynn's "first led to adopt hymnody" and "celebrated this victory" (EPH-G 11226–11262) are the editor's framing and are not used.
   - "Harmonius was the first to put Syriac into meter" as fact → "Sozomen says it is related that he was the first" (SOZ 32082–32085).
   - "Eusebius condemned him" alone → Eusebius both calls him a powerful defender of the faith and says he never wholly washed off his old heresy (EU 26637–26656). Give both.
