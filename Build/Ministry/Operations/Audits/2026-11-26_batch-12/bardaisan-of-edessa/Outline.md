# Outline: Bardaisan of Edessa (`bardaisan-of-edessa`)

Item codes (S, E, I, D) are rows of `Source_Sheet.md`, each with its file and lines. Short file names (BLC, EU, EPI, EPH-P, EPH-G, SOZ, JER-L, PE-E and the rest) are listed at the top of the sheet; all are under `cic/texts/`. The job on the slate: the Syriac teacher at Edessa who argued, in a pupil's dialogue, that people are free and not ruled by the stars; later writers called him a heretic, and Ephrem answered him in song.

- **The essence:** Bardaisan was a Christian teacher at Edessa, a Syriac-speaking city in Mesopotamia with its own king, around the year 200. In a dialogue one of his pupils wrote down, he grants that nature rules the body and fate rules our fortune, but says what we do is our own: every people keeps its own laws under the same stars, and Christians keep Christ's law in every land. His argument was carried into Greek and praised in Latin, while later writers named him among the heretics, and in his own city Ephrem answered his school's songs with hymns of his own. (E4, E5, E8, E9, E12, I1–I4)
- **Also told:**
  - What the dialogue holds, as its substance (E1–E11): a newcomer's question; the harp; nature, fate and freedom, with his admission that he once followed the astrologers; the laws of countries, harsh ones included; the Christians; the new world at the end. Documented, his words in a pupil's record.
  - The teacher (S1, S3, E13): of Edessa, his birth recorded in its chronicle in the middle of the second century; learned in Greek and Syriac and close to the city's ruler (Epiphanius); a skilled debater in Syriac who wrote against Marcion's followers and had very many pupils, who put his works into Greek (Eusebius). Documented as each witness's report.
  - Named a heretic, and how (I3, D1): Eusebius, Epiphanius and a writer at Rome in his own years each tie him to Valentinus's school, in different orders, and each names a different fault. Documented that each says so; the order and the charge Contested.
  - Ephrem's answer (I1, I2): Ephrem read his book and wrote against his teaching in prose and in hymns; Sozomen says Ephrem took up the meters of his son's songs. Documented as their reports.
  - Checked against the Library: all held. "Not ruled by the stars" holds only as the dialogue limits it: fate is real over fortune and the body (E5, E6). "A pupil's dialogue" holds from the text itself (E12). "Ephrem answered him in song" holds for Ephrem's hymns (EPH-G 11200–11207, 11261–11278); that Ephrem used the songs' meters rests on Sozomen and Theodoret (I2).
- **What came of him** (Part 2, ranked heaviest first; about a third of the story):
  1. **Ephrem's answer in his own city** (I1, I2): the prose refutations, the hymns that curse his teaching, and, by Sozomen's account, Ephrem's hymns set to the meters of the Bardaisanite songs. Ephrem is the fullest witness to his teaching beyond the dialogue.
  2. **The heresy charge, carried by many hands** (I3, I5): Hippolytus, Eusebius, Epiphanius, Jerome, Theodoret, and the lists after them.
  3. **The argument carried on** (I4): his pupils put it into Greek; Eusebius quoted it against the astrologers; the *Recognitions* make the same argument with the same peoples; Jerome praised it; Theodoret read it. **Defended:** human freedom against the astrologers' fate, and the one law of Christ kept in every land (E4–E9). **Defended,** by Eusebius's report: the faith, against Marcion's followers (E13).
  - Who keeps him today: no held source names one. Leave out.
- **Length:** about 4:15 (about 640 words), at most 4:30 (675). Must carry: three quotes, 95 words (24, 29, 42); glosses about 85 words (Syriac, fate, astrologers, the dialogue, Valentinus's followers, Marcion's followers, heretic, the Entities, the Seven, the Seres, the goddess); credited witnesses about 80 words (the pupil Philip, Epiphanius, Eusebius twice, the writer at Rome, Ephrem, Sozomen, Jerome). About 260 words before the beats are told. The opening, seven beats and the ending need about 380 more. Why not shorter: the one text in his voice holds a real scene and an argument a listener can follow step by step, and the later quarrel has several voices that disagree and must each be credited. Why not the full 4:30: the facts of his life are few, late and in conflict (D4), and the songs are a clause here because `ephrem-the-syrian` tells them.
- **Setting:**
  - Time: born in the middle of the second century, by the city's chronicle (S1, S2); active around the year 200. No year told (D4). Spoken: "around the year 200".
  - Place: Edessa, a city in Mesopotamia (EU 26526–26527, JER-L 53431, EPI 26327) with its own king (BLC 68960; CHE 53); today in Turkey (census). Do not place it on a Roman or Persian border: no held old source used here says so for his time. The dialogue's eastern kingdom is the Parthians' (BLC 68681, 68795); do not say "Persia" for the empire. The river Daisan runs through it (CHE 53); his name means "son of the Daisan" (S1).
  - Tradition: "the Bardaisanites", the school named after him (census); the city's church, "Syriac Christianity" (census). Syriac: "the language of Edessa" (S5).
- **Words to explain** (the writer works the plain sense in where the word is first heard):
  - **Syriac:** "the language of Edessa".
  - **the dialogue:** "a written conversation". Its name, *The Book of the Laws of Countries*; Eusebius calls it *On Fate* (BLC 67898–67903).
  - **fate:** "the course God gave the heavenly powers, which shapes a person's fortune from the hour of birth" (E6). Never "luck".
  - **astrologers / Chaldaeans:** say "astrologers", "those who read people's lives in the stars" (BLC 68337–68357). Never "Chaldaeans".
  - **the Seres, the Brahmans:** "a people of the far east"; "the Brahmans of India" (E8).
  - **Tharatha:** "a goddess" (the Greek version gives Rhea, S4).
  - **Valentinus's followers:** "the followers of Valentinus, a teacher at Rome whose school taught a heaven of divine powers in pairs" (census `valentinus`).
  - **Marcion's followers:** "followers of Marcion, a teacher at Rome who held that the God of the law was not the Father of Jesus" (census `marcion-of-pontus`).
  - **heretic / heresy:** "a teacher of what the church condemned"; always a witness's word, never the narrator's.
  - **the Entities:** Ephrem's word: "beings he said existed beside God, among them Light, Wind, Fire and Water, with Darkness below, from which the world was ordered" (EPH-P 1003, 2470–2482). Say "first beings"; never "aeons".
  - **the Seven:** in the dialogue, "the seven stars the astrologers said govern our lives" (BLC 68355); in Ephrem's curse, "the Seven, powers he says Bardaisan confessed" (EPH-G 11261–11264). Never "aeons", never "planets".
  - **the resurrection of the body:** "that the dead will rise in the body".
  - **meters:** "the patterns of verse the songs were sung in" (SOZ 32087, 32099).
- **Shape:** Teacher or thinker. The record decides the order: his own scene and argument first, from the one text in his voice; then the teacher as later witnesses describe him; then the quarrel, the answer and the readers. Trap avoided: no list of books; the one idea, that what we do is our own, is shown through the dialogue's scene and his words.

- **Openings:**
  1. **Chosen.** The dialogue's first scene (E1, E2; BLC 67908–67926, 67944–67946, 67975–67977). Some pupils were visiting a friend when their teacher, Bardaisan, came in. He asked after his friend's health and then, as he always did, asked what they had been talking about. A newcomer, Avida, too shy to ask the teacher, had asked the others: if God is one, and made us, and wants us to do right, why did he not make us unable to do wrong? His fellows told him only to believe, but he said he could not believe unless he was convinced. The name comes in the first sentence. Leads straight to the essence, and to the pupil sentence (Beat 1).
  2. Ephrem reading Bardaisan's book (Quote 2). Not chosen: it opens on the enemy and the end, and needs the argument first to make sense.
- **The one-text sentence (early, once):** almost everything in his own voice comes from this one dialogue, which a pupil, Philip, wrote down; the pupil speaks as "I", and the teacher answers him "Philip" (E12). Said in Beat 1, inside the sentence about the dialogue. Never as a stock how-we-know line.
- **Story beats** (the opening, the essence and Beat 1 fill the first minute, about 150 words):
  1. **The teacher at Edessa** (S1, S3, S5, E12, E13). After the essence: Edessa's own chronicle records his birth in the middle of the second century (it names no place; Epiphanius and Theodoret say he came from Edessa, EPI 26327–26328, THD-HF 29819–29820); his name means "son of the Daisan", the city's river (S1; Widely Accepted). Epiphanius, a bishop writing about 150 years later, says he was learned in Greek and Syriac and close to the city's ruler (S3; Documented as Epiphanius's report). Eusebius, writing about a century on, says he was a skilled debater in Syriac, wrote dialogues against the followers of Marcion, and had very many pupils, who put his works into Greek (E13; Documented as Eusebius's report). Then the one-text sentence. Why he wrote against Marcion's followers: Eusebius says he was a powerful defender of the faith (narrated, not quoted).
  2. **The harp** (E3, E4). He tells Avida that teachers are the ones who are asked, and that it is good to know how to ask. Then his answer: had God made people unable to do wrong, they would not belong to themselves; they would be the tool of whoever moved them, like a harp another plays, where the praise and the blame belong to the player. The sun never says, "I will not rise"; people can choose. Had they been made unable to do evil, the good they did would not be their own (Documented, his words, narrated). Reason for the act: Avida's question.
  3. **Nature, fate and freedom** (E5, E6, E7; Quote 1). Nature gives every person the same life: birth, growth, children, eating and sleeping, old age and death. Fate, the course God gave the heavenly powers, gives wealth, health and children unevenly from the hour of birth. He admits he once followed the astrologers himself, and he still says fate is real; Eusebius later introduced him as a man who had gone to the height of the astrologers' learning (E7; credit). But what we do is free. Then Quote 1. Documented.
  4. **The laws of countries** (E8, E9, S4). The proof is the world's laws. People are born under every star in every land, yet each people keeps its own law: the Seres, a people of the far east, do not kill; the Brahmans of India eat no meat and drink no wine; the Persians marry their sisters; among the Britons many men share one wife, among the Parthians one man takes many wives. Among the Edessenes, he says, a woman only suspected of adultery is put to death. Laws change when kings change them, and he tells how, when King Abgar of Edessa became a believer, the king ordered that any man who castrated himself in honour of a goddess should lose his hand. Then the Christians, in the narrator's words: Christ planted a new people in every land; wherever they are, they are called by one name, and the laws of their countries do not stop them obeying the law of Christ (BLC 68966–68991). Keep the force: stoning and death on suspicion are in his list, given as facts of law, not praised. Documented, his words.
  5. **Named a heretic** (I3, E14, E15; D1). A writer at Rome in his own years counted him in the Eastern branch of Valentinus's school (Hippolytus, by role). Eusebius says he began among the followers of Valentinus, then refuted most of their fables, yet never wholly washed off the filth of his old heresy (I3b; keep "filth" or "dirt" for ῥύπος, never the softer "stain"; narrated). Epiphanius tells it the other way: an excellent man of the church, who later joined the Valentinians and denied that the dead will rise, like a fine ship broken up by the harbour's edge, its passengers lost (I3c; narrated). Why, in each witness's terms: Eusebius gives Valentinus's teaching; Epiphanius, the many first principles and the denial of the resurrection. Optional, in Epiphanius's voice only: when an emperor's companion urged him to deny he was a Christian, he refused, saying death would come anyway (E14; Contested; Cut first). Confidence: Documented that each says so; the order Contested.
  6. **Ephrem's answer** (I1, I2; Quote 2). More than a century later, in Edessa, Ephrem the Syrian came upon one of his books: Quote 2. He hurried, he says, to wash his ears with the Scriptures (EPH-G 11205–11207). In prose he answered what Bardaisan taught of the first beings beside God, and his claim that the body would turn to dust even without Adam's sin (EPH-P 1940, 2482; Words to explain). In a hymn he cursed anyone who said, as Bardaisan said, that the rain, the dew and the farmer's seed come from the Seven (EPH-G 11261–11272). He also says Bardaisan had made hymns, a hundred and fifty psalms; Sozomen, a church historian of the next century, gives the songs to his son Harmonius, and says Ephrem learned their meters and wrote hymns of his own in them, so the people would not take in their teaching (I2, D3; a clause, since `ephrem-the-syrian` tells it). Documented as their reports; whose songs Contested.
  7. **The argument kept** (I4). His pupils put his works into Greek (E13). Eusebius, in another book, quoted the dialogue at length against the astrologers and ended, "So far the Syrian" (PE-E 17926–17933, 18368; narrate). Jerome, writing in Latin late in the fourth century, called *On Fate* the strongest of his books, and asked how much force it must have had in its own tongue if it had so much in translation (JER-L 53437–53442; narrate). Documented. The *Recognitions* clause and Theodoret's reading go to the card (Cut first).
- **Quotes** (3; none is used in another story on the tree, and none is shared in the batch; no reuse on the slate):
  1. **Nature, fate, freedom** (E5). Rendered from Pratten's English, BLC 68441–68443 (English printed before 1931; the Syriac is held only in an unreadable print, and Nau's Latin, NAU 51620–51626, confirms the sense; the Greek version omits this passage): "So we humans are found to be governed by nature all alike, by fate in different ways, and by our freedom, each as he chooses." Job: the whole argument in one line, in his own words, after fate has its plain sense.
  2. **Ephrem reads the book** (I1). Rendered from Gwynn's English of *Nisibene Hymn* 51, EPH-G 11200–11204 (English printed 1898; the Syriac is not held in a readable print): "I happened upon a book of Bardaisan, and for an hour I was troubled. It stained my clean ears, and made them a passage for words full of blasphemy." Job: the enemy's own voice, which the narrator cannot give without taking his side; it shows he read Bardaisan's own writing.
  3. **The new world** (E11). Rendered from Pratten's English, BLC 69026–69031 (English printed before 1931; Nau's Latin, NAU 54004–54012, confirms): "When that new world is set up, all evil disturbances will cease, and all rebellions will end. The foolish will be convinced, and every lack will be filled. There will be quiet and peace, through the gift of the Lord of all beings." Job: the ending, in his own words; it brings back Avida, who could not believe unless convinced. "All existing beings" is rendered "all beings" (Nau: "omnium naturarum").

- **Connection to carry into the story:** two things passed from him, by two roads. His argument passed from his teaching circle into writing and across languages: a pupil wrote it down, his pupils put his works into Greek, Eusebius quoted it against the astrologers, and Jerome praised it in Latin (E12, E13, I4). His name passed into the lists of those the church condemned, through Hippolytus, Eusebius and Epiphanius, and above all through Ephrem in his own city, whose answer to Bardaisan's school took the form of hymns (I1–I3). The listener needs to understand that the only work in his voice is a pupil's record; that the charges are about other teachings than the freedom argument (the first beings, the body and its rising, Christ's body, the Seven), each in its own witness's voice; and that Ephrem's hymns, by Sozomen's account, grew out of answering the songs of Bardaisan's school. Carry it through what each did (wrote down, translated, quoted, cursed, sang), never as a "this matters because" sentence.
