# Source sheet: Bardaisan of Edessa (`bardaisan-of-edessa`)

Census: "Bardaisan of Edessa", kind person, told within `bardaisanite-current` ("Bardaisan and the Bardaisanites"; short name "Bardaisanites"; informal "The School of Bardaisan"). The city's church: `syriac-edessa-nisibis` ("Syriac Christianity (Edessa/Nisibis)", short "Syriac Edessa"). Census dates "c. 155-223"; place Edessa, today in Turkey. The card (`Card.existing.json`) and the batch-6 sheet were used as pointers only; every line below was read in its file for this sheet.

## Setting

**S1. Edessa, its river, and his name.** CHE 49: "6. The year 465, in the month Tammuz, on the eleventh day (i.e., July 11th, 154 A.D.), Bardesanes was born." LATER (compiled about 540 from the city's archives, CHE 32); the A.D. year is the translator's gloss (MODERN). CHE 53: the flood came when "the Daisan (river) came, neither in its day, nor in its month": the Daisan is Edessa's river. BLC 67892–67894 (Pratten's note): "Lit. 'Son of Daisan,' from a river so called near Edessa.—Hahn." MODERN, clarifies a word. BHE 4221–4225: "Daisan autem nomen est fluvii Edessam alluentis, a quo vocatus est Bardesanes, quod ad ejus ripam eum genuerit mater ejus" (Daisan is the name of the river that washes Edessa, from which Bardesanes was named, because his mother bore him on its bank). LEGEND. MS 14522–14528: born as his parents crossed the river. LEGEND. The name's meaning: Widely Accepted. Birth on the bank: Inferential-Thin (late legend only).

**S2. When.** CHE 49 (birth, Greek year 465, about 154); ELI 4365 (11th century): "Annus 445 ... Eo natus est Bardaisan syrus die 11° tammuz" (year 445, about 134). MS 14520 (year 475, about 164). BHE 4220–4221: "Vixit Bardesanes annos 68, et obiit anno 533" (he lived 68 years and died in the year 533 [of the Greeks]); Holl (EPI 26426–26427): 533 = 221/2. MODERN, clarifies the era. EU 26581–26593 (McGiffert): Eusebius places him under Marcus Aurelius; Hort and Hilgenfeld take that as a confusion of the Antonines. MODERN, a claim. Old sources: the city chronicle gives the 150s; later chronicles differ by up to thirty years. Spoken: "born in the middle of the second century" (CHE), active "around the year 200". Born in the 150s: Widely Accepted (CHE with BHE); the day and year: Contested (CHE, ELI, MS differ). Death about 222: one late witness (BHE): card only.

**S3. Learned in two languages, close to the ruler.** EPI 26326–26334 (Greek): "ἐκ Μεσοποταμίας μὲν τὸ γένος ἦν, τῶν κατὰ τὴν Ἔδεσσαν ... πόλιν κατοικούντων" (by race from Mesopotamia, of those who lived at the city of Edessa); "λόγιός τις ὢν ἐν ταῖς δυσὶ γλώσσαις, Ἑλληνικῇ τε διαλέκτῳ καὶ τῇ τῶν Σύρων φωνῇ" (learned in the two languages, the Greek tongue and the speech of the Syrians); close to "the ruler of the Edessenes, a most holy and most learned man", "at first becoming his intimate and working with him, and sharing in his learning" (my reading of 26332–26334; the OCR garbles the ruler's name; apparatus 26384 confirms "λογιωτάτῳ" and "ἅμα τε"). LATER (about 375, some 150 years on). Documented as Epiphanius's report. Holl (EPI 26369–26371): the ruler meant is Abgar IX bar Manu. MODERN, clarifies. EU 26633–26634: "a most skillful disputant in the Syriac tongue". LATER (Eusebius, early 300s). Documented.

**S4. A king Abgar who became a believer.** BLC 68959–68965 (the dialogue): "In Syria and in Edessa men used to part with their manhood in honour of Tharatha; but, when King Abgar became a believer he commanded that every one that did so should have his hand cut off, and from that day until now no one does so in the country of Edessa." OWN. PE-E 18323–18327 (the Greek version): "In Syria and Osrhoëne many used to mutilate themselves in honour of Rhea: hereupon king Abgar at one stroke commanded that those who cut off the genital organs should also have their hands cut off" (PE-G 29100–29108). Tharatha (Syriac) is given as Rhea, a goddess, in the Greek. The note dating the king's conversion to 160–170 (BLC 68960–68963, Neander) is MODERN, a claim: not used. NAU 53772–53779 confirms the Syriac sense. Documented as the dialogue's report.

**S5. Language and tradition.** Syriac: the language of Edessa (EU 26633–26634; EPI 26331–26332, "the speech of the Syrians"). Plain word: "Syriac, the language of Edessa". EPH-P 1374 (Ephrem): "the Philosopher of the Syrians (i.e. Bardaisan)" (the gloss is Mitchell's). Tradition spoken name: "the Bardaisanites" (census). Ephrem's writings speak of "the disciples of Marcion and Bardaisan and Mani" (EPH-P 2462).

## Events

**E1. The scene that opens the dialogue.** BLC 67908–67920: "Some days since we were calling to pay a visit to our brother Shemashgram, and Bardesan came and found us there. And when he had made inquiries after his health, and ascertained that he was well, he asked us, 'What were you talking about? for I heard your voice outside as I was coming in.' For it was his habit, whenever he found us talking about anything before he came, to ask us, 'What were you saying?' that he might talk with us about it." OWN (a pupil's record). Documented. No place is given for the house.

**E2. Avida's question.** BLC 67921–67926: "'Avida here,' said we to him, 'was saying to us, "If God is one, as ye say, and if He is the creator of men, and if it is His will that you should do that which you are commanded, why did He not so create men that they should not be able to do wrong, but should constantly be doing that which is right? for in this way His will would have been accomplished."'" BLC 67944–67946: "I began first of all to question my brethren here, because I was too bashful to ask thee." BLC 67961–67962: "a man who has but lately joined the disciples and is a recent inquirer". BLC 67973–67977: his brothers say "'Only believe, and thou wilt then be able to know everything.' But for my part, I cannot believe unless I be convinced." OWN. Documented.

**E3. How he taught.** BLC 67938–67943: "For teachers are 'asked' questions, and do not themselves ask them ... For it is a good thing that a man should know how to ask questions." BLC 67983–67986: "since Avida is not willing to believe, lo! I will speak to you who do believe ... and thus he too will hear something further about it." OWN. Documented.

**E4. The harp: why God made people free.** BLC 68006–68014: "if man had been made so, he would not have belonged to himself, but would have been the instrument of him that moved him ... And how, in that case, would a man differ from a harp, on which another plays; or from a ship, which another guides: where the praise and the blame reside in the hand of the performer or the steersman". NAU 50124–50126 has "a curru quem alius ducit" (a chariot another drives) where Pratten has "a ship": the two translators differ on the second image; the harp stands in both. BLC 68021–68023: "by freedom He exalted him above many of His creatures, and even made him equal with the angels." BLC 68029–68036: "For the sun never says, I will not rise at my appointed time; nor the moon, I will not change ... But all these things are servants, and are subject to one decree". BLC 68066–68071: man is given "to be guided by his own will ... For, had he been made so as not to be able to do evil ... the good which he did would not have been his own". OWN. Documented.

**E5. Nature, fate and freedom.** BLC 68249–68252: "The nature of man is, that he should be born, and grow up, and rise to his full stature, and produce children, and grow old, eating and drinking, and sleeping and waking, and that then he should die." PE-G 28452–28455, PE-E 17939–17942: the Greek opens the same way. BLC 68270–68275: men "in the matters pertaining to their minds ... do that which they choose, as those who are free". BLC 68396–68397: "There is, then, such a thing as Fate, as the astrologers say." BLC 68430–68433: "wealth, and honours, and health, and sickness, and children ... are placed under the control of Fate, and are not in our own power". BLC 68441–68443: "And thus we men are found to be governed by Nature all alike, and by Fate variously, and by our freedom each as he chooses." NAU 51620–51626: "Nos ergo homines inventi sumus ducti a natura uniformiter et a Fato diverse et a libertate nostra sicut unusquisque vult" (confirms). BLC 68557–68560: "so can we also see the freedom of man defeating and crushing Fate itself,—not, however, in everything". OWN. Documented. **He does not deny fate or the stars**: he limits them to fortune and the body.

**E6. Fate, in his terms.** BLC 68445–68463: "that which is called Fate is itself nothing more than a certain order of procession, which has been given to the Potentates and Elements by God ... this order, under the name of Fate and γένεσις" (Pratten glosses γένεσις "nativity, natal hour"). BLC 68381–68395: power is given to "God, and the Angels, and the Potentates, and the Governors, and the Elements, and men, and animals; but ... not ... in respect to everything (for He that has power over everything is One)". Plain sense: fate is the course God gave the heavenly powers, which from the hour of a person's birth shapes his fortune; God alone has power over everything. OWN. Documented.

**E7. He had followed the astrologers himself.** BLC 68337–68342: "'I too am aware, O Philip and Baryama,' said he to us, 'that there are such men: those who are called Chaldæans, and also others who are fond of this subtle knowledge, as I myself also once was.'" NAU 51292–51298: "sicut etiam ego olim eam dilexi" (as I too once loved it). BLC 68355, 68359–68360: "those stars which are called the Seven"; "Chaldæans" glossed "astrologers" by the translator. PE-G 28444–28446, PE-E 17929–17931: Eusebius introduces him as "one who is by birth a Syrian, and has pursued his inquiries to the highest point of Chaldean science" (Greek: "ἐπ᾿ ἄκρον δὲ τῆς Χαλδαϊκῆς ἐπιστήμης ἐληλακότος"). OWN; LATER (Eusebius). Documented.

**E8. The laws of countries.** BLC 68605–68612: "that which the stars decree ... is not practised by all men alike ... For men have made laws for themselves in various countries, in the exercise of that freedom which was given them by God". Examples: the Seres, a people of the far east (BLC 68616–68620: no killing, no idols, "although they, like other men, are born at all hours and on all days"); the Brahmans of India (68629–68636: no killing, no idols, no meat, no wine); the Persians, who marry sisters and daughters (68666–68670); among the Edessenes and others, a woman only suspected of adultery is put to death (68743–68749); in Hatra a petty thief is stoned (68750–68754); among the Britons many men share one wife, among the Parthians one man takes many wives (68793–68798). BLC 68846–68849: "in all countries, every day, and at all hours, men are born under Nativities diverse from one another, and the laws of men prevail over the decree of the stars". BLC 68913–68924: kings change laws; "it is but as yesterday since the Romans took possession of Arabia, and they abolished all the laws previously existing there". BLC 68929–68940: the Jews circumcise on the eighth day wherever they live, "without waiting for the coming of the proper stars". PE-E 18034–18330: the same list in the Greek version. OWN. Documented. **The record's force:** the laws he lists include death on suspicion, stoning for petty theft, and Abgar's order to cut off hands (S4); he cites them as facts of law, without praise or blame.

**E9. The Christians: one name, one law.** BLC 68966–68976: "And what shall we say of the new race of us Christians, whom Christ at His advent planted in every country and in every region? for, lo! wherever we are, we are all called after the one name of Christ—Christians. On one day, the first of the week, we assemble ourselves together". BLC 68976–68991: the brothers in Gaul, Parthia, Judaea, among the Geli, in Persia, Media, Edessa and Hatra do not keep the local customs; those in Edessa "do not ... kill their wives or their sisters when they commit impurity, but they withdraw from them, and give them over to the judgment of God"; "wherever they are ... the laws of the several countries do not hinder them from obeying the law of their Sovereign, Christ". NAU 53785–53790: "solo nomine Christi christiani vocamur" (confirms). PE-G 29110–29114, PE-E 18335–18339 (the Greek version): "we who hold those opinions have arisen in multitudes in different climes, in every nation and region, and though many in number, are called by one name"; PE-E 18346–18351: "wherever they are, they are neither overcome by ill-constituted laws and customs, nor does their nativity ... compel them to practise the evils forbidden by their teacher". OWN. Documented. "New race": Nau "generatione nova" (new generation); say "new people". BLC 68970–68975: the "days of the readings" fast is unclear (translator's note): leave out.

**E10. The pupils convinced; Avida's last word not given.** BLC 68568–68576: "'I am convinced,' said Avida, 'by the arguments thou hast brought forward, that it is not from his nature that a man does wrong ... If thou canst further prove also that it is not from Fate'"; 68587–68589: "'Prove me this,' said Avida, 'and I will be convinced by thee, and whatsoever thou shalt enjoin upon me I will do.'" BLC 68870–68872: "'Of these things, Father Bardesan,' said I to him, 'thou hast convinced us, and we know that they are true.'" OWN. Documented. The dialogue does not record Avida's final answer.

**E11. The new world (the dialogue's last words).** BLC 69023–69031: "A time is also coming when this propensity to inflict harm which still remains in them shall be brought to an end ... And at the establishment of that new world all evil commotions shall cease, and all rebellions terminate, and the foolish shall be convinced, and all deficiencies shall be filled up, and there shall be quietness and peace, through the gift of the Lord of all existing beings." BLC 69032–69033: "Here endeth the Book of the Laws of Countries." NAU 54004–54012 confirms ("ex dono Domini omnium naturarum", the gift of the Lord of all natures). OWN. Documented.

**E12. Who wrote the dialogue down.** In the text, the narrator says "I" and is answered "O Philip" (BLC 68096–68100), "O Philip and Baryama" (68337), "my son Philip" (68878–68879). PE-G 28449–28451, PE-E 17931–17933: Eusebius says that "in his Dialogues with his companions it is recorded that he spake as follows" ("μνημονεύεται φάναι"). LATER, but it frames the work as recorded speech. Eusebius (EU 26641–26643), Epiphanius (EPI 26412–26413) and Jerome (JER-L 53437–53439) treat the book as his. McGiffert (EU 26621–26630): "it was not written by Bardesanes himself ... undoubtedly written by one of Bardesanes' disciples, probably soon after his death". MODERN, a claim. Pratten (BLC 67855–67857): "Many modern critics have come to the conclusion that it was written by a scholar of Bardesanes". MODERN. Nau (NAU 49580–49582): nothing prevents believing Philip wrote it, since the master answers him "Philip". MODERN. Documented from the text itself: a pupil, Philip, tells it as "I". That Philip wrote it after Bardaisan's death: Dominant Modern Reconstruction: leave out.

**E13. His other books, his debates, his pupils.** EU 26633–26639: "a most able man and a most skillful disputant in the Syriac tongue, having composed dialogues against Marcion's followers and against certain others who were authors of various opinions, committed them to writing in his own language, together with many other works. His pupils, of whom he had very many (for he was a powerful defender of the faith), translated these productions from the Syriac into Greek." LATER (early 300s, about a century on). The Greek of the clause "powerful defender of the faith" is not held: the words are McGiffert's. EPI 26333–26334 / 26412–26414: "he argued much against Abeidas the astronomer on fate, and other treatises of his on the godly faith are in circulation" (my reading; Holl at 26430–26444 takes Abeidas as the dialogue's Avida: MODERN, clarifies). THD-HF 29819–29826 (Theodoret, 5th century): "Ἐντετύχηκα δὲ κἀγὼ λόγοις αὐτοῦ, καὶ κατὰ εἱμαρμένης γραφεῖσι, καὶ πρὸς τὴν Μαρκίωνος αἵρεσιν, καὶ ἄλλοις οὐκ ὀλίγοις" (I too have come upon his writings, both those written against fate and against the heresy of Marcion, and not a few others); Latin 29768–29770. LATER. HIP 12054–12060, HIP-G 16091–16095: "in our times, a certain follower of Marcion, (namely) Prepon, an Assyrian, has ... given an account of his heresy in a work inscribed to Bardesanes, an Armenian". CONTEMP (Hippolytus at Rome, "in our times"). Documented. The dialogues against Marcion's followers are not held.

**E14. A stand for the faith (Epiphanius only).** EPI 26417, 26486–26491: "Ἀπολλωνίῳ δὲ τῷ τοῦ Ἀντωνίνου ἑταίρῳ ἀντῆρε, παραιτούμενος ἀρνήσασθαι τὸ Χριστιανὸν ἑαυτὸν λέγειν. ὁ δὲ σχεδὸν ἐν τάξει ὁμολογίας κατέστη, λόγους τε συνετοὺς ἀπεκρίνατο ὑπὲρ εὐσεβείας ἀνδρείως ἀπολογούμενος, θάνατον μὴ δεδιέναι φήσας, ὃν ἀνάγκῃ (ἔφη) ἔσεσθαι, κἂν τε τῷ βασιλεῖ μὴ ἀντείποι." My reading: He stood up against Apollonius, the companion of Antoninus, refusing to deny that he called himself a Christian. He came near to the rank of a confessor; he gave wise answers for the faith, defending himself bravely, and said he did not fear death, which, he said, would come by necessity even if he did not oppose the emperor. LATER (about 375). EU 26641–26645: "other works which they say he wrote on occasion of the persecution which arose at that time." LATER. Holl (EPI 26418–26425): the emperor cannot be Marcus Aurelius; perhaps Caracalla or Elagabalus. MODERN. Contested (one late witness; emperor confused). Reason given: he was urged to deny he was a Christian.

**E15. His own teaching, as an enemy keeps it.** EPH-P 1940–1942 (Ephrem, *A Discourse against Bardaisan*, Mitchell's English): "BARDAISAN, lo, declares--that even without the sin of Adam--the Body would turn to its dust"; "'For if,' says he, '[it is] by Adam--we die the Death of here below,--it would have been right that He who came--should have given Life here below'". OWN (as an enemy quotes it), Documented as Ephrem's quotation. Plain sense: the body is mortal by nature, not by Adam's sin; if Adam had caused our death here, Christ would have given life here. EPH-P 1549: "though Bardaisan said [seven Entities constitute] the world he nevertheless said concerning the Law that it was given by God" (the bracket is the editors' restoration). LATER (Ephrem, Edessa, died 373). Documented as Ephrem's report. EPI 26501–26502, 26564: "he uses the Law and the Prophets, the Old and the New Testament, and certain apocrypha likewise" (my reading). LATER.

## What came of him (heaviest first)

**I1. Ephrem's long answer, in prose and in song, in Bardaisan's own city.** (a) The prose: EPH-P 403: "THE SECOND (DISCOURSE) TO HYPATIUS AGAINST MANI AND MARCION AND BARDAISAN". EPH-P 1352: "A DISCOURSE ... AGAINST THE DISCOURSE WHICH IS CALLED 'OF DOMNUS,' WHICH WAS COMPOSED BY BARDAISAN AGAINST THE PLATONISTS." EPH-P 1937–1942: "A DISCOURSE AGAINST BARDAISAN" (E15). EPH-P 1374: "the Philosopher of the Syrians (i.e. Bardaisan) made himself a laughing-stock among Syrians and Greeks, not only in that he was unable to state but also in that he did not really know the teaching of Plato". EPH-P 2482: "that man spoke falsely who constructed Entities that do not exist. So that from these Entities, about which Bardaisan spoke, there is no way for created things ... to come into being". EPH-P 1003: "Bardaisan supposes that the Darkness was beneath, below everything". EPH-P 2474: "if something from behind moved the Element of Wind and impelled it, as Bardaisan says". EPH-P 628: the Wind "was revealed to this Bardaisan who was unable to prevent the dew which dropped upon his bed! But let them give us the signs and wonders which he did". EPH-P 428: "Bardaisan, the teacher of Mani"; EPH-P 959: Mani's teaching "comes from the party of Marcion and Valentinus and Bardaisan". (b) The hymns: EPH-G 11200–11207 (*Nisibene Hymn* 51, Gwynn's English): "I have chanced upon a book of Bardaisan, / And I was troubled for an hour's space; / It tainted my pure ears, / And made them a passage / For words filled with blasphemy. / I hastened to purge them / With the goodly and pure reading / Of the Scriptures of truth." EPH-G 11208–11223: the charge that he denied the body's rising (told in `ephrem-the-syrian`; not used here). EPH-G 11261–11278 (*Hymns against Heresies*, Gwynn's English; his note cites Opp. Syr. II p. 550): "Cursed be our trust [if it be] on the Seven; the Æons which Bardaisan confesses! Anathema [be he] who says, as he said: that from them descend the rain and the dew! ... that from them are the snow and the ice! ... that from them are the seeds for the husbandmen! ... that from them are famine and plenty!" "Æons" is Gwynn's word (his note: "The Seven Æons (or Beings) of Bardesan's heresy"): MODERN wording; say "the Seven". LATER (Ephrem, Edessa, died 373; about 150 years on). Documented as Ephrem's charges. Weight: Ephrem is the fullest witness to what Bardaisan taught beyond the dialogue, and the reason the later Syriac church remembered him as a heretic; the Syriac hymn tradition grew in answering his school (I2).

**I2. The songs, and the answer in song** (told in `ephrem-the-syrian`; here a clause). EPH-G 11241–11247 (Ephrem, *Hymns against Heresies* 53, Gwynn's English): "He fashioned hymns, and joined them with tunes ... He blended for the simple poison with sweetness ... An hundred and fifty psalms, he likewise composed." (Quoted in a census edge note: not quoted here.) SOZ 32079–32101: "Bardasanes, who devised a heresy designated by his name, and Harmonius, his son. (a) HIP 9656–9663, HIP-G 12595–12607 (apparatus: "Βαρδησιάνης Bunsen, s. LATER. ## Explain it (each thing or act the story could name, from each side held)

- **The dialogue** (*The Book of the Laws of Countries*): a written conversation in which a pupil tells how Bardaisan answered a newcomer's question about why God did not make people unable to do wrong (E1, E2, E12). Its own subject, by its last lines and its argument: that people are free (E4, E5). Eusebius's title for it, *On Fate* (BLC 67898–67903 note; EU 26642).
- **Fate:** in his words, "a certain order of procession" given by God to the heavenly powers, which shapes a person's fortune, health and children from the hour of birth (E6). His own side: real, but not over what we do (E5). The astrologers' side, as he reports it: everything we do, right and wrong, comes from the Seven (BLC 68351–68357).
- **The astrologers (Chaldaeans):** those who said the stars govern all a person does (BLC 68337–68357). He says he was once one of them (E7).
- **The laws of countries:** the proof: each people keeps its own laws, though its people are born under every star (E8).
- **Abgar's law:** why: the king "became a believer"; what: whoever castrated himself in a goddess's honour lost his hand (S4). Told as the dialogue gives it.
- **The charge of heresy** ("teaching the church condemned", always a witness's word). Each side: Eusebius, that he began among Valentinus's followers and never wholly washed off "the filth of the old heresy" (I3b); Epiphanius, that he fell to the Valentinians, brought in many first principles, and denied that the dead will rise (I3c); Hippolytus and Theodoret, on Christ's body (I3a, I3f); Ephrem, that he taught Entities beside God, that the body was mortal even without Adam's sin, and that rain and dew come from the Seven (I1, E15). His side, as the held texts give it: in the dialogue, God alone has power over everything, and Christians keep Christ's law (E6, E9); in Ephrem's own report, he said the Law was given by God (EPH-P 1549); in Ephrem's quotation of him, if death came by Adam, Christ would have given life here (E15).
- **The Entities (Ephrem's word):** self-existing beings Ephrem says Bardaisan set beside God, among them Light, Wind, Fire and Water, with a Darkness below, from which the world was ordered (EPH-P 2470–2474, 2482, 1003; Ephrem's account only). Burkitt's list of them (EPH-P 2656) is MODERN: not used.
- **The Seven:** in the dialogue, "those stars which are called the Seven", which astrologers said governed our lives (BLC 68355; 68995). In Ephrem's curse, powers "which Bardaisan confesses", from which he said came rain, dew, snow and seed (I1b). Each sense with its own witness; the narrator does not equate them.
- **The hymns and Ephrem's answer:** Ephrem's side, that Bardaisan made hymns and 150 psalms and "blended for the simple poison with sweetness"; Sozomen's, that Harmonius set Syriac to meter, and Ephrem feared the people would take in his teaching (I2).
- **The refusal before Apollonius:** why, as Epiphanius gives it: he was urged to deny he was a Christian (E14).

## Where the sources disagree or come late

**D1. A Valentinian first, or last?** Eusebius: first a follower of Valentinus, then refuted him, but never wholly clean (I3b). Jerome follows Eusebius (I3d). Epiphanius: an excellent churchman first, who later joined the Valentinians (I3c); the late chronicles agree with Epiphanius (I6). Hippolytus: one of Valentinus's eastern school (I3a). Theodoret: they say he cut away much of Valentinus's myth-making (I3e). McGiffert (EU 26529–26565): "commonly classed among the Gnostics, but, as Hort shows, without sufficient reason ... It is not improbable that he may, as Eusebius says, have been a Valentinian for a time". MODERN, a claim: not used. Contested.

**D2. Who wrote the dialogue** (E12). Old writers: Bardaisan's book. The text: a pupil, Philip, tells it. Modern editors: a pupil wrote it, probably after his death. Tell: "a dialogue one of his pupils, Philip, wrote down"; the after-his-death clause: leave out.

**D3. Whose songs.** Ephrem: Bardaisan's own hymns and 150 psalms (I2). Sozomen and Theodoret: his son Harmonius's (I2). Gwynn notes the difference (EPH-G 11234–11240): MODERN. Contested.

**D4. Dates.** CHE: born 465 of the Greeks (about 154); ELI: 445 (about 134); MS: 475 (about 164); BHE: died 533 (about 222) aged 68. Eusebius, Jerome and Theodoret place him under Marcus Aurelius (EU 26525; JER-L 53437; THD-HF 29819–29821). Widely Accepted: born in the middle of the second century, active around 200. Tell no year.

**D5. "Addressed to Antoninus."** Eusebius and Jerome say *On Fate* was presented to the emperor Antoninus (EU 26643; JER-L 53437–53438); Epiphanius says he argued it against "Abeidas the astronomer" (EPI 26413); Holl (EPI 26430–26444) and McGiffert (EU 26607–26614) take this as Avida. MODERN, clarifies. Leave out.

**D6. Ship or chariot** (E4). Pratten's English has a ship another guides; Nau's Latin a chariot another drives. Use the harp only.

**D7. Ephrem's knowledge of him.** McGiffert (EU 26574–26577): Ephrem "knows him only through his followers". MODERN, a claim. Ephrem says he came upon a book of Bardaisan's (EPH-G 11200) and answers the *Domnus* by name (EPH-P 1352). Tell what Ephrem says; leave McGiffert out.
