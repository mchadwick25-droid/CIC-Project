Some pupils were visiting a friend when their teacher, Bardaisan, came in and, as he always did, asked what they had been talking about. A newcomer, Avida, too shy to ask the teacher, had asked the others why God, if he is one and made us and wants us to do right, did not make us unable to do wrong. His fellows told him only to believe, but he said he could not believe unless he was convinced.

Bardaisan was a Christian teacher at Edessa, a city in Mesopotamia, around the year 200. In a dialogue one of his pupils wrote down, he grants that fate rules our fortune but says that what we do is our own. Later writers named him among the heretics, teachers of what the church condemned, and Ephrem the Syrian answered him in his own city.

Edessa's chronicle records his birth in the middle of the second century. Eusebius, writing about a century on, calls him a skilled debater in Syriac, the language of Edessa, and a powerful defender of the faith, who wrote dialogues against the followers of Marcion, a teacher at Rome who held that the God of the law was not the Father of Jesus. Almost all we have in Bardaisan's own voice comes from one written conversation, The Book of the Laws of Countries, which a pupil, Philip, set down, speaking as "I".

Had God made people unable to do wrong, Bardaisan tells Avida, they would not belong to themselves. They would be like a harp that another plays, where the praise and the blame belong to the player, and the good they did would not be their own.

Nature, he says, gives everyone the same body's life, from birth to death. Fate, the course God gave the heavenly powers, shapes each person's fortune from the hour of birth, handing out wealth, health and children unevenly. He admits that he once followed the astrologers, who read people's lives in the stars, and he still says fate is real, but what we do is free. "So we humans are found to be governed by nature all alike, by fate in different ways, and by our freedom, each as he chooses."

His proof is the world's laws. People are born under every star in every land, yet each people keeps its own law, and the Seres, a people of the far east, do not kill. Among the Edessenes, he says, a woman only suspected of adultery is put to death, and when King Abgar of Edessa became a believer, the king ordered that any man who castrated himself in honor of a goddess should lose his hand. Christ, too, planted a new people in every land, called by one name wherever they are, and the laws of their countries do not stop them obeying the law of Christ.

His many pupils put his works into Greek. Eusebius quoted the dialogue at length against the astrologers, and Jerome, writing in Latin late in the fourth century, called it, under the title On Fate, the strongest of his books.

Yet Eusebius also says Bardaisan began among the followers of Valentinus, a teacher at Rome whose school taught a heaven of divine powers in pairs, and that he refuted most of their fables but never wholly washed off the filth of his old heresy. Epiphanius, a bishop writing about 150 years after him, tells it the other way round. In his account Bardaisan was an excellent man of the church who later joined the Valentinians and denied that the dead will rise. He was like a fine ship broken up at the harbor's edge, its cargo lost, bringing death to the others aboard.

More than a century after Bardaisan, Ephrem came upon one of his books in Edessa. "I happened upon a book of Bardaisan, and for an hour I was troubled. It stained my clean ears, and made them a passage for words full of blasphemy." In prose Ephrem answered Bardaisan's claim that the body would turn to dust even without Adam's sin. In a hymn he cursed anyone who said, as Bardaisan did, that the rain, the dew and the farmer's seed come from the Seven, powers Ephrem says Bardaisan confessed. Sozomen, a church historian of the next century, says that Bardaisan's son, Harmonius, wrote songs, and that Ephrem wrote hymns of his own in their meters, the patterns of verse they were sung in, so the people would not take in their teaching.

The dialogue closes with Bardaisan telling his pupils of the world to come. "When that new world is set up, all evil disturbances will cease, and all rebellions will end. The foolish will be convinced, and every lack will be filled. There will be quiet and peace, through the gift of the Lord of all beings."
