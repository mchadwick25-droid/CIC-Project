# Source sheet: Josephus

Batch 12, part A (2026-11-27). Job on the slate: a Jewish priest and commander who went over to Rome and wrote his people's history; his lines on John the Baptist, James and Jesus, and how Christian writers kept them and argued over the passage on Jesus. Shape: Outside witness. Every passage below was read in its own file for this sheet. The batch 6 sheet was used only to find passages.

## Languages held

- Josephus's own Greek: **no edition is held.** Two passages survive in Greek only as later writers quote them:
  - The James phrase, in McGiffert's note giving the Greek of Eusebius's quotation: "τὸν ἀδελφὸν ᾽Ιησοῦ τοῦ χριστοῦ λεγομένου, ᾽Ι€κωβος [Ἰάκωβος] ὄνομα αὐτῷ" (EH-ed 13596–13602; the same note on one line at EH 78397). Origen gives the phrase in his own sentence: "τὸν ἀδελφὸν Ἰησοῦ τοῦ λεγομένου Χριστοῦ" (OMG 3729–3730). Two held prints agree.
  - The passage on Jesus, in the Greek *Illustrious Men* (JVL 50993–51039, badly damaged after the first two sentences) and in the Greek *Martyrdom of Donatus* (ADON 80255–80284, damaged but fuller). The two prints agree on the opening sentences and on "ὁ Χριστὸς οὗτος ἦν" ("this man was the Christ"; also clean in JVL-ed 50948–50949 and 50959). Both are copies inside later Christian books, not manuscripts of Josephus.
  - Everything else of Josephus (his life, the prayer, John) is held in English only, in Whiston.
- Origen: Greek held for the *Commentary on Matthew* (OMG); *Against Celsus* in English only.
- Eusebius: *Church History* Books I–V in English only (the held Greek, Schwartz, is Books VI–X).
- Jerome: Latin held (JVL).
- Michael the Syrian: French only.

## Witness kinds and gaps

- **Own words (JW).** Almost the whole of his life rests on his own *Life* and *War*. He wrote the *Life* as a defence against a rival historian: Justus "falsified in what he related about me ... being belied by him, I am under a necessity to make my defence" (JW 4614–4632). The war he says he wrote "because I saw that others perverted the truth of those actions in their writings" (JW 5540–5544).
- **Contemporary Roman (SUET).** Suetonius (CEN: born c. 69 or c. 77) is the only outside witness to any event of his life: the prediction to Vespasian.
- **Later, about a century and a half (CC, OM/OMG).** Origen (CEN c. 203–253) wrote *Against Celsus* when "over sixty years old" (EH 37090–37107, Book VI.36).
- **Later, more than two centuries (EH).** Eusebius of Caesarea (CEN c. 260–339).
- **Later, about three centuries (JV/JVL, JVJ).** Jerome wrote *Illustrious Men* "until the fourteenth year of the Emperor Theodosius" (JV 38780–38785).
- **Later, about three and a half centuries (SOZ).** Sozomen (CEN c. 370–380 to after 443).
- **Late legend, date not given (ADON).** A martyr's acts set under Diocletian. Its speeches are the legend's.
- **Later, about a thousand years (MS).** Michael the Syrian, twelfth century.
- **Modern editors (JW-ed, EH-ed, JVL-ed, MS-ed).** Their views are theirs and are marked so. They clarify the texts; none is a witness to the story.

## 1. Setting

S1. **A priest's family in Jerusalem.** "THz [The] family from which I am derived is not an ignoble one, but hath descended all along from the priests" (JW 1385–1387); "I am not only sprung from a sacerdotal family in general, but from the first of the twenty-four courses" (JW 1391–1393); "by my mother I am of the royal blood" (JW 1398–1399), of the Hasmonean kings and high priests (JW 1399–1402). His father Matthias "was in great reputation in Jerusalem, the greatest city we have" (JW 1437–1441). Own words. Documented as his own account. Plain words: "a priest of the Temple, from a priestly family of Jerusalem". A "course" was one of twenty-four families of priests who served the Temple in turn; Josephus gives the number, not the gloss. Do not name the courses.

S2. **Born 37.** "as was I born to Matthias in the first year of the reign of Caius Cesar" (JW 1426–1428). Gaius (Caligula) began to reign in 37 (JV-ed 39708, NPNF note "Born a.d. 37"; EH-ed 15892–15893 "the year beginning March 16, 37 a.d."). Widely Accepted. CEN `dates`: "37 to early 2nd c."

S3. **Young learning.** At about fourteen "the high-priests and principal men of the city came then frequently to me together, in order to know my opinion about the accurate understanding of points of the law" (JW 1449–1456). At sixteen he tried "the several sects that were among us. These sects are three:—the first is that of the Pharisees, the second that of the Sadducees, and the third that of the Essenes" (JW 1457–1461); three years with a desert ascetic, Banus (JW 1473–1482); at nineteen he "began to conduct myself according to the rules of the sect of the Pharisees" (JW 1484–1486). Own words, his own praise. Documented as his claim. Side story.

S4. **Rome at the end of his life.** Vespasian "gave me an apartment in his own house, which he lived in before he came to the empire. He also honoured me with the privilege of a Roman citizen, and gave me an annual pension" (JW 5407–5413). Titus kept "the same respect" and would not believe accusations against him; Domitian "punished those Jews that were my accusers" and made his land in Judea tax free (JW 5468–5482). Own words. Documented as his account.

S5. **Tradition.** None. CEN `josephus`: `toldWithin` null; `label` "Outside witness" (CARD), `role` "Jewish historian". The corpus map row (`alexandria-catechetical.yaml` 4898–4912, the same row in four other buckets) calls him "Outside witness, not the church's own voice: a first-century Jewish civil historian ... Context only, never tradition." Plain words: "a Jewish historian, outside the church".

## 2. Events

E1. **The rising, and Galilee, 66.** He gives two accounts.
- *Life*: after the Roman governor Gessius was beaten, the leading men of Jerusalem, "seeing that the robbers and innovators had arms in great plenty", "sent me and two others of the priests ... in order to persuade the ill men there to lay down their arms" and keep them ready "but still so, that they should wait to see what the Romans would do" (JW 1659–1681).
- *War*: among the generals named, "Josephus, the son of Matthias, of both the Galilees. Gamala also, which was the strongest city in those parts, was put under his command" (JW 106926–106929).
Own words, in two books that differ on his purpose (D1). Documented that he held command in Galilee; why he was sent: Contested between his own books. Explain it: the rising was the Jewish war with Rome; his own *Life* says the war was "not voluntary, but that, for the main, they were forced by necessity to enter into it" (JW 1655–1658). That is his view; no other held source of the time gives the rebels' side.

E2. **Jotapata falls, 67.** "And thus was Jotapata taken, in the thirteenth year of the reign of Nero, on the first day of the month Panemus [Tamuz]" (JW 110707–110710). His count: 1,200 captives and 40,000 dead "at the taking of the city, and in the former fights" (JW 110700–110704). Own words. Documented (the fall); the figures are his. Nero's thirteenth year falls in 66–67; McGiffert puts the surrender "in the summer of 67" (EH-ed 15896–15897). Widely Accepted: 67. Plain words: "Jotapata, a walled town in Galilee".

E3. **The cave.** The Romans hunted him "both out of the hatred they bore him, and because their general was very desirous to have him taken" (JW 110721–110725). He "leaped into a certain deep pit" beside a hidden den, "and here he met with forty persons of eminence that had concealed themselves" (JW 110736–110742). On the third day "they had taken a woman who had been with them" and he was found (JW 110754–110756). Vespasian sent two tribunes to promise his life; he feared a trap "until Vespasian sent besides these a third tribune, Nicanor ... his familiar acquaintance in old time" (JW 110757–110780). Then he "called to mind the dreams which he had dreamed in the night-time, whereby God had signified to him beforehand both the future calamities of the Jews, and the events that concerned the Roman emperors", being "a priest himself, and of the posterity of priests" (JW 110830–110842). Own words, told of himself in the third person, and the only account. Documented as his account; the dreams are his claim and stay in his voice.

E4. **The prayer.** "he put up a secret prayer to God, and said—"Since it pleaseth thee, who hast created the Jewish nation, to depress the same, and since all their good fortune is gone over to the Romans; and since thou hast made choice of this soul of mine to foretell what is to come to pass hereafter, I willingly give them my hands, and am content to live. And I protest openly, that I do not go over to the Romans as a deserter of the Jews, but as a minister from thee."" (JW 110845–110856). Own words. Documented as his own account of his prayer. English only; the Greek is not held. "Minister" here is Whiston's eighteenth-century word for a servant or agent; its church sense misleads.

E5. **The forty, and the lots.** His companions cried: "O Josephus! art thou still fond of life; and canst thou bear to see the light in a state of slavery?" (JW 110876–110879); "We will lend thee our right hand and a sword; and if thou wilt die willingly, thou wilt die as general of the Jews; but if unwillingly, thou wilt die as a traitor to them" (JW 110897–110901). After his speech against suicide "they ran upon him with their swords ... and called him a coward" (JW 111066–111069). He proposed: "let us commit our mutual deaths to determination by lot. He whom the lot falls to first, let him be killed by him that hath the second lot" (JW 111107–111110). "He who had the first lot laid his neck bare to him that had the next" (JW 111119–111120). "yet was he with another left to the last, whether we must say it happened so by chance, or whether by the providence of God", and he "persuaded him to trust his fidelity to him, and to live as well as himself" (JW 111125–111133). Own words, the only account. Documented as his account. Explain it, from both sides: to his companions, surrender was the act of "a traitor" and death the act of a "general of the Jews" (JW 110897–110901); to Josephus, killing oneself was wrong (his speech, JW 110905 onward) and he went over as God's servant (E4). How the lots fell is his own telling, with his own "chance, or ... providence".

E6. **The prediction, 67.** Alone with Vespasian and Titus: "Thou, O Vespasian, thinkest no more than that thou hast taken Josephus himself captive; but I come to thee as a messenger of greater tidings ... Thou, O Vespasian, art Caesar and emperor, thou, and this thy son. Bind me now: still faster, and keep me for thyself" (JW 111175–111191; OCR "Q", "Ceesar" read as "O", "Caesar"). Vespasian "did not believe him, but supposed that Josephus said this asa [as a] cunning trick, in orderto [in order to] his own preservation" (JW 111198–111200). Own words. **Suetonius confirms:** "unus ex nobilibus captivis Iosephus, cum coiceretur in vincula, constantissime asseveravit fore ut ab eodem brevi solveretur, verum iam imperatore" (SUET 36617–36619); Rolfe: "one of his high-born prisoners, Josephus by name, as he was being put in chains, declared most confidently that he would soon be released by the same man, who would then, however, be emperor" (SUET 36662–36665). Documented that he made the prediction (two witnesses). Explain it, from both sides: Josephus says God sent him (JW 111178–111181); Vespasian at first thought it a trick to save his life (JW 111198–111201). Suetonius gives it among the omens of Vespasian's rise, not as a trick.

E7. **Freed, 69.** When Vespasian was made emperor (69; Widely Accepted) he remembered "what Josephus had said to him when he ventered [ventured] to foretell his coming to the empire while Nero was alive; so he was much concerned that this man was still in bonds" (JW 117837–117841). Titus urged that the chain be cut "to pieces", so that he would be "like a man that hath never been bound at all"; "so there came a man in, and cut the chain to pieces" (JW 117868–117879). Own words. Documented as his account.

E8. **At the walls of Jerusalem, 70.** Titus "sent Josephus to speak to them in their own language; for he imagined they might yield to the persuasion of a countryman of their own" (JW 121492–121495). "So Josephus went round about the wall, and tried to find a place that was out of the reach of their darts, and yet within their hearing, and besought them, in many words, to spare themselves, to spare their country and their temple" (JW 121497–121502). In the *Life*: "I ... was frequently in danger of being put to death, while both the Jews were very desirous to get me under their power, in order to have me punished; and the Romans also, whenever they were beaten, supposed that it was occasioned by my treachery, and made continual clamours to the emperors, and desired that they would bring me to punishment, as a traitor to them" (JW 5336–5345). Own words. Documented as his account. The force: both sides held him a traitor. Jerusalem fell in 70 (Widely Accepted).

E9. **After the fall.** Offered what he wished from "the ruins of my country", he asked "that my family might have their liberty: I had also the holy books by Titus's concession"; he had about 190 captives he knew set free from the Temple court; of three friends he found crucified, Titus had them taken down, "yet two of them died under the physician's hands, while the third recovered" (JW 5349–5392). Own words. Documented as his account. Side story.

E10. **The *War*: first in his own tongue.** He resolved "to translate those books into the Greek tongue, which I formerly composed in the language of our country" (JW 91746–91751). The *War* opens: "I, Joseph, the son of Matthias, by birth a Hebrew, a priest also, and one who at first fought against the Romans myself, and was forced to be present at what was done afterward, [am the author of this work.]" (JW 91778–91783). Why he wrote: others "have given false accounts of things, and this either out of a humour of flattery to the Romans, or of hatred toward the Jews" (JW 91740–91743). Own words. Documented. Whiston dates it "about A. D. 75" (JW-ed 91754–91756), editor's reckoning: tell it as "after the war".

E11. **The *Antiquities*, about 93.** Written for non-Jewish readers: "I have undertaken the present work, as thinking it will appear to all the Greeks worthy of their study; for it will contain all our antiquities, and the constitution of our government, as interpreted out of the Hebrew Scriptures" (JW 5546–5551). It ends: "these Antiquities, which are contained in 20 books, and 60,000 verses", brought down "to this very day, which is the 13th year of the reign of Caesar Domitian, and the 56th of my own life" (JW 91628–91630, 91662–91666). Own words. Documented. Domitian's thirteenth year is 93–94 (JW-ed 91639–91640; EH-ed 15941–15943): Widely Accepted, "about 93". Plain words for the title: "his history of the Jewish people, from the creation to his own day, in twenty books". "Greeks" here means Greek-speaking non-Jews.

E12. **John the Baptist (*Antiquities* XVIII).** Herod's army had been destroyed in a war with Aretas, an Arab king whose daughter Herod meant to divorce (JW 82010–82065). "Now some of the Jews thought that the destruction of Herod's army came from God, and that very justly, as a punishment of what he did against John, that was called the Baptist; for Herod slew him, who was a good man, and commanded the Jews to exercise virtue, both as to righteousness toward one another, and piety toward God, and so to come to baptism; for that the washing [with water] would be acceptable to him, if they made use of it, not in order to the putting away [or the remission] of some sins [only], but for the purification of the body: supposing still that the soul was thoroughly purified beforehand by righteousness" (JW 82087–82102). Herod, "who feared lest the great influence John had over the people might put it into his power and inclination to raise a rebellion, (for they seemed ready to do any thing he should advise,) thought it best, by putting him to death, to prevent any mischief he might cause" (JW 82104–82113). John "was sent a prisoner, out of Herod's suspicious temper, to Macherus, the castle before mentioned, and was there put to death" (JW 82118–82123). Own words. Documented in the text. That Josephus wrote it: Widely Accepted (Origen knew it, CC 37930–37932; McGiffert, "almost universally admitted to be genuine", EH-ed 9141–9142). Explain it, from both sides: Herod's reason, as Josephus gives it, was fear of a rising; "some of the Jews" saw Herod's defeat as God's punishment. Baptism, in Josephus, is a washing of the body, the soul already made clean by right living. About 170 words in Whiston.

E13. **James (*Antiquities* XX).** The younger Ananus, the high priest, "was a bold man in his temper, and very insolent; he was also of the sect of the Sadducees, who are very rigid in judging offenders, above all the rest of the Jews" (JW 90991–90996). "Festus was now dead, and Albinus was but upon the road; so he assembled the sanhedrim of judges, and brought before them the brother of Jesus, who was called Christ, whose name was James, and some others [or some of his companions]; and, when he had formed an accusation against them as breakers of the law, he delivered them to be stoned" (JW 91000–91013). "those who seemed the most equitable of the citizens, and such as were the most uneasy at the breach of the laws, they disliked what was done", and complained to King Agrippa, "for that what he had already done was not to be justified", and to Albinus, that "it was not lawful for Ananus to assemble a sanhedrim without his consent"; and "King Agrippa took the high-priesthood from him, when he had ruled but three months" (JW 91013–91047). Own words. Documented in the text. That the phrase on James is Josephus's: Widely Accepted. McGiffert: "this is now the common opinion of critics", since a Christian would hardly have written "the *so-called* Christ" (EH-ed 13605–13612). Explain it, from both sides: Ananus charged them "as breakers of the law" (the accuser's side; the law broken is not named); the fair-minded citizens held that he had broken the law, by calling the court without the governor (the other side). Festus and Albinus were Roman governors of Judea (JW 91000–91001, 91038–91041). Plain words: "the sanhedrin, the high council of judges"; "the Sadducees, the priestly party, harsh judges, Josephus says". About 130 words in Whiston. Date: McGiffert gives "61 or 62 a.d." (EH-ed 13615–13616), editor's reckoning; tell it as Josephus does, between two Roman governors.

E14. **Jesus (*Antiquities* XVIII).** In Whiston: "Now, there was about this time Jesus, a wise man, if it be lawful to call him a man, for he was a doer of wonderful works,—a teacher of such men as receive the truth with pleasure. He drew over to him both many of the Jews and many of the Gentiles. He was [the] Christ; and when Pilate, at the suggestion of the principal men among us, had condemned him to the cross, those that loved him at the first did not forsake him, for he appeared to them alive again the third day, as the divine prophets had foretold these and ten thousand other wonderful things concerning him; and the tribe of Christians, so named from him, are not extinct at this day." (JW 81405–81421). The Greek, as two later Christian books quote it: "Γίνεται δὲ κατὰ τοῦτον τὸν χρόνον [χαιρὸν, ADON] Ἰησοῦς, σοφὸς ἀνήρ, εἴγε ἄνδρα αὐτὸν λέγειν χρή. Ἦν γὰρ παραδόξων ἔργων ποιητής, διδάσκαλος ἀνθρώπων τῶν ἡδονῇ τἀληθῆ δεχομένων. Καὶ πολλοὺς μὲν Ἰουδαίους, πολλοὺς δὲ [καὶ, JVL] τοῦ Ἑλληνικοῦ ἐπηγάγετο. Ὁ Χριστὸς οὗτος ἦν" (my reading of JVL 50993–51005 and ADON 80255–80262; OCR "Τίνεται ὃ χατὰ", "Ἰουδαίσυς", "ἐλλυνιχοῦ ἐπείγετο" read as "Γίνεται δὲ κατὰ", "Ἰουδαίους", "Ἑλληνικοῦ ἐπηγάγετο"; JVL has "Ἑλληνισμοῦ" for ADON's "Ἑλληνικοῦ", both "the Greek world"). Six printed sentences in Whiston, about 110 words. It sits between a riot Pilate's soldiers put down (JW 81390–81403) and "another sad calamity" (JW 81423–81424). Own words as printed; how much is his is **Contested** (D2). Explain it: "Christ" is the Greek for "anointed", the Messiah the Jews awaited; Whiston glosses the claim the words would make: "the Christ of God, or the true Messias of the Jews" (JW-ed 139288–139290). For a Jewish priest to write "this man was the Christ" would be to say he believed it; Origen says Josephus did not (I2). "Gentiles" is Whiston's word for non-Jews; the Greek says "of the Greek world". "Pilate": the Roman governor of Judea (EH 8924–8925, I.9). The "principal men among us" are the leaders of the Jews.

## 3. Impact, heaviest first

I1. **Eusebius quoted all three passages, and set the one on Jesus against forged records of Pilate (early fourth century).** In *Church History* I.11 he gives the John passage (EH 9145–9164) and the Jesus passage: "And there lived at that time Jesus, a wise man, if indeed it be proper to call him a man. For he was a doer of wonderful works, and a teacher of such men as receive the truth in gladness. And he attached to himself many of the Jews, and many also of the Greeks. He was the Christ." (EH 9228–9232; the rest EH 9234–9240). His use: "Since an historian, who is one of the Hebrews themselves, has recorded in his work these things concerning John the Baptist and our Saviour, what excuse is there left for not convicting them of being destitute of all shame, who have forged the acts against them?" (EH 9242–9246). The forged acts are those "who have recently given currency to acts against our Saviour" (EH 8928–8932, I.9); Eusebius says in IX.5 that, "Having therefore forged Acts of Pilate and our Saviour full of every kind of blasphemy against Christ, they sent them with the emperor's approval to the whole of the empire ... and that the schoolmasters should give them to their scholars ... to be studied and learned by heart" (EH 47920, 47945–47951). In II.23 he gives the James passage (EH 13566–13614). Later, more than two centuries. Documented (that he quoted them, and why). McGiffert calls him "the first one to quote this passage" (EH-ed 9214): an editor's claim from silence, for the card only. What the held old texts show: Origen's held works cite Josephus on John and James and quote nothing of a passage on Jesus (I2); the earliest held quotation of it is Eusebius's. Explain it (the forged Acts), from both sides: to Eusebius they were forgeries "full of every kind of blasphemy"; the forgers' own text is lost, and their side is not held.

I2. **Origen: Josephus wrote of John and James, and did not accept Jesus as the Christ (third century).**
- *Against Celsus* I.47: "Josephus bears witness to John as having been a Baptist, and as promising purification to those who underwent the rite. Now this writer, although not believing in Jesus as the Christ, in seeking after the cause of the fall of Jerusalem and the destruction of the temple ... says nevertheless—being, although against his will, not far from the truth—that these disasters happened to the Jews as a punishment for the death of James the Just, who was a brother of Jesus (called Christ)" (CC 37930–37941). Again in II.13 (CC 39893–39896).
- *Commentary on Matthew* X.17, Greek: "Καὶ τὸ θαυμαστόν ἐστιν, ὅτι τὸν Ἰησοῦν ἡμῶν οὐ καταδεξάμενος εἶναι Χριστὸν, οὐδὲν ἧττον Ἰακώβῳ δικαιοσύνην ἐμαρτύρησε τοσαύτην" (OMG 3731–3733; OCR "Καὶ τὸ 4) ϑαυμαστόν ἔστιν; ὅτι τὸν Ἴη- σοῦν ἡμῶν ... οὐδὲν ἣτ- τον ᾿Ιαχώβῳ", footnote marker "4)" left out). English: "And the wonderful thing is, that, though he did not accept Jesus as Christ, he yet gave testimony that the righteousness of James was so great" (OM 30627–30629). The ANF English drops "ἡμῶν", "our".
- The James line Origen cites (Jerusalem fell to avenge James) is not in the held *Antiquities* (D3).
Later, about a century and a half. Documented (what Origen says). His silence on a passage about Jesus is a fact about his held works, not about all he read.

I3. **Jerome's copy: "he was believed to be the Christ" (late fourth century).** *Illustrious Men* 13, Latin: "plurimos quoque tam de Judeis quam de gentibus sui habuit sectatores, et credebatur esse Christus" (JVL 50905–50907); English: "He had very many adherents also, both of the Jews and of the Gentiles, and was believed to be Christ" (JV 39728–39729). Jerome's notice also says Josephus "most openly acknowledges that Christ was slain by the Pharisees on account of the greatness of his miracles, that John the Baptist was truly a prophet, and that Jerusalem was destroyed because of the murder of James the apostle" (JV 39720–39724; Latin JVL 50852–50857); none of these three is in the held *Antiquities*, and the NPNF English "eighth book" is the Latin's "decimo octavo", eighteenth (JVL 50852). An old editor's note: learned men say the Greek "ὁ Χριστὸς οὗτος ἦν" is not to be taken "ut sonant", as it sounds, but in the author's sense, "quem optime Hieronymus expressit dicens: Et credebatur esse Christus" (JVL-ed 50948–50952): editor's view. In *Against Jovinian* I.39 Jerome repeats that Josephus "relates that the overthrow of Jerusalem was due to his [James's] death" (JVJ 36487–36491). Later, about three centuries. Documented (Jerome's wording).

I4. **Sozomen: almost a believer (fifth century).** Josephus "may be regarded as a noteworthy witness to the truth concerning Christ; for he hesitates to call Him a man since He wrought marvelous works ... but openly calls him Christ ... It appears to me that in narrating these things, he all but proclaims that Christ, by comparison of works, is God. As if struck by the miracle, he ran, somehow, a middle course, assailing in no way those who believed in Jesus, but rather agreeing with them." (SOZ 26287–26304). Later, about three and a half centuries. Documented as Sozomen's reading.

I5. **A martyr's legend makes the passage a proof (date not given).** In the Greek *Martyrdom of Donatus*, Macarius tells the emperor Diocletian that Josephus, "ὁ ἐξ Ἱεροσολύμων ἱερεύς", "μαρτυρεῖ τὸν Χριστὸν τὸν Θεὸν ἀληθινὸν γεγονότα" ("bears witness that Christ was true God"), and quotes the passage (ADON 80250–80284); the Latin: "Christum verum Deum fuisse testatur" (ADON 80364–80367). Late legend. Inferential-Thin as history; Documented as the legend's use. Leave out of the story; card if wanted.

I6. **Eusebius read the *War* as God's judgment on Jerusalem.** "the judgment of God at length overtook those who had committed such outrages against Christ and his apostles, and totally destroyed that generation of impious men"; for the details, "any one that wishes may find accurately described in the history written by Josephus" (EH 15301–15303, 15317–15318, III.5). Documented as Eusebius's use. Its force is hostile to the Jews; if told, credit it to Eusebius in plain words. Outside this story's job (the three passages); Leave out.

I7. **Michael the Syrian's copy: "he was thought to be the Messiah" (twelfth century).** "L'écrivain Josèphe dit aussi dans le livre sur l'Histoire des Juifs: « En ces temps-là il y eut un homme sage nommé Jésus, s'il convient toutefois de l'appeler homme. ... Beaucoup parmi les Juifs et les Gentils se firent ses disciples. On pense que c'était le Messie; mais non d'après le témoignage des chefs du peuple. C'est pourquoi Pilate le livra au supplice de la croix, et il mourut." (MS 11161–11165, 11230–11236; OCR "V" read as "l'", footnote marker "*" left out). Later, about a thousand years; French of a Syriac chronicle. Documented as Michael's wording. Chabot's note: "Il n'est pas douteux que la mention primitive de Josèphe ait été interpolée par un chrétien", citing Théodore Reinach, 1897 (MS-ed 11208–11209): editor's view, not held further.

I8. **Whiston, the eighteenth-century translator, defended the passage** (JW-ed 138244–138252: it "has of late been greatly questioned by many, and rejected by some of the learned as spurious", while he has "ever declared my firm belief that these testimonies were genuine"). He held that "This was Christ" did not mean "that this Jesus was the Christ of God, or the true Messias of the Jews", but marked which Jesus was meant (JW-ed 139286–139298), and answered Origen so (JW-ed 139660–139677). Editor's view. Card.

I9. **McGiffert's four views, 1890.** The passage "was considered genuine until the sixteenth century, but since then has been constantly disputed. Four opinions are held in regard to it; (1) It is entirely genuine ... absolutely untenable ... (2) It is entirely spurious. Such writers as Hase, Keim, and Schürer adopt this view. (3) It is partly genuine and partly interpolated. This opinion has, perhaps, the most defenders, among them Gieseler, Weizsäcker, Renan, Edersheim, and Schaff. (4) It has been changed from a bitter Jewish calumny of Christ to a Christian eulogy of him. This is Ewald's view. The second opinion seems to me the correct one." (EH-ed 9182–9199). His reasons: a Christian hand, the silence of Christian writers before Eusebius, and the break in context (EH-ed 9187–9213). That Eusebius forged it: "This is possible, but there is no reason to suppose it true" (EH-ed 9214–9223). Editor's view. Card.

## 5. Where sources disagree or come late

D1. **Why he went to Galilee.** The *Life*: to persuade the rebels to lay down their arms and wait on Rome (JW 1659–1681). The *War*: sent as general of both Galilees (JW 106926–106929). Both his. Tell "he commanded in Galilee", or give both, crediting each book.

D2. **The passage on Jesus.**
- As printed: "He was [the] Christ" (JW 81411); Greek "Ὁ Χριστὸς οὗτος ἦν" (ADON 80262; JVL-ed 50948–50949).
- Origen, who read the *Antiquities* (he cites Book XVIII on John, CC 37930–37932), says Josephus did "not accept our Jesus as Christ" (OMG 3731–3733; CC 37933–37934) and, in the held texts, quotes no passage on Jesus.
- Jerome's Latin: "credebatur esse Christus", "he was believed to be the Christ" (JVL 50907).
- Michael's French: "On pense que c'était le Messie", "people think he was the Messiah", with "not according to the witness of the chiefs of the people" (MS 11233–11235).
- Eusebius and the Greek quotations: "He was the Christ" (EH 9232).
So the old copies themselves differ at the very clause that sits worst with Origen's word. How much is Josephus's own is **Contested**. The four modern views (I9) and Whiston's defence (I8) go to the card. No held source later than 1899 gives where scholars stand (gap).

D3. **James and the fall of Jerusalem.** Origen (CC 37936–37941; OM 30620–30626), Eusebius (EH 13561–13564: "These things happened to the Jews to avenge James the Just") and Jerome (JVJ 36489–36491) cite Josephus for it. The held *Antiquities* XX does not say it (JW 91000–91047). McGiffert: "not found in our existing mss. of Josephus" (EH-ed 13545–13546). Documented that the held text lacks it; why is the editors' guess. Card.

D4. **Jerome's additions.** That the Pharisees killed Christ for his miracles, that John was "truly a prophet", and "James the apostle" (JV 39720–39724): not in the held text. Card.

D5. **His character.** Both sides of his own day called him a traitor (E5, E8); he calls himself God's servant (E4). McGiffert's judgement, "a favorite and flatterer of the Vespasian house" (EH-ed 15898–15900), is modern: never in the story.

D6. **The statue.** Eusebius says he "was honored by the erection of a statue in Rome, and his works were deemed worthy of a place in the library" (EH 15915–15920); Jerome repeats it (JV 39710–39713). McGiffert: "Eusebius is the only one, so far as we know, to mention this statue" (EH-ed 15917–15919). Inferential-Thin. Leave out.

D7. **His death.** No held old source gives it. Editors: "early in the second century" (EH-ed 15900–15901), "died after 97" (JV-ed 39708). Leave out of the story.
