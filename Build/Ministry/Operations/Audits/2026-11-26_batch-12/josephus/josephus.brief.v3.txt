In 67, when the Roman general Vespasian took Jotapata, a walled town in Galilee, in the north of the country, Josephus hid in a cave with forty leading men. When the Romans found him on the third day and offered him his life, he prayed in secret, telling God he would surrender. "I give them my hands willingly, and I am content to live. And I declare openly that I do not go over to the Romans as a deserter from the Jewish people, but as a servant sent by you."

Josephus was a priest of the Temple in Jerusalem, from a priestly family, who commanded Jewish forces in Galilee when the Jewish people rose against Rome in 66. That year he gave himself up to Vespasian and foretold that the general would be emperor. When that came true, he lived on at Rome under the emperors' favor. In his people's history, finished there about 93, a few lines tell of John the Baptist, of James, the brother of Jesus, and of Jesus himself. Christians copied those lines for centuries as the word of a Jew outside their faith, and argued over the one on Jesus, which the Christian writers who quote it give in different words.

Back in the cave, after his prayer, the men with him held out a sword. He could die as general of the Jewish people, they said, or as a traitor to them. He argued that it was wrong to take one's own life, and they rushed at him and called him a coward. He proposed that they draw lots, each to be killed in turn by the next. He was left last with one other man, by chance or by God's providence, he writes, and persuaded him to live.

At Rome he wrote the books that tell nearly everything known of his life. He wrote a history of the war in Greek, because others, he says, had written falsely of it, out of flattery to Rome or hatred of the Jewish people. Then came the Jewish Antiquities, his people's history in twenty books, to set their story before Greek-speaking readers. In his Life, a book on his own life, he defended himself against a rival historian who, he says, had written falsely of him, that he and the Galileans had begun the revolt against Rome.

In the Antiquities, Josephus tells of John, called the Baptist, a good man, he writes, who urged the Jewish people to deal justly with one another and to honor God. The ruler Herod, he says, feared that John's hold on the crowds might lead to a rising, so he had him imprisoned and killed.

In the same book, between a riot under Pilate, the Roman governor of Judea, and another disaster, the banishing of the Jewish people from Rome, stands a passage of about a hundred words on Jesus. Its key word, the Christ, means the Messiah, the anointed one the Jewish people awaited. "About this time there lived Jesus, a wise man, if one ought to call him a man. For he did astonishing deeds, and taught people who gladly receive the truth. He won over many of the Jewish people, and many of the Greeks as well. This man was the Christ." The Greeks are those who were not Jewish. The passage goes on: Pilate, at the urging of the leading men, had him crucified; he appeared alive on the third day; and the Christians, named after him, have not died out.

Further on, the high priest, the chief priest of the Temple, called a council of judges. He brought before them "the brother of Jesus who was called Christ, James by name", with some others, charged them with breaking the law, and had them stoned. The fairest-minded citizens protested that he had no right to call the court without the Roman governor's consent.

Origen, a Christian teacher of the third century, knew the lines on John and the phrase on James, and wrote that Josephus did not accept Jesus as the Christ. Early in the fourth century, Eusebius of Caesarea copied all three passages into his history of the church. He set the lines on John and Jesus, a Jew's own word, against forged records of Pilate's dealings with Jesus. Those records, he says, were written against Jesus and sent out in his own day with the emperor's approval.

Late in that century, another Christian writer, Jerome, quoted the passage in Latin, and his Latin says only that Jesus was believed to be the Christ. The passage itself says he was the Christ, and Origen wrote that Josephus did not accept him as the Christ.

At the close of his Life, Josephus called it the account of his whole life, and left others to judge his character by it, as they pleased.
