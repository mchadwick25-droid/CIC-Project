In the year 70, as the Romans besieged Jerusalem, their commander Titus sent Josephus to speak to the defenders in their own language, hoping a countryman might persuade them. Going around the wall, out of range of their weapons but within hearing, he begged them to spare themselves, their country and their Temple. The Jews inside wanted him punished, while the Romans, whenever they lost a fight, blamed his treachery and called for his death as a traitor.

Josephus was a priest of the Temple who had commanded Jewish forces in Galilee when the Jews rose against Rome. In 67 he gave himself up to the Roman general Vespasian, and he lived on at Rome under the emperors' favor, writing his people's history. A few lines in it tell of John the Baptist, James and Jesus. Christians copied them for centuries as the word of a Jew outside their faith, and argued over the one on Jesus, which the old writers who quote it give in different words.

Born in 37 to a priestly family, he was sent by the leaders in Jerusalem, when the rising began in 66, to command in Galilee, in the north of the country. In 67 Vespasian took Jotapata, a walled town there, and Josephus hid in a cave with forty men of standing. Found on the third day, he was offered his life through a Roman officer he knew. He says he remembered dreams in which God had shown him the Jews' coming disasters and the fate of the Roman emperors. So he prayed in secret, telling God he would surrender to the Romans: "I give them my hands willingly, and I am content to live. And I declare openly that I do not go over to the Romans as a deserter from the Jews, but as a servant sent by you."

To the men with him, surrender was treason and death was honor. They held out a sword, telling him to die as general of the Jews or as a traitor to them, and called him a coward. Holding that killing oneself was wrong, he proposed that they kill one another by lot. He was left last with one other man, by chance or by God's providence, he writes, and persuaded him to live.

Brought before Vespasian, he asked to speak to him alone and told him that he, and his son with him, would be emperor. Vespasian took it for a trick to save his life and kept him in chains. Suetonius, a Roman writer of the next generation, also records it. When Vespasian became emperor in 69, he set Josephus free.

Nearly everything known of his life comes from his own books, and in one of them, the account of his life, he defends himself against a rival historian. He wrote his history of the war first in his own language and then in Greek, because others, he says, wrote of it falsely, to flatter Rome or out of hatred of the Jews. Then, for readers of Greek, he wrote the Antiquities of the Jews, his people's history from the creation to his own day, in twenty books, finished about 93, when he was fifty-six.

In it, Josephus calls John, known as the Baptist, a good man who urged the Jews to live justly and to come to his washing. That washing, Josephus says, was for the cleansing of the body, the soul already made clean by right living. The ruler Herod feared that John's hold on the crowds might lead to a rising, so he had him imprisoned and killed.

In the same book stands a passage on Jesus, about a hundred words long. It turns on the Christ, the Messiah, the anointed one the Jews awaited: "About this time there lived Jesus, a wise man, if one ought to call him a man. For he did astonishing deeds, and taught people who gladly receive the truth. He won over many Jews, and many of the Greeks as well. This man was the Christ." The Greeks here are non-Jews. The passage goes on that when Pilate, urged by the leading men, had him crucified, those who loved him did not leave him. It says he appeared to them alive on the third day, as the prophets had foretold, and that the Christians, named after him, have not died out.

In the twentieth book, with one Roman governor dead and the next still on the road, the high priest, a Sadducee, of a school that judged more harshly than the rest, called a council of judges. He brought before it "the brother of Jesus who was called Christ, James by name," and some others, charged them with breaking the law and had them stoned. The fairest-minded citizens protested that he had no right to call the court.

Origen, a Christian teacher of the third century, knew the lines on John and the phrase on James, and wrote that Josephus did not accept Jesus as the Christ. Where he cites Josephus, he quotes nothing on Jesus. Early in the fourth century, Eusebius of Caesarea copied all three passages into his history of the church. He set the lines on John and Jesus, as the word of a Hebrew historian, against forged records of Pilate's dealings with Jesus, full of blasphemy against him. Those records, he says, had been sent across the empire with the emperor's approval for schoolboys to learn by heart.

The old writers who quote the passage word its key clause differently. Where it says that this man was the Christ, the Latin of the Christian writer Jerome, near the end of the fourth century, says only that he was believed to be the Christ. Beside Origen's word, these readings leave open how much of the passage Josephus wrote.

His account of his life ends with the emperors' favor, and with the emperor Domitian punishing the Jews who accused him. Then Josephus leaves others to judge his character from his life, as they please.
