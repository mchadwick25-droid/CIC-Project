When the Roman general Vespasian took Jotapata, a walled town in Galilee, in the north of the country, Josephus hid in a cave with forty men of standing. Found on the third day and offered his life, he prayed in secret, telling God he would give himself up to the Romans. "I give them my hands willingly, and I am content to live. And I declare openly that I do not go over to the Romans as a deserter from the Jews, but as a servant sent by you."

Josephus was a priest of the Temple in Jerusalem, born to a priestly family, and in the first century, when the Jews rose against Rome in 66, he commanded their forces in Galilee. In 67 he gave himself up to Vespasian, and he lived on at Rome under the emperors' favor, writing his people's history. In his history of the Jewish people, finished about 93, a few lines tell of John the Baptist, of James, the brother of Jesus, and of Jesus himself. Christians copied those lines for centuries as the word of a Jew outside their faith, and they argued over the one on Jesus, which the Christian writers who quote it give in different words.

Back in the cave, the men with him had held out a sword. He could die as general of the Jews, they said, or die as a traitor to them, and they rushed at him and called him a coward. He proposed that they kill one another by lot. He was left last with one other man, by chance or by God's providence, he writes, and he persuaded that man to live. To the forty, surrender was treason and death was honor. To Josephus, taking one's own life was wrong, and he tells it that he went over as God's servant.

After Jerusalem fell in 70, he settled at Rome. Nearly everything known of his life comes from his own books, and he wrote them partly to defend himself. He wrote a history of the war in Greek because others, he says, had written falsely of it, out of flattery to Rome or hatred of the Jews. Then came the Antiquities of the Jews, that history of his people in twenty books, written to set their story before Greek-speaking readers. In a last book, his Life, he answered a rival historian who had written against him.

In the eighteenth book of the Antiquities, Josephus tells of John, called the Baptist, a good man, he writes, who urged the Jews to live justly. The ruler Herod, he says, feared that John's hold on the crowds might lead to a rising, so he had John imprisoned and killed.

In the same book, between a riot under Pilate and another disaster, stands a passage of about a hundred words on Jesus. It gives him a title, the Christ, which means the Messiah, the anointed one the Jews awaited. "About this time there lived Jesus, a wise man, if one ought to call him a man. For he did astonishing deeds, and taught people who gladly receive the truth. He won over many Jews, and many of the Greeks as well. This man was the Christ." The Greeks here are the non-Jews of the Greek-speaking world. The passage goes on to say that Pilate had him crucified at the urging of the leading men, that he appeared alive to his followers on the third day, and that the Christians, named after him, have not died out.

In the twentieth book, the high priest, the chief priest of the Temple, called a council of judges. He brought before them "the brother of Jesus who was called Christ, James by name", with some others, charged them with breaking the law, and had them stoned. The fairest-minded citizens protested that the high priest himself had no right to call the court.

Origen, a Christian scholar of the third century, knew the lines on John and the phrase on James, and he wrote that Josephus did not accept Jesus as the Christ. Early in the fourth century, Eusebius of Caesarea copied all three passages into his history of the church. He set the lines on John and Jesus against forged records of Pilate's dealings with Jesus, which, he says, had been sent out in his own day with the emperor's approval.

Near the end of that century, Jerome's Latin gave the key line only as saying that Jesus was believed to be the Christ. So the old readings stand side by side: the passage as it comes down to us says he was the Christ, Jerome's Latin says he was believed to be, and Origen says Josephus never accepted him as the Christ at all. How much of the passage Josephus himself wrote is uncertain, and the old sources leave it open.

At the close of his Life, Josephus called it the account of the actions of his whole life, and he left others to judge his character by them, as they pleased.
