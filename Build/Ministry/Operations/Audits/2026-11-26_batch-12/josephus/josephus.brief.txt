In the year 70, with the Roman army camped around Jerusalem, its commander Titus sent Josephus to speak to the defenders in their own language, hoping a countryman might persuade them. Josephus went around the wall, out of range of their weapons but within hearing, and begged them to spare themselves, their country and their Temple. The Jews inside wanted him punished, and whenever the Romans lost a fight, they blamed his treachery and called for his death as a traitor.

Josephus was a priest from Jerusalem who had commanded Jewish forces in Galilee when the Jews rose against Rome. In 67 he gave himself up to the Roman general Vespasian, and he lived on at Rome under the emperors' favor, writing his people's history. In one of his books, a few lines tell of John the Baptist, of James and of Jesus himself. Christians copied those lines for centuries as the word of a Jew outside their faith, and they argued over the one on Jesus, which the old writers who quote it give in different words.

He was born in 37 into a family of priests of the Temple, the first of the priestly families on his father's side, he says, and of the old royal line on his mother's. When the Jews rose in 66, the leaders in Jerusalem sent him to Galilee, in the north of the country. His own books differ on why. The account of his life says he went to persuade the rebels to lay down their arms and wait on Rome, while his history of the war names him general of both Galilees.

In 67 Vespasian took Jotapata, a walled town in Galilee, and Josephus hid in a cave with forty men of standing. On the third day the Romans found him and offered him his life, through a Roman officer he knew. He says he remembered dreams in which God had shown him the Jews' coming disasters and the fate of the Roman emperors. Then he prayed in secret, telling God he would surrender to the Romans: "I give them my hands willingly, and I am content to live. And I declare openly that I do not go over to the Romans as a deserter from the Jews, but as a servant sent by you."

The men with him held out a sword. He could die as general of the Jews, they said, or die as a traitor to them, and they rushed at him and called him a coward. To them surrender was treason and death was honor, but Josephus held that killing oneself was wrong, so he proposed that they kill one another by lot. He was left last with one other man, by chance or by God's providence, he writes, and he persuaded that man to live.

Brought before Vespasian, he asked to speak to him alone and told him that he, and his son with him, would be emperor. Vespasian thought it a trick to save his life and kept him in chains. Suetonius, a Roman writer of the next generation, also records what Josephus told him. When Vespasian was made emperor in 69, he remembered it and had the chain cut through.

After Jerusalem fell, Josephus went to Rome, where Vespasian gave him rooms in his old house, citizenship and a yearly pension. Nearly everything known of his life comes from his own books, and in the account of his life he defends himself against a rival historian. He wrote his history of the war first in his own language, then in Greek, because others, he says, wrote falsely of it out of flattery to Rome or hatred of the Jews. Then came the Antiquities of the Jews, his history of his people from the creation to his own day, in twenty books, for readers of Greek. He finished it about 93, when he was fifty-six.

In it, Josephus tells that some Jews saw a defeat of the army of the ruler Herod as God's punishment for killing John, called the Baptist. John was a good man, he writes, who urged the Jews to live justly and to come to his washing, which was for the cleansing of the body, the soul already made clean by right living. Herod feared that John's hold on the crowds might lead to a rising, so he had him imprisoned and killed.

In the same eighteenth book, between a riot under Pilate and another disaster, stands a passage on Jesus, about a hundred words long. Its last words here turn on the Christ, the Messiah, the anointed one the Jews awaited: "About this time there lived Jesus, a wise man, if one ought to call him a man. For he did astonishing deeds, and taught people who gladly receive the truth. He won over many Jews, and many of the Greeks as well. This man was the Christ." The Greeks here are non-Jews. The passage goes on that when Pilate, at the urging of the leading men, had him crucified, those who loved him did not leave him. It says he appeared to them alive on the third day, as the prophets had foretold, and that the Christians, named after him, have not died out.

In the twentieth book, with one Roman governor dead and the next still on the road, the high priest, the chief priest of the Temple, called a council of judges. He was a Sadducee, of a Jewish school that judged more harshly than the rest, Josephus says. He brought before the council "the brother of Jesus who was called Christ, James by name," and some others, charged them with breaking the law and had them stoned. The fairest-minded citizens protested that he had no right to call the court, and the king removed him after three months.

Origen, a Christian scholar of the third century, knew the lines on John and the phrase on James, and he wrote that Josephus did not accept Jesus as the Christ. Where he cites Josephus, he quotes nothing of a passage on Jesus. Early in the fourth century, Eusebius of Caesarea copied all three passages into his history of the church. He set the lines on John and Jesus, as the word of a Hebrew historian, against forged records of Pilate's dealings with Jesus. Those records, he says, were full of blasphemy against Jesus, and in his own day they had been sent across the empire with the emperor's approval, for schoolboys to learn by heart.

The old writers who quote the passage word its key clause differently. Where the passage says that this man was the Christ, the Christian scholar Jerome, writing in Latin near the end of the fourth century, reads only that he was believed to be the Christ. A twelfth-century chronicle in Syriac, by a patriarch of Antioch, reads that people thought he was the Messiah. Set beside Origen's word, these readings leave open how much of the passage Josephus wrote.

The account of his life ends with the emperors' favor, and with the emperor Domitian punishing the Jews who accused him. Then Josephus left others to judge his character from his life, as they pleased.
