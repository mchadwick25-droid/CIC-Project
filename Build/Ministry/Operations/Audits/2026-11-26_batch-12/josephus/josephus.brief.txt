When the Roman general Vespasian took Jotapata, a walled town in Galilee, in the north of the country, Josephus hid in a cave with forty men of standing. Found on the third day and offered his life, he prayed in secret, telling God he would give himself up to the Romans. "I give them my hands willingly, and I am content to live. And I declare openly that I do not go over to the Romans as a deserter from the Jews, but as a servant sent by you."

Josephus was a priest of the Temple in Jerusalem, from a priestly family, who commanded Jewish forces in Galilee when the Jews rose against Rome in 66. In 67 he gave himself up to Vespasian, and he lived on at Rome under the emperors' favor, writing his people's history. In that history, finished about 93, a few lines tell of John the Baptist, of James, the brother of Jesus, and of Jesus himself. Christians copied those lines for centuries as the word of a Jew outside their faith, and argued over the one on Jesus, which the Christian writers who quote it give in different words.

In the cave, the men with him had held out a sword. He could die as general of the Jews, they said, or as a traitor to them, and they rushed at him and called him a coward. He proposed that they kill one another by lot. He was left last with one other man, by chance or by God's providence, he writes, and persuaded him to live. To the forty, surrender was treason and death was honor; to Josephus, taking his own life was wrong, and he went over, in his telling, as God's servant.

Nearly everything known of his life comes from his own books, and he wrote them partly to defend himself. At Rome he wrote a history of the war in Greek, because others, he says, had written falsely of it, out of flattery to Rome or hatred of the Jews. Then came the Antiquities of the Jews, his people's history in twenty books, to set their story before Greek-speaking readers. In his Life, he answered a rival historian who had written against him.

In the Antiquities, Josephus tells of John, called the Baptist, a good man, he writes, who urged the Jews to live justly. The ruler Herod, he says, feared that John's hold on the crowds might lead to a rising, so he had him imprisoned and killed.

In the same book, between a riot under Pilate and another disaster, stands a passage of about a hundred words on Jesus. It calls him the Christ, the Messiah, the anointed one the Jews awaited. "About this time there lived Jesus, a wise man, if one ought to call him a man. For he did astonishing deeds, and taught people who gladly receive the truth. He won over many Jews, and many of the Greeks as well. This man was the Christ." The Greeks are the non-Jews. The passage goes on: Pilate, at the urging of the leading men, had him crucified; he appeared alive on the third day; and the Christians, named after him, have not died out.

Later, the high priest, the chief priest of the Temple, called a council of judges. He brought before them "the brother of Jesus who was called Christ, James by name", with some others, charged them with breaking the law, and had them stoned. The fairest-minded citizens protested that he had no right to call the court.

Origen, a Christian teacher of the third century, knew the lines on John and the phrase on James, and wrote that Josephus did not accept Jesus as the Christ. Early in the fourth century, Eusebius of Caesarea copied all three passages into his history of the church. He set them against forged records of Pilate's dealings with Jesus, sent out, he says, in his own day with the emperor's approval.

Late in that century, Jerome's Latin reads only that Jesus was believed to be the Christ. Against the passage's own words, and Origen's, how much of it Josephus wrote is uncertain, and the old sources leave it open.

At the close of his Life, Josephus called it the account of his whole life, and left others to judge his character by it, as they pleased.
