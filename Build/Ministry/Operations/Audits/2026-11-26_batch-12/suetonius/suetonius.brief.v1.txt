In his life of the emperor Claudius, a Roman writer sets down the emperor's acts one after another. Claudius freed the people of old Troy from tax forever, as founders, he says, of the Roman race, and he let German envoys sit with the senators at the theater. Between those two comes one sentence: "He drove out of Rome the Jews who kept rioting at the urging of Chrestus."

The writer was Suetonius, a Roman scholar who wrote the lives of Rome's rulers from Julius Caesar to Domitian. This line, and one more under Nero, is all he says of the first Christians, if this one is about them at all. Each is one item in a list of an emperor's acts, and only the second names the Christians. Later, Christians complained, without naming him, that outsiders said their name and Christ's wrong, much as his first line has it. A Christian chronicle set that line down almost in his words and joined it to a story in the New Testament.

His father, in his own telling, was an army officer, and Suetonius remembers being a young man under the emperor Domitian. His lives end with that emperor, so he wrote after Domitian's death, a generation or more after both lines. He later ran the emperor Hadrian's letters.

He tells the expulsion in that one sentence, with no year, number or source. The Acts of the Apostles, in the New Testament, also tells of Claudius ordering the Jews out of Rome. At Corinth, the apostle Paul met a Jew and his wife lately come from Italy because of that order. Suetonius names Chrestus without saying who he was or where. The name is close in sound to Christus, the Latin form of Christ, and it was also a real name.

In his life of Nero, Suetonius lists measures by which, he says, abuses were punished and new rules made. Spending was limited, and taverns were barred from selling cooked food except beans and greens. Then comes the second line: "Punishments were laid on the Christians, a kind of people holding a new and harmful superstition." Superstition was his word for a religion he despised. The list runs straight on to the chariot drivers, who were stopped from roaming the streets and cheating people for fun.

He names no charge, number or kind of punishment, and he files these among Nero's acts that deserve no blame, some even praise, before he turns to Nero's crimes. In another chapter he says Nero himself set Rome on fire, and there he names no Christians. Another Roman, Tacitus, tells at length of Christians put to death under Nero.

About 200, at Carthage, Tertullian had read Suetonius and names him on other matters. In his appeal to the pagans, without naming Suetonius, he says the name "Christian" comes from anointing. Then he turns to them: "You say it wrong, and call us 'Chrestians', for you are not even clearly sure of the name itself. Even so, it rings of sweetness or goodness." In Greek, chrestos means good or kind.

About 725, Bede, in his chronicle of the world, set down the Claudius line almost in Suetonius's words, without Chrestus. He put it in Claudius's ninth year and added that Luke also reports it.

That is all Suetonius set down: seven Latin words between old Troy's tax and the German envoys' seats at the theater, and nine between the taverns' beans and greens and the chariot drivers.
