## BLOCKING

**1. The anointing sentence (SOFTENED OR CHANGED: who says it).** Tertullian says only that the name comes from anointing (TERT-C 3903–3904). The priests, kings and oil are Lactantius's (LAC-L 16816–16821, 16830). After "he says" and a colon, the listener hears them as Tertullian's. Outline Do not say: "Not that Tertullian says the Jews anointed priests and kings."

- **Old:** In his appeal to the pagans, without naming Suetonius, he says the name "Christian" comes from anointing: Christ means "the anointed one", for the Jews anointed their priests and kings with holy oil.
- **New:** In his appeal to the pagans, without naming Suetonius, he says the name "Christian" comes from anointing; in plain words, Christ means "the anointed one", for the Jews anointed their priests and kings with holy oil.

**Which wording, and why.** The two trust readers both split the sentence in two. The first gives "...comes from anointing. Christ means "the anointed one", for the Jews...". The second gives "...comes from anointing. In plain words, Christ means "the anointed one", because the Jews...". The second reader's "In plain words" is kept: heard aloud, a full stop alone does not change the voice, so the listener may still hear the next sentence as Tertullian's. "In plain words" marks it as the narrator's gloss, which fixes the fault. Neither split is taken as given, though. The outline's Do not say list also bars "the name 'Christian' comes from anointing" without its plain sense in the same sentence, and the outline's Words to explain asks for the plain sense "in the same sentence". So the gloss stays in the same sentence after a semicolon, marked by "in plain words". "For" is kept from the outline's own wording rather than the second reader's "because".
