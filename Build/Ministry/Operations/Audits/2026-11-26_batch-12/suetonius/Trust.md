## The quotes, checked against the Latin

- **Q1** (ROL 25841–25843): "Iudaeos impulsore Chresto assidue tumultuantis Roma expulit." Rendered "He drove out of Rome the Jews who kept rioting at the urging of Chrestus." Every word carried (assidue = kept; impulsore = at the urging of); nothing added; no "all", "one" or "since". Seven Latin words. HELD.
- **Q2** (ROL 28458–28460): "afflicti suppliciis Christiani, genus hominum superstitionis novae ac maleficae". Rendered "Punishments were laid on the Christians, a kind of people holding a new and harmful superstition." Impersonal, as the Latin is; "harmful" for *maleficae* is fair, and avoids Rolfe's misleading "mischievous". Nine Latin words. HELD.
- **Q3** (TERT-C 3904–3907): "etiam cum corrupte a uobis Chrestiani pronuntiamur (nam ne nominis quidem ipsius liquido certi estis), sic quoque de suauitate uel bonitate modulatum est." Rendered "You say it wrong, and call us 'Chrestians', for you are not even clearly sure of the name itself. Even so, it rings of sweetness or goodness." The concessive "even when" becomes a statement and then "Even so" (sic quoque). The meaning and force are kept. HELD.

## Sentences that are not HELD, and notes

**S1.** "In his life of the emperor Claudius, a Roman writer sets down the emperor's acts one after another." SUMMARY HOLDS. ROL 25820–25826 and 25841–25845 run act after act: the provinces returned, the Lycians, Ilium, the Jews, the German envoys. A fair picture of the chapter.

**S7.** "Later, Christians complained, without naming him, that outsiders said their name and Christ's wrong, much as his first line has it." SUMMARY HOLDS. TERT-C 3904–3905: "corrupte a uobis Chrestiani pronuntiamur"; LAC-L 16813–16814: "qui eum inmutata littera Chrestum solent dicere". Neither names Suetonius. "Much as his first line has it" compares spellings that both texts show (Chresto, Chrestum). It does not claim that either Christian answered him. Note for the reviewer: Christ's name said wrong rests on Lactantius, who is not named later in the story (he was cut). "Later, Christians" covers him, so this is not a finding.

**S8.** "A Christian chronicle set that line down almost in his words and joined it to a story in the New Testament." SUMMARY HOLDS. BEDE 23216–23217: "Nono regni sui anno Iudaeos tumultuantes Roma expulit, quod et Lucas refert." Bede has four of Suetonius's seven words. The joining is Bede's own "quod et Lucas refert".

**S10.** "His lives end with that emperor, so he wrote after Domitian's death, a generation or more after both lines." SUMMARY HOLDS. The *Domitian* is the last life and tells his death. ROL 32023–32025 (*Nero* 57.2): "cum post viginti annos adulescente me", so he was a young man twenty years after Nero. He wrote later than that, which makes "a generation or more" fair. No year is given.

**S11.** "He later ran the emperor Hadrian's letters." HELD as Spartianus's report: SCH 6074–6076, "Suetonio Tranquillo epistularum magistro"; the same clause at ROL 236–241. Note: the only witness is a much later life of Hadrian, which survives here only as quoted in modern prints (N2). The story states the post without naming its witness. The outline keeps it this way (Cut first 1), and the post itself is not in dispute. Not blocking. The reviewer may weigh it against Part 1's "where the source wrote long after".

**S20.** "Superstition was his word for a religion he despised." SUMMARY HOLDS. ROL 28459: "superstitionis novae ac maleficae". Suetonius uses *superstitio* elsewhere for foreign cults he holds cheap: ROL 16291 (the Egyptian and Jewish rites, *Tiberius* 36), ROL 31941 (*Nero* 56), ROL 38869. "Despised" is a fair reading of "new and harmful".

**S21.** "The list runs straight on to the chariot drivers, who were stopped from roaming the streets and cheating people for fun." SUMMARY HOLDS. ROL 28460–28462: "vetiti quadrigariorum lusus, quibus inveterata licentia passim vagantibus fallere ac furari per iocum ius erat". The ban falls on their sport (*lusus*), which they carried out while roaming. *Furari* ("robbing") is dropped. The sense, that their roaming pranks of cheating were stopped, is kept. Minor at most. No change needed.

## The heart

The heart is right. For an outside witness the sources hold only two short lines, each set inside a list of acts, and a handful of later old readers: Tertullian and Lactantius on the name, and Bede carrying the Claudius line on. The brief tells exactly that and does not stretch the lines. It keeps "if this one is about them at all" open and does not settle who Chrestus was.

Timing: who he was, the two lines, and "only the second names the Christians" are told by about word 130. The later readers close the essence at word 171. That is slightly past 150 words, but within the first minute at the story's own pace (581 words). It is on time, not late. Not blocking.
