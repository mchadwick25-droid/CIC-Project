**BLOCKING: 3.** These are the held-back name, the essence sentence that fails on one hearing, and "anointing" told bare.

## 1. The test (person)

- **Who:** filled. Suetonius was a Roman scholar who wrote the lives of the rulers from Julius Caesar to Domitian. His father was an army officer, he was young under Domitian, and he later ran Hadrian's letters.
- **What happened:** filled. He wrote two short lines, one on Claudius driving out the Jews "at the urging of Chrestus" and one on Nero punishing the Christians.
- **What it was about:** filled. It covers how little the lines say, whether Chrestus is Christ, and "superstition" as his scornful word.
- **Why the connection matters:** filled, but weakened. Tertullian answers the "Chrestians" mistake and Bede copies the Claudius line and ties it to Luke. Tertullian's point only lands if the listener knows why "anointing" matters (see 4). Luke is never tied to Acts (see 3).

## 2. The opening

After the first three sentences, the listener has "a Roman writer", Claudius, old Troy, the German envoys and the quote.

- **Who:** the writer is not named until sentence 4: "The writer was Suetonius, a Roman scholar..." This is the held-back reveal that Part 1 bars ("bringing the name in where the moment allows, never as a held-back reveal"). The moment allows the name in sentence 1. **BLOCKING.** Change: put the name in sentence 1, "In his life of the emperor Claudius, the Roman writer Suetonius sets down the emperor's acts one after another." Sentence 4 then becomes "Suetonius was a Roman scholar who wrote the lives of Rome's rulers from Julius Caesar to Domitian."
- **When:** no century or year is given for Claudius or Nero anywhere in the story. The first date heard is "About 200". A listener with no background cannot place these emperors. MINOR, and not a block under the prompt. The reviewer should check whether the outline's Setting holds a plain time clause, such as "in the first century", that the writer can weave in.
- **Where / why there:** Rome is clear from the quote, and the reason to listen is clear from the quote.

## 3. Sentences that fail on one hearing

1. "Later, Christians complained, without naming him, that outsiders said their name and Christ's wrong, much as his first line has it." The meaning is lost: what was said wrong, and how is it "much as his first line has it"? At this point the listener has not been told that Chrestus sounds like Christus, so they cannot make the link. This sentence carries the essence (what came of him), so the thread is lost inside the first minute. **BLOCKING.** Change: use the plainer form the story later supports, "Later, Christians complained that outsiders called them 'Chrestians', getting their name and Christ's wrong, much as his first line seems to." This holds only if the outline has it. Otherwise cut "much as his first line has it".
2. "He put it in Claudius's ninth year and added that Luke also reports it." The meaning is lost: who is Luke? The listener heard "the Acts of the Apostles" but was never told that Luke wrote it. MINOR. Change: "...added that Luke, in the Acts, also reports it." This holds if the outline names Luke as the writer of Acts. Otherwise use "added that the Acts also reports it."
3. "About 200, at Carthage, Tertullian had read Suetonius and names him on other matters." The listener does not learn who Tertullian is: the story never says he was a Christian writer, and Carthage is not placed. The next sentence, "his appeal to the pagans", only hints at it. MINOR. Change: "About 200, at Carthage in North Africa, the Christian writer Tertullian had read Suetonius..." This holds if the outline's Setting and Names support it.

## 4. Bare statements

1. "...he says the name 'Christian' comes from anointing." The idea is named but its plain sense is not given. A listener with no church background does not know that "Christ" means "the anointed one". Without that, the turn to "you say it wrong" and the meaning of chrestos lose their point. **BLOCKING** (Explain it). Change: give the plain sense if the outline holds it, as in "...comes from anointing, since Christ means 'the anointed one'". Otherwise cut the clause.

## 5. Citations read aloud

None that Part 1 does not call for. "In his life of the emperor Claudius", "In another chapter", "In his appeal to the pagans" and "Luke also reports it" each credit a witness where the old sources differ, or name the very text the story is about.

## 6. Tells and machine-made lines

**Part 1 tells:** there is one, the held-back reveal (see 2). There is no "not X but Y", no dash for drama, no question answered by itself and no stock word. The checker's "stock word" hits on "New Testament" are a false positive: it is a proper name. The close returns to the opening image, which Part 1 allows, so I do not count it as a summing-up close.

**Lines that sound machine-made (not blocking):**
1. "That is all Suetonius set down: seven Latin words between old Troy's tax and the German envoys' seats at the theater, and nine between the taverns' beans and greens and the chariot drivers." The two halves are perfectly matched, and the ending is built to echo the opening. It is allowed, but it is the neat, symmetrical flourish a model reaches for, and it sits right next to a summing-up close. Its first words also repeat the earlier "is all he says of the first Christians".
2. The absence sentences run in triplets: "with no year, number or source", "no charge, number or kind of punishment", "without saying who he was or where". The Outside witness shape asks the story to say what the source leaves open, so each one is licensed. Three matched lists in close order still read as a template.
3. "without naming him" (paragraph 2) and "without naming Suetonius" (paragraph 7). The same hedge is used twice, and the first time it means nothing yet to the listener.

## 7. Words, runs, tone

- **Unexplained words:** "anointing" (counted under 4); "pagans", which is understood loosely, MINOR; "Bede", given with no role or place, so he is just a name that copies a line, MINOR; "ran the emperor Hadrian's letters", which is understood as a court post, but Hadrian and the office are not explained and the fact goes nowhere, MINOR.
- **Runs of short one-act sentences:** none.
- **Talking down:** none.

## 8. Attention

- **Does the opening pull in:** yes. The list of odd acts (Troy's tax, German envoys at the theater) with one sentence about Jews and "Chrestus" between them is a strong, concrete hook.
- **Where attention first drifts:** "His father, in his own telling, was an army officer, and Suetonius remembers being a young man under the emperor Domitian." Just after the essence, the story steps sideways into his life. That paragraph adds dates by relation only ("a generation or more after"), plus Hadrian's letters, which is never used again. The listener is waiting to hear about Chrestus and gets a résumé. For the reviser: this is a matter of pace, not a cut the heart needs. It reads more easily if each fact is tied to why it matters to the two lines, which only the "wrote after Domitian's death" clause does now.
- **Would listen to the end:** yes. The Nero list (beans and greens, chariot drivers) and Tertullian's "Chrestians" carry it.
