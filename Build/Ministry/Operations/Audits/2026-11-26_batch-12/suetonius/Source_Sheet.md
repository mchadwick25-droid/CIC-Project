# Source sheet: Suetonius

Batch 12. The job (Mark, 2026-11-27, "Batch 12: the slate approved"): two short lines in the lives of the emperors, Claudius expelling Jews stirred up by "Chrestus" and Nero punishing the Christians; the story tells how little the lines say, and how later readers used them. Shape: Outside witness; the record decides the order. The batch 6 sheet and outline were used only to find passages. Every passage below was found and read in its own file for this sheet. ## 1. Setting

S1. **His own words about his family and youth.** ROL 34517–34519 (*Otho* 10.1): "Interfuit huic bello pater meus Suetonius Laetus, tertiae decimae legionis tribunus angusticlavius." ROL 34570–34572: "My father Suetonius Laetus took part in that war, as a tribune of the equestrian order in the Thirteenth legion." ROL 32023–32025 (*Nero* 57.2): "Denique cum post viginti annos adulescente me exstitisset condicionis incertae qui se Neronem esse iactaret". ROL 32036–32040: "In fact, twenty years later, when I was a young man, a person of obscure origin appeared, who gave out that he was Nero". ROL 40104–40107 (*Domitian* 12.2): "Interfuisse me adulescentulum memini, cum a procuratore frequentissimoque consilio inspiceretur nonagenarius senex, an circumsectus esset." ROL 40158–40162: "I recall being present in my youth when the person of a man ninety years old was examined before the procurator and a very crowded court, to see whether he was circumcised." OWN. Documented. Plain: his father was an army officer; he was a young man in Rome under the emperor Domitian.

S2. **His birth year.** ROL 149–160 (Rolfe): "He only tells us that he was the son of Suetonius Laetus ... the date of his birth, which Mommsen assigns to the year 77 a.d., Macé with somewhat greater probability to 69. The rest of our information is derived from the Letters of the Younger Pliny and from a single allusion in Spartianus". SCH 6065: "Danach legte Mommsen die Geburt etwa in das Jahr 77, Mace in 69." THF 50–51: "born towards the close of the reign of Vespasian, who died in the year 79". ROL 164: "His birthplace is unknown". MODERN reckonings. Contested (69 or 77). Census `dates` "born c. 69 or c. 77; dismissed c. 121" (CEN): the same two moderns. Not for the story.

S3. **His life in Pliny's letters (not held).** ROL 191–197: "From Pliny we learn that he practised at the bar, although it was apparently only for a short time. That he was a schoolmaster, which is asserted by Macé and others, seems to lack evidence. He took no part in political life, and although he secured a military tribuneship through Pliny's good offices, he soon had it transferred to a relative." ROL 206–208: "In his letter to Trajan Pliny refers to Suetonius as contubernalis, which indicates an intimate friendship and an approximately equal age." ROL 215–216: "The letters of Pliny which refer to Suetonius cover approximately the period from 96 to 112." THF 62–69: "Several of the younger Pliny's letters are addressed to Suetonius, with whom he lived in the closest friendship ... 'a most excellent, honourable, and learned man, whom he had the pleasure of entertaining under his own roof'". SCH 6027–6041 lists the letters (1.18, 1.24, 3.8, 5.10, 9.34, 10.94–95). MODERN, each reporting letters the Library does not hold. The friendship: three moderns agree (Widely Accepted as their report), but no old text is held for it (§7 N1).

S4. **His post under Hadrian, and the dismissal.** SCH 6074–6076 quotes Spartianus (*Life of Hadrian* 11.3) in Latin: "Septicio Claro praefecto praetorii et Suetonio Tranquillo epistularum magistro multisque aliis, quod apud Sabinam uxorem i[niu]ssu eius familiarius se tunc egerant quam reverentia domus aulicae postulabat, successores dedit." ROL 239–241 prints the same clause ("quod apud Sabinam uxorem [iniussu] eius familiarius tunc se egerant quam reverentia domus aulicae postulabat"), so two prints confirm it. Plain sense: Hadrian replaced Septicius Clarus, the guard commander, and Suetonius, master of the letters, and many others, because they had behaved more familiarly toward his wife Sabina, without his leave, than the respect owed the court allowed. LATER (late Roman biography). Documented as Spartianus's report. ROL 242–244 (Rolfe): "this statement is far from clear or definite". ROL 233–235: "secretary to Hadrian, probably during the period when his friend and patron Gaius Septicius Clarus was a prefect of the praetorian guard (119 to 121)". The year 121 is Rolfe's reckoning from Septicius's term (Dominant Modern Reconstruction). Plain: "he ran the emperor Hadrian's letters, until Hadrian dismissed him."

S5. **The book.** ROL 320–326 (Rolfe): "Of all these the only work which has come down to us entire, or nearly so, is the Lives of the Caesars, published in 120. It includes the biographies of twelve 'Caesars,' from Julius to Domitian". The year 120 is Rolfe's (Dominant Modern Reconstruction). That it runs from Julius Caesar to Domitian is plain from the held text (Documented). It ends with Domitian, who died in 96, so it was written after 96 (Documented by its contents; the year 96 Widely Accepted). ROL 262–266: his other books, mostly lost, include "On Illustrious Men (in the field of literature)". ROL 427–432 (Rolfe): great events "are dismissed in a brief chapter, or with a casual allusion ... The acts of the senate and people, and the edicts of the emperors, receive fuller attention, but are wholly subordinate to the personal element." MODERN, a reading of the whole book; the narrator may say only what the text shows (lists of acts, §2).

S6. **When the two events were, and how far he wrote from them.** Claudius ruled 41–54 (MIR 2500, SCH 24275), Nero 54–68 (SCH 24275). MODERN, Widely Accepted. Suetonius gives no year for either line (E1, E3). He wrote after 96 (S5) and was a young man about twenty years after Nero's death (S1). Plain: "a generation or more later". Any year for the expulsion is not his (D5).

S7. **Place.** Rome (both lines; CEN `placeLabel` "Rome"). OWN.

S8. **Tradition.** CEN: `toldWithin` null; status "Within Another World (A5)"; era 1; `kind` "person". MAP 2308: "A pagan Roman biographer writing from outside the church, so context, not tradition." EDITOR. No tradition name; he stands outside the church.

## 2. The two lines, in their place

E1. **The Claudius line.** ROL 25841–25845 (*Claudius* 25.3–4): "... si consanguineos suos Ilienses ab omni onere immunes praestitisset. Iudaeos impulsore Chresto assidue tumultuantis Roma expulit. Germanorum legatis in orchestra sedere permisit". Confirmed in MIR 2506 ("[I]udaeos impulsore C[h]resto assidue t[um]ultuantes Roma expulit") and SCH 24311–24312 ("Iudaeos impulsore Chresto assidue tumultuantes Roma expulit"). English, ROL 25907–25909: "Since the Jews constantly made disturbances at the instigation of Chrestus, he expelled them from Rome." THF 10639–10640: "He banished from Rome all the Jews, who were continually making disturbances at the instigation of one Chrestus". OWN. Documented. Seven Latin words. The Latin has no "since", "all" or "one": those are the translators'. It gives no year, no number, no source, and does not say who Chrestus was or where.

E2. **The list around it.** ROL 25825–25828, 25904–25907 (English): "He allowed the people of Ilium perpetual exemption from tribute, on the ground that they were the founders of the Roman race, reading an ancient letter of the senate and people of Rome written in Greek to king Seleucus ... free from every burden." Then E1. Then ROL 25909–25916: "He allowed the envoys of the Germans to sit in the orchestra, led by their naive self-confidence; for when they had been taken to the seats occupied by the common people and saw the Parthian and Armenian envoys sitting with the senate, they moved of their own accord to the same part of the theatre". Then the Druids abolished, the Eleusinian rites, a temple restored, treaties struck with a pig's sacrifice; ROL 25926–25931: "But these and other acts, and in fact almost the whole conduct of his reign, were dictated not so much by his own judgment as that of his wives and freedmen". OWN. Documented. Ilium is old Troy: the text's own reason is that its people were "the founders of the Roman race".

E3. **The Nero line.** ROL 28454–28463 (*Nero* 16.2): "Multa sub eo et animadversa severe et coercita nec minus instituta: adhibitus sumptibus modus; publicae cenae ad sportulas redactae; interdictum ne quid in popinis cocti praeter legumina aut holera veniret, cum antea nullum non obsonii genus proponeretur; afflicti suppliciis Christiani, genus hominum superstitionis novae ac maleficae; vetiti quadrigariorum lusus, quibus inveterata licentia passim vagantibus fallere ac furari per iocum ius erat; pantomimorum factiones cum ipsis simul relegatae." Confirmed in MIR 2567–2572 ("adflicti suppliciis Christiani, genus hominum superstitionis novae ac maleficae") and SCH 24348–24349. English, ROL 28516–28530: "During his reign many abuses were severely punished and put down, and no fewer new laws were made: a limit was set to expenditures; the public banquets were confined to a distribution of food; the sale of any kind of cooked viands in the taverns was forbidden, with the exception of pulse and vegetables, whereas before every sort of dainty was exposed for sale. Punishment was inflicted [on] the Christians, a class of men given to a new and mischievous superstition. He put an end to the diversions of the chariot drivers, who from immunity of long standing claimed the right of ranging at large and amusing themselves by cheating and robbing the people. The pantomimic actors and their partisans were banished from the city." THF 11612–11614: "He likewise inflicted punishments on the Christians, a sort of people who held a new and impious superstition." OWN. Documented. Nine Latin words, one item in a list of measures. The Latin is impersonal ("punishments were inflicted"), set "under him" (sub eo); THF makes Nero the subject. It gives no number, year, charge, place or kind of punishment.

E4. **Where he files it.** ROL 28653–28656 (*Nero* 19.3): "Haec partim nulla reprehensione, partim etiam non mediocri laude digna in unum contuli, ut secernerem a probris ac sceleribus eius, de quibus dehinc dicam." ROL 28700–28704: "I have brought together these acts of his, some of which are beyond criticism, while others are even deserving of no slight praise, to separate them from his shameful and criminal deeds, of which I shall proceed now to give an account." THF 11656–11659: "These transactions, in part unexceptionable, and in part highly commendable, I have brought into one view, in order to separate them from the scandalous and criminal part of his conduct". OWN. Documented: the punishment of the Christians stands in the group of acts he calls blameless or praiseworthy, before he turns to Nero's crimes. MAL 5183–5185: "Suetonius thinks, Nero did right in punishing the Christians" (MODERN paraphrase; the arrangement itself carries it).

E5. **The fire, told apart.** ROL 30503–30507 (*Nero* 38): "Nam quasi offensus deformitate veterum aedificiorum et angustiis flexurisque vicorum, incendit urbem tam palam, ut plerique consulares cubicularios eius cum stuppa taedaque in praediis suis deprehensos non attigerint". ROL 30560–30566: "For under cover of displeasure at the ugliness of the old buildings and the narrow, crooked streets, he set fire to the city so openly that several ex-consuls did not venture to lay hands on his chamberlains although they caught them on their estates with tow and firebrands". OWN. Documented as his account: he says Nero set the fire, and in that chapter names no Christians; chapter 16 names no fire. ROL 30622–30623 (Rolfe): the accounts of Tacitus and Dio "differ from that of Suetonius". THF 20771–20774 (note 614): the fire "was imputed to the Christians, and drew on them the persecutions mentioned in c. xvi." MODERN: the link is the 1909 editors'. MAP 2324–2327 says the same. ROL 28475–28476 (Rolfe's note on 16.1, the new porches against fire): "This was undoubtedly after the great fire" (MODERN; about the building rules, not the Christians).

E6. **Other lines editors connect with Christians.** ROL 16295 (*Tiberius* 36, "similia sectantes"), THF 19530–19539 (note 335: "we are strongly inclined to think ... an allusion to the small and obscure body of Christians"); ROL 40141–40142 (note on *Domitian* 12.2: "These were doubtless Christians, whom the Romans commonly confounded with the Jews"). MODERN guesses; Suetonius names Christians only in *Nero* 16.2. Inferential-Thin. Not for the story.

## 3. What came of them (ranked heaviest first: what later witnesses carried on and used)

R1. **A Christian chronicle carried the Claudius line on, beside Acts.** BEDE 23216–23217 (§287): "Nono regni sui anno [I]udaeos tum[ul]tuantes Roma ex[pul]it, quod et Lucas refert." Plain sense: "In the ninth year of his reign he drove the rioting Jews from Rome, which Luke also reports." Margin, same lines: "orot.i.e.u." (damaged OCR; read as Mommsen's reference to Orosius) and "act.18,2" (EDITOR: the line came to Bede through Orosius, not held; Inferential-Thin as a reading of the damaged margin). LATER (Bede, about 725). Documented. Bede keeps Suetonius's own words "Iudaeos tumultuantes Roma expulit", drops Chrestus, adds a year (Claudius's ninth), and pairs it with Luke, the writer of Acts. The year is Bede's (and, by the margin, Orosius's), not Suetonius's or Luke's.

R2. **A monk used both lines against a scholar who said the martyrs were few.** RUI 2295–2300: Henry Dodwell's dissertation "de Martyrum paucitate" (on the fewness of martyrs), "Oxonii editae ... anno 1684". RUI 3126–3129: "quarum tamen ipsa urbium regina immunis non fuit. Nam ut testis est Suetonius, Claudius Judaeos, quo nomine etiam Christiani tum designabantur, impulsore Chresto assidue tumultuantes Roma expulit." Plain: the queen of cities was not free of these persecutions; "for, as Suetonius witnesses, Claudius drove from Rome the Jews, by which name the Christians too were then called, who kept rioting at the urging of Chrestus." RUI 3153–3158: "Et quidem Suetonius a Nerone afflictos suppliciis Christianos commemorans, nullam Romani incendii facit mentionem, sed eos genus hominum superstitionis novae ac maleficae appellat; quae cum referat Suetonius inter ea, quae a Nerone instituta fuerunt, haud dubium est quin edictum adversus Christian[o]s ab eo tyranno scriptum fuerit." Plain: Suetonius, recording that Christians were punished by Nero, makes no mention of the fire of Rome; and since he reports it among the things Nero established, "there is no doubt that an edict against the Christians was written by that tyrant." LATE READER (Ruinart, died 1709). Documented as his reading. His "Jews ... Christians too" and his "edict" are his inferences, not Suetonius's words.

R3. **Christians protested that Romans said their name wrong.** TERT-C 3903–3907 (*Ad nationes* 1.3, clean print): "Christianum uero nomen, quantum significatio est, de unctione interpretatur. etiam cum corrupte a uobis Chrestiani pronuntiamur (nam ne nominis quidem ipsius liquido certi estis), sic quoque de suauitate uel bonitate modulatum est." TERT-E 9204–9208: "The name Christian, however, so far as its meaning goes, bears the sense of anointing. Even when by a faulty pronunciation you call us 'Chrestians' (for you are not certain about even the sound of this noted name), you in fact lisp out the sense of pleasantness and goodness." The same point in the *Apology* 3: TERT-O 12453–12456 (damaged: "Sed et cum perperam Chrestianus pronuntiatur a vobis (nam nec nominis certa est notitia penes vos), de suavitate vel benignitate conpositum est"), TERT-E 1712–1716 ("even when it is wrongly pronounced by you 'Chrestianus' (for you do not even know accurately the name you hate), it comes from sweetness and benignity"). LAC-L 16770–16772, 16774, 16813–16814 (*Divine Institutes* 4.7.4–5): "nam Christus non proprium nomen est, sed nuncupatio potestatis et regni: sic enim Iudaei reges suos appellabant. sed exponenda huius nominis ratio est propter ignorantium errorem, qui eum inmutata littera Chrestum solent dicere." LAC-E 3163: "for Christ is not a proper name, but a title of power and dominion; for by this the Jews were accustomed to call their kings. But the meaning of this name must be set forth, on account of the error of the ignorant, who by the change of a letter are accustomed to call Him Chrestus." LATER (Tertullian about 200, at Carthage; Lactantius early fourth century). Documented as their words. Neither names Suetonius. The tie to his line is the editors': TERT-O 12504–12514 (Oehler cites "Sueton. Claud. cap. 25"); ROL 25871 (Rolfe: "Another form of Christus; see Tert. Apol. 3"); LAC-E 3165 (ANF: "Suetonius speaks of Christ as Chrestus"); JUS 14044–14047 (ANF: "the popular blunder of which Suetonius ... gives us an example, 'impulsore Chresto'"); OTTO 3984–3993, 6556–6561; AUD 26695–26697. MODERN. Widely Accepted as the editors' pairing.

R4. **A Christian writer read him and named him.** TERT-C 919–920 (*De spectaculis* 5): "qui quos quem per ordinem et quibus idolis ludos instituerint, positum est apud Suetonium Tranquillum". TERT-E 6789–6791: "As to the idols in whose honour these games were established, ample information is to be found in the pages of Suetonius Tranquillus." TERT-C 21023–21025 (*De anima* 44): "sed et Neronem Suetonius et Thrasymedem Theopompus negant umquam somniasse, nisi uix Neronem in ultimo exitu post pauores suos". TERT-E 18756–18759: "Suetonius, however, informs us that Nero never dreamt, and Theopompus says the same thing about Thrasymedes; but Nero at the close of his life did with some difficulty dream after some excessive alarm." The source: ROL 31284–31287 (*Nero* 46: "Numquam antea somniare solitus ..."). LATER. Documented: Tertullian had read Suetonius, the life of Nero among his books, and names him twice. He never cites the line on the Christians (Do not say).

R5. **A Christian scholar took his book on writers as a model.** JER-L 46924–46933 (*De viris illustribus*, preface): "Hortaris me, Dexter, ut Tranquillum sequens, ecclesiasticos scriptores in ordinem digeram, et quod ille in enumerandis gentilium litterarum viris fecit illustribus, ego in nostris faciam". JER-E 38773–38779: "You have urged me, Dexter, to follow the example of Tranquillus in giving a systematic account of ecclesiastical writers, and to do for our writers what he did for the illustrious men of letters among the Gentiles". JEP-L 17442–17444 (Letter 47): "scripsi librum de inlustribus uiris ab apostolis usque ad nostram aetatem imitatus Tranquillum Graecumque Apollonium". JEP-E 9227–9234: "Following the example of Tranquillus and of Apollonius the Greek, I have written a book concerning illustrious men from the apostles' time to our own". JEP-E 46323–46325 (preface to his *Chronicle*): "From Troy to the twentieth year of Constantine there is much ... which I have gleaned with great diligence from Tranquillus and other famous historians." LATER (Jerome, to Theodosius's fourteenth year, JER-E 38784–38785). Documented. "Tranquillus" is Suetonius's third name (ROL 149; JER-E 38775 note). It is his lost *On Illustrious Men* (ROL 262–266) that Jerome copied as a model, not the two lines.

R6. **Editors on his wider reach.** ROL 365–368: "The historical writers, such as Eutropius, Aurelius Victor, and Orosius drew on him freely". ROL 377–392: "His influence extended to the Christian writers and is seen in the form of the Life of Ambrosius by his secretary Paulinus". PAU 2758–2760 (Kaniecka): the Life of Ambrose "closely resembles the literary form of the Alexandrian biography, which is best seen in the Lives of Suetonius". MODERN claims. Card only.

R7. **Historians print the lines as evidence.** MIR 141–151, 2500–2506, 2566–2572: Mirbt's source book on the papacy opens with the Claudius line as document 1 ("Das Vorgehen des Kaisers Klaudius gegen die Juden in Rom"), with Dio as 2, Tacitus as 3, the Nero line as 4. SCH 24311–24312, 24348–24349: both under "Zeugnisse" (testimonies). SCH 24275–24278: "Schon unter Claudius (41—54) führten Unruhen in der Judenschaft Roms, die mit dem eindringenden Christentum zusammenhingen, zur Einmischung des Staats. Juden und Christen sind dabei aber nicht unterschieden worden." DUC 2051–2063 (the line quoted; "Il y avait donc eu des troubles causés par la prédication de l'Evangile"; "c'est donc en 51 ou 52"; "le premier fait connu, la première date assignable" of the Roman church). BELL 1877–1878: "his expulsion of them from Rome (Suetonius, 25; Acts, xviii, 2; cf. Dio, lx, 6, 6)". THF 19535–19538 (note 335): "How little even the well-informed Suetonius knew of the real facts, we shall find in the only direct notice of the Christians contained in his works (CLAUDIUS c. xxv., NERO, c. xvi.)". MODERN, each with claims of its own. Documented that they print and weigh the lines; every reading (Christian preaching, 51 or 52) is theirs. Card only. The held historians stop at 1924.

R8. **A Reformation note.** FOXE 51576–51579 (Foxe's margin to John Philpot's examination): "it is to be proved by Orosius, Suetonius, Tertullian, and other stories, that the faith of Christ was in Rome in Tiberius's time, before Peter ever saw Rome." ## 5. Where sources disagree, or come late

D1. **Who was Chrestus?** Suetonius does not say (E1). ROL 25871–25874 (Rolfe): "Another form of Christus ... It is uncertain whether Suetonius is guilty of an error in chronology or is referring to some Jew of that name. The former seems probable because of the absence of quodam." THF 20362–20374 (note 528): "the Christians were obscurely alluded to ... The antagonism between Christianity and Judaism appears to have given rise to the tumults ... transplanting Christ from Jerusalem to Rome, and placing him in the time of Claudius". THF 20380–20382: "It was very natural for Suetonius to write Chrestus instead of Christus, as the former was a name in use among the Greeks and Romans." SCH 24275–24278 and DUC 2051–2060, 3889–3892 ("à propos d'un certain Chrestus"): unrest as Christianity reached Rome's Jews. RUI 3127–3128: the "Jews" included Christians. MODERN and LATE READER. Contested. What the old sources do hold: Romans did say "Chrestianus" and "Chrestus" for Christians and Christ (R3); and Chrestus was a real personal name (EUS 50626–50627, a bishop of Syracuse; D2).

D2. **Chrestus as a name.** EUS 50626–50627: "Constantine Augustus to Chrestus". THF 20381–20382: "Cicero mentions a person of that name" (MODERN report; Cicero's letter not held). LATER. Documented that the name existed.

## 6. Explain it: things and acts the story could name

X1. **The Lives of the Caesars.** What it is: lives of twelve rulers of Rome, Julius Caesar to Domitian (ROL 323–324); each life sets out the ruler's acts in groups (E2, E3, E4). Plain: "his lives of Rome's rulers" or "his lives of the emperors". From the Christian side: Tertullian reads it for facts about Nero (R4).

X2. **The expulsion under Claudius.** The act: he drove Jews out of Rome. The reason Suetonius gives: they "kept rioting at the urging of Chrestus" (E1). Acts' version: a command for "all the Jews to depart from Rome", given as the reason a Jewish couple had come to Corinth (D6). Dio's: no expulsion, a ban on gathering (D3, card). Ruinart's: the "Jews" included Christians (R2, his reading).

X3. **Chrestus.** The name as Suetonius wrote it. Plain: a name close in sound to Christus, the Latin form of Christ (ROL 25871; LAC-L 16813–16814: Romans called him "Chrestus" "by the change of a letter"). Chrestus was also a real name (D2). Who is meant: Contested (D1).

X4. **Christus / Christ, from the Christian side.** Lactantius: "Christ is not a proper name, but a title of power and dominion; for by this the Jews were accustomed to call their kings" (LAC-E 3163; LAC-L 16770–16772). Tertullian: "Christian" comes "de unctione", from anointing (TERT-C 3903–3904).

X5. **"Chrestian" (Chrestianus).** The Romans' form of "Christian", as Tertullian heard it (R3). Its sense, by Tertullian: "from sweetness or goodness" (TERT-C 3906). Plain sense to give: the Greek word *chrestos* means good or kind (LAC-E 3165, ANF note; TERT-E 9208–9210 note: "Χρηστός means both 'pleasant' and 'good'"). MODERN for the Greek gloss; Tertullian's own words carry it.

X6. **The punishment under Nero.** The act: "punishments were inflicted on the Christians" (E3). Reason: Suetonius gives only what they were, in his eyes, "a kind of people holding a new and harmful superstition", and files it among "abuses ... severely punished and put down" (ROL 28516–28517) and among acts "beyond criticism" (E4). No charge, kind or number. From the Christian side: Ruinart read it as an edict (R2); Tertullian's *Apology* 5 (RUI 3159–3160 quotes it: "quo tali dedicatore damnationis nostrae gloriamur") glories in Nero as the first to condemn them, but names no Suetonius. The sibling tells the deaths.

X7. **superstition (superstitio).** Suetonius's word, here contempt: a religion he counts new and harmful (E3). Plain: "superstition, his word for a religion he despised". THF 20568–20575 (note 577) sets beside it Tacitus's and Pliny's words. Do not borrow those.

X8. **malefica.** Rolfe "mischievous" (the modern sense, playful, would mislead), THF "impious"; THF note 577 (20568–20569): "conveying the idea of witchcraft or enchantment" (MODERN, Inferential-Thin as his meaning). Plain: "harmful".

X9. **The lists around each line.** Claudius: tax freedom for Ilium, "the founders of the Roman race" (old Troy, in Roman legend); theatre seats for German envoys (E2). Nero: a limit on spending; public dinners cut to handouts; taverns barred from selling cooked food but beans and greens; then the Christians; then the chariot drivers stopped from roaming and "cheating and robbing the people" for fun; the pantomime actors banished (E3). Known words; "pantomime actors" is plain enough.

X10. **Acts of the Apostles.** The New Testament book; Luke, by Bede's naming (R1). Plain: "the Acts of the Apostles".

X11. **To the Nations (Ad nationes).** Tertullian's book addressed to the pagans, against their charges (TERT-E 9200–9212, the chapter on the name). Plain: "his appeal to the pagans".

X12. **Divine Institutes.** Lactantius's work setting out the faith (LAC-E). Plain: "a book setting out the faith". If named at all.

X13. **Jerome's Lives of Illustrious Men.** "A systematic account of ecclesiastical writers" (JER-E 38777–38778). Plain: "short lives of Christian writers".

X14. **Bede's chronicle.** A history of the world year by year (BEDE header; §287 is one entry). Plain: "his chronicle of the world".

X15. **Ruinart's book, and Dodwell's.** Ruinart collected the acts of the martyrs (RUI header); his preface answers Dodwell's dissertation "on the fewness of martyrs", 1684 (RUI 2295–2300). Plain: "a monk who gathered the trials of the martyrs, answering an English scholar who said the martyrs had been few". Dodwell's Englishness: "Oxonii editae" (printed at Oxford), RUI 2297: Documented only that it was printed at Oxford. Say "a scholar at Oxford".

X16. **His post and dismissal.** "epistularum magister", master of the letters: ran the emperor's correspondence (S4; ROL 233, note "Ab epistulis"). Reason for the dismissal: Spartianus's, S4. Plain: "he ran the emperor Hadrian's letters, until Hadrian dismissed him".

N3. Dio's Greek in a clean print (D3).
N4. Orosius's *History*, which Bede's margin names as his source (R1) and Ruinart quotes on Nero (RUI 3166–3169). Not held as a text.
