# Outline: Suetonius

Every line cites `Source_Sheet.md` (S setting, E the two lines, R what came of them, D disagreement, X the explain-it list in §6, N not held), which gives each file and line. ROL = Rolfe's Latin and English (1914); TERT-C = Tertullian's Latin, CSEL 20 (1890).

**The heart**
- **The essence:** Suetonius, a Roman scholar who wrote the lives of Rome's rulers from Julius Caesar to Domitian, gives the first Christians two short lines in passing, each one item in a list of an emperor's acts: under Claudius, Jews were driven from Rome for rioting at the urging of "Chrestus"; under Nero, the Christians were punished as people of a new and harmful superstition. Later readers took that little up: a Christian chronicle copied the first line, and a monk read a law against the faith into the second; and Christians, without naming him, complained that Romans said "Chrestus" for Christ, the very name in his first line. (E1, E3, R1–R3: Documented as their words; S5: the span of the book, Documented.)
- **Also told:** What the lines say and leave out, as substance: each sits between ordinary measures (E2, E3); neither gives a year, a number or a source (E1, E3); he does not say who Chrestus was (D1); he files the punishment among Nero's acts that deserve no blame, some even praise (E4); he blames Nero for the fire in another chapter and there names no Christians (E5). Who he was, from his own words: an army officer's son, a young man under Domitian (S1), who later ran the emperor Hadrian's letters until Hadrian dismissed him (S4). What later readers did with the lines (R1–R4).
- **What came of him** (ranked under "What came of it" below). Nothing defended: he is an outside witness.

**Length:** about 3 minutes 20 seconds, about 500 words. The story must carry first: three quotes (about 58 words); the plain sense of the words to explain (Chrestus beside Christus, superstition, Chrestians and the Greek *chrestos*, old Troy, Acts, Tertullian's book, Bede's chronicle, Ruinart's book and his opponent; about 75); the witnesses credited (Acts, Spartianus for the dismissal's reason, Tertullian, Lactantius, Bede, Ruinart; about 40): about 175 words before the narration. Beats as planned run to about 585; the Cut first list brings them to 500. The record is two lines of seven and nine Latin words and a thin life (E1, E3, S1–S4), so shorter than the kit's aim would drop either the lines in their lists or the readers who used them, and longer would stretch the lines past what they say.

**Shape:** Outside witness. The record decides the order: his life in his own words, the Claudius line, the Nero line, then the readers in time order. Trap: making him a friend or a foe, or stretching two lines. He is brief and contemptuous; tell both, and say plainly how short the lines are (E1, E3).

**Openings**
- (a) Chosen. The line in its list (E1, E2). In his life of the emperor Claudius, a Roman writer sets down the emperor's acts one after another. Claudius freed the people of old Troy from tax for ever, as founders of the Roman race, and let German envoys sit with the senators at the theatre. Between those two comes one sentence: Q1. Then the name and the essence.
- (b) The Nero line in its list: spending limited, taverns allowed to sell only beans and greens, then the Christians, then the chariot drivers stopped from cheating people for fun (E3). Not chosen: told first, it would put Nero before Claudius and spend Q2 before its beat.

**Story beats** (the opening, the essence and beat 1 are the first minute)
1. **Opening and essence.** As above. Then: the writer was Suetonius, and in his lives of Rome's rulers this line, and one more under Nero, is all he says of the first Christians, if this one is about them at all (E1, E3, D1). Then the rest of the essence. Documented.
2. **Who he was, in his own words.** His father, in his own telling, was an army officer who fought in the emperor Otho's war (S1, *Otho* 10). No year. He remembers being a young man in Rome under Domitian (S1, *Domitian* 12, *Nero* 57). His lives run from Julius Caesar to Domitian, so he wrote after Domitian's death, a generation or more after both lines (S5, S6). He ran the emperor Hadrian's letters, until Hadrian dismissed him; a much later life of Hadrian says it was for behaving too familiarly toward the empress (S4: Documented as Spartianus's report, credited). Two sentences at most.
3. **The Claudius line.** He gives no year, no number and no source (E1). The Acts of the Apostles also tells of Claudius ordering the Jews out of Rome: Paul, at Corinth, met a Jew named Aquila and his wife, lately come from Italy because of that order (D6: Documented as Acts; the same event, Widely Accepted, not said by either text). Suetonius does not say who Chrestus was, or where he was. The name is close in sound to Christus, the Latin form of Christ, and was also a real name of the time and after (X3, D1, D2). Contested; the narrator does not settle it.
4. **The Nero line.** In his life of Nero, among measures by which, he says, abuses were punished and new rules made: a limit on spending, public dinners cut to handouts, taverns barred from selling cooked food except beans and greens; then Q2; then the chariot drivers stopped from roaming the streets and cheating people for fun (E3: Documented). He gives no number, no charge and no kind of punishment (E3). He sets these among Nero's acts that deserve no blame, some even praise, before he turns to Nero's crimes (E4: Documented; paraphrase). In another chapter he says Nero himself set Rome on fire; there he names no Christians (E5: Documented as his account). Another Roman, Tacitus, tells at length of Christians put to death under Nero (sibling; one clause).
5. **The readers, in time order** (each credited):
   - About 200, at Carthage, Tertullian had read Suetonius and names him twice, once for the detail that Nero never dreamed (R4: Documented). In his appeal to the pagans he says the name "Christian" comes from anointing, and then: Q3 (R3). Give the plain sense: *chrestos*, in Greek, means good or kind (X5). He does not name Suetonius.
   - A century later Lactantius wrote that the ignorant call Christ "Chrestus" by changing a letter (R3; narrator's words, no quote). Neither Christian names Suetonius (R3).
   - Near the end of the fourth century Jerome, writing short lives of Christian writers, was asked to follow the example of Tranquillus, another of Suetonius's names, and did for Christian writers what Suetonius had done for Rome's men of letters (R5: Documented; narrator's words). It was Suetonius's book on writers he followed, not the emperors' lives.
   - About 725, Bede, in his chronicle of the world, set down the Claudius line almost in Suetonius's words, without Chrestus. He put it in Claudius's ninth year and added that Luke also reports it (R1: Documented). The year is Bede's.
   - Around 1700 a French monk, Thierry Ruinart, who gathered the trials of the martyrs, answered a scholar at Oxford who argued the martyrs had been few. He used both lines. The Jews Claudius drove out, he wrote, included the Christians, for they too were then called Jews. And since Suetonius lists the punishment among the things Nero established, he had "no doubt" Nero wrote an edict against the Christians (R2: Documented as his reading; narrator's words around the two-word phrase). Suetonius names no law.
6. **Ending.** See Ending.

**What came of it** (ranked heaviest first; weight is what later witnesses carried on and used)
1. **A Christian chronicle carried the Claudius line on, in Suetonius's own words, and joined it to Acts** (R1). Beat 5, Bede.
2. **A later reader built on both lines, reading Christians into the first and a law into the second** (R2). Beat 5, Ruinart.
3. **Christians protested the very confusion the first line may show**: Romans said "Chrestians" and "Chrestus" (R3). Beat 5, Tertullian and Lactantius. Editors, not the Christians, tie this to Suetonius.
4. **A Christian read him, and another took his book on writers as a model** (R4, R5). Beat 5, Tertullian's reading and Jerome.
Nothing defended. No line on who keeps him today. The modern source books (R7) go to the card.

**Quotes** (three; none is in a live story on the tree or elsewhere in this batch; no reuse)

Rendered from the Latin: **"He drove out of Rome the Jews who kept rioting at the urging of Chrestus."** Job: the whole Claudius line, which the story opens on; the narrator then gives the plain sense of the name. The Latin has no "all", "one" or "since" (E1): do not add them.
- **Q2.** ROL 28458–28460 (Latin, Ihm's text, clean print; confirmed by MIR 2570, "adflicti", and SCH 24348–24349): "afflicti suppliciis Christiani, genus hominum superstitionis novae ac maleficae". Rendered from the Latin: **"Punishments were laid on the Christians, a kind of people holding a new and harmful superstition."** Job: the whole Nero line, his contempt in his own words, heard inside its list. - **Q3.** TERT-C 3904–3907 (Tertullian, *To the Nations* 1.3, Latin, CSEL 20, clean print; the same point in Oehler's damaged *Apology* 3, TERT-O 12453–12456, and in the 1885 English, TERT-E 9205–9208): "etiam cum corrupte a uobis Chrestiani pronuntiamur (nam ne nominis quidem ipsius liquido certi estis), sic quoque de suauitate uel bonitate modulatum est." **Connection to carry into the story:** What passed from Suetonius was two lines, and a puzzle in a name. The listener must hear three things to follow it. First, that he was not writing about Christians at all: each line is one item in a list of an emperor's acts, and he files Nero's punishment of them among the acts deserving no blame (E2, E3, E4). Second, that "Chrestus" is close in sound to Christus, and also a real name, and that he does not say who it was (D1, D2). Third, that later readers took the lines up and did things with them: Christians complained that Romans garbled their name (R3), Bede copied the first line into his chronicle and joined it to Acts (R1), and Ruinart read Christians into the first and an edict into the second (R2). Carried by these acts, never by a "this matters because" sentence.

**Ending:** (a) Chosen: the opening image seen again. Seven Latin words between old Troy's tax and the Germans' theatre seats, and nine between the taverns' beans and greens and the chariot drivers: that is all Suetonius set down, and readers have weighed each word since (E1, E3; the counts are Documented). Keep "readers have weighed" to what beat 5 told; no "still today". (b) Possible: Hadrian dismisses him, and the man drops from the record while the two lines remain (S4). Not chosen: it ends on his career, not on the lines.

**Do not say**
- Not "Claudius expelled the Jews because of Christ" or "because of the Christians". He says "Chrestus" (E1); who that was is not his to tell (D1).
- Not "Chrestus was a misspelling of Christ", "historians agree it means Christ", or "Chrestus was a Jewish agitator". Say the name is close in sound to Christus and was also a real name, and that he does not say who (D1, D2).
- Not that Chrestus was in Rome or led the riots in person. "At the urging of" is all (E1).
- Not "all the Jews", "one Chrestus" or "since" as Suetonius's words (E1, D4). "All" is in Acts.
- Not "in 49", "in 51" or any year for the expulsion in the narrator's voice. The ninth year is Bede's (D5, R1).
- Not that Acts and Suetonius say they describe the same event. Neither does; Bede joins them (D6, R1).
- Not "Nero blamed the Christians for the fire" or "punished them for the fire" as Suetonius's. He never joins the two (E5, D7).
- Not "Nero executed", "killed" or "burned" the Christians as Suetonius's words. He says punishments, and gives no kind, number or charge (E3).
- Not "Nero punished the Christians" with Nero as Suetonius's grammatical subject in a quote. The Latin is "punishments were laid", "under him" (E3). The narrator may say "under Nero".
- Not "Suetonius condemned Nero for it" or "praised Nero for it". He files it among acts deserving no blame, some even praise; say exactly that (E4).
- Not "mischievous", "impious", "witchcraft" or "magic" for *malefica*. Say "harmful" (X8).
- Not "the first", "the earliest" or "the only" outside mention of Christians or of Christ. Say "two short lines" (E1, E3).
- Not "Suetonius proves Jesus lived", or anything about Jesus beyond the name.
- Not that Tertullian or Lactantius answered, quoted or named Suetonius on the Christians. Tertullian names him on other matters (R4); neither names him on the name (R3).
- Not that Tertullian read the line on the Christians. He cites Suetonius on the games and on Nero's dreams only (R4).
- Not that Jerome modelled his book on the lives of the emperors. It was the book on writers (R5).
- Not that Bede named Suetonius or Chrestus. He names Luke, and drops Chrestus (R1).
- Not that a law or edict against Christians existed under Nero. It is Ruinart's "no doubt" (R2).
- Not "Ruinart proved" or "Ruinart showed". He argued (R2).
- Not "born in 69" or "77", "born in Rome", "published in 120" or "dismissed in 121" (S2, S4, S5). Not "senator", "lawyer" or "schoolmaster" (S3).
- Not the dismissal's reason as fact, or anything about an affair. Credit the much later life of Hadrian, and keep to "behaving too familiarly toward the empress" (S4, D8).
- Not "his friend Pliny" in the audio unless the outline check allows it (N1). Never that Suetonius knew of Pliny's trials of Christians.
- Not "Suetonius wrote little about great events" as fact: that is Rolfe's reading (S5). Show the lists instead.
- Not "historians still rely on them today" or "still argue today". The held readers stop at 1924 (R7).
- Not a sentence that only says what is missing. Each gap goes inside a sentence about what he does (Kit, Craft).
- Any word in Words to explain used without its plain sense the first time, "Chrestus", "Chrestians" and "superstition" among them.
