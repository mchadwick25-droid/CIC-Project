In the year 70, during the Jewish War, Roman soldiers set fire to the Temple in Jerusalem, the sanctuary of the Jews, against their commander's wish. Josephus, a Jewish commander who had surrendered to the Romans and was now with their army, wrote that the starving in the city broke out in groans and cries. "Perea sent back the echo, and so did the mountains all around, and made the whole noise louder."

Perea was the land across the Jordan. Eusebius, a church historian writing over two centuries later, says that Jerusalem's Christians had already gone there, to a town called Pella, warned by a revelation before the war.

In the two and a half centuries that followed, the churches the apostles left grew in the cities of the Roman empire and past its eastern edge. Letters linked them, and by the late second century so did meetings of a region's bishops. Under an emperor's ruling, a Christian who was accused and would not deny the name of Christian could be punished. In the 200s emperors began to give orders against them across the whole empire, until in 311 the emperors let them be Christians again.

That ruling came in the second century. Christians were not to be hunted out, the emperor said, though those who were accused and found must be punished. So, Eusebius writes, there was no great persecution, only local ones in one province or another, stirred up by crowds or by governors. In 177 the crowds at Lyon, in Gaul, rose against them.

About the year 200, at Carthage in Roman Africa, Tertullian boasted to the governors who judged Christians that his people were now in every part of Roman life. "We have filled everything of yours: cities, islands, forts, towns, meeting places, the very camps, the citizen tribes, the jury panels, the palace, the senate, the forum. We have left you only the temples." It was a boast made to men who could have them killed, to show that Christians were many and had never risen in arms.

The bishops' meetings by then reached from Gaul to Mesopotamia, and a book from Edessa, a city with its own king on Rome's eastern edge, speaks of Christians in Persia. By the early 300s, as historians now map it, Christians were thickest in Asia Minor, today's Turkey, and at Edessa, then around Antioch, in Egypt, at Rome and in Roman Africa. They were fewest in northern Gaul and along the Rhine.

In the 200s the empire itself shook, with war on the Danube and with Persia. In 250 the emperor Decius ordered people across the empire to offer at the gods' altars, and those who would not were punished. Eusebius blames his hatred of the emperor before him, while historians since have seen a wish to bring back the old religion. At Carthage many ran to sacrifice, and others bought a paper saying they had. Christians who gave way like this were called the fallen. Decius himself was killed fighting invaders on the Danube.

A later emperor turned on them again, and his officers banned their meetings and their burial grounds. Then the Persians took him captive, and his son stopped the hunt and wrote to the bishops themselves, giving back their places of worship. War, famine and plague followed. In the years after that, Eusebius writes, Christians built large new churches in every city.

By then Diocletian shared the rule of the empire with three other emperors. In 303 he ordered that the churches be leveled and the Scriptures burned, that Christians of rank be stripped of it, and that servants who held to the faith lose their freedom. Then the prisons filled with bishops, priests and deacons, and those who would not sacrifice were tortured. The emperors' own reason, given later in their own words, was to bring the Christians back to their ancestors' ways, since they had made laws for themselves and met apart. The hunt weighed less in the West.

Galerius, whom Eusebius blames for the whole persecution, fell sick, and in 311 an order went out in his name and his fellow emperors'. They set one condition, that the Christians keep good order, and gave their purpose in a single line. "So they may be Christians again, and may set up the houses where they used to meet." In return, the order said, they must pray to their own God for the emperors' safety.

The next year Constantine defeated his rival at Rome, when a bridge of boats broke. The age ended with its questions still open. The churches were already arguing over which teachers and books carried what the apostles handed down. They argued, too, over whether those who gave way could come back, and who should decide, and over how to speak of the Son beside the Father. And the order of 311 put a new question to them: what they owed an emperor who no longer hunted them, and who wanted their prayers.
