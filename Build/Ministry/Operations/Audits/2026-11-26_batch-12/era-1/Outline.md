# Outline: Era 1, The Early Church Era (70 to 312)

Kind: era (`kinds/era.md`). Every line points to `Source_Sheet.md` (row ids) and through it to file and line. Short names of files as on the sheet.

## The heart

- **The essence:** In the two and a half centuries after Rome burned the Temple in Jerusalem in 70, the churches the apostles left grew in the cities of the Roman empire and past its eastern edge, each under its bishop and linked by letters and meetings. Roman law let an accused Christian be punished for the name alone; in the 200s emperors began ordering it across the whole empire, until in 311 the emperors let them be Christians again. (S1, E1–E2, E2a, E4, E6, E8, E10–E12, E23, E28; "for the name alone": Pliny's question and practice, EUS 19468–19476, and Trajan's answer, E1; Documented, the regional picture Dominant Modern Reconstruction.)
- **What was happening to the church:** local trials and crowds under Trajan's ruling (E1–E3); bishops meeting in synods across the regions (E4); the first empire-wide order to sacrifice under Decius, and the fallen who gave way (E10–E12); a second hunt under Valerian, ended by his son, who gave the bishops back their places of worship (E14–E16); a long quiet with large new churches in every city (E20); the Great Persecution from 303 (E23–E25); the edict of 311 (E28).
- **What was happening in the world:** the Jewish War and the fall of Jerusalem (S1–S2); in the 200s, war on the Danube and with Persia, an emperor killed by invaders and another taken captive (E13, E16, E18); war, famine and plague (E17); four emperors sharing the empire under Diocletian (E21); civil war ended at Rome's bridge in 312 (E29).
- **Where:** from Gaul to Mesopotamia and Persia (E4, E6). By the early 300s Christians were thickest in Asia Minor (today's Turkey) and Edessa, then around Antioch, in Egypt, at Rome and in Roman Africa, and fewest in northern Gaul and along the Rhine (E8; DMR). The era's traditions belong to these regions: Rome, Carthage, Alexandria, Antioch, Asia Minor, Roman Palestine, Edessa, the Danube.
- **Setting the stage:** the questions the age left open, which the traditions answered in their own ways: who may come back after giving way, and who decides (Impact 1); what Christians owe an emperor who no longer hunts them (Impact 2); how to speak of the Son beside the Father (Impact 3); which teachers and books carry what the apostles handed down (Impact 4).

## Openings

1. **(Chosen) The fire heard across the Jordan, 70.** Roman soldiers set fire to the Temple in Jerusalem, and it burned down (S1). Why, as Josephus gives it: the soldiers' hatred of the Jews and hope of plunder, against their commander's wish (JW 126002–126020); a clause, or the bare act if the opening runs long. Josephus, a Jewish commander who had surrendered to the Romans and was now with their army, wrote what was heard that day: **Quote 1** (S2). Perea was the land across the Jordan, and Eusebius, writing over two centuries later, says Jerusalem's Christians had already gone there, to a town called Pella, warned by a revelation before the war (S3, S4; the revelation in his voice). Then the essence.
2. **(Possible) Tertullian's boast, about 200.** Quote 2 as the opening, then the essence, and the story told from the Temple on. Not chosen: it opens mid-age, and the story would go back in time.

## Story beats (after the opening and the essence, in time order)

1. **The ruling, and the crowds (second century).** The emperor Trajan ruled that Christians were not to be hunted out, but those accused and found must be punished (E1; Documented). So, Eusebius writes, there was no great persecution, but local ones in one province or another, stirred by crowds or by governors (E2; his summary). In 177 at Lyon, in Gaul, the city crowds rose against the Christians (E3; Documented). Explain it: the reason for punishment is the ruling itself, refusing to deny the name (E1; sheet §5); Lyon's, the crowds' rising (E3). Confidence: Documented.
2. **Spread, about 200.** At Carthage, in Roman Africa, Tertullian told the governors who judged Christians how far they had spread: **Quote 2** (E5). It was a boast to men who could have them killed, made to show they were many and had never risen in arms (TERT-E 3790–3797, the sentences before it). Bishops by then met in synods, meetings of a region's bishops, from Gaul and Rome to Pontus on the Black Sea and Osrhoene in Mesopotamia (E4); a book from Edessa, a city on Rome's eastern edge, speaks of Christians in Persia too (E6). By the early 300s, as historians now map it, they were thickest in Asia Minor and Edessa, then around Antioch, in Egypt, at Rome and in Roman Africa, and fewest in northern Gaul and along the Rhine (E8; DMR; no numbers, D5). Confidence: E4–E6 Documented as their claims; E8 DMR.
3. **The empire shakes; Decius (250).** In the 200s the empire fought invaders on the Danube and the Persians in the east (E13, E16, E18). In 250 the emperor Decius ordered sacrifice to the gods across the empire (E10, E12a; the edict's text is lost, E12a). Eusebius blames his hatred of the emperor before him; historians since have seen a wish to bring back the old religion (E10a; Explain it). At Alexandria, its bishop wrote, people were called by name to the altars, and some went pale and trembling (E11); at Carthage many ran to sacrifice, and others bought a paper saying they had (E12). Those who gave way were the fallen (Words to explain). Decius was killed fighting invaders on the Danube (E13; D3: "invaders", not "Goths" or "Carpi"). Confidence: Documented; the empire-wide reach Widely Accepted (E2a, E12a).
4. **Valerian, and his son (250s–260).** A later emperor, friendly at first, turned on them, persuaded, the bishop of Alexandria wrote, by an adviser who led Egypt's magicians (E15, E15a). His officers banned their meetings and their burial grounds (E14), and an order to the senate had bishops, presbyters and deacons put to death at once (E14a; the deaths at Carthage and Rome are the siblings', told only as this fact). Then the Persians took him captive, and his son stopped the hunt and wrote to the bishops themselves, giving back their places of worship (E16; Documented). War, famine and plague followed (E17; the nursing of the sick is the sibling's). Confidence: Documented.
5. **The long quiet (to 303).** In the decades that followed, Eusebius writes, Christians governed provinces without being made to sacrifice, served in the palace, and built large new churches in every city, because the old buildings were too small (E20; Documented as his account; no count of years: Valerian's capture rests on editors' dates, E16). Confidence: Documented.
6. **The Great Persecution (303–311).** Diocletian, who shared the empire with three other emperors (E21), ordered in 303 that the churches be leveled and the Scriptures burned, Christians of rank be stripped of it, and servants who held to the faith lose their freedom (E23; month and day differ, D1, not told). Then the prisons filled with bishops, presbyters and deacons, and those who would not sacrifice were tortured (E25). Explain it: the emperors' own reason, given in their edict of 311, was to bring the Christians back to their ancestors' ways, since they had made laws for themselves and met apart (E28, EUS 45615–45620; DMP 10482–10484). It weighed less in the West (E26; the two old sources differ, D2: say only "less", or leave it out). Confidence: Documented.
7. **The edict of 311.** Galerius, whom Eusebius blames for the whole persecution (E27; blame in Eusebius's voice), lay sick, and an order went out in his name and his fellow emperors' (E28). It said many had died and many still held out (E28, EUS 45627–45629). **Quote 3.** In return, it said, they must pray to their own God for the emperors' safety (E28, EUS 45669–45671). Confidence: Documented.

## What came of it (ranked heaviest first)

For an era, the questions it left, which the traditions answered (sheet §3):
1. Who may come back after giving way, and who decides: asked after Decius and again after 303 (Impact 1). **Defended**, in opposite ways, by the councils of 251 and by the stricter church that broke away (siblings).
2. What Christians owe an emperor who stops hunting them: the edict asks for prayer (E28); an emperor had already judged a church house at Antioch (E19); Constantine follows (E29).
3. How to speak of the Son beside the Father (Impact 3; siblings).
4. Which teachers and books carry what the apostles handed down (Impact 4; E4; siblings).

Only, they must do nothing against good order."** (42 words; the Greek is one sentence, split at its clauses: ἵνα, "so", and οὕτως ὥστε, "only … must", the condition.)

## Connection to carry into the story

What passed from this age to every tradition in it: a church of many cities, held together by bishops, letters and meetings of bishops (E4), which had lived for two and a half centuries under a law that could kill it, and in its last sixty years had been hunted across the empire on emperors' orders (E10–E12, E14–E14a, E23–E25), and then, in 311, was told it might exist again (E28). The listener must hear three things to follow it: that the danger was local for most of the age and empire-wide only at its end (E2, E2a); that each empire-wide hunt left people who had given way, and the churches had to decide whether and how to take them back (E11–E12; Impact 1); and that the age ends with emperors asking Christians for their prayers (E28), so the next question is what the church owes such an emperor (Impact 2). Carried by these acts and by Quote 3, never by a "this matters because" sentence.

## Ending

1. **(Chosen) The age's questions, after Constantine's victory.** After Quote 3 and the prayer it asked for, the next year Constantine defeated his rival at Rome when a bridge of boats broke (E29; one clause, no wonder). The age ended with its questions open, each told as what people were already arguing over, in plain words and in time order of their asking: whether those who gave way could come back, and who should decide (Impact 1); how to speak of the Son beside the Father (Impact 3); and what the churches owed an emperor who no longer hunted them and wanted their prayers (Impact 2, E28). No summing-up, no moral; it stops on the last question.
2. **(Possible) The edict as the close.** End on Quote 3 and the prayer, with the questions told before it. Not chosen: the questions are the era's job, and Quote 3 would end on the emperors, not the age.
