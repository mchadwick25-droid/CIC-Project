In the year 70, Roman soldiers set fire to the Temple in Jerusalem, the sanctuary of the Jews. Josephus, a Jewish commander who had surrendered to the Romans and was now with their army, wrote what was heard that day. The starving in the city broke out in groans and cries, and then "Perea sent back the echo, and so did the mountains all around, and made the whole noise louder."

Perea was the land across the Jordan. Eusebius, a church historian writing over two centuries later, says Jerusalem's Christians had already gone there, to a town called Pella, warned by a revelation before the war.

In the two and a half centuries that followed, the churches the apostles left grew in the cities of the Roman empire and past its eastern edge. Letters linked them, and by the late second century so did meetings of their bishops, from Gaul to Mesopotamia. Under an emperor's ruling, a Christian who was accused and would not deny the name could be punished. In the 200s emperors began to hunt them across the whole empire, until in 311 the emperors let them be Christians again.

The same ruling, in the second century, also said Christians were not to be hunted out. So, Eusebius writes, there was no great persecution, only local ones in one province or another, stirred by crowds or by governors. In 177 the crowds at Lyon, in Gaul, rose against them.

About the year 200, Tertullian, at Carthage in Roman Africa, boasted to the governors who judged Christians that they had filled every part of Roman life and left the governors only their temples. He made it to men who could have them killed, to show they were many and had never risen in arms. A book from Edessa, a city on Rome's eastern edge, speaks of Christians in Persia. By the early 300s, as historians now map it, Christians were thickest in Asia Minor, today's Turkey, and at Edessa. Next came Antioch and its region, Egypt, Rome and Roman Africa, and they were fewest in northern Gaul and along the Rhine. The age's traditions belong to these regions, and to Roman Palestine and the Danube.

In 250 the emperor Decius ordered sacrifice to the gods across the empire, and those who refused were punished. Eusebius blames his hatred of the emperor before him, while historians since have seen a wish to bring back the old religion. At Carthage many ran to sacrifice, and others bought a paper saying they had. Those who gave way were called the fallen. Decius was killed fighting invaders on the Danube.

A later emperor turned on them, and his officers banned their meetings and their burial grounds. Then the Persians took him captive, and his son stopped the hunt and wrote to the bishops themselves, giving back their places of worship. War, famine and plague followed. Afterwards, Eusebius writes, Christians built large new churches in every city.

Later, Diocletian shared the rule of the empire with three other emperors. In 303 he ordered that the churches be leveled and the Scriptures burned, that Christians of rank be stripped of it, and that servants who held to the faith lose their freedom. The prisons filled with bishops, priests and deacons, and those who would not sacrifice were tortured. The emperors' reason, given later, was to bring the Christians back to their ancestors' ways, since they had made laws for themselves and met apart.

In 311 Galerius, one of those emperors, lay sick, and an order went out in his name and his fellow emperors'. If the Christians kept good order, it said, the emperors would let them be, "So they may be Christians again, and may set up the houses where they used to meet." In return, it said, they must pray to their own God for the emperors' safety.

The next year, Constantine ended a civil war by defeating his rival at Rome, when a bridge of boats broke. The churches were already arguing over which teachers and books carried what the apostles had handed down. They argued over whether those who gave way could come back, and who should decide, and over how to speak of the Son beside the Father. And the order of 311 had put a new question to them: what they owed an emperor who no longer hunted them, and wanted their prayers.
