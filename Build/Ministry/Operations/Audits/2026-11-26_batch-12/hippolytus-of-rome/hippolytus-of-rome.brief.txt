A teacher from Smyrna named Noetus said that Christ was the Father himself, so that the Father was born, suffered and died. Twice the elders called him before the church, where he asked what harm he was doing by glorifying Christ, and they put him out.

Hippolytus, who tells that story, taught in the church at Rome in the early 200s and wrote in Greek. Against Noetus's teaching he held that the Son was another beside the Father, yet one God with him. He would not give way on this, or on how far the church could forgive, to two bishops of Rome, Zephyrinus and Callistus. He set the quarrel down in a book against every teaching he held false. In 235 he was sent to Sardinia with Rome's bishop, and Rome kept his day among its martyrs.

In Hippolytus's telling, the only account of the quarrel that survives, Zephyrinus let Noetus's teaching spread at Rome, with Callistus as his adviser. He and his side stood against them for the truth.

He called the Son the Word, a name from John's gospel for the Son as he was with the Father before he became man: "When I say 'another,' I do not mean two Gods. I mean it as light from light, or water from a spring, or a ray from the sun. For there is one power, which comes from the whole. The whole is the Father, and from him comes the power, the Word."

When Zephyrinus died, Callistus took his place and, Hippolytus says, called his side worshippers of two gods, because they spoke of the Father and the Son as two. Hippolytus answered that he spoke of one God, in two persons, with the grace of the Holy Spirit as a third.

Hippolytus charges that Callistus forgave every sin of the body to anyone who came over to him, and held that a bishop should not be removed even for the gravest sin. Some whom Hippolytus's own side had judged and put out of the church went over to Callistus.

His book, the Refutation of All Heresies, traced the teachings he held false back to Greek philosophers, mystery cults and astrologers. Its last part turned to the teachings of his own day, Callistus's among them. In its opening he wrote that the apostles had received the Holy Spirit and handed it on to those who believed rightly: "We are their successors. We share in the same grace, in the high priesthood and in the teaching, and we are counted guardians of the church. We do not let our eyes droop, and we do not keep the right word silent." By the high priesthood he meant the church's chief priestly office.

He drew up a table, on a cycle of sixteen years, for finding the day of Easter, and Jerome says it gave Eusebius the occasion for his own nineteen-year cycle. He wrote many other books, and Eusebius says many people kept them.

In 235, Rome's list of bishops records, Pontianus, the bishop, and Hippolytus, whom it calls an elder, one of the priests who led the church with its bishop, were deported to Sardinia. Rome's calendar of martyrs kept them both on 13 August. In the East, Eusebius wrote that Hippolytus had led another church, without saying which, and later bishops there quoted him for the faith and called him bishop and martyr.

More than a century later, Damasus, bishop of Rome, set a verse on his tomb on the Tiburtine Road, telling what he had heard. Hippolytus, an elder, it was said, had always stayed in the split of Novatus while a tyrant's orders pressed. Damasus means Novatian, whose party split from the church at Rome in 251, refusing to take back those who had given way in persecution. As Hippolytus went to the kingdom of the faithful, the people asked where to go, and he told them all to follow the catholic faith, the faith of the whole church. So, the verse says, he earned his place as a martyr.
