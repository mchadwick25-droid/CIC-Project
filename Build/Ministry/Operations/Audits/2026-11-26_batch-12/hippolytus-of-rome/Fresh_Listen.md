## 1. The test (person kit)

- **Who:** filled. "Hippolytus ... taught in the church at Rome in the early 200s and wrote in Greek."
- **What happened:** filled. He opposed two bishops, wrote a book against them, was "sent to Sardinia with Rome's bishop" in 235, and Rome kept his day among its martyrs.
- **What it was about:** filled. Whether Christ is "the Father himself" or "another beside the Father, yet one God with him", and "how far the church could forgive", which the Callistus paragraph makes concrete.
- **Why the connection matters:** filled, but weak at the end. His idea of the Word and his place among Rome's martyrs come through. The tie to Novatian (the Damasus verse) is hard to follow on one hearing; see item 3.

## 2. The opening

After three sentences (Noetus; the elders put him out; "Hippolytus, who tells that story..."), I have who (Hippolytus), where (Rome), when (the early 200s) and what language he wrote in. Missing: **why there**. I don't yet know why a quarrel with Noetus opens Hippolytus's story. The fourth sentence ("Against Noetus's teaching he held...") answers it at once, so I was never lost. Not blocking.

## 3. Sentences that fail on one hearing (all MINOR)

1. "For there is one power, which comes from the whole. The whole is the Father, and from him comes the power, the Word." What gets lost: what "the whole" and "power" mean. The three images just before it (light, spring, ray) carry the idea. These two lines do not. They are in the quote, so I only note them.
2. "He imagined someone asking whether he spoke of two Gods, and answered that he spoke of one God, in two persons, with the grace of the Holy Spirit as a third." What gets lost: "persons" in this sense, and a third *what*. With no church background, "Holy Spirit" is new here and never explained.
3. "In the East, Eusebius wrote that Hippolytus had led another church, without saying which, and a later bishop there quoted him for the faith and called him bishop and martyr." What gets lost: whether "another church" means a church in another city or a rival church in Rome, where "there" is, and what "quoted him for the faith" means.
4. "Hippolytus, an elder, it was said, had always stayed in the split of Novatus while a tyrant's orders pressed." What gets lost: who the "tyrant" is (I guess a ruler who persecuted), and what "stayed in the split" means. The split only gets explained in the next sentence.
5. "Novatus is his name for Novatian, whose party split from the church at Rome in 251, sixteen years after the exile, ..." What gets lost: the dates. How could Hippolytus stay in a split that began sixteen years after he was sent away? The sentence lets me notice this, but nothing tells me what to make of it. This is where the story's tie to Novatian should become clear, and it blurs. MINOR, but it is the closest thing in the story to losing the thread.
6. "As Hippolytus went to the kingdom of the faithful, the people asked where to go, ..." What gets lost: whether "went to the kingdom of the faithful" means he was dying or travelling. I took it as dying, but only on a guess.

A related puzzle: the story calls him a martyr twice, but never says how or where he died. It only says he was exiled. I note this as a listener's question; the trust read decides whether the sources say more.

## 4. Bare statements (Part 1, Explain it)

- **BLOCKING:** "He drew up a table, on a cycle of sixteen years, for finding the day of Easter, and Jerome says it gave Eusebius the occasion for his own nineteen-year cycle." The story says what the table was, but not why it mattered to the people at the time. A listener with no church background does not know that Easter falls on a different day each year, or why churches needed a table to agree on it. The table also comes with nothing linking it to the quarrel, so it sounds like a list item.
  **Exact change:** after "for finding the day of Easter," add the plain reason the outline gives for it (for example, that Easter's day moved each year and churches had to work it out ahead), worded only as the outline holds it. The trust reader confirms the wording against the outline and source. If the outline holds no reason, move the sentence to the card instead of adding one.
- Checked and not counted: "His book, the Refutation of All Heresies, traced the teachings he held false back to Greek philosophers, mystery cults and astrologers." The story has already said what the book is ("a book against every teaching he held false"), and the quote ("We are their successors ... guardians of the church") shows why it mattered to him. This line passes.

None. "When I say 'another,' I do not mean two Gods. I mean it as light from light ..." is Hippolytus's own words, not a narrator's "not X but Y" turn. The imagined question is his own move in the argument, reported, not a question the narrator asks. There are no dashes for drama, no stock words, and no moral close. The last line ("So, the verse says, he earned his place as a martyr.") is the verse's claim, credited to it.

## 8. Pull, drift, staying

- **Pull:** yes. Noetus asking "what harm he was doing by glorifying Christ" is a real human moment, and "Hippolytus, who tells that story" turns it neatly toward the subject.
- **First drift:** "For there is one power, which comes from the whole." The idea goes abstract right after three clear images, and I lost hold of it for a moment. A second drift came at the Easter-table sentence, which arrives with no tie to the quarrel and no reason given.
- **To the end:** yes. The exile and the Damasus verse pulled me back, though the dates in the verse left me puzzled (item 3.5).
