Methodius of Olympus wrote a book called the Banquet. In it a young woman and two friends climb a rough, steep path to a garden where ten guests will meet. A tall woman in a robe white as snow embraces them like a mother, and her name means Virtue. They feast under a wide, shady tree, and she asks each guest to praise the virgin life.

The Banquet is a story within a story, in which one friend begs another to repeat the speeches. One guest says praising virginity, the unmarried life given to God, does not abolish marriage, which is still God's work. "The moon may be greater than the stars, but its light does not put out the light of the other stars." Virtue, speaking last, says it is no chastity to keep the body pure but not the tongue, the eyes, the hands or the mind.

Methodius was bishop of Olympus, a city in Lycia, in the south of Asia Minor. In a dialogue, On the Resurrection, he argued against Origen, the great teacher of Alexandria and Caesarea, that the body is no chain on the soul and that the same body will rise. At issue was what the body is to the soul.

Origen, Methodius wrote, held that the soul lived without a body before the fall, the first sin, and was given the body afterwards as a chain. At the resurrection, Origen held, the same flesh does not return, only each person's shape, stamped on a different, spiritual body. Methodius could not bear, he says, those who turn bones and flesh into symbols, so that the dead rise without flesh.

If the body were a chain to hold back evil, he answered, it had failed, since Cain committed murder in this body. As for what the body is to the soul, he held that a human being is soul and body joined in one. In his telling, a craftsman finds that an envious man has spoiled a beautiful statue he made, so he melts it down and casts it again. "For the statue cannot be lost while it is in the same craftsman's hands, even if it is melted down into its material again. It can be restored. But the blemishes and the damage can be lost, for they melt away. They cannot be restored." So, he wrote, God, seeing his finest work spoiled by envy, dissolves the body in death and remakes it in the resurrection.

Eusebius of Caesarea, in a book he wrote in Origen's defense, asked how Methodius dared to write against Origen. Jerome, who reports the question, says Methodius was crowned with martyrdom at Chalcis in Greece, at the end of the last persecution, though others, he adds, placed it under earlier emperors, Decius and Valerian.

Later in the fourth century, Epiphanius of Salamis wrote against Origen and chose to rest his case on On the Resurrection, setting out its words on the rising of the same body word for word.

At the Banquet's close, Virtue tells the guests to rise and stand under the tree. One guest begins a hymn, and the others answer her, each keeping herself for Christ, the Bridegroom.
