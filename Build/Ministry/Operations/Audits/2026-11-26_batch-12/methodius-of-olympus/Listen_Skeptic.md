## 1. The test (person kit)

- **Who he was:** filled. "Methodius was bishop of Olympus, a city in Lycia, in the south of Asia Minor."
- **What happened:** filled. He wrote the Banquet and On the Resurrection, argued with Origen, and Jerome says he died a martyr.
- **What it was about:** filled. Chastity praised without running down marriage. The body is no chain on the soul, and the same body rises (the statue).
- **Why the connection matters:** thin, not unfilled. The listener hears that Epiphanius rested his case against Origen on Methodius, and that Socrates called him the first of Origen's attackers. So the link to Origen is clear. What stays unclear is why it mattered to the people then whether the same body rises. The statue carries some of this, but no one in the story says what was at stake for them.

## 2. The opening

After sentences 1 to 3 the listener knows: a book called the Banquet, by a man called Methodius of Olympus, and its garden scene.

- **Missing:** who he was (no role), where Olympus is, when, and why there.
- P2's first sentence gives who and where at once, so the listener is not lost. Not blocking.
- **When is never said plainly anywhere in the story.** The listener has to work it out from "the end of the last persecution" (P8), "Decius and Valerian" (P8) and "Later in the fourth century" (P9). MINOR. Change: give the time in a clause in P2, in the wording the outline's Setting holds (for example "at the end of the age of persecution", if that is the outline's plain word).

## 3. Sentences that fail on one hearing

1. **BLOCKING.** P6: "If the body were a chain on evil, he answered, it had failed, since Cain committed murder in this body." Meaning lost: the reply hangs on the body being given to hold back sin. But P5 only said the body was given "as a chain", which the ear hears as a prison or a punishment. So "chain on evil" arrives as a new idea, and the reply's logic cannot be followed. This is the heart's argument. Change: in P5, say what the chain was for, as the outline gives it ("was given the body afterwards as a chain, to hold it back from sin"), only if the outline holds that wording. If it does not, cut "on evil" and tell the reply as the outline gives it.
2. **BLOCKING.** P8: "Eusebius of Caesarea, in a book he wrote in Origen's defense, asked how Methodius dared to write against Origen now, after what he had said of Origen's teachings." Meaning lost: what had he said, praise or attack? The ear guesses earlier praise. Then P10 says Methodius attacked first and praised later. The two lines seem to contradict each other, and the listener loses the thread of the quarrel, which is the story's question. Change: say what Methodius had said, as Jerome gives it, or cut "after what he had said of Origen's teachings".
3. MINOR. P5: "At the resurrection, Origen held, the same flesh does not return, only each person's shape, stamped on another, spiritual body." The comma after "another" makes it sound like a noun. Change: "stamped on a different, spiritual body".
4. MINOR. P5: "This account of Origen is kept in a summary a later reader made centuries on." Meaning lost: which account, Methodius's? Is the dialogue lost? Whose summary? See also 6 below.
5. MINOR. P8: "...though others, he adds, placed it under the emperors Decius and Valerian." The listener does not know these emperors ruled decades earlier, so the size of the disagreement is lost. Change: "placed it under earlier emperors, Decius and Valerian".
6. MINOR. P10: "More than a century later, the church historian Socrates..." It follows "a bishop of the next century", so the ear anchors it to that bishop and places Socrates still later. Change: "More than a century after Methodius, the church historian Socrates..."

## 4. Bare statements

1. **BLOCKING.** P9: "A bishop of the next century kept Methodius's words on martyrdom among earlier bishops' testimonies, in arguing that God was made man." The listener has never heard that Methodius wrote on martyrdom. Nothing says what those words said or what "testimonies" are. Nothing says what the argument that "God was made man" was against, or why his words helped it. Change: say in plain words what the passage said and what the argument was for, as the outline gives it, or cut the sentence. It is also the clearest thing the heart does not need at 671 words.
2. **BLOCKING.** P8, the Eusebius clause "after what he had said of Origen's teachings". It names something Methodius said without saying what it was. The change is the one in 3.2.

## 5. Citations read aloud

- MINOR. P5: "This account of Origen is kept in a summary a later reader made centuries on." This is a provenance note, said as a separate sentence. Narrator's-voice change: fold it into the sentence it qualifies. "In the part of the dialogue a later reader summed up centuries on, Methodius says Origen held that..." Then cut the stand-alone sentence.
- The checker's two lines (the Jerome credits in P2 and P8) are called for. The sources differ on when he died, and the martyrdom is a judgment that stays in its witness's voice. "Socrates alone tells of this change of heart" is also called for, since it rests on one late witness. Keep all three.

## 6. Tells

**Part 1 tells**
- **BLOCKING.** The stock how-we-know sentence: P5, "This account of Origen is kept in a summary a later reader made centuries on." Part 1 says to credit a witness "inside a sentence about that witness, never as a stock how-we-know sentence." Change: the one in section 5.
- None of the other tells: no "not X but Y" turn, no dash, no rhetorical question, no stock word, and no moral close. The story ends on the Banquet's hymn.

**Lines that sound machine-made**
1. Attribution tags dropped into the middle of sentences, seven in about three paragraphs: "Origen, Methodius wrote, held"; "At the resurrection, Origen held,"; "Methodius could not bear, he says,"; "If the body..., he answered,"; "A human being, he held,"; "So, he wrote, God,"; "though others, he adds,". Each one is defensible. Together they make a mechanical stop-start cadence that reads like hedging by attribution, the habit of a system tagging every clause to stay safe. For the reviser: where the speaker is already clear from the sentence before, tell the line plainly, keeping each credit the outline requires.
2. P2, the essence paragraph, is a stack of facts: bishop, Banquet, dialogue, later bishop, Jerome. Each sentence opens a new item, like an abstract. Its last sentence then tells, ahead of time, two things that P8 and P9 tell again. That is allowed for the essence, but the hook-summary-body template is audible.
3. P9 and P10, the reception run: "Later in the fourth century... A bishop of the next century... More than a century later..." This is a chronicle of who-used-him-next, the shape of a generated "influence" section. It does not use the word legacy, but it has the same cadence.
4. "The dialogue asks what the body is to the soul." (P5) An essay topic sentence that signposts the section rather than telling it. A small flag.
- The two "tall"s in P1 ("tall woman", "tall, shady tree") repeat, which slightly flattens a scene that is otherwise vivid.

## 8. Attention

- **Opening pull:** yes. The steep path, the woman in snow-white, and her name meaning Virtue form a real image. Saying "a book by" in the first clause keeps it honest.
- **First drift:** P5, "This account of Origen is kept in a summary...". The story stops to file a note, just as the argument is building. It drifts firmly at P8 to P9, where the Eusebius puzzle goes unanswered and the "words on martyrdom" sentence comes with nothing to hold it.
- **Would listen to the end:** yes. The Socrates quote ("Worthless people...") pulls the listener back, and the return to the garden closes well.
