A young woman and two friends climbed a rough, steep path to a garden where ten guests would meet. A tall woman in a robe white as snow met and embraced them like a mother. Her name meant Virtue; she was Philosophy's daughter. They feasted under a tall, shady tree, and she asked each guest to speak in praise of the virgin life.

This is the garden of the Banquet, a book by Methodius of Olympus, a bishop in Lycia, in the south of Asia Minor. In a dialogue, On the Resurrection, he argued against Origen, the great teacher of Alexandria and Caesarea, that the body is no chain on the soul and that the same body will rise. A later bishop copied his words into his own case against Origen, and the scholar Jerome says he died a martyr.

The book is a story within a story: one friend begs another to repeat the young women's speeches on chastity, the unmarried life given to God. One guest says that praising virginity does not abolish marriage, which is still God's work. She puts it in an image: "The moon may be greater than the stars, but its light does not put out the light of the other stars." At the close, Virtue says it is no chastity to keep the body pure but not the tongue, eyes, hands or mind.

The dialogue asks what the body is to the soul. Origen, Methodius wrote, held that the soul lived without a body before the fall, the first turning from God into sin, and was given the body afterwards as a chain, the cause of its sins. At the resurrection, he held, the same flesh does not return, only each person's shape, stamped on another, spiritual body. This account of Origen is kept in a summary a later reader made centuries on. Methodius could not bear, he says, those who turn bones and flesh into symbols to make the resurrection fleshless.

If the body was a chain against evil, Methodius answered, it failed, for Cain committed murder in this body. A human being is neither soul without body nor body without soul, but the two joined in one. Then he gave an image: a craftsman finds his finest statue spoiled by an envious man, so he melts it down and casts it again. "For the statue cannot be lost while it is in the same craftsman's hands, even if it is melted down into its material again. It can be restored. But the blemishes and the damage can be lost, for they melt away. They cannot be restored." So God, seeing his finest work spoiled by envy, dissolves the body in death and remakes it in the resurrection.

While Methodius lived, Jerome reports, Eusebius of Caesarea, in a book written in Origen's defense, asked how he dared to write against Origen now, after what he had said of Origen's teachings.

Methodius was crowned with martyrdom at Chalcis in Greece, Jerome says, at the end of the last persecution, though others, he adds, said under the emperors Decius and Valerian.

Later in the fourth century, Epiphanius of Salamis, writing against Origen, chose to rest his case on what the blessed Methodius had said in On the Resurrection, and set it out word for word. A bishop of the next century kept Methodius's words on martyrdom among the testimonies of earlier bishops, defending the faith that God was made man.

More than a century after Methodius, the church historian Socrates of Constantinople, who defended Origen, put Methodius first among four men who attacked Origen. "Worthless people, who cannot be noticed on their own, want to be seen by finding fault with their betters. Methodius was the first to fall into this." Socrates adds that Methodius, after railing against Origen in many places, took it all back, as it were, and praised him in a dialogue he called the Xenon.

At the book's end, Virtue told the guests to rise and stand under the tree. One guest began a hymn, and the rest, standing in a circle, answered her, keeping themselves for Christ, the Bridegroom.
