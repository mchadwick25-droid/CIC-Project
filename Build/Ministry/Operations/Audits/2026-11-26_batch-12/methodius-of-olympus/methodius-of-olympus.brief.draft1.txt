A young woman and two friends climb a rough, steep path to a garden. A tall woman in a robe white as snow meets them and embraces them like a mother, and her name means Virtue. Under a tall, shady tree whose Greek name sounds like the word for pure, she asks each of the ten guests to praise the virgin life.

This is the Banquet, a book by Methodius of Olympus, a bishop among the churches of Asia Minor, in Lycia in its south. It praises chastity, the unmarried life given to God. In a dialogue, On the Resurrection, he argued against Origen, the great teacher of Alexandria and Caesarea, that the body is no chain on the soul and that the same body will rise. A later bishop copied his words into his own case against Origen, and Jerome says he died a martyr.

Jerome, writing about eighty years later, makes him bishop of Olympus and afterwards of Tyre, and the church historian Socrates also places him at Olympus. In his list of Methodius's books, Jerome calls On the Resurrection an excellent work against Origen.

The Banquet is a story that one friend begs another to repeat. The first speaker says the virgin life came late, a plant sent down from heaven. The next answers that praising virginity does not abolish marriage, which is still God's work, and she says it in her own image: "The moon may be greater than the stars, but its light does not put out the light of the other stars." Virtue, closing, says it is no chastity to keep the body pure but not the tongue, the eyes, the hands or the mind. She crowns them all, and Thekla, one of the guests, with a larger crown.

The resurrection book asks what the body is to the soul. Origen, Methodius wrote, held that the soul lived without a body before the fall, the first turning from God into sin, and was then given the body as a chain, the cause of its sins. At the resurrection, in this account, the same flesh does not return, only each person's shape, stamped on another, spiritual body. Methodius could not bear, he says, those who turn bones and flesh into symbols to make the resurrection fleshless. His account of Origen is kept in a summary that a later reader made centuries on.

If the body were a chain to hold back evil, Methodius replied, it failed, since Cain committed murder in this body. A person, he wrote, is soul and body joined in one. Then he told of a craftsman who finds that an envious man has spoiled his finest statue, so he melts it down and casts it again. "For the statue cannot be lost while it is in the same craftsman's hands, even if it is melted down into its material again. It can be restored. But the blemishes and the damage can be lost, for they melt away. They cannot be restored." In the same way God, seeing his finest work spoiled by envy, dissolves the body in death and remakes it in the resurrection.

He also answered a pagan philosopher of the same age who wrote books against the Christians and their Scriptures, in a work that Jerome says ran to ten thousand lines. Origen still had defenders, and in a book he wrote in Origen's defense, Eusebius of Caesarea asked how Methodius dared to write against Origen now, after what he had said of Origen's teachings. Jerome reports the question.

Jerome says Methodius was crowned with martyrdom at Chalcis in Greece, at the end of the last persecution, or, as others said, under the emperors Decius and Valerian.

Later in the fourth century Epiphanius of Salamis, writing against Origen, chose to rest on what the blessed Methodius had said, and set out On the Resurrection word for word. He called him a learned man who fought hard for the truth. In the next century Theodoret of Cyrrhus carried his words on martyrdom into his own arguments about God made man.

More than a century after Methodius, the church historian Socrates, a defender of Origen, put him first among four men who attacked Origen. "Worthless people, who cannot be noticed on their own, want to be seen by finding fault with their betters. Methodius was the first to fall into this." Socrates adds that Methodius, after railing against Origen in many places, took it all back, as it were, and praised him in a dialogue he called the Xenon.

At the Banquet's close, Virtue tells the guests to rise and stand under the tree. Thekla begins a hymn, and the rest, standing in a circle, answer her, keeping themselves for Christ, the Bridegroom of Jesus' parable of the virgins who wait with their lamps.
