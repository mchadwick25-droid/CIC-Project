- **The essence:** Methodius of Olympus was a bishop in Lycia, in the south of Asia Minor. He wrote the Banquet, in which ten young women each praise the chaste life, and a dialogue On the Resurrection, which argued against Origen that the body is no chain on the soul and that the same body will rise. A later bishop copied his words into his own case against Origen, and Jerome says he died a martyr. (S1, S2, W1, W6, R1-R4, I1, X1)
- **Also told:**
  - The book: a story within a story, set in Virtue's garden, where each guest praises virginity; one speaker defends marriage as still God's work; Virtue says chastity must keep the tongue, eyes, hands and mind as well as the body; the guests end in a hymn (W2-W11, W14, Quote 1). Documented, his own words.
  - The question: what the body is to the soul. Origen's teaching as Methodius gave it: the soul lived without a body before the fall and was given the body afterwards as a chain; at the resurrection only the person's shape rises, on another, spiritual body (R1, R4). Documented as Methodius's account.
  - His answer, in one image: the craftsman who melts down a spoiled statue and casts it again, so the flaws melt away and the statue itself is not lost; death is the melting, the rising the recasting (R8, R9, Quote 2). A person is soul and body joined (R3). Documented.
  - The quarrel over him: Eusebius asked how he dared write against Origen now (D4); the church historian Socrates scorned him as the first of Origen's detractors and says he later praised Origen (D1, D2, Quote 3). Each credited.
  - Checked against the Library: all held. "Bishop of Patara" is held only in later writers and editors' notes (D6). "Died in 311 or 312" is an editor's note; Jerome gives the end of the recent persecution, or by others' account under Decius and Valerian (S3, D5). "A Christian rewriting of Plato's Symposium" and "written against Origen" for the Banquet are modern notes only (D9, M2). How he died is not told in any held source (X3).

- **Openings:**
  1. The garden (W3-W6). A young woman and her two friends climb a rough, steep path to a garden, where ten guests will meet. A tall woman in a robe white as snow meets them and embraces them like a mother; her name means Virtue. They feast under a wide, shady tree, and she asks each to speak in praise of the virgin life. Then the name: this is the Banquet, a book by Methodius of Olympus, and the essence. Preferred.
  2. The statue (R8), told as a reach ahead: a craftsman finds that an envious man has spoiled his finest statue, so he melts it down and casts it again. Use only if Opening 1 is not; Beat 4 then tells only the meaning and Quote 2.
- **Story beats:**
  1. **A bishop in Lycia.** Jerome makes him bishop of Olympus in Lycia and afterwards of Tyre; Socrates also has Olympus (S1, S2). Jerome lists his books, among them an answer to a pagan philosopher, the Banquet and On the Resurrection, "an excellent work... against Origen", with "many others which are widely read" (W1); the rest of the list goes to the card. Confidence: Documented as Jerome's account.
  2. **The Banquet.** It is told as a story one friend begs another to repeat: the meeting of the virgins and their speeches on chastity (W2). Each speaker takes up the theme. The first says the virgin life came late, a plant sent down from heaven, after the race had moved by stages from marrying sisters to one wife, to restraint, to virginity (W7). The next answers that the praise of virginity does not abolish marriage, and says so in her own image (W8): Quote 1. Virtue, closing, says it is no chastity to keep the body pure but not the tongue, the eyes, the hands or the mind (W9). She crowns them all, and Thekla with the larger crown (W10). In a closing talk, the friend argues that the soul which feels desire and holds its course is better, as the pilot who brings his ship through a storm is better than one who sails in a calm (W12). The hymn is kept for the Ending. Explain it: chastity and virginity, as above. Confidence: Documented, his own words.
  3. **Origen's teaching on the body.** Origen, Methodius wrote, held that the soul lived without a body before the fall, and was given the body afterwards as a chain, the cause of its sins (R1); and that at the resurrection the same flesh does not return, only each person's shape, stamped on another, spiritual body (R4). Methodius could not bear, he says, those who turn bones and flesh into symbols to make the resurrection fleshless (R5). Credit: this is Methodius's account of Origen, kept in a summary a later reader made centuries on (R1, Who tells it). Explain it: the fall, the chain. Confidence: Documented as Methodius's account.
  4. **The answer.** If the body were a chain on evil, it failed: Cain committed murder in this body (R2). A human being is neither soul without body nor body without soul, but the two joined in one (R3). God clothed Adam and Eve in "coats of skins", which Methodius reads as death, given so that sin would perish with the body (R6). Then the statue: a craftsman sees his finest work spoiled by an envious man, so he melts it down and casts it again (R8). Quote 2. So God, seeing his finest work spoiled by envy, dissolves the body in death and remakes it in the resurrection (R9). The fig tree torn from a temple's stones may stand beside it (R7; Cut first). Confidence: Documented, his own words (Greek through Epiphanius).
  5. **The pagan philosopher.** A pagan philosopher of the same age wrote books against the Christians and their Scriptures (P1). Jerome says Methodius answered him ably, in a work of ten thousand lines (P2). Confidence: Documented as Jerome's account and judgment.
  6. **The quarrel in his lifetime.** Eusebius of Caesarea, in his Defense of Origen, asked how Methodius dared to write against Origen now, after what he had said of Origen's teachings; Jerome reports the question (D4). Confidence: Documented as Eusebius's question; what he had said is not given.
  7. **A martyr.** Jerome says he was crowned with martyrdom at Chalcis in Greece, at the end of the recent persecution; others, Jerome adds, said under Decius and Valerian (S3, X1). Jerome, Theodoret and Photius all call him bishop and martyr (X2). How he died is not told (X3): tell only Jerome's words. Confidence: Documented as Jerome's account; the time Contested.
- **What came of it** (ranked; about an eighth of the story):
  1. **Epiphanius.** Writing against Origen later in the fourth century, Epiphanius chose to rest on what the blessed Methodius had said in On the Resurrection, and set it out word for word (I1); he called him a learned man who fought hard for the truth (I2). **Defended:** the rising of the same body.
  2. **Theodoret.** In the next century Theodoret of Cyrrhus kept his words on martyrdom among the testimonies of earlier bishops, and named him with Ignatius, Irenaeus and Hippolytus among the "bishops and martyrs" (I4; told in narration), and among the teachers of the church he followed (I5). **Defended:** faith in the incarnation, God made man (NP3 20249-20250, "his mind about the divine incarnation").
  3. **Jerome.** He set Methodius among the church's writers, praised his polished style, and called On the Resurrection excellent (I3).
  4. **Socrates.** More than a century later, the church historian Socrates, who defended Origen, put Methodius first among four men who attacked him (D1). Quote 3. He adds that Methodius, after railing against Origen in many places, as if taking it all back, praised him in a dialogue he called the Xenon (D2), and argues that their attacks commend Origen, since none charged him with error about the Trinity (D3; Cut first). Confidence: each Documented as Socrates's; the change of heart Contested (Socrates alone; the Xenon is not held).
  5. Card: Photius's summary (I7); the Syriac chronicle's Phidias story (I8); the images quarrel (I9); the Father lists (I10).
