Eusebius of Caesarea opened his History of the Church with an admission. "I admit that a perfect and complete history is beyond my power. I am the first to take up this subject, so I am trying to travel, as it were, a lonely and untrodden path." Those before him, he wrote, had left only brief fragments, their voices calling from far off like torches from a watchtower.

From those fragments Eusebius, bishop of Caesarea on the coast of Roman Palestine, built the church's story from Christ to his own day, out of the letters, lists and books of the Christians before him. He lived through the emperors' persecution of the church, came to know the emperor Constantine and wrote his Life, and signed the faith agreed at Nicaea after asking what its words meant. Years later, at Tyre, a bishop who had suffered in the persecution charged him with giving way to get out of prison. Later historians began their own books where his ended.

Eusebius was the friend of Pamphilus, a priest of Caesarea, and took his name, calling himself Eusebius Pamphili. His History, a book of the church's past from Christ to his own day, gives its plan in his own list. It would tell the lines of bishops in the great churches, those who spoke or wrote the faith, those who led others into error, and those who stood firm under torture down to his own time. He gathered fitting passages from older writers and drew on letters kept in the library at Jerusalem. Other letters he copied whole, so that his pages keep earlier writers' letters word for word.

In 303 the emperors ordered the churches torn down and the Scriptures burned. Soon after, church leaders were jailed and forced by every means to sacrifice, to make an offering to the Roman gods. Christians call this the Great Persecution. Eusebius was there, and he wrote of the churches' leaders, whom he calls their shepherds, as well as their buildings. "We saw with our own eyes the houses of prayer thrown down from top to ground, foundations and all. We saw the inspired and holy Scriptures handed to the fire in the middle of the market squares. We saw the shepherds of the churches hiding shamefully here and there, and some of them caught in disgrace and mocked by their enemies."

His dearest friend Pamphilus was jailed and killed with his companions. Together they had written a Defense of Origen, a careful answer to those who found fault with that teacher. In his History Eusebius set himself a rule. He would not record those who were shaken by the persecution, only what would help his readers and those who came after them.

By the council of 325, Eusebius was bishop of Caesarea. A quarrel had divided the churches: whether God's Son had always been, or had a beginning, as Arius taught. At Nicaea, Eusebius tells his church in a letter, its own creed, the short statement of belief it said at baptism, was read before Constantine and praised. Then the emperor urged one added word, the Greek homoousios, saying the Son is of the same substance as the Father. Eusebius asked what it meant. After questions and answers he signed, he wrote, for the sake of peace and so as not to fall from the right belief. Socrates, writing a century later, says he held back a short time before signing. Athanasius, who was there, says he had refused the day before.

In 335 Constantine called bishops to Tyre, the chief city of Phoenicia, up the coast. They were to judge charges that opponents in Egypt brought against Athanasius, now bishop of Alexandria: that a cup used at the Lord's Supper had been broken, and that a priest's hand had been cut off. Epiphanius, who tells the story about forty years later on Athanasius's side, calls them slanders. He says the emperor ordered Eusebius to judge, with others.

Potamo, a bishop from Egypt, was a confessor, a Christian who had suffered for the faith in the persecution and lived. He had lost an eye. Seeing Eusebius in the judge's seat and Athanasius standing, he wept and asked who could bear such things, when an innocent man was being judged by Eusebius. "Tell me, were you not with me in prison in the persecution? I lost an eye for the truth. But you seem to have nothing maimed in your body. You did not suffer as a martyr. You stand alive, with nothing cut away. How did you get out of prison, unless you promised those who forced the persecution on us to do the forbidden thing, or did it?"

Eusebius rose in anger and broke up the court. If they had come there to speak against him like this, he said, then their accusers must be telling the truth, for if they played the tyrant here, they did so much more at home. Later the bishops of Egypt, on Athanasius's side, wrote that their confessors had accused Eusebius of sacrificing. The council removed Athanasius.

After the death of Constantine, Eusebius wrote the Life of Constantine, a book on the emperor's faith. By his own rule, he set aside the emperor's wars and laws to speak only of that faith. In its pages Tyre is a cloud of envy over the churches of Egypt, and neither Athanasius nor Potamo is named. Eusebius died after the emperor, in the reign of his sons, and Socrates says his disciple Acacius followed him as bishop.

Socrates began his own history where Eusebius stopped, with what Eusebius had left out. Rufinus put the History into Latin and wrote two books more. Jerome said it had been of the utmost help to his own book on Christian writers. They judged him both ways. Socrates defended him against the charge of favoring Arius, since he had signed at Nicaea. Jerome called the History fine, and Eusebius the open champion of Arius's teaching.

Eusebius had also drawn up a Chronicle, a table of the years of the world from Abraham down to his own day, and Jerome put it into Latin. At the year Constantine had reigned twenty years, Jerome marks the place where his own work begins. Up to here, the line says, the history is written by Eusebius, the companion of the martyr Pamphilus.
