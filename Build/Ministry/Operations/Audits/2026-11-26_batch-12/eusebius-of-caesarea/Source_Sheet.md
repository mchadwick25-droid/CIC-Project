## Setting

**S1. Name and Pamphilus.** Eusebius: "In his time we became acquainted with Pamphilus" (HE VII.32.25; EUS 43009–43010). Jerome: "His surname Pamphilus arose from his friendship for Pamphilus the martyr" (*Lives* 81; THD 41076–41077). Jerome's Chronicle calls him "Eusebius Pamphili martyris contubernalis" ("companion of the martyr Pamphilus", JCHR 45062–45064). Witness: own words; Jerome, later. Documented (the friendship, the name). EUS-ed calls Pamphilus his "teacher" (43010–43011): editor only.

**S2. Caesarea, and the tradition.** Eusebius calls his see "Cæsarea in Palestine" (Socrates's form, SOC 2972; VC III.61, the emperor's letter "declining the bishopric of the church at Antioch, and desiring to continue in that church of which you first received the oversight", EUS 65967–65969). CEN: `placeLabel` "Caesarea Maritima"; tradition `palestinian-church-pre-constantinian`, name "The Church of Roman Palestine before Constantine", dates "c. 185-312"; `shortName` "Pre-Nicene Palestine". Plain words: "Caesarea, on the coast of Roman Palestine". Spoken name of the tradition: "the Church of Roman Palestine before Constantine".

**S3. Presbyter, then bishop, before Nicaea.** His letter: "as we believed and taught in the presbytery, and in the Episcopate itself, so believing also at the time present" (EUS 2058–2060). Witness: own words. Documented that he was presbyter and then bishop before 325. The year he became bishop is given by no old source held: EUS-ed "between about 311 and 318" (modern; not told).

**S4. Birth and death.** No held old source gives his birth. Death: Socrates, in the reign of Constantine's sons: "At this time Eusebius, who was bishop of Cæsarea in Palestine, and had the surname of Pamphilus, having died, Acacius, his disciple, succeeded him in the bishopric. This individual published several books, and among others a biographical sketch of his master" (SOC 5802–5806). Witness: later, about a century. Documented (died after Constantine; Acacius followed). The years "about 260 to 339" (CARD `datesNote`, CEN `dates` "c. 260-339") rest on McGiffert's reckoning: Dominant Modern Reconstruction. Tell "after the emperor's death" only.

**S5. Tyre.** "the capital city of Phœnicia" (VC IV.41; EUS 67227). Epiphanius: "κατὰ τὴν Φοινίκην ἐν Τύρῳ τῇ πόλει" (EPI 44479). Plain words: "Tyre, the chief city of Phoenicia, up the coast".

## Events

### The History and how he made it

**E1. His plan.** HE I.1.1: "It is my purpose to write an account of the successions of the holy apostles, as well as of the times which have elapsed from the days of our Saviour to our own; and to relate the many important events which are said to have occurred in the history of the Church; and to mention those who have governed and presided over the Church in the most prominent parishes, and those who in each generation have proclaimed the divine word either orally or in writing" (EUS 6878–6885). I.1.2: also "the names and number and times of those who through love of innovation have run into the greatest errors" (6887–6889). I.1.3: also the attacks on the Word, those who "contended for it in the face of blood and of tortures, as well as the confessions which have been made in our own days" (6896–6901). Witness: own words (English only). Documented.
- *Explain it (the History):* a book of the church's past from Christ to his own day, told through its lines of bishops, its writers, its quarrels and its persecutions (E1, his own list).

**E2. The lonely and untrodden path** (Quote 1). HE I.1.4: "for I confess that it is beyond my power to produce a perfect and complete history, and since I am the first to enter upon the subject, I am attempting to traverse as it were a lonely and untrodden path. […] I pray that I may have God as my guide and the power of the Lord as my aid, since I am unable to find even the bare footsteps of those who have traveled the way before me, except in brief fragments, in which some in one way, others in another, have transmitted to us particular accounts of the times in which they lived. From afar they raise their voices like torches, and they cry out, as from some lofty and conspicuous watch-tower, admonishing us where to walk and how to direct the course of our work steadily and safely" (EUS 6934–6937, 6952–6960). I.1.6: "I know of no ecclesiastical writer who has devoted himself to this subject" (6977–6979). Witness: own words; Greek not held. Documented as his claim. Where the old sources differ: D1.

**E3. Passages gathered.** HE I.1.5: "Having gathered therefore from the matters mentioned here and there by them whatever we consider important for the present work, and having plucked like flowers from a meadow the appropriate passages from ancient writers, […] we shall endeavor to embody the whole in an historical narrative" (EUS 6962–6965, 6971). Witness: own words. Documented. (The meadow image: Jerome uses a like one of his own predecessors, THD 38809–38811; not needed.)

**E4. Where he found them.** Jerusalem: letters of learned men "have been kept until our time in the library at Ælia, […] which was established by Alexander, who at that time presided over that church. We have been able to gather from that library material for our present work" (HE VI.20.1; EUS 35434–35443). Edessa: "the epistles themselves which we have taken from the archives and have literally translated from the Syriac language" (HE I.13.5; EUS 9522–9524). Witness: own words. Documented. EUS-ed holds the Edessa letters forged before his day (9510–9512): modern; Leave out.
- *Explain it (Aelia):* the Roman name for Jerusalem in his day (EUS-ed 35437–35438, clarifying).

**E5. What his pages keep: one case.** HE V.24.1–2: Polycrates, leader of the bishops of Asia, "in a letter which he addressed to Victor and the church of Rome, set forth in the following words the tradition which had come down to him", followed by the letter itself, "We observe the exact day; neither adding, nor taking away" (EUS 31310–31316, 31324–31325). Witness: own text (the letter quoted). Documented that he quotes it word for word. That it survives *only* here is EUS-ed (19079): modern; not told as the narrator's.

**E6. The Defense of Origen with Pamphilus.** "the Apology in his behalf written by us and Pamphilus, the holy martyr of our day. We prepared this carefully and did the work jointly on account of faultfinders" (HE VI.33.4; EUS 36974–36976). Witness: own words. Documented. The Pamphilus story's job (sibling): a clause here at most.

### The Great Persecution

**E7. The first edict, 303.** HE VIII.2.4: "It was in the nineteenth year of the reign of Diocletian, […] in the month Dystrus, […] called March by the Romans, when the feast of the Saviour's passion was near at hand, […] that royal edicts were published everywhere, commanding that the churches be leveled to the ground and the Scriptures be destroyed by fire, and ordering that those who held places of honor be degraded, and that the household servants, if they persisted in the profession of Christianity, be deprived of freedom" (EUS 43460–43461, 43482–43491); Greek GRK 13717–13724 ("ἔτος τοῦτο ἦν ἐννεακαιδέκατον τῆς Διοκλητιανοῦ βασιλείας, Δύστρος μήν"). Witness: own words. Year 303 (the 19th year, EUS-ed 43461–43465): Widely Accepted. The month: D6.
- *Explain it (the persecution):* the emperors' orders against the churches: buildings torn down, Scriptures burned, then church leaders jailed and pressed to sacrifice (E7, E8).

**E8. The second order: leaders jailed, then made to sacrifice.** HE VIII.2.5: "Such was the first edict against us. But not long after, other decrees were issued, commanding that all the rulers of the churches in every place be first thrown into prison, […] and afterwards by every artifice be compelled to sacrifice" (EUS 43578–43580, 43637). Witness: own words. Documented.
- *Explain it (to sacrifice):* to make an offering to the Roman gods, which the orders forced on jailed church leaders; to refuse could mean torture or death (E8; E11).

**E9. What he saw** (Quote 2). HE VIII.2.1, English: "All these things were fulfilled in us, when we saw with our own eyes the houses of prayer thrown down to the very foundations, and the Divine and Sacred Scriptures committed to the flames in the midst of the market-places, and the shepherds of the churches basely hidden here and there, and some of them captured ignominiously, and mocked by their enemies" (EUS 43431–43437). Greek (GRK 13614–13620, damaged OCR, given as printed): "συντετέλεσται δῆτα xcÜ' ἡμᾶς ἅπαντα, ὁπηνίχα τῶν μὲν προσευχτηρίων τοὺς οἴχους ἐξ ὕψους εἰς ἔδαφος αὐτοῖς ϑεμελίοις καταρριπτουμένους. τὰς δ᾽ ἐνϑέους xci ἱερὰς γραφὰς κατὰ μέσας ἀγορὰς πυρὶ παραδιδομένας αὐὖὐτοῖς ἐπείδομεν ὀφϑαλμοῖς τούς τε τῶν ἐκκλησιῶν ποιμένας αἰσχρῶς ὧδε χἀχεῖσε χρυπταζομένους, τοὺς δὲ ἀσχημόνως ἁλισκομένους καὶ πρὸς τῶν ἐχϑρῶν καταπαιζομένους". Rufinus's Latin (GRK 13673–13678) confirms the core: "domus orationis … ab ipsis subversae sunt fundamentis, divinae vero scripturae in medio platearum igni crematae. pro nefas! ipsi haec nostris vidimus oculis". Witness: own words, eyewitness. Documented.
- *Explain it (shepherds):* the leaders of the churches, its bishops and priests (EUS-ed 43629–43634 reads the next section's "rulers of the churches" as all church officers; clarifying).

**E10. What he chose not to tell.** HE VIII.2.2–3: "But it is not our place to describe the sad misfortunes which finally came upon them, as we do not think it proper, moreover, to record their divisions and unnatural conduct to each other before the persecution. […] Hence we shall not mention those who were shaken by the persecution, nor those who in everything pertaining to salvation were shipwrecked, and by their own will were sunk in the depths of the flood. But we shall introduce into this history in general only those events which may be usefull first to ourselves and afterwards to posterity" (EUS 43441–43453). Greek GRK 13709–13714 ("οὐκοῦν οὐδὲ τῶν πρὸς τοῦ διωγμοῦ πεπειραμένων … μνήμην ποιήσασϑαι προήχϑημεν"). Witness: own words. Documented (the rule). EUS-ed reports Gibbon's attack on it and rejects it (43453–43456): modern views both; Leave out.

**E11. Eyewitness at Tyre and in Egypt.** HE VIII.7.2, at Tyre: "We were present ourselves when these things occurred" (EUS 44161–44162); the beasts would not touch the martyrs (44164–44171): a wonder, his voice only. HE VIII.9.4, in the Thebais (upper Egypt): "We, also being on the spot ourselves, have observed large crowds in one day; some suffering decapitation, others torture by fire; so that the murderous sword was blunted, and becoming weak, was broken, and the very executioners grew weary and relieved each other" (EUS 44268–44272); Greek GRK 14540–14545 ("ἱστορήσαμεν δὲ καὶ αὐτοὶ ἐπὶ τῶν τόπων γενόμενοι"). Witness: own words. Documented that he says he saw. When he went: EUS-ed (44240–44244) guesses late in the persecution: modern; not told.

**E12. Pamphilus jailed and killed** (sibling: a clause). MP 7: "Among these was my dearest friend Pamphilus, who was by reason of every virtue the most illustrious of the martyrs in our time" (EUS 46694–46698); MP 11: "the great and celebrated spectacle of Pamphilus, a man thrice dear to me, and of those who finished their course with him. They were twelve in all" (47068–47070). Witness: own words. Documented. Dates and the prison are the Pamphilus story's.

**E13. His own prison: not in his own works.** Eusebius nowhere says he was jailed. ATH-ed: "It appears that Eusebius was prisoner at Cæsarea with S. Pamphilus; yet he never mentions the fact himself" (ATH 22313–22315). EUS-ed (1490–1510, 1556–1566) offers guesses (friends in office; release at the war's end) and defends him: modern. Confidence that he was jailed: Contested (only Potamo's question, E21, says so); where and how he came out: not given. The narrator says nothing of it beyond the charge.

### Constantine

**E14. First sight.** VC I.19: "An instance of this we have ourselves seen, when he passed through Palestine with the senior emperor, […] at whose right hand he stood, and commanded the admiration of all who beheld him by the indications he gave even then of royal greatness" (EUS 60955–60959). EUS-ed: the senior emperor is Diocletian, "on his way to Egypt in the famous campaign against Achilleus in 296–297" (60956–60957): the name is clarifying; the year, modern (not told). Witness: own words. Documented. Plain: "when Constantine was a young man at the old emperor's side".

**E15. The vision, told him under oath** (sibling `constantine-the-great` tells it; a clause here; "Conquer by this", EUS 61206, barred). VC I.28: "since the victorious emperor himself long afterwards declared it to the writer of this history, […] when he was honored with his acquaintance and society, and confirmed his statement by an oath, who could hesitate to accredit the relation" (EUS 61196–61202). EUS-ed (61208–61211) notes doubters "from the very beginning, commencing with Eusebius himself": an editor's reading; not told. Witness: own words. Documented as his report of the emperor's word; the wonder stays in that voice.

**E16. The emperor stood to listen.** VC IV.33: "I had begged permission to pronounce a discourse on the subject of our Saviour's sepulchre in his hearing. […] in the interior of the palace itself, he stood and listened with the rest. I entreated him, but in vain, to seat himself on the imperial throne which stood near […] he in his turn was displeased and said that it was not right to listen in a careless manner to the discussion of doctrines relating to God" (EUS 66987–67001). Witness: own words. Documented as his account. No year given. Optional.

### Nicaea, 325

**E17. Arius names him, before the council.** Arius to Eusebius of Nicomedia, in Theodoret I.4: "Eusebius, your brother bishop of Cæsarea, Theodotus, Paulinus, Athanasius, Gregorius, Aetius, and all the bishops of the East, have been condemned because they say that God had an existence prior to that of His Son" (THD 6031–6034). Witness: contemporary (Arius, on his own side), kept by Theodoret a century later. Documented as Arius's claim. Arius's own line "We are persecuted…" (6045–6046) is barred (`eusebius-of-nicomedia`); not used.
- *Explain it (the quarrel):* whether God's Son had always been, or had a beginning, as Arius taught (THD 6045–6046, narrated; CEN `arius`).

**E18. His church's creed, and the added word** (sibling `council-of-nicaea-325` tells the word; a clause here; "of one being" barred). His letter to his church: "we have been obliged to transmit to you, first, the formula of faith presented by ourselves; and next, the second, which the Fathers put forth with some additions to our words. Our own paper, then, which was read in the presence of our most pious Emperor, and declared to be good and unexceptionable" (EUS 2048–2053). The emperor "advised all present to agree to it, and to subscribe its articles and to assent to them, with the insertion of the single word, 'One in substance' (ὁμοούσιος)" (EUS 2088–2091). Witness: own words, written to his own church soon after. Documented as his account.
- *Explain it (creed):* a short statement of what a church believes, recited at baptism (his letter: "in our first catechisings, and when we received the Holy Laver", EUS 2056–2057) and here signed by the bishops.
- *Explain it (the added word):* the Greek *homoousios*, "of the same substance" as the Father (McGiffert prints "One in substance", EUS 2091).

**E19. Questions asked; assent for peace.** "On their dictating this formula, we did not let it pass without inquiry in what sense they introduced 'of the substance of the Father,' and 'one in substance with the Father.' Accordingly questions and explanations took place, and the meaning of the words underwent the scrutiny of reason" (EUS 2121–2126). "On this account we assented to the sense ourselves, without declining even the term 'One in substance,' peace being the object which we set before us, and steadfastness in the orthodox view" (EUS 2131–2134). "to which all of us assented, not without inquiry, but according to the specified senses" (EUS 2159–2160). Greek, SOCG 7309–7314 ("οὐκ ἀνεξέταστον αὐτοῖς καταλιμπάνομεν. Ἐπερωτήσεις τοιγαροῦν καὶ ἀποκρίσεις ἐντεῦθεν ἀνεκινοῦντο"), 7326–7328 with 7403–7405 ("οὐδὲ τὴν φωνὴν τοῦ ὁμοουσίου παραιτούμενοι, τοῦ τῆς εἰρήνης σκοποῦ πρὸ ὀφθαλμῶν ἡμῖν κειμένου, καὶ τοῦ μὴ τῆς ὀρθῆς ἐκπεσεῖν διανοίας", as normalised from the OCR), 7518–7522 ("ᾗ συνεφωνήσαμεν οἱ πάντες οὐκ ἀνεξετάστως"); Hussey's Latin "pacis intuitu, et ne a recta intelligentia excidamus" (SOCG 7392–7393). Witness: own words. Documented as his account. "not without inquiry" stands in the Pamphilus outline's list of the live text's quotes: narrated here, never quoted.

**E20. How others told his signing.** Socrates I.8: "Eusebius, surnamed Pamphilus, bishop of Cæsarea in Palestine, who had held aloof for a short time, after mature consideration whether he ought to receive this definition of the faith, at length acquiesced in it, and subscribed it with all the rest: he also sent to the people under his charge a copy of the Creed, with an explanation of the word homoousios, that no one might impugn his motives on account of his previous hesitation" (SOC 2971–2978). Athanasius, *De Decretis* 3: "Eusebius of Cæsarea in Palestine, who had denied the day before, but afterwards subscribed, sent to his Church a letter, saying that this was the Church's faith, and the tradition of the Fathers; and made a public profession that they were before in error, and were rashly contending against the truth. For though he was ashamed at that time to adopt these phrases, and excused himself to the Church in his own way, yet he certainly means to imply all this in his Epistle" (ATH 26780–26787). Witness: Socrates, a century later, friendly; Athanasius, present at Nicaea, hostile. Documented as each one's account. See D2.

**E21-pre. After Nicaea: Eustathius; Antioch.** Socrates I.23: "Eustathius, bishop of Antioch, accuses Eusebius Pamphilus of perverting the Nicene Creed; Eusebius again denies that he violates that exposition of the faith, and recriminates, saying that Eustathius was a defender of the opinion of Sabellius" (SOC 4708–4711). Socrates I.24: after Eustathius was deposed, one party at Antioch "vehemently contended for the translation of Eusebius Pamphilus from Cæsarea in Palestine to Antioch"; violence was checked "through letters, and Eusebius by refusing to accept the bishopric" (SOC 4752–4761). Constantine's letter, in VC III.61: "your Prudence whose resolve it is to observe the ordinances of God and the apostolic canon of the Church, has done excellently well in declining the bishopric of the church at Antioch, and desiring to continue in that church of which you first received the oversight" (EUS 65965–65969). Witness: Socrates, later; the emperor's letter, contemporary, kept by Eusebius. Documented. No year in the old sources.
- *Explain it (why he refused):* the church's rule that a bishop stay with the church he first received (the emperor's letter, EUS 65965–65969).
- *Explain it (Sabellius's opinion):* would need a gloss of its own (CEN `modalist-monarchianism`); Leave out.

### Tyre, 335: the charges

**E21. Why Tyre met; his seat.** Eusebius's own telling, VC IV.41: "that spirit of envy which is the enemy of all good, like a dark cloud intercepting the sun's brightest rays, endeavored to mar the joy of this festivity, by again raising contentions to disturb the tranquillity of the Egyptian churches. Our divinely favored emperor, however, once more convened a synod composed of many bishops […] in order, first, to decide the questions in dispute, and afterwards to perform the dedication of the sacred edifice above mentioned. He enjoined them, by the way, to adjust their differences at the capital city of Phœnicia" (EUS 67217–67227). He names neither Athanasius nor Potamo. Epiphanius, *Panarion* 68.7: the accusers brought charges against Athanasius to the emperor (a broken cup used in the mysteries; the cut-off hand of Arsenius, a presbyter, carried in a box; EPI 44528–44534, Latin); the emperor, not knowing they were slanderers, "ordered a synod to be gathered in Phoenicia, in the city of Tyre. He ordered Eusebius of Caesarea to judge, and some others" (my English of EPI 44478–44480, "Κελεύει δὲ συγκροτηϑῆναι σύνοδον κατὰ τὴν Φοινίκην ἐν Τύρῳ τῇ πόλει. Ἐκέλευσε δὲ δικάζειν Εὐσέβιον τὸν Καισαρείας, καὶ ἄλλους τινάς"; Latin "eique judicio praeesse cum aliis nonnullis Caesariensem Eusebium", 44539–44541); Epiphanius adds that these judges "were inclined somewhat more to the Arians' vulgar talk" (my English of EPI 44480–44482; Latin "qui omnes in Arianorum inanissimum dogma propensiores erant", 44541–44542). ## What came of him (heaviest first)

**I1. Later historians began where he stopped.** Socrates I.1: "Eusebius, surnamed Pamphilus, writing the History of the Church in ten books, closed it with that period of the emperor Constantine, when the persecution which Diocletian had begun against the Christians came to an end. […] Now, as we propose to write the details of what has taken place in the churches since his time to our own day, we begin with the narration of the particulars which he has left out" (SOC 1842–1843, 1850, 1861–1869). Theodoret I.1: "Eusebius of Palestine […] has written a history of the Church from the time of the holy Apostles to the reign of Constantine, the prince beloved of God. I shall begin my history from the period at which his terminates" (THD 5219–5224). Sozomen I.1 counts him among those who had recorded the church's past, "a man intimately acquainted with the sacred Scriptures and the writings of the Greek poets and historians", and so writes on from where they ended (SOC 26359–26381). Evagrius, late sixth century, names him first of the four whose histories he follows (EVA 1106–1116; EVAG 1250–1259): its words are quoted in the live `theodoret-of-cyrrhus` story ("a most excellent record"), so narrated at most. Witness: later historians, each in his own preface. Documented.

**I2. Carried into Latin.** Rufinus's prologue: Chromatius, bishop of Aquileia, at the time when, "diruptis Italiae claustris Alarico duce Gothorum", a deadly plague poured in, asked him to put into Latin "ecclesiasticam historiam, quam vir eruditissimus Eusebius Caesariensis Graeco sermone conscripserat", so that readers' minds might be held by better things and forget the evils of the day (GRK 26042–26058). Rufinus says Eusebius's tenth book held little history and much praise of bishops; he joined what history it had to the ninth, and wrote Books X–XI himself (GRK 26094–26103). Jerome's *Chronicle*: from Troy to Constantine's twentieth year he translated and added; from there on "totum meum est" (JCHR 2311–2326); at Constantine's twentieth year: "Huc usque historiam scribit Eusebius Pamphili martyris contubernalis, cui nos ista subiecimus" ("Up to here Eusebius, the companion of the martyr Pamphilus, writes the history; to it we have added what follows", my English; JCHR 45062–45064). Jerome's *Lives*, preface: having no predecessor, "yet I must acknowledge that Eusebius Pamphilus in the ten books of his Church History has been of the utmost assistance" (THD 38812–38815; Latin JERL 47183–47185, "maximo nobis adjumento fuerit"); *Lives* 81 lists the History, the Chronicle, the Preparation and Demonstration of the Gospel, Against Porphyry, the Defense of Origen, the Life of Pamphilus and more (THD 41061–41077). The live `rufinus-of-aquileia` story tells Chromatius and the Goths: a clause here. Witness: later writers who used and continued him. Documented.

**I3. Judged both ways, as historian and as believer.** Socrates I.1: in the Life of Constantine he "has but slightly treated of matters regarding Arius, being more intent on the rhetorical finish of his composition and the praises of the emperor, than on an accurate statement of facts" (SOC 1863–1866). Socrates I.23: by not giving the reason for Egypt's troubles after Nicaea, "he has won the reputation of disingenuousness" (SOC 4678–4681). Socrates II.21, defending him: "some have attempted to stigmatize even Eusebius Pamphilus himself as having favored the Arian views in his works", but "he was both present at the council of Nicæa, which defined the doctrine of the homoousion and gave his assent to what was there determined" (SOC 6916–6921). Sabinus, who scorned Nicaea, "commends Eusebius Pamphilus as a trustworthy witness" (SOC 2890–2891). Athanasius, *De Synodis* 17: "Eusebius of Cæsarea in Palestine, in a letter to Euphration the Bishop, did not scruple to say plainly that Christ was not true God" (ATH 57219–57222). Philostorgius, through Photius: "Though Philostorgius praises Eusebius Pamphilus as well on other grounds as on account of his Ecclesiastical History, yet he accuses him of erroneous opinions in matters relating to religion" (PHIL 88). Jerome, Letter 84: "Eusebius has composed a fine history of the Church; yet […] the latter is the most open champion of the Arian impiety" (JEP 18977–18983; the sentence after it, "Eusebius, although he sided with the Arians…", is JEP-ed). Jerome, *Apology* I: "Eusebius Bishop of Cæsarea, […] who was in his day the standard bearer of the Arian faction, wrote a large and elaborate work in six books in defence of Origen" (THD 51379–51381; Latin JERL 31183–31186). The Second Council of Nicaea (787): the opponents of images brought him as a witness with his letter to Constantia; the council answered by reading his letter to Euphration, "Non enim coexistere dicimus Filium Patri, verum praeexistere Patrem Filio" (MAN13 64550–64563, poor OCR). Witness: contemporary hostile (Athanasius); later friendly (Socrates), Arian (Philostorgius), hostile (Jerome), a council four and a half centuries on. Contested.

**I4. What his pages keep.** His History quotes earlier writers word for word, such as the letter of Polycrates of Ephesus on Easter (E5), and the letters he gathered at Jerusalem (E4). Witness: own text. Documented that he quotes them; that many survive only through him is EUS-ed (6965–6970; 19079): modern, not told as the narrator's.

**Defended** (for the outline's marking): Origen's good name, with Pamphilus (E6); his own reading of the Nicene creed, against Eustathius (E21-pre) and in his letter (E19).

## Where sources disagree or come late

**D1. Was he the first?** He says he is the first, finding only "bare footsteps" of others (E2, I.1.6). Sozomen, a century later, names Clement, Hegesippus and Africanus as having recorded the church's past before him, with Eusebius (SOC 26359–26368). McGiffert agrees he was the first with a "comprehensive historical plan", naming Hegesippus and Julius Africanus as partial forerunners (EUS-ed 6937–6952): modern. Told: "the first, he wrote"; Sozomen's list to the card.

**D2. How he signed at Nicaea.** Eusebius: his church's creed was approved; the added word came at the emperor's urging; he asked what it meant and assented "not without inquiry", for peace (E18–E19). Socrates: he "held aloof for a short time" and wrote to his people "that no one might impugn his motives on account of his previous hesitation" (E20). Athanasius: he "had denied the day before", then signed, and "excused himself to the Church in his own way" (E20). Eustathius, in Socrates: he twisted the creed after (E21-pre). Each told as its witness's; the narrator does not settle his motive.

**D3. Who spoke at Tyre, and to whom.** Epiphanius: Potamo to Eusebius (E22). Rufinus X.18, a like scene without Eusebius: Paphnutius, a confessor, seeing Maximus, bishop of Jerusalem, "qui una secum effosso oculo et poplite succiso confessor extiterat", sitting with the rest, said "te, inquam, non patiar sedere in concilio malignantium", and led him out to Athanasius's side (GRK 28008–28017, 28051–28052). Hefele thinks Epiphanius's account may be "another and a false version" of Rufinus's, and Rufinus's "plainly more probable" (HEF2 1826–1835): modern. Told: Epiphanius tells it so; Rufinus, writing later, tells a like scene with other men and does not name Eusebius. Hefele's view: card's Modern works only.

**D4. The charge itself.** Potamo asks how Eusebius got out of prison "unless" he promised or did the forbidden thing (E22): a question that charges, not a claim of knowledge. The Egyptian bishops say he "was accused by our confessors" over a sacrifice (E24): an accusation, not a finding. His own works never say he was jailed (E13). No held old source says he sacrificed, or that he was cleared. McGiffert (EUS-ed 1528–1566) and Robertson (ATH-ed 22315–22316) call it groundless: modern. Contested; told as charges only, in their own content.

**D5. Did he preside at Tyre?** Epiphanius: the emperor ordered him to judge, "and some others" (E21). McGiffert: "Whether Eusebius of Cæsarea, as is commonly supposed, or Eusebius of Nicomedia, or some other bishop, presided at this Council we are not able to determine" (EUS-ed 2556–2558): modern. Hefele: he presided (HEF2 1790–1791): modern. Told: "sat among the judges, Epiphanius says"; never "presided".

**D6. The month of the edict.** HE VIII.2.4 gives March; EUS-ed says MP gives April and Lactantius (*Deaths of the Persecutors* 12, not checked here) February 23 (43465–43474). Told: "in 303"; the month to the card.

**D7. The Life: history or praise?** His rule (E27); Socrates's judgment (I3); Rufinus's view of Book X as praise of bishops (I2). Told as each one's.

**D8. Arian or not?** I3, every side credited. Card for the full list; the story gives one hostile and one friendly voice at most.
