Eusebius of Caesarea opened his History of the Church with an admission. "I admit that a perfect and complete history is beyond my power. I am the first to take up this subject, so I am trying to travel, as it were, a lonely and untrodden path." Those before him, he wrote, had left only brief fragments, voices calling from far off like torches from a watchtower.

Eusebius, bishop of Caesarea on the coast of Roman Palestine, set out to tell the church's past from Christ to his own day: its bishops, its writers, its quarrels and its persecutions. The first to try, he believed, he built his History from the letters, lists and books of the Christians before him. He lived through the emperors' persecution of the church and wrote the emperor Constantine's Life. At Nicaea he signed the creed of a council of bishops, its statement of faith, after asking what its words meant. At Tyre a bishop who had suffered in the persecution charged that he had got out of prison by giving way. Later historians began their books where his History ended.

His friend in Caesarea was Pamphilus, a priest there. His plan, in his own list, was to trace the lines of bishops in the great churches, the men who spoke or wrote for the faith, those who led others into error, and those who stood firm under torture down to his own day. He told it by gathering fitting passages from the old writers and copying letters whole.

In 303 the emperors ordered the churches leveled and the Scriptures burned, and soon after, church leaders were jailed and forced by every means to sacrifice, to make an offering to the Roman gods. Eusebius saw it, and he calls the leaders of the churches shepherds: "We saw with our own eyes the houses of prayer thrown down from top to ground, foundations and all. We saw the inspired and holy Scriptures handed to the fire in the middle of the market squares. We saw the shepherds of the churches hiding shamefully here and there, and some of them caught in disgrace and mocked by their enemies." His dearest friend, Pamphilus, was jailed and killed with his companions. In his History he set himself a rule: he would not record those who had been shaken by the persecution, only what would help his readers and those after them.

By the council at Nicaea in 325 he was bishop, and the church was divided over whether God's Son had always been, or had a beginning, as Arius taught. There, Eusebius wrote to his church, the emperor urged one added word, the Greek homoousios, saying the Son is of the same substance as the Father. Eusebius asked what it meant, and after questions and answers he signed, he wrote, for the sake of peace and so as not to fall from the right belief. Socrates, writing a century later, says he held back a short time before signing. Athanasius, then a deacon of Alexandria who was there, says he had refused the day before.

In 335 Constantine called bishops to Tyre to judge charges that opponents in Egypt had brought against Athanasius, by then bishop of Alexandria. They said a cup used at the Lord's Supper had been broken, and a priest's hand cut off. Epiphanius, telling it about forty years later and on Athanasius's side, calls them slanders and says the emperor ordered Eusebius to judge, with others.

Potamo, a bishop from Egypt, was a confessor, a Christian who had suffered for the faith in the persecution and lived. When he saw Eusebius seated as judge and Athanasius standing, he wept, then spoke. "Tell me, were you not with me in prison in the persecution? I lost an eye for the truth. But you seem to have nothing maimed in your body. You did not suffer as a martyr. You stand alive, with nothing cut away. How did you get out of prison, unless you promised those who forced the persecution on us to do the forbidden thing, or did it?" Eusebius rose in anger and broke up the court. If they came here to speak against him so, he said, their accusers must be telling the truth, for if they played the tyrant here, they did much more at home.

The bishops of Egypt, on Athanasius's side, later wrote that their confessors had accused Eusebius of sacrificing. The council removed Athanasius.

After the death of Constantine, whom he had come to know, Eusebius wrote the emperor's Life, a book on his faith that by its own rule set aside his wars and laws. In its pages Tyre is a cloud of envy over the churches of Egypt, and Athanasius and Potamo go unnamed. He died after the emperor, in his sons' reign.

Socrates began his own history of the church with what Eusebius had left out, and he defended Eusebius against the charge of favoring Arius, since he had signed at Nicaea. Jerome, writing in Latin, called his History fine, and called him the open champion of Arius's teaching.
