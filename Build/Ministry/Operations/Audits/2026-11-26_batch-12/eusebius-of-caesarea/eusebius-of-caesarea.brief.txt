Eusebius of Caesarea opened his History of the Church with an admission. "I admit that a perfect and complete history is beyond my power. I am the first to take up this subject, so I am trying to travel, as it were, a lonely and untrodden path." Those who went before him, he wrote, had left only brief fragments, voices calling from far off like torches from a watchtower.

From those fragments Eusebius, bishop of Caesarea on the coast of Roman Palestine, built his History, a book of the church's past from Christ to his own day: its bishops, its writers, its quarrels and its persecutions. He was the first to try, he believed, and he made it from the letters, lists and books of the Christians before him. He lived through the emperors' attack on the church, and he came to know the emperor Constantine and wrote his Life. At Nicaea he signed the creed, the statement of faith of a council, a meeting of bishops, after asking what its words meant. At Tyre a bishop who had suffered in the persecution charged that he had got out of prison by giving way. Later historians began their own books where his History ended.

In Caesarea his friend was Pamphilus, a priest of that church. Eusebius set out his plan in his own list: the lines of bishops in the great churches, the men who spoke or wrote for the faith, those who led others into error, and those who stood firm under torture down to his own day. He did not tell it all in his own words, since he gathered fitting passages from the old writers and copied letters whole.

In 303 the emperors ordered the churches leveled and the Scriptures burned, and soon after, church leaders were jailed and forced by every means to sacrifice, to make an offering to the Roman gods. Eusebius was there, and he wrote of what he saw happen to the churches and to their leaders, whom he calls shepherds: "We saw with our own eyes the houses of prayer thrown down from top to ground, foundations and all. We saw the inspired and holy Scriptures handed to the fire in the middle of the market squares. We saw the shepherds of the churches hiding shamefully here and there, and some of them caught in disgrace and mocked by their enemies." His dearest friend, Pamphilus, was jailed and then killed with his companions. In his History Eusebius set himself a rule: he would not record those who had been shaken by the persecution, only what would help his readers and those who came after them.

By the time of the council at Nicaea in 325, Eusebius was bishop of Caesarea. The church was divided over whether God's Son had always been, or had a beginning, as Arius taught. At Nicaea, Eusebius wrote in a letter to his own church, the emperor urged one added word, the Greek homoousios, saying the Son is of the same substance as the Father. Eusebius asked what it meant, and after questions and answers he signed, he wrote, for the sake of peace and so as not to fall from the right belief. Socrates, a historian writing a century later, says he held back a short time before signing, while Athanasius, then a deacon of the church of Alexandria and there at the council, says he had refused the day before.

In 335 Constantine called bishops to Tyre to judge charges that opponents in Egypt had brought against Athanasius, by then bishop of Alexandria: that a cup used at the Lord's Supper had been broken, and that a priest's hand had been cut off. Epiphanius, a bishop who tells the story about forty years later and on Athanasius's side, calls the charges slanders, and he says the emperor ordered Eusebius to judge, with others.

Potamo, a bishop from Egypt, was a confessor, a Christian who had suffered for the faith in the persecution and lived. When he saw Eusebius seated as judge and Athanasius standing, he wept, and then he spoke. "Tell me, were you not with me in prison in the persecution? I lost an eye for the truth. But you seem to have nothing maimed in your body. You did not suffer as a martyr. You stand alive, with nothing cut away. How did you get out of prison, unless you promised those who forced the persecution on us to do the forbidden thing, or did it?" Eusebius rose in anger and broke up the court. If they came here to speak against him like that, he said, their accusers must be telling the truth, for if they played the tyrant here, they did much more at home.

Later the bishops of Egypt, writing on Athanasius's side, said that their confessors had accused Eusebius of sacrificing. The council removed Athanasius.

After the death of Constantine, whom he had come to know, Eusebius wrote the emperor's Life, a book on his faith. By his own rule he set aside the emperor's wars and laws to speak only of that faith. In its pages Tyre is a cloud of envy over the churches of Egypt, and Athanasius and Potamo go unnamed. Eusebius died after the emperor, in the reign of his sons.

Socrates began his own history of the church with what Eusebius had left out. He also defended Eusebius against the charge of favoring Arius, since he had signed at Nicaea. Jerome, writing in Latin, called his History fine, and called him the open champion of Arius's teaching.
