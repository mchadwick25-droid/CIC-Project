## Blocking findings

**1. ¶9, "In its pages Tyre is a cloud of envy over the churches of Egypt, and Athanasius and Potamo go unnamed."** SOFTENED OR CHANGED. In the Life, the cloud is the envy that stirred up quarrels in Egypt. The council at Tyre is the emperor's answer to it, not the cloud. VC IV.41, EUS 67217–67224: "that spirit of envy which is the enemy of all good, like a dark cloud intercepting the sun's brightest rays, endeavored to mar the joy of this festivity, by again raising contentions to disturb the tranquillity of the Egyptian churches. Our divinely favored emperor, however, once more convened a synod … against this malignant spirit". The "unnamed" half holds: VC IV.41–42 names neither man (a search of the VC text finds "Athanas" only in editors' notes, and no "Potam").
Exact change: "In its pages the quarrel that brought the bishops to Tyre is the work of envy, a dark cloud over the churches of Egypt, and Athanasius and Potamo go unnamed."
Do not say line: "Tyre is a cloud of envy" → "the quarrel that brought the bishops to Tyre is the work of envy, a dark cloud over the churches of Egypt" (VC IV.41).

**2. ¶10, "Jerome, writing in Latin, called his History fine, and called him the open champion of Arius's teaching."** SOFTENED OR CHANGED. Jerome, Letter 84, JEP 18977–18983: "Eusebius has composed a fine history of the Church; yet of these … the latter is the most open champion of the Arian impiety". The brief drops "most" and turns "impiety" into neutral "teaching", which softens a hostile judgment. (The Latin of Letter 84 is not held; Fremantle's English is the only text.) "Fine" holds.
Exact change: "Jerome, writing in Latin, called his History fine, and called him the most open champion of the godless Arian teaching."
Do not say line: "the open champion of Arius's teaching" → "the most open champion of the godless Arian teaching" (JEP 18977–18983).

## SUMMARY HOLDS (each checked)

- ¶1 "Those before him, he wrote, had left only brief fragments, voices calling from far off like torches from a watchtower." HE I.1.4, EUS 6952–6960: "except in brief fragments … From afar they raise their voices like torches, and they cry out, as from some lofty and conspicuous watch-tower".
- ¶2 "set out to tell the church's past from Christ to his own day: its bishops, its writers, its quarrels and its persecutions." HE I.1.1–3, EUS 6878–6901: "from the days of our Saviour to our own … those who have governed … those who … proclaimed the divine word … those who through love of innovation have run into the greatest errors … contended for it in the face of blood and of tortures". "Quarrels" is a loose word for the men who "run into errors", and the outline's gloss holds it.
- ¶2 "The first to try, he believed, he built his History from the letters, lists and books of the Christians before him." EUS 6934–6936 ("I am the first to enter upon the subject"), 6977–6979, 6962–6965 ("appropriate passages from ancient writers"), 6878 ("successions"); letters, EUS 9522–9524, 31310–31316.
- ¶2 "At Tyre a bishop who had suffered in the persecution charged that he had got out of prison by giving way." EPI 44598–44604, "Πῶς ἀνεχώρησας ἀπὸ τῆς φυλακῆς, εἰ μὴ ὅτι ὑπέσχου … τὸ ἀθέμιτον πρᾶξαι, ἢ ἔπραξας;". "Giving way" fairly compresses "promised … the forbidden thing, or did it". This line reaches ahead, and ¶6 credits Epiphanius.
- ¶3 "His plan, in his own list, …" HE I.1.1–3 as above. "Led others into error" compresses "run into the greatest errors … devastated the flock of Christ" (EUS 6888–6891).
- ¶3 "He told it by gathering fitting passages from the old writers and copying letters whole." EUS 6962–6965. "Letters whole" holds best on HE I.13.5, EUS 9521–9524, "to hear the epistles themselves which we have taken from the archives". The Polycrates passage (E5) is a quoted part of a letter: McGiffert calls it a "fragment", EUS 31313.
- ¶4 "In his History he set himself a rule: … only what would help his readers and those after them." HE VIII.2.3, EUS 43448–43453: "only those events which may be usefull first to ourselves and afterwards to posterity".
- ¶5 "By the council at Nicaea in 325 he was bishop, and the church was divided over whether God's Son had always been, or had a beginning, as Arius taught." EUS 2058–2060 ("in the presbytery, and in the Episcopate itself"); Arius, THD 6045–6046, "We are persecuted, because we say that the Son has a beginning".
- ¶5 "Athanasius, then a deacon of Alexandria who was there, says he had refused the day before." ATH 26780–26781, "who had denied the day before, but afterwards subscribed"; SOC 2793–2794, "Athanasius, a deacon of the Alexandrian church". "Refused" is a fair sense of "denied" here, since he denied his signature and then gave it. The Greek at ATHG 74630–74655 is too damaged at this point to check.
- ¶9 "After the death of Constantine, whom he had come to know, Eusebius wrote the emperor's Life, a book on his faith that by its own rule set aside his wars and laws." VC I.11, EUS 60653–60663, 60668–60672; VC I.28, EUS 61199–61200 ("honored with his acquaintance").
- ¶9 "He died after the emperor, in his sons' reign." Socrates II.4, SOC 5802–5804: "At this time Eusebius … having died". Book II tells the reign of Constantine's sons.
