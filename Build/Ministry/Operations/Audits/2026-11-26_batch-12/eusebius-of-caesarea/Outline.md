- **The essence:** Eusebius, bishop of Caesarea on the coast of Roman Palestine, set out to write the church's story from Christ to his own day. He believed he was the first to try, and he built his History from the letters, lists and books of the Christians before him. Later historians of the church began their books where his History ended. He lived through the Great Persecution, knew the emperor Constantine and wrote his Life, and signed the creed of Nicaea after asking what its words meant. At Tyre a bishop who had suffered in the persecution charged that Eusebius had got out of prison by giving way. (E1, E2, E9, E14, E19, E22, I1)
- **Also told:**
  - What the book is: the lines of bishops in the great churches, the writers, those who led others into error, and the persecutions down to his own day, told through passages he gathered from earlier writers and letters he copied whole (E1, E3, E5; Quote 1). Documented, his own words.
  - What he saw and what he chose to tell: the churches thrown down and the Scriptures burned (Quote 2), and his rule to leave out those shaken by the persecution (E10). Documented.
  - The creed signed "after questions", told from three sides (E19, E20, D2), and Tyre told as charges in their own content (E22–E24, D3, D4).
  - Checked against the Library: all held, with four limits. "The first" is his own claim; Sozomen names earlier recorders (D1, card). That he was jailed rests only on Potamo's question in Epiphanius (E13, E22). That he presided at Tyre is only modern (D5): the story says Epiphanius has him among the judges. No old source gives his birth or the year he became bishop (S3, S4).

- **Setting:**
  - Time: the persecution's first orders "in the nineteenth year of the reign of Diocletian", 303 (E7; Widely Accepted); Nicaea, 325 (E18; the year known to every account); Tyre, 335 (E21; Widely Accepted); his death after Constantine's (S4). No birth year (S4). The opening's "when": the first date the record gives is 303, with the persecution's first orders (E7); no held old source dates when he began the History, so "around the year 300" is not told.
  - Place: Caesarea, "on the coast of Roman Palestine" (S2). Tyre (S5), named without a gloss (Cut first 22). Nicaea, "Nicæa in Bithynia" (Socrates I.8, SOC 2652–2654).
  - Tradition: the census tells him within "the Church of Roman Palestine before Constantine" (S2); direct line, Jerusalem's church of worship and pilgrimage (Inferential-Thin), carried only by his orations at the Jerusalem dedication (E26), which are out of the story (Cut first 5); the direct line is not told.
  - Power: the emperors' orders (E7, E8); Constantine (E14–E16, E18, E27).
- **Words to explain:**
  - the persecution: "the emperors' orders against the church: its buildings torn down, its Scriptures burned, then its leaders jailed and forced to sacrifice" (E7, E8). The label "the Great Persecution" is not used (Cut first 20).
  - to sacrifice: "to make an offering to the Roman gods, which the orders forced on jailed church leaders" (E8). Needed again at Tyre (E24).
  - shepherds, in Quote 2: "the leaders of the churches" (E9), in the setup.
  - the History: "a book of the church's past from Christ to his own day: its bishops, its writers, its quarrels and its persecutions" (E1).
  - the added word: "the Greek word *homoousios*, saying the Son is of the same substance as the Father" (E18). Never the barred "of one being".
  - the quarrel: "whether God's Son had always been, or had a beginning, as Arius taught" (E17). Arius: a priest of Alexandria, "one of the presbyters" under its bishop (Socrates I.5, SOC 2063–2076).
  - the council at Tyre: a meeting of bishops the emperor called to judge charges his opponents in Egypt brought against Athanasius, bishop of Alexandria: among them, that a cup used at the Lord's Supper had been broken (EPI 44528–44529, "vas quoddam ut usum divinorum mysteriorum fractum"), and other charges besides (EPI 44530–44531, "de aliis quoque criminibus"). The hand charge is not told (Cut first 33). The Lord's Supper: the church's shared bread and wine, the "divine mysteries" (EPI 44529, "divinorum mysteriorum"). Epiphanius, who tells it, calls them slanders (E21; EPI 44454, 44477, "συκοφάνται"; Petavius's Latin, EPI 44522, 44537, "calumniatores").
  - confessor: "a Christian who suffered for the faith in the persecution and lived" (E22).
  - the Life of Constantine: "a book on the emperor's faith, written after his death, that by its own rule left out his wars and laws" (E27).
- **Shape:** Teacher or thinker. The question he tried to answer: how to tell the church's whole story when, as he saw it, no one had tried, and only fragments lit the way (Opening, Quote 1). What he taught: the History itself, built from others' words (B1), with his own eyes on the persecution (B2). Who learned it: Socrates began where he stopped (What came of it 1); Theodoret and Sozomen, likewise, are on the card (Cut first 6). The record decides the order: the History's making first, then the life told once in time, with the impact after. Trap: a list of books. Only the History and the Life are named as books; the one idea, a church history built from its own documents, is shown through Quote 1 and the gathered letters (B1).
- **The story's own question:** what the record holds of a man under pressure, the teller of others' trials whose own conduct was put on trial. He chose to leave out those shaken by the persecution (B2); at Tyre a confessor asked how he got out of prison (B6); his own Life tells Tyre without the charge (B7). It resolves where the record does: charges told as charges, never settled (D4). The narrator never joins these by comment.

- **Openings:**
  1. The first page (E2, Quote 1). Eusebius of Caesarea opened his History of the Church with an admission. Quote 1. Those before him, he wrote, had left only brief fragments, voices calling from far off like torches from a watchtower (E2, narrated). Leads into the essence: he built the church's story from those fragments. Preferred.
  2. The broken churches (E9, Quote 2): the eyewitness line first, then the essence. Use only if Opening 1 is not; B2 then tells the edict and his rule without Quote 2.
- **Story beats:**
  1. **Caesarea, and how he worked.** Friend of the priest Pamphilus (S1; one clause; his priesthood from HE VII.32.25, EUS 43037–43038: "esteemed worthy of the office of presbyter in that parish", own words, Documented). Eusebius planned to trace the lines of bishops of the great churches, those who spoke or wrote the faith, those who led others into error, and those who stood firm under torture down to his own day (E1). He gathered fitting passages from old writers (E3) and copied letters whole (E5); Polycrates's letter as the example is on the card (Cut first 7). Explain it: the History (E1), once, where the essence tells it (Cut first 18). Confidence: Documented, his own words.
  2. **The persecution, seen.** In 303 the emperors ordered the churches leveled and the Scriptures burned; soon after, church leaders were jailed and forced by every means to sacrifice (E7, E8). Quote 2, with shepherds glossed in the setup. His dearest friend Pamphilus was jailed and killed with his companions (E12). In his History he set a rule: he would not record those shaken by the persecution, only what would help his readers and those after them (E10). Reason for the orders: none given in the lines used; tell the orders as given. Confidence: Documented, eyewitness.
  3. **Bishop, and the emperor.** Dropped: "by 325 he was bishop" is told in B4, "whom he had come to know" in B7; the rest is on the card or Leave out (Cut first 2, 9, 10).
  4. **Nicaea.** By the council of 325 he was bishop of Caesarea (S3). The quarrel: Arius taught that God's Son had a beginning (E17, plain sense). At Nicaea, by Eusebius's own letter to his church, the emperor urged one added word, *homoousios* (E18; plain sense). He asked what it meant; after questions and answers he signed, he wrote, for the sake of peace and so as not to fall from the right belief (E19; narrated). Socrates, a century later, says he held back a short time before signing; Athanasius, who was there, says he had refused the day before (E20, D2; Socrates I.8, SOC 2793–2796: 'Athanasius, a deacon of the Alexandrian church'). Confidence: Documented as each one's account; his motive not settled.
  5. **Antioch** (Cut first). One party at Antioch wanted him moved there as bishop; he refused, keeping the church rule that a bishop stays with his first church, and Constantine wrote to praise him (E21-pre). Confidence: Documented (Socrates; the emperor's letter). Eustathius's charge and Eusebius's answer: card (needs a gloss of Sabellius).
  6. **Tyre, 335.** Reason: Constantine called bishops to Tyre to judge charges brought against Athanasius, bishop of Alexandria, by his opponents in Egypt (E21). Epiphanius, writing about forty years later on Athanasius's side, says the emperor ordered Eusebius of Caesarea to judge, with others (E21; credited). Potamo, a bishop from Egypt and a confessor, saw Eusebius seated as judge and Athanasius standing. He wept, then cried out at Eusebius in a loud voice (E22, narrated; EPI 44584–44590, "ἀπετείνατο φωνῇ μεγάλῃ Εὐσεβίῳ λέγων"; Petavius 44668–44669, "magna voce adversus Eusebium invectus est", OCR "inveelus"), then: Quote 3. Eusebius rose in anger and broke up the court, saying that if Athanasius's side came here to speak against him so (the bishops of Egypt called with Athanasius, Potamo among them: EPI 44483–44490, "ἐκλήϑησαν ἐπίσκοποι τῆς Αἰγύπτου … ὑπὸ Ἀθανάσιον … Ἐν οἷς ἦν … Ποτάμων"), their accusers must be telling the truth: if they played the tyrant here, much more at home (E23; narrated, inside Epiphanius's account, not credited to him again). Socrates says the council removed Athanasius as bishop when its inquiry in Egypt sided with his accuser (E25; Socrates I.31–32, SOC 5185–5211: those sent to Mareotis, "having made an ex parte investigation, held that what the accuser said was true"; then "they voted to depose him"; Mareotis by Alexandria, Sozomen I.17, SOC 27741). Later the bishops of Egypt, on Athanasius's side, wrote that he had been accused by their confessors of sacrificing (E24; narrated, "accused" kept; never "to idols"). Rufinus, writing later in Latin, tells a like scene with other men, a confessor and the bishop of Jerusalem, and does not name Eusebius (D3; Cut first). Explain it: Tyre, confessor, to sacrifice. Confidence: Documented as each witness's account; the charge, Contested (D4).
  7. **The Life, and the end.** He spoke at the dedication of the church in Jerusalem (E26; Cut first). After the death of Constantine, whom he had come to know (E15), he wrote the emperor's Life, setting aside, by his own rule, the wars and laws, to speak only of the emperor's faith (E27). In it envy, like a dark cloud, stirs up quarrels in Egypt's churches, and the emperor calls the bishops to Tyre against it, while Athanasius and Potamo go unnamed (E21, VC IV.41, EUS 67217–67227, own words). He died after the emperor, in his sons' reign (S4). Confidence: Documented.
- **What came of it** (ranked; about an eighth of the story):
  1. **Others began where he stopped.** Socrates began his own history with what Eusebius had left out (I1). Documented. Theodoret and Sozomen, who likewise began after him (I1), are on the card (Cut first 6).
  2. **Judged both ways.** Socrates defended Eusebius against the charge of favoring Arius, since he had signed at Nicaea (I3). Jerome called his History fine and him the most open champion of Arius's ungodly teaching (I3; JEP 18977–18983). One friendly and one hostile voice in the story, told with no signpost sentence before them (Cut first 26); the story ends here (Ending); Athanasius on the Euphration letter, Philostorgius and the council of 787 go to the card. Contested.
  3. **Defended:** his own reading of the creed: his questions on the added word and his reasons for signing, in his letter (E19), in B4. Origen's good name, with Pamphilus (E6), and his church's creed read and praised are on the card (Cut first 29, 30).
  4. His pages keep earlier writers' letters whole (E5, I4): told in B1 only, as "copied letters whole"; the "word for word" line is out (Cut first 19); "known only through him" is modern, card's Modern works.

- **Connection to carry into the story:** What passed from Eusebius was a way of telling the church's past, and the book itself. He told it through the church's own documents, its lists of bishops, its writers and its letters, quoted in their own words (E1, E3–E5), and those who came after took up the story where his ended: Socrates in his first lines (I1); Theodoret and Sozomen likewise, on the card (Cut first 6). The writer must make plain that they did not only praise him: they used and continued him, and some judged him hard (I3), and that the charges at Tyre came from his opponents' side and stand as charges (D4). Carry it through the Opening, B1, What came of it and the Ending, never as a "this matters because" sentence.
