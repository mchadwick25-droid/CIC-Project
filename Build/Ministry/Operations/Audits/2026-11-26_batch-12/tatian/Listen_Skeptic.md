## 1. The test (person kit)

- **Who he was:** filled. A man from Assyria, trained in Greek speaking, who became a Christian, heard Justin, wrote the Address to the Greeks and made one Gospel from four.
- **What happened:** filled. He converted, wrote, was threatened by Crescens, taught at Rome, left the church after Justin's death, set up a school in the East, and his book was later gathered up by Theodoret.
- **What it was about:** filled for the Address (Greek learning is younger and borrowed; the "barbarian" writings of Moses are older and truer). Partly filled for the break. I can say "he rejected marriage". I cannot say why aeons or Adam's fate were faults (see 4).
- **Why the connection matters:** thinly filled. I can say "his one-book Gospel was what the Syriac churches read until a bishop replaced it." But I cannot tell that Cyrrhus was one of those churches, or who Ephrem was. So the link to the Syriac tradition rests on one clause in paragraph 2 and must be rebuilt from there. Not blocking, because I can fill it, but this is the weakest line.

## 2. The opening

The first three sentences: "Tatian tells it himself. … at Rome, he says, he found Jupiter honored with human blood. He went off alone and asked how he might find the truth."

- **Who:** named, but nothing about him yet (no people, no role). He becomes "a Christian" only in sentence 4. Not lost.
- **Where:** Rome. Clear.
- **When:** missing until sentence 4 ("the middle of the second century").
- **Why there:** missing. I don't know why he was in Rome or why the rites troubled him enough to leave.
- "Tatian tells it himself" points forward to an "it" not yet heard. On one hearing it is a small stumble.

## 3. Sentences that fail on one hearing

1. "Tatian tells it himself." The meaning lost: what "it" is.
2. "Irenaeus, his opponent, says he heard the teacher Justin and left the church after Justin died." "His" could be Justin's opponent. "Heard" can be taken literally by a second-language listener when it means "studied under."
3. "The Greeks called every other people barbarians, and Tatian took the word for his own faith." "Took the word for" can be heard as "mistook". The meaning is that he used the word for his own faith.
4. "A pupil from Asia, Rhodon, says Tatian taught him at Rome." "Asia" is heard as the continent. And I can't tell if this teaching came before or after the break.
5. "As long as he was with him, he let nothing of this kind show." This comes before the charges. So "of this kind" points to nothing I have heard, and "he … him" leaves me to work out who is who.
6. "And on his own he taught that Adam, the first man, was not saved, because, a later Roman teacher explains, Adam began disobedience." The citation is nested inside the "because". I lose whose reason this is, Tatian's or the Roman teacher's, and "Adam began disobedience" is hard to parse.
7. "Eusebius, about 150 years after the break, says Tatian combined the Gospels into one, how he could not say, and called it the Diatessaron…" (34 words). "How he could not say" interrupts the sentence, and on one hearing "he" could be Tatian.

None of these loses the thread of the story. All MINOR.

## 4. Bare statements (Part 1, Explain it) — BLOCKING

1. "Like the followers of Valentinus, Tatian spoke of aeons, invisible heavenly powers." It says what aeons are, but not why speaking of them counted against him. Valentinus is a name that carries no meaning for a newcomer.
2. "And on his own he taught that Adam … was not saved." It does not say why this mattered to the church. The clause about the Roman teacher gives a reason for the teaching, not for why it was a fault.
3. "Later writers of the Church of the East say Ephrem the Syrian wrote on the Diatessaron." Ephrem's writing is named, but not what it was or why it mattered. Neither Ephrem nor the Church of the East is explained.
4. "Even believers true to the apostles' teaching … used it simply as a short book, not seeing the harm." The harm is never said in plain words. A listener with no church background does not know why removing Jesus's descent from David matters. The ending turns on this harm.

Exact change: give each one its plain sense from the record, or cut it, as Part 1 directs. For 3, if the outline gives no plain line, cut the sentence. Its link to the Syriac churches already comes from Theodoret, if Cyrrhus is placed (see 7).

## 5. Citations read aloud not called for

The craft check flagged s18, s20, s31 and s32. My judgment on each:
- s18 "his Address says": called for. This is Tatian's claim, not fact. It reads naturally.
- s20 Rhodon: crediting the only witness is fine. As a lone sentence it plays as a footnote. Narrator's voice: "At Rome he took pupils of his own, among them Rhodon, from the province of Asia."
- s31 "the only writer to name the place": MINOR, not called for in the audio. This belongs on the card. Narrator's voice: "Some two centuries later, Epiphanius says Tatian crossed to the East and set up his school in Mesopotamia."
- s32 "about 150 years after the break": called for, because the source wrote long after.

Also not called for:
- "Eusebius's Chronicle names Tatian…": the book's title is not needed. Narrator's voice: "Eusebius makes Tatian the source of the Encratites, about 172, under the emperor Marcus Aurelius."
- "a later Roman teacher explains": an unnamed witness inside a "because" (see 3.6). Narrator's voice: tell the reason as Irenaeus's own, or cut the clause.

All MINOR.

## 6. Tells and machine-made lines

**Part 1 tells:** none. There is no "not X but Y" turn ("citing … rather than Christian ones" gives information), no dash for drama, no question asked to answer itself, no stock word and no moral close. The ending is a scene.

**Lines that sound machine-made:**
1. "Tatian tells it himself." A stagy cold-open frame that announces the telling before telling it.
2. "The answer he found made him a Christian…" A neat setup-and-payoff hinge from the opening into the essence.
3. "Irenaeus brings three charges." A signposted list of three ("Like … Like … And on his own"). It is one of the commonest machine patterns.
4. "He calls Moses the founder of all barbarian wisdom, so he meant the writings of Moses and the prophets." A narrator footnote glued on after the quote.
5. "Some still had it, he says, and the Address was Tatian's most used book." Two unrelated facts joined by "and", like inventory.

**Whole-text pattern (BLOCKING):** in paragraphs 7 to 9 the brief becomes a survey of witnesses. In order: Irenaeus, a later Roman teacher, Irenaeus, Eusebius's Chronicle, Epiphanius, Eusebius, "later writers of the Church of the East", then Theodoret. "Says" appears 11 times in 673 words. Part 1 asks for "never a report about the record". Here the narrator stops telling and starts citing, and that is what makes it sound assembled rather than told. The cure is not to cut the credits that sources disagreeing or writing long after require. Tell the events in the narrator's voice, and credit a witness only where Part 1 calls for it.

## 8. Attention

- **Pull of the opening:** yes. "Jupiter honored with human blood" is a strong, concrete image, and the triple "I was won over" quote is the most alive passage in the piece.
- **Where attention first drifts:** s20, "A pupil from Asia, Rhodon, says Tatian taught him at Rome." It stands alone, and nothing follows from it. It does not say what he taught or what came of it. Attention is truly lost at "Eusebius's Chronicle names Tatian as the source of the Encratites, about 172…". By then the story has given way to a list of who said what, and the listener is tracking witnesses, not Tatian.
- **Would I listen to the end:** yes, just. The Theodoret scene (two hundred copies, gathered and put away) wins attention back and is a good close.
