Tatian tells it himself. He had been initiated into the mysteries, the secret rites of the gods, and at Rome, he says, he found Jupiter honored with human blood. He went off alone and asked how he might find the truth.

The answer he found made him a Christian in the middle of the second century. In his Address to the Greeks, a book written as a speech, he scorned their learning as younger and worse than the old writings of Moses. Irenaeus, his opponent, says he heard the teacher Justin and left the church after Justin died. He also wove the four Gospels into one book, read in worship by the Syriac-speaking churches of the East.

Born, he says, in the land of the Assyrians, he was trained in Greek learning, followed rhetoric, the art of public speaking, and visited many lands. The Greeks called every other people barbarians, and Tatian took the word for his own faith. "While I was thinking hard about what mattered, I came upon some barbarian writings. They were older than the teachings of the Greeks, and more divine than their error. I was won over by their plain words, and by the artlessness of those who spoke them. I was won over by their clear account of how all things were made, and by their foreknowledge of what was to come. I was won over by their excellent commands, and by the rule of one over all things." He calls Moses the founder of all barbarian wisdom, so he meant the writings of Moses and the prophets.

Tatian charges that Crescens, a philosopher at Rome who claimed to despise death, so feared it that he worked to bring death on Justin, and on Tatian too. Justin, he says, had shown the philosophers to be gluttons and cheats.

Every art the Greeks boast of, his Address says, came from barbarians: astronomy from Babylon, geometry from Egypt, letters from Phoenicia. And Moses is older than Homer, he argues, citing Greek, Babylonian, Phoenician and Egyptian writers rather than Christian ones.

A pupil from Asia, Rhodo, says Tatian taught him at Rome.

Irenaeus, writing not long after, tells what followed Justin's death. "As long as he was with him, he let nothing of this kind show. But after his martyrdom he left the church. Lifted up by the conceit of being a teacher, and puffed up as if he were better than the rest, he set up a school with a character all its own."

Irenaeus brings three charges. Like the followers of Valentinus, Tatian spoke of aeons, invisible heavenly powers. Like Marcion, he called marriage nothing else than corruption and fornication. And on his own he taught that Adam, the first man, was not saved, because, a later Roman teacher explains, Adam began disobedience. Irenaeus puts him among the Encratites, the self-controlled, who refused marriage and meat, a group he says sprang from earlier teachers. Eusebius's Chronicle names Tatian as the source of the Encratites, about 172, in the reign of Marcus Aurelius. Some two centuries later Epiphanius, the only writer to name the place, says Tatian crossed to the East and set up his school in Mesopotamia.

Eusebius, about 150 years after the break, says Tatian combined the Gospels into one, how he could not say, and called it the Diatessaron, Greek for "through four": one gospel story made from the four. Some still had it, he says, and the Address was Tatian's most used book. Later writers of the Church of the East say Ephrem the Syrian wrote on the Diatessaron.

In the 400s Theodoret, bishop of Cyrrhus, found more than two hundred copies held in honor in his churches. Tatian, he says, had cut out the lists of Jesus's ancestors and other passages showing the Lord born of David's line. Even believers true to the apostles' teaching, he writes, used it simply as a short book, not seeing the harm. He gathered them all, put them away, and brought in the Gospels of the four evangelists.
