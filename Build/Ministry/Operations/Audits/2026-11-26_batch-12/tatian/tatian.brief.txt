Tatian says he had been initiated into the mysteries, the secret rites of the gods, and that at Rome he found Jupiter honored with human blood. He went off alone and asked how he might find the truth.

The answer he found made him a Christian in the middle of the second century. In his Address to the Greeks, a book written as a speech, he scorned their learning as younger and worse than the old writings of Moses. Irenaeus, his opponent, says he heard Justin teach and left the church after Justin died. He also wove the four Gospels into one book, read in worship by the Syriac-speaking churches of the East. In the 400s one bishop put away the copies in his own churches.

Born, he says, in the land of the Assyrians, he was trained in Greek learning and followed rhetoric, the art of public speaking. The Greeks called every other people barbarians, and Tatian claimed the word for his own faith. "While I was thinking hard about what mattered, I came upon some barbarian writings. They were older than the teachings of the Greeks, and more divine than their error. I was won over by their plain words, and by the artlessness of those who spoke them. I was won over by their clear account of how all things were made, and by their foreknowledge of what was to come. I was won over by their excellent commands, and by the rule of one over all things." He calls Moses the founder of all barbarian wisdom, so he meant the writings of Moses and the prophets.

Every art the Greeks boast of, his Address says, came from barbarians: astronomy from Babylon, geometry from Egypt, letters from Phoenicia. And Moses is older than Homer, he argues, citing Greek, Chaldean, Phoenician and Egyptian writers rather than Christian ones.

Tatian charges that Crescens, a philosopher at Rome who claimed to despise death, so feared it that he worked to bring death on Justin, and on Tatian too. Justin, he says, had shown the philosophers to be gluttons and cheats. At Rome Tatian taught Rhodon, a pupil from Asia.

Irenaeus, writing not long after, tells what Tatian taught after Justin's death. "As long as he was with him, he let nothing of this kind show. But after his martyrdom he left the church. Lifted up by the conceit of being a teacher, and puffed up as if he were better than the rest, he set up a school with a character all its own."

Irenaeus says Tatian spoke of aeons, invisible heavenly powers, like the followers of Valentinus, a teacher at Rome. Like Marcion, another Roman teacher, he called marriage nothing else than corruption and fornication. And on his own he taught that Adam, the first man, was not saved. A later Roman teacher gives the reason: disobedience began with Adam. Irenaeus puts him among the Encratites, the self-controlled, who refused marriage, and some of them meat, a group he says sprang from earlier teachers. Eusebius, a bishop, makes Tatian the source of the Encratites, about 172. Some two centuries later Epiphanius, a bishop in Cyprus, says Tatian crossed to the East and set up his school in Mesopotamia.

Eusebius, about 150 years after the break, did not know how Tatian combined the Gospels into one. He says Tatian called it the Diatessaron, Greek for "through four": one gospel story made from the four. Tatian's most used book, he says, was the Address. Later writers of the Church of the East say Ephrem, a deacon of Nisibis, wrote a commentary explaining the Diatessaron.

Theodoret, bishop of Cyrrhus under Antioch, found more than two hundred copies held in honor in his churches. Tatian, he says, had cut out the lists of Jesus's ancestors and other passages showing the Lord born of David's line. Even believers true to the apostles' teaching, he writes, used it simply as a short book, not seeing the harm. He gathered them all, put them away, and brought in the four Gospels.
