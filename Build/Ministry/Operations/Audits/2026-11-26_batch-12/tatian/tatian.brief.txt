Tatian tells the story himself. He had been initiated into the mysteries, the secret rites of the gods, and at Rome, he says, he found Jupiter honored with human blood. So he went off by himself and asked how he might find the truth.

His answer made him a Christian in the middle of the second century. In his Address to the Greeks, a book written as a speech, he scorned their learning as younger and worse than the old writings of Moses. Irenaeus, his opponent, says he heard the teacher Justin and left the church after Justin died. Tatian also wove the four Gospels into one book, which the Syriac-speaking churches of the East read in worship.

He was born, he says, in the land of the Assyrians, and trained first in Greek learning. He followed rhetoric, the art of public speaking, and visited many lands. Then his search led him to old writings. The Greeks called every other people barbarians, and Tatian took the word for his own faith. "While I was thinking hard about what mattered, I came upon some barbarian writings. They were older than the teachings of the Greeks, and more divine than their error. I was won over by their plain words, and by the artlessness of those who spoke them. I was won over by their clear account of how all things were made, and by their foreknowledge of what was to come. I was won over by their excellent commands, and by the rule of one over all things." Since he calls Moses the founder of all barbarian wisdom, these were the writings of Moses and the prophets.

Tatian charges that Crescens, a philosopher at Rome who claimed to despise death, so feared it that he worked to bring death on Justin, and on Tatian too. Justin, he says, had shown the philosophers to be gluttons and cheats.

Every art the Greeks boast of, the Address says, came from barbarians: astronomy from Babylon, geometry from Egypt, letters from Phoenicia. And Moses is older than Homer, as Tatian proves from Greek, Babylonian, Phoenician and Egyptian writers rather than Christian ones.

A pupil from Asia, Rhodo, says Tatian taught him at Rome.

Writing not long after, Irenaeus tells what happened once Justin died. "As long as he was with him, he let nothing of this kind show. But after his martyrdom he left the church. Lifted up by the conceit of being a teacher, and puffed up as if he were better than the rest, he set up a school with a character all its own."

He brings three charges. Like the followers of Valentinus, Tatian spoke of aeons, invisible heavenly powers. Like Marcion, he called marriage nothing else than corruption and fornication. And on his own he taught that Adam, the first man, was not saved, because, a later Roman teacher explains, Adam began disobedience. Irenaeus puts him among the Encratites, the self-controlled, who refused marriage and meat, a group he says sprang from earlier teachers. Eusebius's Chronicle places Tatian about 172, in the reign of Marcus Aurelius, as the one from whom the Encratites come. Some two centuries later Epiphanius, the only writer to name the place, says Tatian crossed to the East and set up his school in Mesopotamia.

Eusebius, writing about 150 years after the break, says Tatian combined the Gospels into one, how he could not say, and called it the Diatessaron, Greek for "through four": one gospel story made from the four. Some still had it in his day, he says, and he calls the Address the most used of Tatian's books. The Syriac-speaking churches read the Diatessaron, and later writers of the Church of the East say Ephrem the Syrian wrote on it.

In the 400s Theodoret, bishop of Cyrrhus, found more than two hundred copies held in honor in his churches. Tatian, he says, had cut out the lists of Jesus's ancestors and the other passages that show the Lord born of David's line. Even believers who held to the apostles' teaching used it, not seeing the harm, simply as a short book. Theodoret gathered them all, put them away, and brought in the Gospels of the four evangelists.
