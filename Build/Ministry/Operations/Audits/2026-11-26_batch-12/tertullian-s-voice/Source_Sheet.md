**Witness kinds.**
- OWN: his own books, written at Carthage under the emperors Severus and Caracalla (Jerome, below). Nearly all that is known of his life is in them and in Jerome (EUS-ed 10267–10268, McGiffert: "Our main sources for a knowledge of his life are his own writings, and Jerome's de vir. ill. chap. 53").
- LATER, about a century: Eusebius (Church History, early 4th c.); Lactantius (Divine Institutes, about 305–310).
- LATER, about 170 to 200 years: Jerome. Against Helvidius, "This tract appeared about a.d. 383" (JERW 32903–32904, editor); Lives of Illustrious Men, written to "the fourteenth year of the Emperor Theodosius" (JER 38784–38785), that is 392/3 (the note's "a.d. 492" is a misprint); Letter 84, "The date of the letter is 400 a.d." (JERW 18931–18932, editor).
- LATER, about two centuries: Augustine, On Heresies, "Scriptus circiter annum 428" (AUG 1098, the Maurist editors); Vincent of Lérins, Commonitory, 434 (VIN 11809, Heurtley).
- No other contemporary writes of him. No late legend is held.

## 1. Setting

S1. **Carthage, Roman Africa.** Jerome: "was from the city of Carthage in the province of Africa" (JER 40496); Latin "provinciae Africae, civitatis Carthaginiensis" (JERL 55614, OCR "provincie Africe"). LATER, ~170 years. Documented. Census place: Carthage, Tunisia (CEN `modernCountries`). Plain words: "Carthage, in Roman Africa, today in Tunisia".

S2. **His time.** Jerome: "he flourished chiefly in the reign of the emperor Severus and Antoninus Caracalla" (JER 40498–40499); Latin "sub Severo principe et Antonino Caracalla maxime floruit" (JERL 55616–55617). LATER. Documented for the reigns. Census `dates` "c. 197-220" (CEN): a map placing. Plain words: "around the year 200".

S3. **Tradition.** Census `toldWithin`: `latin-apologists`, informal name "The Case Made in Latin" (CEN). Plain words: "the Christians who first made the faith's case in Latin".

S4. **His father.** Jerome's Latin: "patre centurione proconsulari" (JERL 55614–55615, OCR "GCenturione"): his father was a centurion on the governor's staff. Richardson's English, "son of a proconsul or Centurion" (JER 40497), misreads it. Jerome's Chronicle: "Tertullianus Afer centurionis proconsularis fllius omnium ecdesiarum / sermone celebratur" (JERC 43479–43482, OCR "fllius", "ecdesiarum"): "Tertullian the African, son of a proconsular centurion, is celebrated in the speech of all the churches." LATER, one witness twice. Documented as Jerome's. Card only.

S5. **Versed in Roman law.** Eusebius: "These things are recorded by Tertullian, […] a man well versed in the laws of the Romans, […] and in other respects of high repute, and one of those especially distinguished in Rome" (EUS 10241–10242, 10272–10273, 10279–10280). LATER, ~a century. Documented as Eusebius's word. McGiffert's note: "His accurate acquaintance with the laws of the Romans is not very conspicuous in his writings" (EUS-ed 10273–10274), and "He very likely practiced law and rhetoric in Rome until his conversion" (EUS-ed 10291–10292): editor, a claim of his own. Monceaux: his legal learning is beyond question ("Ce qui est incontestable, et intéressant, c'est la solidité de son éducation juridique", MON 15498–15499), but the jurist Tertullian of the law books is more likely a relative ("Nous croirions plus volontiers que le juriste romain est un parent de l'apologiste", MON 15492–15493): modern. **Contested** that he worked as an advocate; the story says only what Eusebius says.

S6. **The law court's word.** Holmes's note on the title: "The term præscriptio is a legal one, meaning a demurrer, or formal objection" (ANF3-ed 20037–20038); and on *constitutionis*, "a term of the law courts" (ANF3-ed 20779–20780). Editor, clarifying the text. Widely Accepted. Plain words: "a plea from the law courts that throws a case out before it is argued".

S7. **A convert.** "Once these things were with us, too, the theme of ridicule. We are of your stock and nature: men are made, not born, Christians" (ANF3 2710–2712, Apology 18). Latin: "Haec et nos risimus aliquando. De yestris "siimus. / Fiunl, non nascuntur ^Christiani*" (OEH1 18939–18940; *Haec et nos risimus aliquando. De vestris sumus. Fiunt, non nascuntur Christiani*). OWN. Documented that he came to the faith from the pagans. When and why: no source says; "converted to Christianity probably between 180 and 190" is McGiffert's (EUS-ed 10249–10250).

## 2. Events, in the order the story would tell them

E1. **The Apology: to the governors.** "Rulers of the Roman Empire, if, seated for the administration of justice on your lofty tribunal, […] you may not openly inquire into and sift before the world the real truth in regard to the charges made against the Christians; […] if, finally, the extreme severities inflicted on our people in recently private judgments, stand in the way of our being permitted to defend ourselves before you, you cannot surely forbid the Truth to reach your ears by the secret pathway of a noiseless book. […] One thing, meanwhile, she anxiously desires of earthly rulers—not to be condemned unknown" (ANF3 1448–1465). Latin, opening: "Si non licet vobis, Romani imperii antistites*, in aperto *et … edito, in ipso fere yertice civitatis praesidentibus ad iudicandum" (OEH1 11033–11034); and "liceat yeritati yel oc- / cnlta yia tacitarum litterarum' ad aures vestras pervenire. … Unum gestit interdum, ne ignorata damneUir" (OEH1 11256–11261; *liceat veritati vel occulta via tacitarum litterarum ad aures vestras pervenire … Unum gestit interdum, ne ignorata damnetur*). OWN. Documented. The addressees are the *antistites* "presiding to judge" (OEH1 11033–11034): the provincial governors sitting as judges. **Why he wrote it** (his reason): they would not hear the case openly, and recent harsh private trials blocked a defense (ANF3 1449–1459). **The charge as he names it:** "your hatred to the name of Christian is unjust" (ANF3 1472–1473), since they hate "a thing of which you know nothing" (ANF3 1475–1476); and those who come to know it "lay down at once their enmity. From being its haters they become its disciples" (ANF3 1488–1490). **Date:** Coxe, "Great diversity exists … Mosheim says, a.d. 198, Kaye a.d. 204" (ANF3-ed 1443–1446); McGiffert, "almost all scholars are agreed in assigning it to the years 197–204", and after Bonwetsch, Harnack and Nöldechen its date of 197 "may be accepted as practically established" (EUS-ed 10296–10303): editors. Widely Accepted for about 197; the story says "around 200" (S2).

E2. **The crowd's cry.** "they think the Christians the cause of every public disaster, of every affliction with which the people are visited. If the Tiber rises as high as the city walls, if the Nile does not send its waters up over the fields, if the heavens give no rain, if there is an earthquake, if there is famine or pestilence, straightway the cry […] is, 'Away with the Christians to the lion!'" (ANF3 3995–4002, Apology 40). Latin: "Si Tibe- / ris ascendil *in moenia, si Nilus non ascendit *in anra, si caehim / stetit^, si terra "^movit^ si fames, "siiues, statim Christianos / ad leonem! " addamatur^" (OEH1 27573–27576; *Si Tiberis ascendit in moenia, si Nilus non ascendit in arva, si caelum stetit, si terra movit, si fames, si lues, statim "Christianos ad leonem!" adclamatur*). Coxe's note gives the Latin "Christianos ad leonem" (ANF3-ed 4000–4001): singular, "the lion". OWN. Documented as what he reports. **Why the cry** (his account): the crowd blamed the Christians for every disaster.

E3. **The Apology's close.** "kill us, torture us, condemn us, grind us to dust; your injustice is the proof that we are innocent. […] The oftener we are mown down by you, the more in number we grow; the blood of Christians is seed" (ANF3 4653–4661, Apology 50). Latin: "^Flures ^'effidmur quotiens metimur a vobis; semen est sanguis / Christianorum" (OEH1 30954–30955; *Plures efficimur quotiens metimur a vobis; semen est sanguis Christianorum*). OWN. Documented. The common form "the blood of the martyrs is the seed of the church" is not his wording. Card.

E4. **The Prescription: whose are the Scriptures?** Holmes's title note (S6). The argument, in his words: "we oppose to them this step above all others, of not admitting them to any discussion of the Scriptures. […] it ought to be clearly seen to whom belongs the possession of the Scriptures, that none may be admitted to the use thereof who has no title at all to the privilege" (ANF3 20759–20768, ch. 15); the first question is "With whom lies that very faith to which the Scriptures belong. […] From what and through whom, and when, and to whom, has been handed down that rule […] by which men become Christians?" (ANF3 20860–20866, ch. 19); what Christ revealed to the apostles can "properly be proved in no other way than by those very churches which the apostles founded in person" (ANF3 20938–20941, ch. 21). Then: "run over the apostolic churches, in which the very thrones […] of the apostles are still pre-eminent in their places, […] Achaia is very near you, (in which) you find Corinth. […] Since you are able to cross to Asia, you get Ephesus. Since, moreover, you are close upon Italy, […] you have Rome" (ANF3 21655–21676, ch. 36). OWN. Documented as his argument. **What a heretic was, in his own terms:** "as they are heretics, they cannot be true Christians, because it is not from Christ that they get that which they pursue of their own mere choice, and from the pursuit incur and admit the name of heretics" (ANF3 21730–21734, ch. 37; the Greek word behind "heretic" means a choosing, as Holmes's note quoting Dodgson says, ANF3-ed 21734–21737). Therefore, "not being Christians, they have acquired […] no right to the Christian Scriptures" (ANF3 21740–21742). **Their side**, as he gives it: they "put forward the Scriptures" (ANF3 20755–20756) and argue from them. No held writing of theirs answers this book. **Date:** Monceaux's second period, about 200 to 206 (MON 17156–17162): modern, Dominant Modern Reconstruction. No year in the story.

E6. **The turn: the New Prophecy.** In Against Praxeas he names its prophets, "Montanus, Prisca, and Maximilla" (ANF3 51046–51047; KRO 11732–11733 "prophetias Mon- / tani Priscae Maximillae"). The Paraclete, in his words: "the Paraclete, who leads men indeed into all truth" (ANF3 51099–51100); Latin "per paracle- / tum, deductorem scilicet omnis ueritatis" (KRO 11770–11771): "the Paraclete, the guide into all truth". **His break, in his words:** "We indeed, on our part, subsequently withdrew from the carnally-minded on our acknowledgment and maintenance of the Paraclete" (ANF3 51074–51076); Latin "et nos quidem postea agnitio paracleti atque defen- / sio disiunxit a psychicis" (KRO 11745–11746): "and later our acceptance and defense of the Paraclete parted us from the psychics". *Psychici*, "the natural men", is his name for church members who refused the new prophets (Holmes's note, ANF3-ed 51070–51072, points to his use elsewhere). OWN. Documented that he says this. **From whom he parted** is read differently by editors: Coxe, "This statement may only denote a withdrawal from the communion of the Bishop of Rome, […] it does not, necessarily, […] define his ultimate separation from his own bishop and the North-African church" (ANF3-ed 51076–51081); Monceaux, Against Praxeas is the book "où l'auteur parle lui-même de sa rupture avec les catholiques" (MON 17465–17466): both modern. **Contested.**

E7. **What the New Prophecy asked, in his late books.** One marriage only: "Heretics do away with marriages; Psychics accumulate them. The former marry not even once; the latter not only once" (ANF4 5731–5733, On Monogamy 1). Stricter fasts: the psychics accuse "the spiritual discipline, […] imposing, as it does, reins upon the appetite, through taking, sometimes no meals, or late meals, or dry meals, just as upon lust, through allowing but one marriage" (ANF4 9760–9765, On Fasting 1). No flight in persecution: the Spirit "incites all almost to go and offer themselves in martyrdom, not to flee from it" (ANF4 11455–11457, Flight in Persecution 9). OWN. Documented. The `montanism-the-new-prophecy` story tells these rules; here one clause.

E8. **Why he turned: two later witnesses.** Jerome: "He was presbyter of the church until middle life, afterwards driven by the envy and abuse of the clergy of the Roman church, he lapsed to the doctrine of Montanus, and mentions the new prophecy in many of his books" (JER 40506–40509); Latin "invidia / postea et contumeliis clericorum Romane Eeccle- / si», ad Montani dogma delapsus" (JERL 55627–55629). Augustine: "Non ergo ideo est Tertullianus factus hereticus: / sed quia transiens ad Cataphrygas, quosante destru- / xerat, ccpit etiam secundas nuptias contra apostoli- / cam doctrinam (I Tim. 1v, 3) tanquam stupra dam- / nare, et postmodum etiam ab ipsis divisus, sua con- / ventieula propagavit" (AUG 2868–2873): "So Tertullian was not made a heretic for that, but because, going over to the Cataphrygians, whom he had once overthrown, he began also to condemn second marriages as fornication, against the apostle's teaching; and afterwards, divided from them too, he spread gatherings of his own." *Cataphrygians*, "those of the Phrygians", is a hostile name for the New Prophecy. LATER, each about two centuries. **Contested:** Jerome gives a grievance at Rome; Augustine a teaching and a second break. Credit each. That he was a presbyter rests on Jerome alone (EUS-ed 10257–10258, McGiffert: "That he was a presbyter rests only upon the authority of Jerome").

E9. **Against Praxeas: who Praxeas was, by his account.** "the devil […] maintains that there is one only Lord, the Almighty Creator of the world, in order that out of this doctrine of the unity he may fabricate a heresy. He says that the Father Himself came down into the Virgin, was Himself born of her, Himself suffered, indeed was Himself Jesus Christ" (ANF3 51010–51016); Latin "ipsum dicit patrem descendisse in uirginem, ipsum ex ea / natum, ipsum passum; denique ipsum esse lesum Christum" (KRO 11692–11693). Praxeas "was the first to import into Rome from Asia this kind of heretical pravity, a man in other respects of restless disposition, and above all inflated with the pride of confessorship simply and solely because he had to bear for a short time the annoyance of a prison" (ANF3 51038–51041; KRO 11727–11729). The bishop of Rome "had acknowledged the prophetic gifts of Montanus, Prisca, and Maximilla", and Praxeas, "by importunately urging false accusations against the prophets themselves and their churches, […] compelled him to recall the pacific letter" (ANF3 51046–51053). "By this Praxeas did a twofold service for the devil at Rome: he drove away prophecy, and he brought in heresy; he put to flight the Paraclete, and he crucified the Father" (ANF3 51054–51057); Latin "ita duo / negotia diaboli Praxeas Romae procurauit: prophetiam expulit / et haeresim intulit, paracletum fugauit et patrem crucifixit" (KRO 11737–11739). OWN, a hostile witness: Documented as his account; what Praxeas taught is known only from it. Coxe names the bishop "Probably Victor" (ANF3-ed 51044): editor; leave unnamed. That line is quoted on the census edge `tertullian-s-voice--modalist-monarchianism`; not used as a quote here. **Written after the turn:** "more especially since we have been better instructed by the Paraclete" (ANF3 51098–51099; KRO 11770). Documented.

E10. **Against Praxeas: his answer, and the words.** The rule: "this one only God has also a Son, His Word" (ANF3 51101–51102; KRO 11772–11773 "ut unici dei sit et filius, sermo ipsius"). The dispensation "which distributes the Unity into a Trinity, placing in their order the three Persons—the Father, the Son, and the Holy Ghost: three, however, not in condition, but in degree; not in substance, but in form; not in power, but in aspect; yet of one substance, and of one condition, and of one power, inasmuch as He is one God" (ANF3 51144–51149); Latin "quae unitatem in trinitatem disponit, tres dirigens / patrem et filiiim et spiiitum, — tres autem non statu, sed graclu. / nec substantia, secl forma, nec potestate, sed specie, — unius / autem substantiae et unius status et unius potestatis, quia / unus deus" (KRO 11802, 11815–11818). Holmes's "Persons" here is his supply: the Latin has only "tres", three. Ch. 12, after "Let there be light" and the Word who makes it: "then you have two Beings—One that commands that the thing be made, and the Other that executes the order and creates. In what sense, however, you ought to understand Him to be another, I have already explained, on the ground of Personality, not of Substance—in the way of distinction, not of division. […] But although I must everywhere hold one only substance in three coherent and inseparable (Persons), yet I am bound to acknowledge, from the necessity of the case, that He who issues a command is different from Him who executes it" (ANF3 51893–51902). Latin: "habes duos, aiium dicentem, / ut fiat, alium facientem. alium autem quomodo accipere / debeas, iam professus sum, personae, non substantiae nomine, / ad distmccionem, non ad diuisionem. ceterum, (etsi) ubique / teneo unam substantiam in tribus cohaerentibus, tamen aiium / dicam oportet ex necessitate sensus eum, qui iubet, et eum, / qui facit" (KRO 12557–12563; OCR "aiium" = *alium*, "distmccionem" = *distinctionem*). Oehler agrees: "personae, non substantiae, nomine, ad distin- / ctionem, non ad divisionem. ''Ceterum ubique teneam' unam sub* / stantiam in tribus cohaerentibus" (OEH2 43866–43868). Holmes's "inseparable (Persons)" is his supply; the Latin has "in three that cohere". OWN. Documented. **The words** *trinitas* (KRO 11802; also CSEL20 15308 "trinitas unius diuinitatis"), *persona* and *substantia* (KRO 12559, 12561) stand in his text. Documented. **What they mean, from his own lines:** substance is what God is, one and not divided ("unius substantiae … quia unus deus"); person is how the Son is "another" than the Father, "for distinction, not division". **That he coined them or was the first to use them in Latin:** census `why`, "he invented them" (CEN), and modern editors; no old held source says it. Inferential-Thin as a claim of first use; stays out. The Greek word for a triad is older: Coxe on Theophilus of Antioch, "The earliest use of this word 'Trinity'" (`anf02_hermas-tatian-athenagoras-theophilus-clement-alexandria.xml` 8993–8997, editor).

E11. **On Modesty: the bishop's edict and his answer.** "I hear that there has even been an edict set forth, and a peremptory one too. The Pontifex Maximus […]—that is, the bishop of bishops […]—issues an edict: 'I remit, to such as have discharged (the requirements of) repentance, the sins both of adultery and of fornication'" (ANF4 7178–7189); Latin "audio / etiam edictum esse propositum, et quidem peremptorium, pon- / tifex scilicet maximus, quod est episcopus episcoporum, edicit: / ego et moechiae et fornicationis delicta paenitentia functis di- / mitto" (CSEL20 12495–12499). *Pontifex maximus* was the title of Rome's pagan high priest; he uses it in scorn. His answer, the Paraclete speaking through "the new prophets": "The Church has the power to forgive sins; but I will not do it, lest they commit others withal" (ANF4 9470–9473); Latin "qui ipsum paracletum in prophetis nouis habeo dicentem: potest / ecclesia donare delictum, sed non faciam, ne et alia delinquant" (CSEL20 15213–15214). And: "'the Church,' it is true, will forgive sins: but (it will be) the Church of the Spirit, by means of a spiritual man; not the Church which consists of a number of bishops" (ANF4 9543–9545); Latin "et ideo ecclesia quidem delicta do- / nabit, sed ecclesia spiritus per spiritalem hominem, non ecclesia / numerus episcoporum" (CSEL20 15312–15314). OWN. Documented. **The other side:** the edict itself, as he quotes it. Which bishop: editors' notes give Zephyrinus or Victor (ANF4-ed 7180–7186), Monceaux "pape Calliste" (MON 17468): modern, disputed; leave unnamed. Date: Monceaux "entre 217 et 222 … très probablement le dernier des ouvrages conservés" (MON 17467–17469): modern, Dominant Modern Reconstruction. The `hermas` story tells the edict and his scorn of Hermas's book.

E12. **Old age.** Jerome: "He is said to have lived to a decrepit old age, and to have composed many small works, which are not extant" (JER 40514–40516); Latin "Ferturque vixisse usque / ad decrepitam etatem, et multa qua non exstant" / opuscula condidisse" (JERL 55634–55636). LATER, hearsay ("is said"). Documented as Jerome's report. No held old source gives his death. Richardson's "died about 245" (JER-ed 40493–40494) and Nöldechen's "lived until about 230" (EUS-ed 10245–10248) are editors'.

## 3. Impact, heaviest first (what later witnesses carried on and used)

I1. **Cyprian read him as his master.** Jerome: "I myself have seen a certain Paul an old man of Concordia, a town of Italy, who, while he himself was a very young man had been secretary to the blessed Cyprian […]. He said that he himself had seen how Cyprian was accustomed never to pass a day without reading Tertullian, and that he frequently said to him, 'Give me the master,' meaning by this, Tertullian" (JER 40500–40505). Latin: "Vidi / ego quemdam Paulum Concordie, quod oppidum / Italie est ?, senem qui se beati Cypriani, jam / grandis eíalis, notarium, cum ipse admodum / esset adolescens, Rome vidisse diceret, … re- / ferreque ? sibi solitum nunquam Cyprianum abs- / que Tertulliani lectione unum diem preterisse. ac / sibi crebro dicere, Da magistrum : Tertullianum / videlicet significans" (JERL 55618–55626). The Latin says Paul, as a very young man, had seen at Rome Cyprian's secretary, then old, who told him this; Richardson's English makes Paul himself the secretary, wrongly. LATER, hearsay through two people. Documented as Jerome's report. Jerome again, about 400: "The blessed Cyprian takes Tertullian for his master, as his writings prove; yet, delighted as he is with the ability of this learned and zealous writer he does not join him in following Montanus and Maximilla" (JERW 18969–18972, Letter 84; Latin not held). LATER. Documented as Jerome's. Benson: "Cyprian henceforth was his devoted yet discriminating disciple" (BEN 2388–2389), with a "Table shewing the verbal debts to Tertullian in Cyprian's Treatise de Dominica Oratione" (BEN 40590–40592): modern; card's Modern works. Sibling `cyprian-of-carthage`.

I2. **Novatian's On the Trinity.** Jerome: Novatian wrote "a great volume On the Trinity, a sort of epitome of the work of Tertullian, which many mistakenly ascribe to Cyprian" (JER 40895–40897); Latin, damaged: "operis Tertulliani faciens, quod plerique ne-" (JERL 58279). LATER. Documented as Jerome's judgment. Harnack: the claim "bestätigt sich nicht", is not borne out (HARN 22547–22548); Fausset: "Some perplexity has been created by Jerome's description of the work as an epitome of a work of Tertullian's" (NOV 739–741): both modern, card only. Novatian's own Latin uses *persona* of the Son: "secundam personam efficiens post" (NOV 12454). Story: Jerome's word, credited.

I3. **Read, and praised for his force.** Augustine: "à Tertulliano, cujus / multa leguntur opuscula eloquentissime scripta" (AUG 2827–2828): "from Tertullian, many of whose little works, most eloquently written, are read". Jerome, Letter 58: "Tertullianus creber est in sententiis, sed difficilis in / loquendo" (JERH 27044–27046); "Tertullian is packed with meaning but his style is rugged and uncouth" (JERW 14107–14108). Jerome, Letter 70: "Can anything be more learned or more pointed than the style of Tertullian? […] His Apology and his books Against the Gentiles contain all the wisdom of the world" (JERW 16704–16707). Vincent of Lérins: "as Origen holds by far the first place among the Greeks, so does Tertullian among the Latins. […] Every word almost was a sentence; every sentence a victory" (VIN 13555–13568). Lactantius: "Septimius quoque Tertullianus fuit omni / genere litterarum peritus, sed in eloquendo parum facilis et / minus comptus et multum obscurus fuit. ergo ne hic quidem / satis celebritatis inuenit" (LACT 22707–22710); "quamquam Tertullianus eandem causam plene perorauerit / in eo libro cui Apologeticum nomen est" (LACT 23225–23226), English LACT-E 4472, 4569. LATER, each a century or two on. Documented. The Lactantius lines belong to the `lactantius` sibling and the live `latin-apologists` story; not here.

I4. **Judged for the turn.** Augustine lists the Tertullianists among the heresies, and says why Tertullian became a heretic (E8; AUG 2868–2873). Jerome, Against Helvidius 19 (383): "Of Tertullian I say no more than that he did not belong to the Church" (JERW 33680–33681); Latin "Et / de Tertulliano quidem nihil amplius dico, quam / Ecclesie hominem non fuisse" (JERL 15618–15620): "Of Tertullian I say nothing more than that he was not a man of the Church." Context: Helvidius had cited him as a witness (JERW 33677–33679). Jerome, Lives 53, says he wrote some books "directly against the church" (JER 40511–40512; JERL 55630–55631 "specialiter autem adversum Ecclesiam texuit volumina"). Vincent: "this Tertullian, I say, too little tenacious of Catholic doctrine, that is, of the universal and ancient faith, more eloquent by far than faithful, […] changed his belief, and justified what the blessed Confessor, Hilary, writes of him, namely, that 'by his subsequent error he detracted from the authority of his approved writings.' […] He also was a great trial in the Church" (VIN 13572–13580). Heurtley notes a variant for "faithful" ("Fidelior, Baluz, Felicior, others", VIN-ed 13575–13576). Hilary's own text is not held. LATER. Documented as each one's verdict. No held source records any council or bishop condemning him; the census's "never made a saint" (CEN `legacy`) has no held old source.

I5. **His followers at Carthage.** Augustine: "usque ad nostrum tempus paulatim deficientes, in / extremis reliquiis durare potuerunt in urbe Cartha- / ginensi: me autem ibi posito ante aliquot annos, / … omni ex parte / consumpti sunt. Paucissimi enim qui remanserant, / in Catholicam transierunt, suamque basilicam, quae / nuncetiam notissima est, Catholicz tradiderunt" (AUG 2829–2835): dwindling little by little down to his own time, they lasted in a last remnant at Carthage; some years before, while he was there, they came wholly to an end; the very few left crossed to the Catholic church and handed over their basilica, still very well known. LATER. Documented as Augustine's report. Isidore repeats the name: "Tertullianistae dicti a TertuUiano presbytero" (`isidore-seville_etymologiae-origines-lat_lindsay1911.txt` 17908): later still, adds nothing.

I6. **The Apology in Greek.** Eusebius quotes "his apology for the Christians, […] which was written by him in the Latin language, and has been translated into Greek" (EUS 10292, 10303–10304). LATER. Documented. The `pliny-the-younger` story tells it. Card.

I7. **Counted among the church's champions.** Optatus of Milevis, Against Parmenian I.9: "Marcion Praxeas Sabellius Ualentinus et / ceteri usque ad Cataphrygas temporibus suis a Uictorino / Petauionensi et Zephyrino Urbico et a Tertulliano Carthaginensi / et ab aliis adsertoribus ecclesiae catholicae superati / sunt" (`optatus_libri-vii-lat_ziwsa1893-csel26-tei.txt` 493–497, CSEL 26, Ziwsa 1893); English: "Marcion, Praxeas, Sabellius, Valentinus, and the rest up to the Cataphrygae, all of whom were confuted in their time by Victorinus of Pettau, by Zephyrinus of Rome, by Tertullian of Carthage, and by other champions of the Catholic Church" (`optatus_against-the-donatists.txt` 139). The apparatus says manuscript P has "usque ad catafregas" here, and RBGvb omit it here and place it after "Tertulliano carthaginensi" (522–525). LATER, African, later fourth century. Documented as Optatus's word.

**What he defended:** the Christians' innocence before Rome's judges (E1–E3); the apostles' churches' title to the Scriptures (E4–E5); one God in three against Praxeas (E9–E10); and later the New Prophecy and its rules (E6–E7, E11).

## 5. Where sources disagree or come late

D1. Why he turned: Jerome (envy and insults of the Roman clergy) and Augustine (going over to the Phrygians, condemning second marriage, then a split from them too) (E8). Both about two centuries late.
D2. How Cyprian's words reached Jerome: the Latin chain, Paul who had seen the old secretary; the English misreads it (I1).
D3. A lawyer? Eusebius says versed in Roman law; McGiffert says he very likely practised; Monceaux doubts he is the jurist of the law books (S5).
D4. From whom he withdrew: Coxe, perhaps only Rome's bishop; Monceaux, the catholics at Carthage (E6). Augustine adds a later split from the New Prophecy (E8).
D5. First in Latin: Jerome's Latin, "nunc demum primus / post Victorem et Apollomium Latinorum ponitur" (JERL 55610–55611), "now at last he is placed first of the Latins after Victor and Apollonius", gives his place in Jerome's list of writers, after two earlier Latins: not a ranking. Richardson's "now regarded as chief of the Latin writers" (JER 40495) is the translator's. Only Vincent ranks him: "the first place … among the Latins" (VIN 13555–13557), rank, not order. The census calls him "the first major Christian author to write in Latin" (CEN `why`). Whether Minucius Felix's Octavius is older is disputed among held editors (CEN `latin-apologists` corpus notes; `cic/corpus-map/latin-apologists.yaml` 470–473: "Two poles. Pole A lies before Tertullian's Apology"). Say "the foremost early writer", never "the first".
D6. Dates of his books after the Apology: Monceaux only (E4, E11). His death: no old source.
D7. Who Praxeas was and what he taught: Tertullian alone, a hostile witness (E9).
