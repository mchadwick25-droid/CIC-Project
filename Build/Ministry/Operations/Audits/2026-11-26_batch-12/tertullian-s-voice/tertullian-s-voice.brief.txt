If the Tiber flooded the walls or the Nile failed to rise over the fields, if no rain fell, if the earth shook or famine or plague came, the crowd had one cry, Tertullian wrote: "Christians to the lion!" Around the year 200, in Carthage, in Roman Africa, he put this before the governors who judged for Rome, in his Apology, a defense of the Christians. Since harsh private trials had shut their courts to a defense, he asked that the truth reach them in writing: "For now, she longs for one thing: not to be condemned unknown."

Vincent of Lérins, about two centuries later, gave him the first place among the Latins, as the foremost early writer of the faith in that language. Eusebius calls him well versed in Roman law. He defended the Christians before Rome's judges and claimed the Scriptures for the churches the apostles founded. He joined a strict movement, the New Prophecy, and after that turn wrote of God as one substance and three persons. Cyprian, as Jerome heard it, read him every day, yet Jerome and Augustine counted him outside the church.

The governors, he argued, hated the very name of Christian without learning what it meant, and hatred of a thing unknown is unjust.

Teachers he called heretics, Marcion and Valentinus among them, argued their case from Scripture. His Prescription, named for a plea that throws a case out of court before it is argued, answered that the first question is whose the Scriptures are. The faith passed from Christ to the apostles, and from them to the churches they founded, such as Ephesus and Rome. A heretic teaches a faith of his own choosing, he said, and has no right to the Scriptures, while the church can answer as an owner in court: "This property is mine. I have held it long; I held it first. I hold firm deeds from the very owners whose estate it was. I am the heir of the apostles."

He came to believe that the Paraclete, the Holy Spirit whom Jesus promised, was speaking anew through prophets in Phrygia, in Asia Minor. Defending the Paraclete, he wrote, parted his side from those he called the psychics, the merely natural. Two later writers explain his turn differently. Jerome says the envy and insults of the clergy at Rome drove him to it. Augustine says he went over to the prophets of Phrygia, whom he had once refuted, and began to call second marriage, after a husband or wife had died, fornication.

Praxeas, by Tertullian's account, had brought to Rome from Asia the teaching that the Father himself came down into the Virgin, was born and suffered, and was himself Jesus Christ. Tertullian answered that the one God has a Son, his Word. God said, "Let there be light," and the Word appeared, so there is one who commands and another who makes. Substance means what God is, one and undivided, and person marks how the Son is another than the Father: "I have already said how you should take the word 'another'. It means another person, not another substance. It marks a distinction, not a division. Everywhere I hold one substance in three that hold together." The Latin words for Trinity, person and substance stand in this book, written after his turn, for he says the Paraclete had taught him better.

A bishop, whom he mocked with a pagan high priest's title, issued an edict, a public ruling, forgiving adultery and fornication to those who had done penance. In On Modesty, Tertullian answered that the Paraclete, speaking through the new prophets, had said the church could forgive such sins but would not, lest people sin again. Only the church of the Spirit, through a spiritual man, could forgive them, he wrote, not the bishops as a body.

Jerome heard from an old man of Italy, who as a youth had met Cyprian's secretary, that Cyprian, later bishop of Carthage, never let a day pass without reading Tertullian, and would say, "Give me the master." Cyprian, Jerome adds, did not follow him into the New Prophecy.

Augustine counts his followers among the heresies and says the turn made him a heretic, and Jerome would say no more of him than that he was not a man of the church. Yet Optatus, an African bishop a century and a half later, still named him among the champions of the main church who had overcome Marcion, Praxeas and Valentinus.

Augustine says he broke from the New Prophecy too and gathered followers of his own. At Carthage these Tertullianists dwindled, until in Augustine's day the very few left joined the main church and handed over their church building.

He is said, Jerome writes, to have lived to a frail old age, and to have written many short works that are lost.
