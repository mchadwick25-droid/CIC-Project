In the palace quarter of Alexandria, which held the royal palaces and the citadel, the bread had run out, and hunger was harder to bear than the enemy outside. Anatolius, a learned Christian, had urged the city council to make peace with Rome, and the council had grown angry. So he tried again: "But this, I think, you will never speak against. I advise sending out of the gates those we have too many of, who are of no use to us at all: old women, infants and old men. Let them walk wherever they wish."

He used the vote that followed to slip out almost all the besieged, his own church's people first. Later he was kept as bishop of Laodicea in Syria, on his way to a meeting of bishops. He wrote a rule for finding Easter on a cycle of nineteen years, and three and four centuries later, Irish churchmen and the defenders of Rome's Easter both claimed him.

He was born in Alexandria and lived in the later 200s. Eusebius, the church historian, counted him among the men of his own time. He ranked him first in arithmetic, geometry, astronomy, logic, physics and rhetoric, and reports that the citizens asked him to found a school of Aristotle's philosophy. Eusebius tells one of his deeds in the siege, as others told it to him.

The palace quarter held out against a Roman army, while the rest of the city sided with Rome. Anatolius had sent word to a deacon of the church in the free part of the city, whose name had reached the Roman general. As the greatest favor, the deacon asked safety for any who deserted the besieged side, and won it.

At the council, Anatolius went on: "Why keep them here for nothing, when they will die any day now?" The crippled and maimed should not be worn down by hunger, he said, and the city should feed only men and youths. He rose first and voted that all not needed for the fighting, men or women, should leave, since if they stayed they would starve, and the council agreed.

Under cover of that decree he saved almost all the besieged: the church's people first, then others of every age, and with them many more, men among them, secretly dressed in women's clothes. By night they went out of the gates to the Roman camp, where the deacon took them in like a father and a doctor and nursed them.

After the war he left Alexandria, and at Caesarea in Palestine its bishop made him a bishop beside himself, to take over at his death. Then he was called to a meeting of bishops at Antioch, held to judge its own bishop, Paul of Samosata, who taught that Christ was by nature an ordinary man. On the way he passed through Laodicea, whose bishop, the same deacon, had died, and the believers there kept Anatolius as their bishop.

Eusebius kept part of his Easter book. Easter's date was tied to the Jewish Passover, kept at full moon on the fourteenth day of the first month of spring, counted by the moon. In his writings on the Passover, Anatolius held that this month must never begin from a new moon before the spring equinox, in March, when day and night are equal. Of the weeks just before the equinox, the last twelfth of the sun's yearly path, he wrote: "So we say that those who put the first month in it, and count the Passover's fourteenth day by it, make no small mistake, and no careless one." He set his count on a nineteen-year table of the spring new moons, which then begins again, and said the Jews had kept the rule before Christ.

While Gregory the Great was pope, Columbanus, an abbot who kept his homeland's Easter, wrote to him to defend the Irish Easter. He cited Anatolius, a man of wonderful learning, and quoted a Latin Easter book under his name: a moon past its twentieth day rises after midnight, so darkness rules most of the night, but the feast of the resurrection is a feast of light.

The quarrel came to a meeting at Whitby in 664, in the monastery of Abbess Hild, in the north of England. Bede, writing about two generations later, gives the speeches. Colman, the bishop who led the Irish side, asked whether Anatolius, a holy man praised in Eusebius's history, had gone against the law and the gospel when, as Colman put it, he wrote that Easter could fall on any day from the moon's fourteenth to its twentieth. Wilfrid, for Rome's side, answered: "It is agreed that Anatolius was a most holy man, most learned and most worthy of praise. But what is he to you, when you do not keep what he laid down? For in his Easter he truly followed the rule of truth. He set out a cycle of nineteen years." That cycle, he said, the whole church of Christ keeps. The king ruled for Peter, who held the keys of heaven, and so for Rome's Easter.

Bede, writing in 731 of the Irish before Whitby, said they believed they followed the holy and praiseworthy Father Anatolius, and any skilled reader could tell whether that was true.
