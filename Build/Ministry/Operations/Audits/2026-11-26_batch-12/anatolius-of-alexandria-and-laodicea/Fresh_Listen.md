## 1. The test (person)

- **Who:** a learned Christian of Alexandria in the later 200s, later bishop of Laodicea in Syria. Filled.
- **What happened:** he got the weak and the church's people out of a starving, besieged quarter through a council vote. Then he was made bishop, first in Caesarea, then in Laodicea. He wrote a rule for dating Easter. Filled.
- **What it was about:** Easter's date follows the Passover full moon, and his rule fixes the month from the spring equinox with a nineteen-year table. Filled. This is the hardest part to follow (see 8).
- **Why the connection matters:** three and four centuries later, Irish and Roman churchmen both used his name in the Easter quarrel, at Whitby among other places. Filled. I am never told who won at Whitby, so the quarrel ends on Bede's doubt and not on an outcome. I can still explain why he is on the tree, so this is not a gap in the test.

## 3. Sentences that fail on one hearing (all MINOR)

1. "On the way, at Laodicea, whose bishop, the same deacon, had died, the believers kept him as bishop." The news that the rescuing deacon had become bishop of Laodicea is tucked into a clause, and I lost it. **Change:** "On the way he passed through Laodicea, whose bishop had died: the same deacon who had taken in the people from the siege. The believers there kept Anatolius as their bishop."
2. "About 600, Columbanus, an Irish abbot, wrote to Pope Gregory the Great to defend his homeland's Easter against an Easter past the moon's twentieth day." On one hearing I cannot tell what "an Easter past the moon's twentieth day" is, or who kept it. The next sentence but one (the light argument) partly rescues it. **Change:** none required if the outline gives no plainer wording. Otherwise, "...against keeping Easter later than the moon's twentieth day", which is more literal.
3. "He set his count on a nineteen-year table of the spring new moons, which then begins again." "Which then begins again" hangs loose: I was not sure what begins again. **Change:** "He set his count on a table of the spring new moons that runs nineteen years and then begins again."

Understood though formal, not counted: the Antioch sentence about Paul of Samosata (29 words with a nested clause) and "That month, his rule ran, must not be counted from a new moon before the spring equinox."

## 8. Attention

The opening pulls me in strongly: hunger, the council, and a quote that sounds cruel until paragraph 2 turns it. Attention first drifts at "That month, his rule ran, must not be counted from a new moon before the spring equinox, in March, when day and night are equal." Why: it is three calendar sentences in a row, and I have to hold "fourteenth day", "first month" and "new moon" in my head at once. Columbanus's light-and-darkness argument pulls me back. I would listen to the end. The last line ("anyone skilled could very easily tell whether that was true") is dry irony, and a second-language listener may miss that Bede means the Irish were wrong.
