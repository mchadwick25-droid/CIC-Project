# Outline: Anatolius of Alexandria and Laodicea

Kind: person. Items S, E, I, D are in `Source_Sheet.md`, which gives each file and line. Short names as there: EUS (Eusebius, McGiffert's English), EUS-gr (Schwartz's Greek, with Rufinus's Latin), BEDE / BEDE-lat (Giles / Plummer), COL / COL-lat (Columbanus, Barmby's English / Migne's Latin), LATCAN (the Latin Easter book under his name, in English).

## The heart

- **The essence:** In a besieged quarter of Alexandria, Anatolius, a learned Christian, talked the starving city council into letting its "useless" people go, then used that vote to slip out almost everyone (E2–E4; Documented as Eusebius's report). He was later kept as bishop of Laodicea in Syria, on his way to a synod. He wrote a rule for finding Easter on a cycle of nineteen years, and three and four centuries later, Irish churchmen and the defenders of Rome's Easter both claimed him (E5, E6, I1; Documented).
- **Also told:**
  - **The learned man.** Eusebius ranks him first among the ablest men of his time in arithmetic, geometry, astronomy, logic, physics and rhetoric, and reports that the citizens asked him to found a school of Aristotle's philosophy (S1; Documented as praise; the school as a report).
  - **The siege and the stratagem.** A deacon outside the lines won from the Roman general a promise of safety for deserters. Anatolius first urged peace; when the council grew angry, he argued them into sending out the old and the weak, and under that decree sent out many more, men dressed as women among them, by night to the Roman camp (E2–E4).
  - **Bishop on the road.** The bishop of Caesarea in Palestine made him a second bishop to succeed him. Called to the synod at Antioch, he passed through Laodicea, whose bishop, the same deacon, had died, and the believers kept him (E5).
  - **The Easter rule.** The first month, whose fourteenth day is the Passover, must not be counted from a new moon before the spring equinox; those who do so, he wrote, make no small mistake. He claimed the Jews kept that rule before Christ, and he set his count on a nineteen-year cycle (E6; his own words).
  - **What came of him:** see below. Defended: the equinox rule (E6).
- **Length:** 675 words, as approved (Mark, 2026-11-27). Planned at about 665 once Cut first items 1–29 below are applied, measured against the writer's draft (`Fixes_Length.md`).
- **Setting:**
  - Time: the later 200s; Jerome says he flourished under the emperors Probus and Carus (S2). No year for birth, death, siege or ordination (S2, D2).
  - Place: Alexandria, in Egypt; Caesarea in Palestine; Laodicea in Syria (S3, Jerome's words; "on the coast" rests only on Loofs, so leave it out); Antioch, for the synod (E5).
  - Tradition: "Alexandrian Christianity" (S4): an Alexandrian by birth who became a bishop in Syria.
  - Language: he wrote in Greek (sheet, Language).

**Words to explain** (worked into the sentence where first heard)

- **Pyrucheium / Brucheium:** "the palace quarter" (E1, McGiffert's note). That it held the royal palaces and the citadel goes to the card (Cut first 15). Use neither name.
- **the senate / the assembly:** "the city council" (E3, Greek βουλή).
- **reconciliation with the Romans:** "to make peace with Rome" (E3, "give the Romans the hand of friendship").
- **synod:** "bishops meeting at Antioch to judge its own bishop, Paul of Samosata, who, Eusebius says, taught that Christ was by nature an ordinary man" (E5, EUS 41838–41840). The listener needs the reason in one clause; Eusebius gives it.
- **ordained him … to make him his successor:** "made him a second bishop there, to take over at his death" (E5). Not "coadjutor", not "assistant bishop".
- **Passover / the fourteenth:** "the Jewish Passover full moon, the fourteenth day of the first month of spring" (E6, EUS 42866–42878). Easter's date was tied to it; say no more about why.
- **spring equinox:** "the spring equinox, in March, when day and night are equal" (E6; LATCAN 14983 speaks of the equality of light and darkness). By his own count it falls a few days before 22 March: on that day the sun is already in its fourth day past it (EUS 42807–42809). Give no date.
- **cycle of nineteen years:** "a nineteen-year table of the spring new moons, which then begins again" (E6; his "new moon of the first month, which is the beginning of every cycle of nineteen years", EUS 42789–42791). Keep to that.
- **Phamenoth, Dystrus, Kalends:** not used; Quote 3 is cut before them.
- **the Scots:** "the Irish, whom Bede calls the Scots" (I1d).
- **the church history** (Colman's words): not used; "much praised in Eusebius's history" is on the card (Cut first 11, 26).
- **from the fourteenth to the twentieth:** "on any day from the fourteenth day of the moon's month to the twentieth" (I1d).
- **Columbanus's darkness argument:** a moon past its twentieth day rises after midnight, so darkness rules most of the night; the feast of the resurrection is a feast of light (I1a, COL 4349–4355). One sentence.
- **Aristotelian:** "Aristotle's philosophy". **dialectics:** "logic". **Do not use:** computus, Metonic, Quartodeciman, Brucheium, coadjutor, dodecatomorion.

## Shape, sibling, openings

- **Shape:** Founder or leader. What he led: a besieged council, then a church in Syria. How he ran it: by argument, a vote and a disguise; then by a rule for the year's greatest feast. Who it served: the starving of the palace quarter, his church's people first; then churches trying to find Easter. What outlasted him: a name that both sides at Whitby, and Columbanus before them, wanted on their side. Trap: titles. The acts carry it: the message, the speech, the vote, the night exit, the book.
- **Sibling:** none in the batch. Live stories that touch his: `polycrates-of-ephesus` (the second-century Easter quarrel; not retold here), `bede-of-jarrow` and `hild-of-whitby` (Whitby told from Hild's house; here only the claims on Anatolius), `columbanus` (here only his Easter letter's use of Anatolius), `wilfrid-of-ripon` (here only his answer about Anatolius). Each was read; no quote of theirs is used.
- **Openings:**
  1. **Chosen.** The famine and the speech (E2, E3). In a quarter of Alexandria under siege, the bread had run out, and hunger was harder to bear than the enemy outside. Anatolius stood before the city council and proposed sending out the old women, the infants and the old men (Quote 1, its second sentence only; Cut first 16). Then the essence: what he did with that vote.
  2. Not chosen: Whitby, 664, two churchmen claiming the same long-dead bishop (I1d). It is the connection and the close; told first, it would be told twice.

## Story beats

The opening, the essence and beat 1 fill the first minute (about 150 words).

1. **The learned man, and who tells it.** Born in Alexandria. Eusebius, the church historian, who counts him among the men of his own time, ranks him first in arithmetic, geometry, astronomy, logic, physics and rhetoric, and reports that the citizens asked him to found a school of Aristotle's philosophy (S1; praise and report, credited). Eusebius knew "many other" deeds of his in the siege, for which those in high office honored him, and tells one; he tells it as others told it to him (E1; "they relate", "they say"). Here, once, the story says the siege rests on Eusebius's report.
2. **The siege and the message.** The palace quarter held out against a Roman army; the rest of the city sided with Rome (E1, E2). Anatolius sent word to a deacon of the church in the free part of the city, a man whose name had reached the Roman general. The deacon asked safety for any who deserted the besieged side, and won it (E2). Deacon by role (Names; old: EUS 40140–40141, 40420, tied to this man at EUS 42662–42664). Why it happened: the famine; Eusebius gives no reason for the siege, so tell none.
3. **The council, the vote, the disguise.** He called the city council and first urged peace with Rome; they grew angry (E3). Then, in paraphrase only, that the city should feed only men and youths (Cut first 5, 20). He rose first and voted that all not needed for the fighting, men or women, should leave, since if they stayed they would starve; the council agreed (E4). Under cover of the decree, by Eusebius's account, he saved almost all the besieged: the church's people first, and with them a great many more, secretly dressed in women's clothes. By night they went out of the gates to the Roman camp, where the deacon took them in "like a father and a doctor" and nursed them (E4). The listener must hear the force: a vote won by an argument about who was useless, and fighting men smuggled out as women past the decree. Optional, one credited sentence (Cut first 1): Rufinus, in Latin a century later, has Anatolius in the free part of the city and gives him the deacon's part (D1).
4. **Caesarea, then Laodicea.** After the war (E5), the bishop of Caesarea in Palestine made him a second bishop there, to take over at his death, and for a short time they led that church together. Then the synod on Paul of Samosata called him to Antioch. Passing through Laodicea, whose bishop, the same deacon, had died, he was "detained" by the believers there and became their bishop (E5). Eusebius's phrase, "one good man, as they say, following another", may stand in the narrator's sentence.
5. **The Easter book.** Eusebius kept part of his Easter book; when or where he wrote it, no old source says, so the story does not place it. He did not write many works, Eusebius says, but in them he gave his views on the Passover (E6). Easter's date was tied to the Passover moon. His rule: the first month must not be counted from a new moon before the spring equinox; then, led in by "Those who begin it earlier, he wrote", Quote 3's last clause only, "make no small mistake, and no careless one" (Cut first 23). He set the count on a nineteen-year cycle, and said the rule was no idea of his own: the Jews kept it before Christ, and he named their writers (E6). He also wrote ten books on arithmetic (E6; one clause, Cut first 3).

## What came of him

Heaviest first; about a third of the story.

1. **Columbanus.** Columbanus, an abbot who kept his homeland's Easter, wrote to Pope Gregory the Great to defend the Irish Easter against an Easter past the moon's twentieth day (I1a; COL 4320–4322). He cited Anatolius (his praise of him, and Eusebius's and Jerome's: card, Cut first 12, 24) and quoted from a Latin Easter book under his name: if the moon rises after midnight, darkness rules, and the feast of the resurrection is a feast of light (I1a; COL 4320–4355; the words are in LATCAN 15014–15024). Narrator's words, credited to Columbanus. To the bishops of Gaul he wrote that he trusted his homeland's tradition, and Anatolius, over Victorius, who, he wrote, had written lately and in doubt (I1b; COL-lat 20796–20797; Cut first 2). If kept, gloss him in one clause: "Victorius, the author of an Easter table" (Columbanus to Gregory: "Victorius has gone beyond in his cycle", COL 4372–4379, old).
2. **Whitby, 664** (I1d). The quarrel came to a synod at Whitby, in the monastery of Abbess Hild, in Northumbria, in the north of England (one clause; BEDE 7951–7952, "the province of the Northumbrians"). Bede, writing in 731, tells it. Colman, for the Irish side, said that Anatolius, a holy man, had written that Easter could fall on any day from the moon's fourteenth to its twentieth (paraphrase; Bede gives it as a question, Cut first 26). Wilfrid, for Rome's side, answered with Quote 2's first sentence; then, in the narrator's words, Anatolius had set out a cycle of nineteen years, which the whole church of Christ keeps (Cut first 27). The listener must hear that both sides claimed the same man, each crediting him with a different rule (I1d, D5). The king's ruling for Peter, and so for Rome's Easter: card (Cut first 28).
3. **Bede's doubt.** Told once only, as Ending 2, not here. It is Bede's judgment, written in 731, on the Irish practice of Aidan's time, before Whitby (I1c; BEDE 5745–5757); never an event after Whitby.

Card only: Jerome's praise and Letter 70; the chronicles that copy Jerome; Barhadbeshabba, Bar Hebraeus, Michael the Syrian (I2). Elias of Nisibis, Socrates, Bede's letter "according to Anatolius" (I3). The Bollandists' saint's-day note (I5). The authorship debate on the Latin book (D4). From the length cut: what the palace quarter held (Cut first 15); the deacon's "greatest favor" (19); "others of every age" (21); "a man of wonderful learning" (24); Colman's question as Bede gives it, "much praised in Eusebius's history", and "the bishop who led" (26); the rest of Quote 2 (27); the king's ruling for Peter (28).

## Quotes

Only these are quoted aloud. Each is rendered for this outline.

1. **His speech to the council.** EUS-gr 12485–12492 (VII.32.9), rendered from the Greek; checked against McGiffert, EUS 42735–42743. Stands behind it: the original in one held print, OCR-damaged (`xci` for καί, `ϑ` for θ, `oc` for ὡς), every word legible and confirmed by McGiffert's English (1890), printed before 1931. Eusebius's report, from hearsay, of what Anatolius said.
   Rendering: "But this, I think, you will never speak against. I advise sending out of the gates those we have too many of, who are of no use to us at all: old women, infants and old men. Let them walk wherever they wish. Why keep them here for nothing, when they will die any day now? Why wear down with hunger the crippled and those maimed in body? We ought to feed only men and youths. And the wheat we need should be stored for those fit to guard the city."
   Job: the opening, its second sentence only (Cut first 16, 20); beat 3 keeps in paraphrase only "the city should feed only men and youths"; the rest is Leave out. The council's own hard sum, which he turned into a way out. The listener learns at once, from what he did next, that those he called useless were the first he saved, with many more.
2. **Wilfrid at Whitby.** BEDE-lat 21581–21587 (III.25), rendered from the Latin; checked against Giles, BEDE 8134–8141. Stands behind it: the original in a clean held print (legible for these lines), with Giles's English (1903). Wilfrid's words in Bede's account.
   Rendering: "It is agreed that Anatolius was a most holy man, most learned and most worthy of praise. But what is he to you, when you do not keep what he laid down? For in his Easter he truly followed the rule of truth. He set out a cycle of nineteen years. You either do not know it, or you know it and scorn it as nothing, though the whole church of Christ keeps it."
   Job: What came 2. Both sides honor him; Rome's side names the cycle as what passed from him to the whole church. Spoken: its first sentence only; the cycle in the narrator's words (Cut first 27).
3. **His own rule.** EUS-gr 12608–12616 (VII.32.15), rendered from the Greek; checked against McGiffert, EUS 42812–42817. Stands behind it: the original in one held print; Schwartz marks two words (κατ᾽ αὐτὴν) as corrupt, which does not touch the sense rendered; McGiffert's English (1890) confirms. Anatolius's own words.
   Rendering: "So we say that those who put the first month in it, and count the Passover's fourteenth day by it, make no small mistake, and no careless one."
   Plain sense alongside: "it" is the last twelfth of the sun's yearly path, the weeks just before the spring equinox (EUS 42810–42815). Job: beat 5. The rule he defended, in his own voice. Spoken: its last clause only, "make no small mistake, and no careless one", after the rule in the narrator's words; the plain sense is then not needed (Cut first 23).

Short phrases that may stand in the narrator's sentence: "one good man, as they say, following another" (EUS 42683–42684); "like a father and a doctor" (EUS-gr 12574, rendered from πατρὸς καὶ ἰατροῦ δίκην).

## Connection, ending, links

- **Connection to carry into the story:** what passed from him is a rule and a name. The rule, in his own Greek words kept by Eusebius: no Passover month before the spring equinox, on a nineteen-year cycle (E6). The name: by about 600 a Latin Easter book went under it, and Columbanus used it against the Gaulish and Roman Easter (I1a); at Whitby Colman claimed him for keeping Easter from the moon's fourteenth day to its twentieth, and Wilfrid answered that he had set out the nineteen-year cycle, which the whole church kept (I1d; BEDE 8117–8121, 8134–8141). The listener must understand that both sides called him holy and claimed his authority, each for a different rule; which text either man drew on, no old source says. Bede doubted the Irish claim (I1c). Tell it through what Columbanus, Colman and Wilfrid said; no "this matters because".
- **Ending:**
  1. The close of What came 2: Quote 2's first sentence and Wilfrid's cycle, told once there; the king's ruling is on the card (Cut first 28). Then back to the subject: whatever they made of his rule, both men had called him holy (BEDE 8117–8118, 8134–8135; narrator's close on the subject, no moral). With Cut first 6 applied, only Ending 2 follows.
  2. Bede's judgment, written in 731, on the Irish practice of Aidan's time, before Whitby, led in by "Bede doubted the Irish claim.": the northern Irish and Picts before Whitby, he wrote, believed they followed the writings of "the holy and praiseworthy Father Anatolius", and anyone skilled could very easily tell whether that was true (I1c, BEDE 5752–5758; BEDE-lat 18705–18709). Ends on words about him, in Bede's voice.
- **Links:** Eusebius of Caesarea (wrote of; kept his book's words). Jerome (wrote of). Rufinus of Aquileia (retold the siege). Columbanus (quoted a book under his name). Gregory the Great (was written to about him). Hild of Whitby (the synod in her house named him). Bede (wrote of; doubted the Irish claim on him). Wilfrid of Ripon (cited him). Polycrates of Ephesus (the Latin book under his name tells Polycrates's quarrel). Elias of Nisibis (used his words). Socrates of Constantinople (used the writers his extract names, through Eusebius; Inferential-Thin).

- **Eusebius of Caesarea** (old; a younger contemporary, writing early in the 300s): everything in Alexandria, Caesarea and Laodicea, and the extract. The siege is report ("they relate", "they say"): said once in beat 1, inside a sentence about Eusebius. His praise of Anatolius stays his.
- **Anatolius** (old; his own words): Quote 3 and the rule (E6); his speech, as Eusebius reports it (Quote 1).
- **Rufinus** (old, a century later): the variant siege (D1), credited if told.
- **Jerome** (old, a century later): the learning; card. Columbanus cites his praise.
- **Columbanus** (old, about three centuries later): his letters (I1a, I1b), credited.
- **Bede** (old, writing in 731, about two generations after Whitby, the only held account of it): Whitby (I1d) and his own doubt (I1c). Credited once at Whitby.
- **Modern, clarifies:** McGiffert (the palace quarter; Phamenoth as 22 March; the deacon's office), Barmby (the letter's addressee and his notes), Schwartz (the Greek text). **Modern, own claims, kept out of story and card:** McGiffert's "first Christian, so far as we know"; Hefele's "discovered"; years from Richardson, Salmond, Harnack, Hefele, Loofs; Harnack's and Duchesne's political readings; Bardy on the synod; Harnack on Dionysius. ## Do not say

- "Anatolius invented the nineteen-year cycle" or "the first Christian to use it" → "he set his Easter rule on a nineteen-year cycle"; at Whitby, Wilfrid credited him with it (D7).
- "He saved everyone" → "almost all the besieged", as Eusebius says (E4).
- "He saved the old and the weak" alone → he got out the old and the weak under the decree, and with them many others beyond it, men in women's clothes among them, the church's people first (E4).
- "He was a leader of the rebels", "he was compromised", "he sat on the city council as a statesman", "he headed the Aristotelian school" → none; Eusebius says he called the council, voted first, was honored by those in high office, and was reportedly asked to found a school (D1, D3).
- A year for the siege, his ordination or his death → none (S2, D2).
- "He took part in the synod that deposed Paul" → he was called to it and kept at Laodicea on the way (E5, D6).
- "The deacon was his old friend" → by role only; no relation given (E5).
- "Anatolius wrote that Easter may be kept from the fourteenth to the twentieth" → Colman said so; the words are in a Latin book under his name; Wilfrid read him otherwise (I1d, D5).
- "The Latin book is a forgery" or "is genuine" → "a Latin Easter book that went under his name" (D4).
- "He died a martyr", any death scene → none; Eusebius gives only that Stephen came after him (E7).
- "Dionysius of Alexandria sent him" → no old source says so.
- "Columbanus wrote in 599" → "wrote to Pope Gregory the Great" (I1a).
- "The equinox on 21 March" or any date → by his own count it fell a few days before 22 March; give no date (E6).
- "Bede proved the Irish wrong" → Bede said anyone skilled could very easily tell whether their claim was true (I1c).
- 'Colman quoted the Latin book and Wilfrid the Greek' or 'they quoted two different books' → each claimed Anatolius; no old source says which text either used (BEDE 8117–8150; LATCAN 14840–14843).
- 'As bishop he wrote…' or 'at Laodicea he wrote…' → he wrote an Easter book; no time or place for it (E6).
- 'a revolt against Rome' or any cause for the siege → none; Eusebius gives none (E1).
- 'Columbanus quoted Anatolius's own words' → words from a Latin Easter book under his name (I1a, D4).
- Paul's teaching told as the narrator's fact → 'who, Eusebius says, taught that Christ was by nature an ordinary man' (E5, EUS 41838–41840).
- 'the Irish' alone for Bede's doubt, or his doubt without 'very easily' or 'the writings' → 'the northern Irish and Picts … believed they followed the writings of … Anatolius, and anyone skilled could very easily tell whether that was true' (I1c, BEDE-lat 18705–18709); Bede's line never without 'Bede doubted the Irish claim' before it.
- The Easter quarrel named without what the Easter Columbanus opposed allowed → 'an Easter past the moon's twentieth day' (I1a, COL 4320–4322). No Roman range of days from Wilfrid's Egyptian count (Leave out).
- The essence names the rescue without its detail; 'the church's people first' is told once, in beat 3.
