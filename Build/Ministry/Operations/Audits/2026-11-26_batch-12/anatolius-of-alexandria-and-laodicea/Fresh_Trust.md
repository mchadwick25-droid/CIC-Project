## Heart

The heart is the right one. The record's weight is the famine rescue (Eusebius gives it as his one example of the siege). Next come the bishopric he took on the road, and the Easter rule kept in his own Greek words. Last is his name being claimed by both sides, from Columbanus to Whitby. The heart is told in paragraphs 1–2, about 105 words in, well inside the first minute.

## SUMMARY HOLDS (evidence; no change needed)

2. "Anatolius, a learned Christian, stood before the city council." He convened the council and spoke in it (EUS-gr 12483). His learning is EUS 42684–42690. That he was a Christian follows from the church's people, the deacon and his later office.
6. "He also wrote a rule for finding Easter, and three and four centuries on, Irish churchmen and the defenders of Rome's Easter both claimed him." EUS 42769–42773: "his opinions on the passover". Columbanus (COL 4320ff, about 600), Colman and Wilfrid (BEDE 8117–8141, 664) all claimed him.
7. "Born in Alexandria, he lived in the later 200s." EUS 42684: "an Alexandrian by birth". JER 40933–40934: "flourished under the emperors Probus and Carus". Those reigns are Widely Accepted as the 270s–280s.
8. The ranking and the school. EUS 42684–42690: "he stood first among the ablest men of our time … at the head in rhetorical science. It is reported that … he was requested by the citizens … to establish there a school of Aristotelian philosophy". The report is credited. Dropping "ablest" keeps the force.
10. "The palace quarter held out against a Roman army, while the rest of the city sided with Rome." EUS 42721–42723: "the other part of the city was allied with the Roman army". The deserters go to "the Roman camp" (EUS 42759). "Palace quarter" is McGiffert's gloss of Pyrucheium, used only to clarify the name.
12. "The deacon, known to the Roman general, won from him safety for any who deserted the besieged side." EUS 42724–42731: his "renowned name which had reached even the Roman general" and "to grant safety to deserters from the enemy. Having obtained his request…"
16. "Under cover of that decree he saved almost all the besieged … men among them secretly dressed in women's clothes." EUS-gr 12498–12503: ἐν πρώτοις μὲν τῶν ἀπὸ τῆς ἐκκλησίας … τῇ δὲ τούτων προφάσει καὶ μυρίους ἄλλους, λεληθότως γυναικείαν στολὴν ἀμπισχομένους. That the disguised were men follows from the disguise itself and from the decree, which freed only those "not needful for the army".
18. "After the war, the bishop of Caesarea … made him a second bishop there, to take over at his death." EUS 42925–42930: "first ordained him as bishop, designing to make him his successor … for a short time both of them presided over the same church". "After the war" rests on EUS 42764–42767: "came after the aforesaid war from Alexandria".
22. "Easter's date was tied to the Jewish Passover full moon, the fourteenth day of the first month of spring." EUS 42864–42878: "after the vernal equinox, in the middle of the first month … the fourteenth of the month … as may be seen in full moons". The extract applies this to Christians' "fourteenth of the passover" (EUS 42815–42817).
23. "That month … must not be counted from a new moon before the spring equinox, in March, when day and night are equal." EUS 42788–42817 gives "the new moon of the first month" and says those who "place the first month in" the segment before the equinox err. 22 March, "the eleventh before the Kalends of April", is already four days past the equinox. "When day and night are equal" glosses the word.
25. "He set his count on a nineteen-year table of the spring new moons, which then begins again." EUS 42788–42790: "the new moon of the first month, which is the beginning of every cycle of nineteen years"; EUS 42811: "the head of the cycle".
26. "The rule was not his own, he said, since the Jews kept it before Christ." EUS 42819–42821: "this is not an opinion of our own; but it was known to the Jews of old, even before Christ, and was carefully observed by them."
27. "About 600, Columbanus, an Irish abbot, wrote to Pope Gregory the Great to defend his homeland's Easter against an Easter past the moon's twentieth day." COL 4320–4322: "Easter on the 21st or 22nd day of the moon". COL 4420ff (COL-lat 20397: "nostris magistris et Hibernicis antiquis"). COL-lat 20775 and 20796 ("traditioni patriae meae"). "About 600" is as rough as Gregory's pontificate allows. "Abbot" is Widely Accepted.
29. "Such a moon, he argued, rises after midnight, so darkness rules most of the night, but the feast of the resurrection is a feast of light." COL 4392–4394: "a moon in its 21st or 22nd day is out of the dominion of light, as having risen at that time after midnight". COL 4350–4355: "darkness light … the solemnity of the Lord's Resurrection is light".
31. Colman's claim, told as a statement. BEDE 8117–8121 gives it as a question: "Did Anatolius, a holy man … act contrary … when he wrote, that Easter was to be celebrated from the fourteenth to the twentieth?" The question asserts what he wrote, so a statement keeps its sense.
34. "Bede doubted the Irish claim." BEDE-lat 18710: "Quod an uerum sit, peritus quisque facillime cognoscit". With BEDE 5747–5748 ("not altogether according to knowledge"), this is a fair reading of Bede's ironic aside.
