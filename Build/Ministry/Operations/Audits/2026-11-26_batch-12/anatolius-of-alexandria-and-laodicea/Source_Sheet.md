## Witness kinds

- **His own words.** The extract from his *Paschal Canons* that Eusebius copies (EUS 42788–42897; EUS-gr 12595–12621). His speech to the city council, as Eusebius reports it from what he was told (EUS 42735–42743; EUS-gr 12485–12492).
- **Contemporary, a younger man.** Eusebius of Caesarea, *Church History* VII.32, written early in the 300s. He counts Anatolius among "the ablest men of our time" (EUS 42688) and calls the two Laodicean bishops "our contemporaries" in sense (EUS 42662: "Among our contemporaries"). The siege he tells from report: "They relate", "They say" (EUS 42696, 42718; Greek μνημονεύουσιν, φασίν, EUS-gr 12400, 12402).
- **Later, about a century.** Jerome, *Lives* 73 (its year not checked here; tell it only as "a century later"), Letter 70, *Chronicle*. Rufinus's Latin version of VII.32, which retells the siege (RUF, EUS-gr 12444–12451, 12533–12545, 12654–12663).
- **Later, about three centuries.** Columbanus, letter to Pope Gregory the Great (COL 4320–4363), and letter to a synod of Gallic bishops (COL-lat 20792–20797).
- **Later, about four centuries.** Bede, *Ecclesiastical History*, finished in 731, telling the synod at Whitby of 664 (BEDE 8237) and the Irish Easter (BEDE 5749–5757).
- **Late.** Socrates (fifth century, using what Eusebius "related"); Barhadbeshabba (about 600, a Church of the East writer, BHB 2–3); Elias of Nisibis (eleventh century); Michael the Syrian and Bar Hebraeus (twelfth and thirteenth); the Coptic *History of the Patriarchs* (a late compilation; Evetts notes "The rest of this life is from Eus., H. E., VII, 32", HP 16927).
- **Modern** (tools only): McGiffert, Salmond, Richardson, Barmby, Hefele, Harnack, Duchesne, Loofs, Bardy, the Bollandists, the PL 67 editors.
- **Not held:** Stephen's *Life of Wilfrid* (the other early account of Whitby); a Greek text of the *Paschal Canons* beyond Eusebius's extract; the Greek or Latin original of the *Institutes of Arithmetic*; Zahn, Ideler, Krusch and Hagen, whom McGiffert and Harnack cite; Bede's letter "according to Anatolius" itself (only its title in his list, BEDE 15512–15513).

**Language.** He wrote in Greek; Eusebius's Greek extract is held (EUS-gr). The Latin *Paschal Canon* under his name is held only in English (LATCAN) and is disputed (D4). Bede is held in Latin and English; Columbanus in Latin and (the Gregory letter) English.

## Setting

S1. **Alexandria, and his learning.** EUS 42684–42690 (VII.32.6), their words of praise, Documented as Eusebius's judgment: "He also was an Alexandrian by birth. In learning and skill in Greek philosophy, such as arithmetic and geometry, astronomy, and dialectics in general, as well as in the theory of physics, he stood first among the ablest men of our time, and he was also at the head in rhetorical science. It is reported that for this reason he was requested by the citizens of Alexandria to establish there a school of Aristotelian philosophy." Greek EUS-gr 12392–12399: "γένος μὲν καὶ αὐτὸς Ἀλεξανδρεύς … τῆς ἐν Ἀλεξανδρείᾳ Ἀριστοτέλους διαδοχῆς τὴν διατριβὴν λόγος ἔχει πρὸς τῶν τῇδε πολιτῶν συστήσασθαι αὐτὸν ἀξιωθῆναι" (λόγος ἔχει, "the report has it"). The school: Documented as a report only. McGiffert: "It is not said that he held any ecclesiastical office in Alexandria" (EUS-ed 42669–42670, modern, clarifies).
- Explain it: "dialectics" = the art of argument, logic; "school of Aristotelian philosophy" = a school teaching the philosophy of Aristotle (Greek τῆς Ἀριστοτέλους διαδοχῆς, "of Aristotle's succession", EUS-ed 42691–42693).
- Jerome, *Lives* 73 (JER 40932–40938), later, about a century: "Anatolius of Alexandria, bishop of Laodicea in Syria, who flourished under the emperors Probus and Carus, was a man of wonderful learning in arithmetic, geometry, astronomy, grammar, rhetoric, and dialectic." Documented (Jerome); the list differs from Eusebius's (D3).
- Rufinus (EUS-gr 12444–12447): "qui et ipse genere Alexandrinus erat studiis liberalium litterarum et in philosophicis a puero adprime eruditus, ita ut apud Alexandriam dialecticam docere ab omnibus eruditis viris et filosofis cogeretur" (trained from boyhood, so that all the learned men and philosophers made him teach dialectic at Alexandria). Later; differs from Eusebius (D3).

S2. **Time.** No held old source gives his birth or death year. Jerome: "flourished under the emperors Probus and Carus" (JER 40933–40934); Jerome's *Chronicle*, under Probus: "Anatolius Laodicenus episcopus philosophorum disciplinis eruditus plurimo sermone celebratur" (JCHRON 44129–44131; a second copy 44453–44455). Eusebius puts the final synod on Paul in Aurelian's reign (EUS 41936; McGiffert says Eusebius is "entirely mistaken" there, EUS-ed 41950–41951). Modern years, each a claim of its own: Richardson "Born about 230, bishop 270, died about 283" (JER-ed 40932, no source given); Salmond "[a.d. 230–270–280.]" and "The dates which are known suggest conjectural dates" (ANF6-ed 14711–14714); Harnack bishop "im J. 268 oder um dieses Jahr", Jerome's Chronicle dates "wertlos", and "Wann Anatolius gestorben ist, wissen wir nicht" (HARN 4799–4807); Hefele "Bishop of Laodicea in Syria since 270" (HEF 20639). Plain words for the story: "in the later 200s" (the emperors he flourished under, Jerome). Contested (any single year); the century, Documented.

S3. **Laodicea.** "Laodicea in Syria" (JER 40933; BCHRON 23993 "Laodiciae Syriae"). CEN `modernCountries`: Laodicea, Syria. Loofs: "im syrischen Laodicea (am Meere)" (LOOFS 13957–13958, modern, clarifies the place). Plain words: "Laodicea in Syria" (Jerome). "On the coast" rests only on Loofs; leave it out. Documented.

S4. **Tradition.** CEN: `toldWithin` `alexandria-catechetical`; spoken name "Alexandrian Christianity". The corpus map files his Easter canon there "provisional because his episcopate was Syrian" (`cic/corpus-map/alexandria-catechetical.yaml` 105–107).

## Events

E1. **The siege of the palace quarter.** EUS 42696–42716 (VII.32.7), Documented as report: "They relate of him many other eminent deeds during the siege of the Pyrucheium […] in Alexandria, on account of which he was especially honored by all those in high office; but I will give the following only as an example." Greek EUS-gr 12399–12402 (μυρίας … ἀριστείας … μνημονεύουσιν; "countless … deeds of valour … they recall").
- Explain it (modern, clarifies): the Pyrucheium or Brucheium "was one of the three districts of Alexandria and was inhabited by the royal family and by the Greeks. It was the finest and most beautiful quarter of the city, and contained, besides the royal palaces, many magnificent public buildings. Comprising, as it did, the citadel as well, it was besieged a number of times, and it is uncertain which siege is meant" (EUS-ed 42702–42710). Plain words: "the palace quarter of the city".
- Why it was besieged: Eusebius gives no reason. "The other part of the city was allied with the Roman army" (EUS 42721–42723); so the besieged quarter was holding out against Rome. Rufinus: a war "quod inter cives exortum est apud Alexandriam", a war that broke out among the citizens (EUS-gr 12447–12448). Jerome's *Chronicle*: "In Alexandria Bruchium quod per multos annos fuerat obsessum" (JCHRON 44299–44301; the next words are not in the lines read, so only "besieged for many years" is used). The year: D2.

E2. **The famine and the deacon outside.** EUS 42718–42731 (VII.32.8–9), Documented as report: "They say that bread had failed the besieged, so that it was more difficult to withstand the famine than the enemy outside; but he being present provided for them in this manner. As the other part of the city was allied with the Roman army, and therefore was not under siege, Anatolius sent for Eusebius,—for he was still there before his transfer to Syria, and was among those who were not besieged, and possessed, moreover, a great reputation and a renowned name which had reached even the Roman general,—and he informed him of those who were perishing in the siege from famine. When he learned this he requested the Roman commander as the greatest possible favor, to grant safety to deserters from the enemy. Having obtained his request, he communicated it to Anatolius." Greek EUS-gr 12402–12404, 12472–12482 (τοῦ πυροῦ, φασίν, … ἐπιλελοιπότος, "the wheat, they say, had run out"; σωτηρίαν τοῖς ἀπὸ τῶν πολεμίων αὐτομόλοις, "safety for those who desert from the enemy").
- Who the deacon was: "Eusebius, who had come from the city of Alexandria" (EUS 42657–42658); McGiffert: "a deacon in the church of Alexandria" (EUS-ed 42641–42642, modern, from Dionysius's letter in VII.11). Name him by role: he shares the historian's name.

E3. **The council, and his speech.** EUS 42733–42743 (VII.32.9), his words as Eusebius reports them: "As soon as he received the message he convened the senate of Alexandria, and at first proposed that all should come to a reconciliation with the Romans. But when he perceived that they were angered by this advice, he said, 'But I do not think you will oppose me, if I counsel you to send the supernumeraries and those who are in nowise useful to us, as old women and children and old men, outside the gates, to go wherever they may please. For why should we retain for no purpose these who must at any rate soon die? and why should we destroy with hunger those who are crippled and maimed in body, when we ought to provide only for men and youth, and to distribute the necessary bread among those who are needed for the garrison of the city?'"
- Greek EUS-gr 12482–12492 (OCR normalized only where shown): "ὁ δὲ αὐτίκα τὴν ἐπαγγελίαν δεξάμενος, βουλὴν τῶν Ἀλεξανδρέων συναγαγών, τὰ μὲν πρῶτα πάντας ἠξίου φιλικὴν δοῦναι Ῥωμαίοις δεξιάν, ὡς [OCR `oc`] δ᾽ ἀγριαίνοντας ἐπὶ τῷ λόγῳ συνεῖδεν, 'ἀλλ᾽ οὐ τούτῳ γε,' φησίν, 'ἀντιλέξειν ποθ᾽ ὑμᾶς οἴομαι, εἰ τοὺς περιττοὺς καὶ [`xci`] ἡμῖν αὐτοῖς οὐδαμῇ χρησίμους, γραΐδας καὶ [`xci`] νήπια καὶ πρεσβύτας, ἐκδοῦναι πυλῶν ἔξω βαδίζειν ὅποι καὶ βούλοιντο, συμβουλεύσαιμι. τί γὰρ δὴ τούτους εἰς μάτην, ὅσον οὔπω τεθνηξομένους, παρ᾽ ἑαυτοῖς ἔχομεν; τί δὲ τοὺς ἀναπήρους καὶ τὰ σώματα λελωβημένους τῷ λιμῷ κατατρύχομεν, τρέφειν δέον μόνους ἄνδρας καὶ [`xci`] νεανίας καὶ τὸν ἀναγκαῖον πυρὸν τοῖς ἐπὶ φυλακῇ τῆς πόλεως ἐπιτηδείοις ταμιεύεσθαι;'" Apparatus (EUS-gr 12509–12513): manuscripts vary on συμβουλεύσαιμι/-οιμι, ἔχομεν/ἔχωμεν/ἔχοιμεν, κατατρύχομεν/-ωμεν; the sense is the same.
- Explain it: "senate" (βουλή) = the city council of Alexandria (EUS-gr 12483, and 12498 "ἐν τῇ βουλῇ"). "Reconciliation" = φιλικὴν δοῦναι Ῥωμαίοις δεξιάν, "give the Romans the hand of friendship": make peace with Rome.
- Why: the council was angry at the call for peace (ἀγριαίνοντας, "growing savage"); the speech then argues from the council's own need, bread for the defenders. Documented (Eusebius's report).

E4. **The vote, the night exit, the disguise.** EUS 42745–42762 (VII.32.10–11), Documented as report: "With such arguments he persuaded the assembly, and rising first he gave his vote that the entire multitude, whether of men or women, who were not needful for the army, should depart from the city, because if they remained and unnecessarily continued in the city, there would be for them no hope of safety, but they would perish with famine. As all the others in the senate agreed to this, he saved almost all the besieged. He provided that first, those belonging to the church, and afterwards, of the others in the city, those of every age should escape, not only the classes included in the decree, but, under cover of these, a multitude of others, secretly clothed in women's garments; and through his management they went out of the gates by night and escaped to the Roman camp. There Eusebius, like a father and physician, received all of them, wasted away through the long siege, and restored them by every kind of prudence and care." Greek EUS-gr 12492–12502, 12570–12578 (μικροῦ δεῖν τοὺς πάντας … διεσώσατο, "he saved all but a few"; ἐν πρώτοις μὲν τῶν ἀπὸ τῆς ἐκκλησίας, "first of all those of the church"; τῇ δὲ τούτων προφάσει καὶ μυρίους ἄλλους, λεληθότως γυναικείαν στολὴν ἀμπισχομένους, νύκτωρ, "under cover of these, countless others too, secretly put into women's dress, by night"; πατρὸς καὶ ἰατροῦ δίκην, "like a father and a doctor").
- The force, to keep: the vote let go only those "not needful for the army"; under its cover he sent out men of fighting age dressed as women, and the church's people first. Documented as report.

E5. **Caesarea, then Laodicea.** Documented.
- EUS 42683–42684 (VII.32.6): "was appointed his successor; one good man, as they say, following another" (EUS-gr 12391–12392: ἀγαθός, φασίν, ἀγαθοῦ). "His" = the deacon Eusebius, bishop of Laodicea.
- EUS 42764–42767 (VII.32.12): "The church of Laodicea was honored by two such pastors in succession, who, in the providence of God, came after the aforesaid war from Alexandria to that city." Greek EUS-gr 12578–12583 (μετὰ τὸν δηλωθέντα πόλεμον, "after the war described").
- EUS 42925–42940 (VII.32.21): "Theotecnus, bishop of Cæsarea in Palestine, first ordained him as bishop, designing to make him his successor in his own parish after his death. And for a short time both of them presided over the same church. But the synod which was held to consider Paul's case called him to Antioch, and as he passed through the city of Laodicea, Eusebius being dead, he was detained by the brethren there."
- The deacon's own road: "The occasion of his removal thither was the affair of Paul. He went on this account to Syria, and was restrained from returning home by those there who were zealous in divine things" (EUS 42660–42662).
- Explain it: "Paul's case" = the case of Paul of Samosata, bishop of Antioch, who "held, contrary to the teaching of the Church, low and degraded views of Christ, namely, that in his nature he was a common man" (EUS 41838–41840, VII.27.2). The final synod: "a final synod composed of a great many bishops was held, and the leader of heresy in Antioch was detected, and his false doctrine clearly shown before all, and he was excommunicated from the Catholic Church under heaven" (EUS 41936–41954, VII.29.1). "Synod" = a meeting of bishops. "Ordained him as bishop … his successor" = made him a second bishop beside himself, to take over at his death (McGiffert's "assistant bishop", EUS-ed 42671, is modern; Harnack "Koadjutor", HARN 4793).
- McGiffert: "When on his way to (possibly on his return from) the synod" (EUS-ed 42673–42674), and "Eusebius, his old friend" (EUS-ed 42677): neither is in Eusebius. Harnack: the church there "nötigte" him (HARN 4797). Eusebius's own word is "detained" (Greek not re-read for this phrase; use the English).

E6. **His Easter book.** His own words, Documented.
- EUS 42769–42773 (VII.32.13): "Anatolius did not write very many works; but in such as have come down to us we can discern his eloquence and erudition. In these he states particularly his opinions on the passover." Greek EUS-gr 12584–12591.
- Extract (EUS 42788–42817; EUS-gr 12595–12616): "There is then in the first year the new moon of the first month, which is the beginning of every cycle of nineteen years, on the twenty-sixth day of the Egyptian Phamenoth; […] but according to the months of the Macedonians, the twenty-second day of Dystrus, […] or, as the Romans would say, the eleventh before the Kalends of April. On the said twenty-sixth of Phamenoth, the sun is found not only entered on the first segment, […] but already passing through the fourth day in it. They are accustomed to call this segment the first dodecatomorion, […] and the equinox, and the beginning of months, and the head of the cycle, and the starting-point of the planetary circuit. But they call the one preceding this the last of months, and the twelfth segment, and the final dodecatomorion, and the end of the planetary circuit. Wherefore we maintain that those who place the first month in it, and determine by it the fourteenth of the passover, commit no slight or common blunder."
- Greek of the last sentence (EUS-gr 12608–12616): "δι᾽ ὃ καὶ τοὺς ἐν αὐτῷ τιθεμένους τὸν πρῶτον μῆνα καὶ τὴν τεσσαρεσκαιδεκάτην τοῦ πάσχα κατ᾽ αὐτὴν λαμβάνοντας οὐ μικρῶς οὐδ᾽ ὡς ἔτυχεν ἁμαρτάνειν φαμέν." Schwartz flags κατ᾽ αὐτὴν as wrong ("falsch, man verlangt etwa κατὰ τὴν (τούτου νουμηνίαν)", EUS-gr 12632–12633); the sense of the main clause is not affected. Rufinus confirms the cycle: "decem et novem annorum circuli principium" (EUS-gr 12666–12667).
- He says the rule is old (EUS 42819–42871): "And this is not an opinion of our own; but it was known to the Jews of old, even before Christ, and was carefully observed by them. This may be learned from what is said by Philo, Josephus, and Musæus; […] the two Agathobuli, […] and the famous Aristobulus […]. These writers, explaining questions in regard to the Exodus, say that all alike should sacrifice the passover offerings after the vernal equinox, in the middle of the first month."
- Then (EUS 42886–42893): "But I refrain from demanding this sort of demonstration for matters from which the veil of the Mosaic law has been removed, so that now at length with uncovered face we continually behold as in a glass Christ and the teachings and sufferings of Christ." (Paul, 2 Cor. 3:18, EUS-ed 42890.)
- Also "the Institutes of Arithmetic, in ten books, and other evidences of his experience and proficiency in divine things" (EUS 42917–42922).
- Explain it, from his own words: the Passover falls on "the fourteenth of the month, beginning with the evening", at full moon, "after the vernal equinox" (EUS 42876–42878, 42866–42868). "First month" = the first month of the Jewish year, counted from a new moon. "Cycle of nineteen years" = a table of years after which the count starts again from its "head" (EUS 42811, "the head of the cycle"); McGiffert: "the old Metonic nineteen-year cycle" (EUS-ed 42791–42793, modern, clarifies). "Phamenoth 26" = 22 March (EUS-ed 42794–42798; Anatolius himself gives the Roman form, "the eleventh before the Kalends of April"). "The sun … passing through the fourth day" of the first segment: by his count the spring equinox is four days before 22 March (EUS 42807–42809). "Segment" = a twelfth of the sun's yearly path (EUS-ed 42806).
- What he argued against: putting the first month, and so the Passover's fourteenth day, in the month before the equinox (EUS 42812–42817). That is what he defended. No old source says whom he meant.
- Where and when he wrote it: no old source says. Harnack thinks at Alexandria (HARN 4855–4856, modern, own claim). Hefele: "began it with the year 277" and "He considers the 19th March as the equinox" (HEF 20642–20651, modern, own claim).

E7. **The end.** EUS 42942–42944 (VII.32.22): "And after Anatolius had departed this life, the last bishop of that parish before the persecution was Stephen". No held old source tells his death, its place or year. Documented (succession only).

## Impact, ranked: what later witnesses carried on and used

I1. **His name in the Easter quarrel of the Irish and Rome.** The heaviest: three held witnesses across sixty years cite him on the date of Easter.
- (a) **Columbanus to Pope Gregory the Great** (COL 4320–4363; COL-lat 20307–20347), later, about three centuries: "What, then, dost thou say concerning Easter on the 21st or 22nd day of the moon, which (with thy peace be it said) is proved by many calculators not to be Easter, but in truth a time of darkness? For it is not unknown, as I believe, to thy Efficiency, how Anatolius […] (a man of wonderful learning, as says Saint Hieronymus, extracts from whose writings Eusebius, bishop of Cæsarea, inserted in his Ecclesiastical History, and Saint Hieronymus praised this same work about Easter in his catalogue) disputes with strong disapprobation about this age of the moon. For against the Gallican Rimarii […], who erred, as he says, about Easter, he introduced an awful sentence, saying, Certainly, if the rising of the moon be delayed till the end of two watches, which indicates midnight, light does not overcome darkness, but darkness light; which thing is certainly not allowable in the Easter Festival, namely, that any part of the darkness should dominate over the light, since the solemnity of the Lord's Resurrection is light, and there is no communion of light with darkness." Latin COL-lat 20323–20341 ("Non latet enim, ut credo, efficaciam tuam, quantum Anatolius, mire doctrinae vir, ut sanctus ait Hieronymus …"; OCR: "luam" for tuam, "doctrine" for doctrinae, "in'eeclesiastica"). Documented (Columbanus's letter). The words Columbanus quotes are in the Latin *Paschal Canon* under Anatolius's name (LATCAN 15014–15024: "Certainly if the rising of the moon should be delayed on to the end of two watches, that is to say, to midnight, the light would not then exceed the darkness, but the darkness the light"), not in the Greek extract. "Hieronymus" = Jerome. Date of the letter: Barmby gives the Benedictines' "a.d. 598–9" (COL-ed 4179–4180, modern); tell it as "while Gregory the Great was pope".
- (b) **Columbanus to a synod of Gallic bishops** (COL-lat 20591–20597, 20791–20797), Latin only, OCR damaged: "Sed confiteor conscientiae meae secreta, quod plus credo traditioni patriae meae juxta Do….. et calculum lxxxiv annorum et Anatolium, ab Eusebio Ecclesiasticae Historiae auctore episcopo, et sancto Catalogi scriptore Hieronymo laudatum, Pascha celebrare, quam juxta Victorium nuper dubie scribentem" (I confess the secrets of my conscience, that I trust more the tradition of my homeland, … the reckoning of 84 years, and Anatolius, praised by Bishop Eusebius and by Saint Jerome, to keep Easter, than Victorius, who wrote of late and doubtfully). A word is lost at "Do….."; the rest is legible. Documented; narrator's words only (one damaged print, no English).
- (c) **Bede on Aidan** (BEDE 5749–5757; BEDE-lat 18702–18709), later, about a century after Aidan: Aidan kept Easter Sunday "from the fourteenth to the twentieth moon; the northern province of the Scots, and all the nation of the Picts, celebrating Easter then after that manner, and believing that they therein followed the writings of the holy and praiseworthy Father Anatolius; the truth of which every skilful person can discern." Latin: "aestimans se in hac obseruantia sancti ac laude digni patris Anatolii scripta secutam. Quod an uerum sit, peritus quisque facillime cognoscit" (whether this is true, any skilled person can very easily tell). Bede's doubt, credited to him. Documented.
- (d) **Whitby, 664** (BEDE 7956–7970, 8117–8150, 8195–8218, 8226–8237; BEDE-lat 21561–21594), Bede writing in 731. The synod met "in the monastery of Streaneshalch, which signifies the Bay of the Lighthouse, where the Abbess Hilda … then presided"; "the Abbess Hilda and her followers were for the Scots" (BEDE 7958–7967). Earlier in the debate Wilfrid had cited Nicaea "as the Church History informs us" (BEDE 8083–8085).
  - Colman (BEDE 8117–8121): "Did Anatolius, a holy man, and much commended in church history, act contrary to the law and the Gospel, when he wrote, that Easter was to be celebrated from the fourteenth to the twentieth?" Latin BEDE-lat 21561–21571: "Numquid Anatolius uir sanctus, et in praefata historia ecclesiastica multum laudatus, legi uel euangelio contraria sapuit, qui a XIIII usque ad XX pascha celebrandum scripsit?" (OCR damaged at 21570–21571: "laiulatus", "contraiia .sapiiit, <itii", "sc lii.sit"; Giles confirms the reading). "In praefata historia ecclesiastica" = in the church history already named: Eusebius's.
  - Wilfrid (BEDE 8134–8150): "It is evident," said Wilfrid, "that Anatolius was a most holy, learned, and commendable man; but what have you to do with him, since you do not observe his decrees? For he, following the rule of truth in his Easter, appointed a revolution of nineteen years, which either you are ignorant of, or if you know it, though it is kept by the whole church of Christ, yet you despise it. He so computed the fourteenth moon in the Easter of our Lord, that according to the custom of the Egyptians, he acknowledged it to be the fifteenth moon in the evening; so in like manner he assigned the twentieth to Easter-Sunday, as believing that to be the twenty-first moon, when the sun had set, which rule and distinction of his it appears you are ignorant of, in that you sometimes keep Easter before the full of the moon, that is, on the thirteenth day." Latin BEDE-lat 21581–21594 (legible): "Constat," inquit, "Anatolium uirum sanctissimum, doctissimum, ac laude esse dignissimum; sed quid uobis cum illo, cum nec eius decreta seruetis? Ille enim in pascha suo regulam utique ueritatis sequens, circulum X et VIIII annorum posuit, quem uos aut ignoratis, aut agnitum et a tota Christi ecclesia custoditum pro nihilo contemnitis. Ille sic in pascha dominico XIIII lunam conputauit, ut hanc eadem ipsa die more Aegyptiorum XV lunam ad uesperam esse fateretur …"
  - The outcome: King Oswy ruled for Peter, "who is proved to have the keys", and "all present … gave their assent" (BEDE 8204–8218); "Colman, perceiving that his doctrine was rejected, … took with him such as would not comply with the Catholic Easter and the tonsure … and went back into Scotland" (BEDE 8227–8233); "the year of our Lord's incarnation 664" (BEDE 8236–8237). Documented as Bede's account.
  - Explain it: "the Scots" = Bede's word for the Irish (BEDE 5757–5758, "the Scots which dwelt in the South of Ireland"). "From the fourteenth to the twentieth" = the days of the moon's month on which Easter Sunday may fall. Where the two sides' words come from: Colman's range is in the Latin book under his name ("it is granted that we may extend the beginning of our celebration even to the moon's twentieth", LATCAN 15010–15012; "we decide that it is better that it should be extended even on to the twentieth day, on account of the Lord's day", LATCAN 15150–15153); Wilfrid's cycle of nineteen years is in the Greek extract (E6). That match is a reading of the texts, Documented on both sides.
- (e) What the Latin book is: no old source questions its authorship except by implication (Bede's doubt, Wilfrid's answer). Modern: D4. Story: say "a Latin Easter book that went under his name", crediting Columbanus and Colman with citing it.

I2. **Remembered for learning.** Jerome, *Lives* 73 (S1) and "We can get an idea of the greatness of his genius from the volume which he wrote On the passover and his ten books On the institutes of arithmetic" (JER 40935–40938). Jerome's Letter 70, defending Christian writers' use of pagan learning, lists the works "of Anatolius chief priest of the church of Laodicea" among those who "so frequently interweave in their books the doctrines and maxims of the philosophers that you might easily be at a loss which to admire most, their secular erudition or their knowledge of the scriptures" (JEP 16665–16702). Jerome's *Chronicle* (JCHRON 44129–44131), copied by Bede's *Greater Chronicle* (BCHRON 23993–23998) and Prosper (`prosper_chronica-minora-1-lat_mommsen1892.txt` 51520). Church of the East: Barhadbeshabba lists "Anatolius (Anfounis) , évêque de Laodicée" (OCR as printed) among the teachers living "dans le pays des Romains" (BHB 13022–13023; French translation). Bar Hebraeus: Eusebius and Anatolius, "Alexandrini ambo atque in disciplinis tam sacris quam profanis vere principes" (BARH 4961–4965). Michael the Syrian: "Il eut pour successeur Anatolius qui était célèbre" (MICH 15951–15952). Documented. Card material.

I3. **His extract used by later reckoners.** Socrates, against Sabbatius's Easter: "The ancient Hebrews, as is related by Eusebius, on the testimony of Philo, Josephus, Aristobulus, and several others, offered the sacrifices after the vernal equinox" (SOC 41696–41705). The names are those of Anatolius's extract (EUS 42822–42867); the note "Extracts from the canons of Anatolius" is the translator's (SOC 41699–41700). Socrates does not name him: Inferential-Thin as a use of Anatolius. Elias of Nisibis, eleventh century: "Et demonstrant verba Anatolii, episcopi Laodiceae, qui vixit tempore Galli imperatoris, aequinoctium vernale incidisse, eius tempore, in diem 22am adar" (ELIAS 41059–41062): his words show the equinox fell on 22 Adar in his time, two days earlier than in Christ's (41062–41066). Bede lists among his own works "one of the Reason of the Bissextile, or Leap-Year, and of the Equinox, according to Anatolius" (BEDE 15512–15513). Documented. Card material.

I4. **The siege remembered.** Rufinus retells it (D1). The Coptic *History of the Patriarchs*, a late compilation, retells it with changes: "Anatolius the teacher did not cease negotiating between the two parties … until he improved the state of affairs, and established peace, and the war ceased", and his words "Let the old men and old women and young children leave the city, for they are not required here" (HP 17072–17083; OCR damaged, "neu'Otia liner heUvcen tin*" read as "negotiating between the"). Late legend; Leave out.

I5. **A saint's day.** The Bollandists: "S. Anatolius Episcopus Laodiceae memoratur in fastis Maurolyci, Felicii, Galesinii, Ferrarii. Ast in Martyrologio Romano [3?] Julii" (AASS 69809–69811; OCR "Laodicecde", "Mauvolyci", "Itomano ur Julii"; the day number is not legible). Modern report of old calendars. Card only, as the Bollandists' note.

## Where sources disagree, or come late

D1. **The siege in Rufinus.** Eusebius: Anatolius was inside ("he being present"), sent word to the deacon outside, and the deacon won the promise and received the refugees (E2, E4). Rufinus (EUS-gr 12533–12545, 12654–12658): the city in three parts, one of which "quasi fidem Romanis servabat. in qua parte tunc etiam supra dictus Anatolius erat absque inpugnatione. hic ad magistrum militiae refert" (Anatolius was in the part loyal to Rome and went to the commander himself); his speech asks only "ut omnem vestrorum manum inbellem … exire portas sinatis" (that you let all your people unfit for war go out of the gates); those who escaped were "praecipue nostri quibus bellum gerere contra propositum erat" (above all our own people, whose way of life forbade making war); and "Anatolius suscipiens parentis simul ac medici fovebat affectu" (Anatolius received them with a parent's and a doctor's care), freeing the city "fame pariter … et bello" (from famine and war alike). Later, Contested. Story: follow Eusebius, crediting him; Rufinus on the card, or one credited sentence.

D2. **The year of the siege.** McGiffert: "uncertain which siege is meant … most likely … the revolt of Æmilian … in 260 a.d. … Valesius and others think of a later siege under Claudius" (EUS-ed 42706–42714); elsewhere he gives Eusebius's *Chronicle* date of 272 (EUS-ed 41937–41938) and, for the deacon, "in 262" (EUS-ed 42646). Salmond: "a.d. 262" (ANF6-ed 14731). Harnack: Aemilian's revolt under Gallienus (HARN 4773–4775). Loofs: the quarter's fall "wahrscheinlich aber erst 270" (LOOFS 13979–13980). Jerome's *Chronicle*: destroyed in Claudius's first year after a siege of "many years" (JCHRON 44298–44301). Contested. Story: no year.

D3. **His subjects, and the school.** Eusebius: arithmetic, geometry, astronomy, dialectic, physics, rhetoric; a report that he was asked to found an Aristotelian school (S1). Jerome: grammar in place of physics (JER 40935). Rufinus: made to teach dialectic (S1). Duchesne: "chef de l'école aristotélicienne d'Alexandrie" (DUCH 18887–18888, modern, own claim). Story: Eusebius's list, credited; the school as a report.

D4. **The Latin Easter book.** McGiffert: "supposed to be the work of Rufinus (though this is uncertain) … Ideler … claims that this supposed translation of Anatolius is a work of the seventh century. But there are the best of reasons for supposing it an early translation of Anatolius' genuine work (see Zahn …)" (EUS-ed 42773–42780). Harnack: shown spurious by Hagen, with Ideler and Krusch; Zahn raised doubts; it holds only §§14–18 of Eusebius's extract and matches Rufinus's version; "Die Schrift wird im 6. Jahrhundert oder schon früher im Zusammenhang mit der britisch-römischen Osterkontroverse gefälscht worden sein" (HARN 4822–4873). Salmond: "generally ascribed to Rufinus" (ANF6-ed 14747). Barmby: "its genuineness is doubted" (COL-ed 4327–4328). Contested; modern only. Story: "a Latin book under his name"; the authorship debate on the card.

D5. **What his days of the moon meant.** Colman: he wrote Easter could be kept from the fourteenth to the twentieth. Wilfrid: he counted in the Egyptian way, so his fourteenth was the fifteenth by evening, and his twentieth the twenty-first (I1d). Bede: whether the Irish truly followed him, any skilled person can tell (I1c). Each credited.

D6. **Did he reach the synod?** Eusebius: called to Antioch, detained at Laodicea on the way through (E5). McGiffert: "on his way to (possibly on his return from)". Bardy: "Plusieurs témoignages s'accordent à dire cependant qu'il était présent au concile qui déposa Paul" (BARDY 11412–11413; the witnesses sit in a footnote not read). Inferential-Thin. Story: Eusebius's words only.

D7. **Who first used the nineteen years.** Wilfrid: Anatolius "appointed a revolution of nineteen years" kept by the whole church (I1d). Abbot Ceolfrid's letter in Bede: the nineteen-year circle "began long since, that is, in the very times of the apostles, especially at Rome and in Egypt" (BEDE 14738–14742), and does not name him. McGiffert: "so far as we know, the first Christian to employ the old Metonic nineteen-year cycle for the determination of Easter" (EUS-ed 42791–42793; again EUS-ed 40908–40910). Hefele: "He discovered the Easter cycle of nineteen years" (HEF 20641–20642). Bucherius, quoted by the PL 67 editors: used "ad paucos annos Laodiceae … aut vicinis etiam locis" and soon pushed out by "Eusebii Caesariensis cyclus" (PL67-ed 36039–36046). "First Christian" rests only on modern works: out of the story and card. Story: his own words name the cycle; Wilfrid credits him with it.

D8. **How the cycle spread.** McGiffert: "soon adopted in the Alexandrian church … the nineteen-year cycle gradually won its way everywhere" (EUS-ed 40910–40917). Hefele: "soon subjected to different modifications, after which it was generally adopted in Alexandria from the time of Diocletian" (HEF 20658–20660). Modern only: Leave out of story and card as claims; the works may go in Modern works.
