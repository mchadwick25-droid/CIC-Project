# Outline: Tacitus

Every line cites `Source_Sheet.md` (S setting, C the chapter, R what came of it, D disagreement, X explain-it list in §6, N not held), which gives each file and line. MIR = the Latin of *Annals* 15.44 in Mirbt's print of Halm's text; TAC = the 1876 English.

**The heart**
- **The essence:** Tacitus, a Roman historian writing a generation or more after the great fire of Rome in 64, gives in a few sentences the fullest account from outside the church of how Nero punished the Christians, and names Christus, from whom their name came, put to death under Pontius Pilate. He despised them, and later Christians retold his scene, answered him by name, and argued over it. (C2–C7: Documented as his account; the fire's year, S1: Widely Accepted; "a generation or more", S3: Widely Accepted; R1–R3: Documented.)
- **Also told:** What the chapter says, in its order: the rites that failed to stop the rumour that the fire was ordered; Nero's blame on people the crowd hated and called Christians; Christus under Pilate, and the "deadly superstition" breaking out again; the arrests, first of those who confessed, then of a huge number on their word; a conviction less for the fire than for "hatred of the human race"; the deaths as a show; the pity that arose because they seemed to die for one man's cruelty (C1–C7: Documented as his). What it leaves open, told inside what he does: he names no source for Christus, none of the dead, and not what they confessed (C3–C5, D6). What later readers did with it (R1–R3).
- **What came of him** (ranked under "What came of it" below). Nothing defended: he is an outside witness.

**Length:** about 3 minutes 30 seconds, about 530 words. The story must carry: three quotes (about 75 words), the plain sense of eight words or works (about 50), and the credited witnesses, Tacitus as the one source, Tertullian, Sulpicius, and the two men of the 1876 debate (about 55): about 180 words before the narration. Beats as planned run to about 590; the Cut first list brings them to 530. Shorter would drop either the chapter in its order or the readers who used it; the record is one short chapter, so longer would stretch it (S6).

**Setting** (facts for the writer, a clause at a time)
- Rome, after the great fire of 64 (S1). The year comes from the held moderns; Tacitus's chapter gives none.
- Tacitus wrote a generation or more later (S3). Never a year, never "about fifty years".
- Nero's gardens, and the chariot-racing ground where he held games (C6).
- Judaea, where, Tacitus says, the movement began (C4).
- No tradition name: he stands outside the church (S5).

**Words to explain** (plain sense where first heard)
- **Annals:** "his year-by-year history of Rome" (X).
- **procurator:** say "the governor" (C4, X). Never "procurator".
- **superstition:** his word of contempt for their religion, which he calls deadly: "a deadly superstition, he calls it" (C4, X). Do not copy the sibling's wording ("Superstition was his word of contempt for their religion").
- **circus:** "chariot races" or "the chariot-racing ground" (C6, X).
- **hatred of the human race:** told in his words, with no gloss added (C5, D3).
- **Histories** (beat 7): "another of his histories, which told where the Jews came from" (R3, X).
- **Apology** (beat 7): "his defence of the Christians" (X).
- **Chronicle** (beat 7): "a short history of the world" (X).
- Leave out: the Sibylline books and the gods' names (C1). Say "prayers and offerings to the gods".

**Shape:** Outside witness. The record decides the order: the chapter as he wrote it, then its readers in time order, then his last sentence. Trap: making him a friend or a foe, or stretching his few sentences. His contempt and his pity are both his; say how short the passage is (S6).

**Openings**
- (a) Chosen. When daylight failed in Nero's gardens, people were set alight to light the dark. Others, covered in animal skins, were torn apart by dogs, or nailed to crosses (C6). The Roman who tells it, the historian Tacitus, thought them guilty and despised them (C3, C7). Then the essence.
- (b) A Roman historian, writing of the fire, stops to explain a name, and in one sentence names Christus, put to death under Tiberius by the governor Pontius Pilate (C4). Not used: it spends Q1 before the story reaches it.

**Story beats** (beat 1 is the first minute)
1. **Opening and essence.** As above. Fold in how short it is: about 140 words of Latin, a few sentences (S6: Documented as a count). Say here, once, inside a sentence about him, that the details of these deaths rest on him alone (S6; of the held old sources, Suetonius gives one clause, §4, and Sulpicius writes centuries later from Tacitus's words, R1: Documented within the held texts).
2. **The writer and the rumour.** In his year-by-year history of Rome, Tacitus tells that after the fire Rome turned to the gods, with prayers, offerings and night vigils kept by married women. But nothing, not human effort, not the emperor's gifts, not the offerings, ended the shameful talk that the fire had been set on an order (C1, C2). He reports a belief; he does not say who gave the order. Documented as his account.
3. **The blame, and the name.** So, to kill the rumour, Nero put forward others as the guilty: people the common crowd hated for their shameful acts and called Christians. He punished them with the most refined cruelty (C3). Tacitus does not say what the shameful acts were (X). Then he stops to explain the name: Q1 (C4). He names no source for it (D6). He goes on: this deadly superstition, as he calls it, checked for a moment, broke out again, in Judaea where the evil began, and in Rome itself, where, he says, everything horrible or shameful flows in from everywhere and finds a following (C4). Documented as his account.
4. **Arrests and verdict.** First those who confessed were seized. Then, on their information, a huge number were convicted, less, he says, of setting the fire than of hatred of the human race (C5). He does not say what the first ones confessed. Documented as his account.
5. **The show.** Mockery was added to their deaths: the deaths the opening showed. Do not list the skins, dogs and crosses again; say only that Nero gave his gardens for the show and put on chariot races, mixing with the crowd dressed as a charioteer, or standing in a chariot himself (C6). Documented as his account. His closing sentence (Q3) is held for the ending.
6. **The readers, in time order** (each credited):
   - About 200, in Carthage, Tertullian, in his defence of the Christians, answered Tacitus by name, about another of his histories, which told where the Jews came from. Tacitus had written that the Jews, dying of thirst in the desert, followed wild asses to water and honoured the animal's head; people took it that the Christians, close to the Jews, worshipped the same. Tertullian called him a liar, and a wordy one (narrator's words from "mendaciorum loquacissimus", not a quote), and caught him out: the same Tacitus tells that Pompey, who took Jerusalem, went into the temple and found no image there (R3). Narrator's words; no quote. Documented. It is about the Jews, not the fire.
   - About 400, Sulpicius Severus, a Christian writing a short history of the world, told the fire, the blame and the same deaths, in nearly Tacitus's own Latin words, though he names no source (R1: Documented; that he drew on Tacitus, Widely Accepted). But where Tacitus called them guilty, Sulpicius wrote: Q2.
   - In 1876, at a public debate in England over whether the Bible was divine, a Christian speaker used the chapter, with a Roman governor's letter, to show that a vast multitude believed in Christ in the first century. Charles Bradlaugh, an atheist, said the passage was open to challenge: the church historian Eusebius never quotes it, and the early centuries were full of forgeries. He would not deny it outright; he put the burden of proof on his opponent (R2d). Documented as an event. This is the one doubt among held witnesses (D2).
7. **Ending.** See Ending.

**What came of it** (ranked heaviest first; weight is what later witnesses carried on and used)
1. **A Christian history carried his scene on, in his own words, and called the dead innocent** (R1, R1a). Beat 6, second item.
2. **Later readers used it to count and weigh the early church, and one doubted it** (R2: Hooker, Ruinart against Dodwell, Whiston, Roberts and Bradlaugh). Beat 6, third item; the rest to the card.
3. **A Christian answered him by name**, about his account of the Jews (R3). Beat 6, first item.
Nothing defended. No line on who keeps him today.

**Quotes** (all three new; none is in a live story on the tree or elsewhere in this batch; no reuse)
- **Q1.** MIR 2541–2543 (Latin, Halm's text, clean print; OCR "Cliristus"; confirmed by HOOK 13988–13994 and OTTO's context, and in English by WH 138268–138271): "Auctor nominis eius Christus Tiberio imperitante per procuratorem Pontium Pilatum supplicio adfectus erat". Rendered from the Latin: **"Christus, from whom that name comes, had been put to death in the reign of Tiberius by the governor Pontius Pilate."** Job: a Roman enemy's own line naming Christus and Pilate, which the narrator cannot carry. Beat 3. The 1876 English's "one of our procurators" is not in the Latin and is not used.
- **Q2.** SUL-L 4789–4790 (Latin, Halm's CSEL text; damaged OCR, "^tur uertit inuidiam in Christianos, actaeque in innoiios cnidelissimae quaestiones", confirmed by Roberts's English, SUL-E 10659–10661): "[igi]tur uertit inuidiam in Christianos, actaeque in innoxios crudelissimae quaestiones". Rendered from the Latin: **"So he turned the blame onto the Christians. And the cruellest tortures were used on the innocent."** Job: a Christian keeps Tacitus's scene and reverses its verdict. Beat 6.
- **Q3.** MIR 2563–2564 (Latin, Halm's text; the last words damaged in the OCR, "saevitiara unius desumerentur"; the first half confirmed by RUI 3134–3136, the sense of the whole by TAC 68 s12, English of 1876): "Unde quamquam adversus sontes et novissima exempla meritos miseratio oriebatur, tamquam non utilitate publica sed in saevitia[m] unius [ab]sumerentur." Rendered from the Latin: **"So pity began to rise, even toward the guilty, who had earned the harshest punishment as a warning. It was as if they were being destroyed not for the public good, but for one man's cruelty."** Job: his contempt and his judgement of Nero in his own last sentence. The ending.

**Connection to carry into the story:** A man who despised the Christians gave the church, and everyone after, the fullest outside record of how Christians died at Rome under Nero, and a Roman's line that Christus was put to death under Pilate (C3–C6). The listener must hear three things to follow it: that Tacitus was a Roman historian who was hostile throughout (C3, C4, C7); that the details of these deaths rest on his few sentences (S6, D8); and that Christians and later readers took the passage up and changed or tested it: Tertullian called him a liar on another matter (R3), Sulpicius kept the scene in Tacitus's words and called the dead innocent (R1), and in 1876 a debater used it to prove the first church was large while his opponent doubted it was genuine (R2d). Carried by these acts, never by a "this matters because" sentence.

**Ending:** (a) Chosen: his own last sentence, Q3, after the readers: the guilty, as he calls them, pitied because they died for one man's cruelty (C7). It turns back to the gardens of the opening without retelling them. (b) Possible: Q3 in time order at the end of beat 5, and the story ending on Bradlaugh's challenge. Not chosen: it ends on a later debater, not on the subject.

**Do not say**
- Not "one of our governors" or "one of our procurators". The Latin says "by the governor" (Q1, C4).
- Not "the governor of Judaea" as Tacitus's words. He says "the procurator"; Judaea comes in the next clause (C4).
- Not "Nero started the fire" in the narrator's voice. People believed it had been set on an order (C2).
- Not "the Christians set the fire" or "were convicted of arson". Say "less of setting the fire than of hatred of the human race" (C5).
- Not "convicted not of arson but of hatred". Keep "less ... than" (C5).
- Not what the shameful acts were (no child-eating, incest or the like). He does not say (C3).
- Not "they confessed to the fire" or "to being Christians". Say "those who confessed" (C5).
- Not "hundreds" or "thousands". Say "a huge number" (C5).
- Not "burned as torches", "covered in pitch", "in the Vatican" or "in summer". Say they were burned to light the night, in Nero's gardens (C6). Pitch, the Vatican and summer are Duchesne's (DUC 2362–2372, sheet S1).
- Not "Tacitus pitied them" or "came to respect them". He reports that pity arose, names no one who felt it, and still calls them guilty (C7).
- Not "the first", "the earliest" or "the only" outside mention of Jesus or the Christians. Say "the fullest account from outside the church" of these deaths (S6).
- Not "Tacitus proves Jesus lived", or anything past Q1 (C4). Not "their founder": his words are "from whom that name comes" (auctor nominis).
- Not "senator", "consul" or any office. "A Roman historian" (S2).
- Not "in 116", "about 110" or "about fifty years later". Say "a generation or more after the fire" (S3).
- Not that Tertullian answered this chapter or called the fire account a lie. He answered Tacitus's account of the Jews (R3).
- Not "Sulpicius copied Tacitus word for word" or "Sulpicius names Tacitus". Say "in nearly Tacitus's own words", and that he names no source (R1).
- Not that Tacitus mentions Antium, laws or edicts, or Peter and Paul. Those are Sulpicius's (R1).
- Not that the victims were really Jews, or that historians agree they were Christians (D1: card).
- Not what the verdict "really meant" (D3: moderns' reading, card).
- Not "Bradlaugh proved it a forgery" or "historians doubt it". He thought it open to challenge and would not deny it outright; he is the one held doubter (R2d, D2).
- Not "historians still rely on it today". The held historians stop at 1922 (R2e).
- Not "Chrestians", or anything on the spelling of the name (D5).
- Not where Tacitus learned of Christus (D6).
- Not a sentence that only says what is missing. Each gap is told inside a sentence about what he does (Kit, Craft).
- Any word in Words to explain used without its plain sense the first time, "procurator", "circus" and "superstition" among them.
