When daylight failed in Nero's gardens, people were set alight to light the dark. Some, covered in the skins of animals, were torn apart by dogs, and many were nailed to crosses. The Roman who tells it, the historian Tacitus, thought them guilty and despised them.

Writing a generation or more after the great fire of Rome in 64, Tacitus gave the fullest account from outside the church of how Nero punished the Christians. In it he named Christus, from whom their name came, put to death under Pontius Pilate. His account runs to a few sentences, about 140 words of Latin, and the details of these deaths rest on him alone. Later, one Christian, Tertullian, answered him by name on another matter, and another, Sulpicius Severus, retold his scene in nearly his own words and called the dead innocent.

In the Annals, his year-by-year history of Rome, Tacitus tells how Rome turned after the fire to prayers and offerings to the gods. But nothing, not human effort, not the emperor's gifts, not the offerings, ended the shameful talk that the fire had been set on an order.

So, to kill the rumor, Nero put forward others as the guilty: people the common crowd hated for their shameful acts and called Christians. He punished them, Tacitus writes, with the most refined cruelty. Then Tacitus stops to explain the name, naming no source for it: "Christus, from whom that name comes, had been put to death in the reign of Tiberius by the governor Pontius Pilate." Their religion was a deadly superstition, he says with contempt, and though it was checked for a moment, it broke out again, in Judaea, where the evil began, and in Rome itself.

First those who confessed were seized. Then, on their information, a huge number, none of them named, were convicted, less, he says, of setting the fire than of hatred of the human race. Mockery was added to their deaths, and these were the deaths in the gardens, which Nero gave for the show. He put on chariot races there too, mixing with the crowd dressed as a charioteer, or standing in a chariot himself.

About 200, in Carthage, the Christian Tertullian answered Tacitus by name in his Apology, his defense of the Christians. His quarrel was with another of Tacitus's histories, which told where the Jews came from. That account of the Jews, Tertullian says, started the tale that the Christians, close to the Jews, worshiped an ass's head. Tertullian called Tacitus a liar, and a wordy one, and caught him out: the same Tacitus tells that the Roman general who took Jerusalem went into the temple and found no image there.

About 400, Sulpicius Severus, a Christian writing a short history of the world, told the fire, the blame and the same deaths in nearly Tacitus's own Latin words, though he names no source. Where Tacitus called the dead guilty, Sulpicius wrote: "So he turned the blame onto the Christians. And the cruellest tortures were used on the innocent."

Tacitus closed his own account with a sentence that still called them guilty: "So pity began to rise, even toward the guilty, who had earned the harshest punishment as a warning. It was as if they were being destroyed not for the public good, but for one man's cruelty."
