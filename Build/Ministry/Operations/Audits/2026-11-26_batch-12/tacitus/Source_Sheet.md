# Source sheet: Tacitus

## 1. Setting

S1. **The fire, 64.** McG (EUS 13843–13844): "the great Roman conflagration, which took place in 64 a.d." DUC 2362–2363: "Pendant l'été de l'année 64, un incendie terrible détruisit les principaux quartiers de Rome." SCH 24282–24283: "Als eine große Feuersbrunst Rom im Jahre 64 verheerte". MIR 2518: "Die Verfolgung der Christen unter Kaiser Nero i. J. 64." MODERN, all agreeing. Widely Accepted (the kit itself names the fire of 64 as a date told plainly). Tacitus's chapter gives no year and does not tell the fire itself: it opens after it, with the rites (C1). *Annals* 15.38–43, the fire, is not held (N1).

S2. **Who Tacitus was, as the held old sources show him.** A Roman historian. Tertullian names him "Cornelius Tacitus" and cites "the fifth book of his histories, beginning the (narrative of the) Jewish war with an account of the origin of the nation" (TERT-E 2570–2573; Latin TERT-L 17833–17836, "Hanc Cornelius Tacitus suspicionem eiusmodi dei inseruit. Is enim in quinta historiarum suarum bellum Iudaicum exorsus ab origine gentis"). LATER, Documented. So he wrote at least two histories: the *Histories* (not held) and the *Annals* (the chapter held). MON 9287–9289 (Montaigne, 1580s, reporting a passage not held): Tacitus "having said, how he had exercised a certaine honourable magistracy in Rome". WH 141169–141171: "Tacitus was an eminent pleader and writer of history at Rome". Both late reports with no held old source: Inferential-Thin. TAC-H 5 "Roman senator": EDITOR. Plain word: "a Roman historian".

S3. **When he wrote.** No date in his own text. Tertullian's report that the *Histories* told the Jewish war (S2) puts that work after the war of 70: Documented as Tertullian's report. YON 757–759: Ammianus's history "began with the accession of Nerva, A.D. 96, where Tacitus and Suetonius end". RB 2566: "Tacitus, the Roman historian, who lived at the close of the first century". WH 138262: "About A. D.110." MAP 2344–2345 and TAC-H 8: "c. 116". CEN `tacitus.dates`: "wrote after 96". MODERN and EDITOR. Plain: "a generation or more after the fire", Widely Accepted. Any year, or "about fifty years later", is Dominant Modern Reconstruction.

S4. **Where.** Rome. Nero's gardens and the circus (C6). Judaea named as where the movement began (C4). OWN, Documented as his account.

S5. **Tradition.** CEN `tacitus`: `toldWithin` null, status "Within Another World (A5)", era 1, region Rome. No tradition's spoken name applies. MAP 2344–2346: "Tacitus is a hostile outside witness ... he is not a voice of the tradition and must never be compiled as one." EDITOR.

S6. **How short the passage is.** The Library's English of the whole chapter is 368 words (counted on TAC 68; TAC-H 38 says 375); the part on the Christians, from s6 to the end, is 252 English words. In Latin (MIR 2527–2564) the whole chapter is about 205 words, and the part on the Christians, from "Ergo abolendo rumori" to the end, about 140 words in six sentences as Halm punctuates them. Counted for this sheet. Documented as a count of the held text. Plain: "a few sentences", "about a hundred and forty words of Latin".

## 2. The chapter, in its order

All OWN. Latin from MIR (confirmations noted); English from TAC 68. Documented as Tacitus's account; everything it says of the Christians rests on him alone (D1–D4).

C1. **After the fire, the rites.** MIR 2527–2535: "Mox petita dis piacula aditique Sib[y]llae libri, ex quibus supplicatum Volcano et Cereri Proserpinaeque ac propitiata Iuno per matronas ... et sellisternia ac pervigilia celebravere feminae, quibus mariti erant." TAC 68 s2–s4: "recourse was had to the Sibylline books, by the direction of which prayers were offered to Vulcanus, Ceres, and Proserpina ... And there were sacred banquets and nightly vigils celebrated by married women." Plain: Rome sought peace with the gods, by prayers from its sacred books of prophecy, offerings and night vigils kept by married women.

C2. **The rumour.** MIR 2535–2537: "Sed non ope humana, non largitionibus principis aut deum placamentis decedebat infamia, quin iussum incendium crederetur." Confirmed SCH 24313–24314. TAC 68 s5: "did not banish the sinister belief that the conflagration was the result of an order." Plain: nothing, not human effort, not the emperor's gifts, not the offerings, ended the shameful talk that the fire had been ordered. Tacitus reports a belief; he does not say who gave the order.

C3. **Nero puts the blame on the Christians.** MIR 2537–2541: "Ergo abolendo rumori Nero subdidit reos et quaesitissimis poenis adfecit, quos per flagitia invisos vulgus Christianos appellabat." Confirmed OTTO 12176–12189, HOOK 13982–13988, RUI 3147–3149. TAC 68 s6: "to get rid of the report, Nero fastened the guilt and inflicted the most exquisite tortures on a class hated for their abominations, called Christians by the populace." Plain: to kill the rumour, Nero put forward others as the guilty and punished them with the most refined cruelty: people the common crowd called Christians, hated for their shameful acts. He does not say what the acts were.

C4. **Christus under Pilate, and the "superstition."** MIR 2541–2547: "Auctor nominis eius Christus Tiberio imperitante per procuratorem Pontium Pilatum supplicio adfectus erat; repressaque in praesens exitiabilis superstitio rursum [er]umpebat, non modo per Iudaeam, originem eius mali, sed per urbem etiam, quo cuncta undique atrocia aut pudenda confluunt celebranturque." Confirmed HOOK 13988–13998 ("Auctor nominis ejus Christus, qui Tiberio imperitante per procuratorem Pontium Pilatum supplicio affectus erat. Repressaque in praesens exitiabilis superstitio rursus erumpebat"), OTTO 12189–12192. TAC 68 s7 (English): "Christus, from whom the name had its origin, suffered the extreme penalty during the reign of Tiberius at the hands of one of our procurators, Pontius Pilatus". **The Latin says "per procuratorem", "by the procurator"; the English "one of our procurators" adds "one of our".** Whiston's English agrees with the Latin: "brought to punishment by Pontius Pilate the procurator" (WH 138268–138271). Plain: the name came from Christus, executed under Tiberius by the governor Pontius Pilate; the deadly superstition, checked for a moment, broke out again, in Judaea where the evil began, and in Rome itself, where everything horrible or shameful flows in from everywhere and finds a following. ROL 25875–25876 (Rolfe): "Tacitus, Ann. 15. 44, uses the correct form, Christus, and states that He was executed in the reign of Tiberius." MODERN, clarifies.

C5. **Arrests, informers, verdict.** MIR 2547–2551: "Igitur primum correpti qui fatebantur, deinde indicio eorum multitudo ingens haud perinde in crimine incendii quam odio humani generis convicti sunt." Confirmed SCH 24316–24318, 24348; RUI 3150–3151 and 5221–5223 (which read "coniuncti", joined, for "convicti", D5). TAC 68 s8: "an arrest was first made of all who pleaded guilty; then, upon their information, an immense multitude was convicted, not so much of the crime of firing the city, as of hatred against mankind." Plain: first those who confessed were seized; then, on what they told, a huge number were convicted, less of setting the fire than of hatred of the human race. He does not say what they confessed.

C6. **The deaths made a show.** MIR 2551, 2560–2563: "Et pereuntibus addita ludibria, ut ferarum tergis contecti laniatu canum interirent, multi crucibus adfixi aut flamma usti, aliique, ubi defecisset dies, in usum nocturni luminis urerentur. Hortos suos ei spectaculo Nero obtulerat et circense ludicrum edebat, habitu aurigae permixtus plebi vel curriculo insistens." RUI 5223–5228 prints the first sentence with "aut crucibus affixi, aut flammandi, atque ubi defecisset dies" (D5). TAC 68 s9–s11: "Covered with the skins of beasts, they were torn by dogs and perished, or were nailed to crosses, or were doomed to the flames and burnt, to serve as a nightly illumination, when daylight had expired. Nero offered his gardens for the spectacle, and was exhibiting a show in the circus, while he mingled with the people in the dress of a charioteer or stood aloft on a car." Plain: mockery was added to their deaths. Covered in animal skins, they were torn apart by dogs; others were nailed to crosses or burned, and when daylight failed, burned to light the night. Nero gave his gardens for the show and put on games at the chariot-racing ground, mixing with the crowd dressed as a charioteer, or standing in a chariot.

C7. **Pity, and his judgement.** MIR 2563–2564: "Unde quamquam adversus sontes et novissima exempla meritos miseratio oriebatur, tamquam non utilitate publica sed in saevitia[m] unius [ab]sumerentur." The last words are damaged in MIR's OCR ("saevitiara unius desumerentur"); RUI 3134–3136 confirms the first half ("quamquam adversus sontes et novissima exempla meritos ... miseratio ... oriebatur"), and TAC 68 s12 confirms the sense of the whole: "Hence, even for criminals who deserved extreme and exemplary punishment, there arose a feeling of compassion; for it was not, as it seemed, for the public good, but to glut one man's cruelty, that they were being destroyed." He names no one who felt the pity. He still calls them guilty; he blames Nero's cruelty. Both judgements are his.

## 3. What came of it (heaviest first)

R1. **A Christian history carried the scene on, in Tacitus's own Latin, and called the dead innocent.** SUL-L 4783–4795: "Interea abundante iam Christianorum multitudine accidit uti Roma incendio conflagraret, Nerone apud Antium constituto. sed opinio omnium invidiam incendii in principem retorquebat, credebaturque imperator gloriam innovandae urbis quaesisse. neque ulla re Nero efficiebat, quin ab eo iussum incendium putaretur. [igi]tur uertit inuidiam in Christianos, actaeque in inno[x]ios crudelissimae quaestiones: quin et nouae mortes excogitatae, ut ferarum tergis contecti laniatu canum interirent, multi crucibus affixi aut flamma usti, plerique in id reseruati, ut cum defecisset dies, in usum nocturni luminis urerentur. hoc initio in Christianos saeuiri coeptum." SUL-E 10651–10666: "He therefore turned the accusation against the Christians, and the most cruel tortures were accordingly inflicted upon the innocent. Nay, even new kinds of death were invented ... In this way, cruelty first began to be manifested against the Christians." LATER. Documented: set beside MIR 2537, 2560–2561, the clauses "quin ... iussum incendium" and "ut ferarum tergis contecti laniatu canum interirent, multi crucibus affixi aut flamma usti ... ubi/cum defecisset dies, in usum nocturni luminis urerentur" are nearly word for word Tacitus's. Sulpicius names no source; that he took them from Tacitus is Widely Accepted (the wording; Halm's apparatus points to Tacitus at this passage, "Tac. Ann. 15, 37" SUL-L 4744 and "Tac ann. 15, 40" SUL-L 4801). Caution: the held prints of Tacitus differ at "multi crucibus adfixi aut flamma usti" (MIR) against "aut crucibus affixi, aut flammandi" (RUI 5224–5225), so say "nearly the same words", never "word for word". Sulpicius goes past Tacitus: Nero at Antium; laws and edicts later made it unlawful to be a Christian; Paul and Peter condemned then (SUL-E 10653, 10667–10670). Those are his, not Tacitus's.

R1a. **The same phrase turned on heretics.** SUL-L 5669–5671: "infamis illa Gnosticorum haeresis ... superstitio e[x]itiabilis arcanis occultata secretis. origo istius mali Oriens atque Aegyptus". SUL-E 11453–11455 (Roberts's note): "'superstitio exitiabilis': the very words which Tacitus employs, when speaking of Christianity itself (Annal. xv. 44)." Halm at "origo istius mali": "cf. Tac. Ann. 15, 44" (SUL-L 5695). Tacitus's words, MIR 2543–2545: "exitiabilis superstitio ... originem eius mali". Documented that the words recur; the reading is the editors'. Card only.

R2. **Later readers used it to count the church, and one doubted it.**
- R2a. Richard Hooker, in the 1590s, cites the Latin of s6–s7 in a footnote for the claim that "in the estimation of the rest of the world even Christ Jesus himself was execrable" (HOOK 13948, 13980–13998). LATE READER. Documented.
- R2b. Thierry Ruinart, a Benedictine monk who died in 1709 (RUI 789–792), answering Henry Dodwell's Oxford dissertation of 1684 "de Martyrum paucitate", which argued that very few martyrs suffered (RUI 2295–2301), puts Nero's persecution first, "in qua ingens Christianorum multitudo, teste Cornelio Tacito lib. 15. Annalium, incendi[i] Romani occasione interemta fuit" (RUI 3129–3131; marginal notes run into the OCR are dropped), and says Dodwell "inficiari non audet" (does not dare to deny) what Tacitus reports, "ab auctore minime suspectae fidei" (RUI 3136–3138). LATE READER. Documented as Ruinart's account of the dispute; Dodwell is held only through Ruinart.
- R2c. Whiston sets s6–s7 first among "the ancient citations" beside Josephus (WH 138258–138271). LATE READER.
- R2d. In 1876, in a six-night public debate "Is the Bible Divine?" held in Leicester and Birmingham (RB title), Robert Roberts quoted Tacitus with Pliny to prove "the existence, in the first century, of a vast multitude throughout the Roman Empire believing in Christ" (RB 2565–2569, 2622–2633). Charles Bradlaugh, "an Atheist gentleman" in his own words (RB 6612), answered: "I have not admitted the reliability of the quotation from Tacitus. I am inclined to think that the quotation from Tacitus is liable to impeachment." (RB 3162–3164.) Pressed, he gave his reason: "I find that the author Eusebius don't quote it. The whole of the early ages abounded in [f]orgeries, and I cannot think so important a statement would have been missed." (RB 6675–6676.) He would not go "the length of absolute denial, but they put upon you the burden of proving it" (RB 6671–6672). Roberts then read out Gibbon: "The most sceptical are obliged to respect the truth of this extraordinary fact, and the integrity of this passage" (RB 6628–6630). Documented as an event. Bradlaugh's fact checks: Eusebius's *Church History* 2.25 gives Nero as the first persecutor from Tertullian, not Tacitus (EUS 13836–13842); Tacitus is named in that file only in McGiffert's notes and prolegomena (searched).
- R2e. Modern historians weigh it. McG (EUS 13847–13849): "the terrible Neronian persecution which Tacitus describes so fully was the result." SCH 24279–24281: "das wichtige Zeugnis des Tacitus, das vielfach erläutert, aber auch vielfach mißverstanden wurde." DUC 2373: "Tacite, de qui nous tenons ces détails". HAR 4052–4053, 11645–11647: Tacitus's "multitudo ingens" beside 1 Clement. MODERN. For the card's Modern works list only.

R3. **A Christian answered him by name, about another book.** TERT-L 17832–17921 (*Apology* 16): Tacitus first put about the notion that the Christians' god is an ass's head, through his account of the Jews in the fifth book of his *Histories*. TERT-L 17915–17919: "At enim idem Cornelius Tacitus, sane ille mendaciorum loquacissimus, in eadem historia refert Gneum Pompeium, cum Hierusalem cepisset proptereaque templum adisset speculandis Iudaicae religionis arcanis, nullum illic reperisse simulacrum." The same in *To the Nations* 1.11, TERT-C 4918–4920 ("idem Cornelius Tacitus, sane ille mendaciorum lo[quacissimus], oblitus affirmationis suae in posterioribus refert Pompeium Magnum ... templum adisse et perscrutatum nihil simulacri reperisse"; Oehler's text of the same, TERT-L 33311–33315). English TERT-E 2566–2588 ("the said Cornelius Tacitus (the very opposite of tacit in telling lies)"), TERT-E 10093–10098 ("most loquacious in falsehood"). LATER. Documented. Tertullian does not mention *Annals* 15.44 or the fire. The *Histories* are not held. Plain of Tertullian's point: Tacitus wrote that the Jews, dying of thirst in the desert, followed wild asses to water and so honoured the animal's head; people then assumed the Christians, close kin to the Jews in religion, worshipped the same image (TERT-E 2575–2582). But the same Tacitus tells that Pompey, who took Jerusalem, went into the temple and found no image there.

## 4. Links to other people

- **Tertullian** (`tertullian-s-voice`, live beside): answered him by name (R3). No quote from its live story is used.
- **Sulpicius Severus** (`sulpicius-severus`): retold the fire and the deaths in nearly Tacitus's words (R1). His live story names his *Chronicle* (CEN).
- **Eusebius of Caesarea** (`eusebius-of-caesarea`): gives Nero as first persecutor from Tertullian, not Tacitus (EUS 13836–13842); Bradlaugh's reason turns on this (R2d).
- **Suetonius** (`suetonius`, sibling, researched after this outline): his live text gives Nero's punishment of the Christians in one line and says "Another Roman, Tacitus, tells in full of Christians put to death under Nero" (CEN `suetonius`). MIR 2566–2570 prints the Suetonius line right after Tacitus.
- **Pliny the Younger** (`pliny-the-younger`, live beside): paired with Tacitus by Roberts (RB 2565–2569) and Harnack (HAR 4052–4054). No held line on the two men themselves.
- **Josephus** (`josephus`): Whiston thought Tacitus took "almost all that was true of the Jews" from him (WH 139597–139602). Not this story.
- **Ammianus Marcellinus** (`ammianus-marcellinus`): began his history where Tacitus ended (YON 757–759); Rolfe: "Tacitus did not find a follower until the time of Ammianus Marcellinus (330-400)" (ROL 375–376). Not this story.

## 5. Where sources disagree, or come late

D1. **Christians or Jews?** McG (EUS 13849–13857): "Gibbon, and in recent times especially Schiller ... have maintained that Tacitus was mistaken in calling this a persecution of Christians, which was rather a persecution of the Jews as a whole. But we have no reason for impeaching Tacitus' accuracy in this case ... Josephus is entirely silent in regard to a persecution of his countrymen under Nero." MODERN opinion. Contested. Card only.

D2. **Genuine?** Bradlaugh's doubt (R2d), against Gibbon as Roberts read him (RB 6628–6630). TAC-H 53–54: "The passage's authenticity is not seriously disputed." EDITOR. Every held historian uses it as Tacitus's. Contested only by Bradlaugh among held witnesses, and he would not deny it outright. Told as the debate, an event.

D3. **What were they convicted of?** Tacitus gives only "haud perinde in crimine incendii quam odio humani generis" (C5). Readings: SCH 24300–24304 ("die Untersuchung stellte das Christentum der Angeschuldigten fest, nicht aber die Brandstiftung"); McG EUS 13862–13864 ("it was not aimed against their religion as such"); DUC 2385–2386 ("personne n'attribuait l'incendie aux chrétiens"); RUI 3147–3151 (Tacitus shows the Christians were judged worthy of death even before the fire). MODERN and LATE READER opinion. Dominant Modern Reconstruction. Story tells only Tacitus's words.

D4. **His contempt.** DUC 2392–2394 ("l'appréciation de Tacite, qui étend ici aux chrétiens l'injustice et le mépris dont il aime à accabler les juifs"); DUC 7234–7236; SCH 25275–25277 (Pliny, Tacitus and Suetonius "zeugen von der tiefen Verachtung"). MODERN. His own words carry it plainly (C3, C4, C7): Documented as his.

D5. **Readings of the Latin.** (a) "Christianos" or "Chrestianos": TAC-H 45–51 (the Medicean manuscript "chrestianos", corrected by a later hand); DUC 2078–2082 (note, after Harnack); MIR 2541 and SCH 24316 print "Christianos". (b) "convicti" (MIR 2551, SCH 24348) or "coniuncti" (RUI 3151, 5223). (c) "multi crucibus adfixi aut flamma usti, aliique" (MIR 2560–2561) or "aut crucibus affixi, aut flammandi, atque" (RUI 5224–5225). EDITOR and LATE READER. Contested readings; card only. None changes what the story tells.

D6. **Where did he learn of Christus?** Not said in the chapter. Nothing held answers it. The story says only that he names no source.

D7. **A separate, fire-linked persecution of Christians as a named group?** TAC-H 54–59 names a modern debate (Shaw against Jones); neither work held. Leave out.

D8. **Lateness.** Tacitus wrote a generation or more after the fire (S3). Tertullian about 135 years after it; Sulpicius about 335.

## 6. Explain it: things and acts the story could name

- **The Annals.** Tacitus's history of Rome, told year by year (the Latin title *Annales*, MIR 2520; TAC title). Plain: "his year-by-year history of Rome".
- **The rites** (C1): seeking peace with the gods after the fire, from Rome's sacred books of prophecy. Plain: "prayers and offerings to the gods". The gods' names: leave out.
- **The rumour and Nero's blame** (C2–C3). Tacitus's reason: "abolendo rumori", to kill the rumour that the fire was ordered. Sulpicius's side: people blamed the emperor, believing he sought "the glory of building a new city" (SUL-E 10654–10656), and Nero could not escape the charge, so turned it on the Christians, "the innocent" (SUL-E 10656–10661).
- **"Hated for their shameful acts"** (flagitia, C3). The enemy's charge, given only so. Tacitus names no act. The Christian side, in the held texts: Tertullian answers one charge Tacitus himself started, the ass's head (R3); Sulpicius calls them innocent (R1).
- **"Superstition"** (exitiabilis superstitio, C4). Tacitus's word for their religion, which he calls deadly and an evil that began in Judaea. Plain: "a deadly superstition, he calls it". A Christian reply in kind: Sulpicius uses the same words for a heresy of his day (R1a).
- **Procurator** (C4). The official by whom Pilate had Christus executed. Plain: "the governor".
- **The arrests and the verdict** (C5). Why: Nero's blame (C3). Who first: those who confessed; then others on their information. The charge: less the fire than "hatred of the human race". Tacitus gives no more.
- **The deaths as a show** (C6). Why, in Tacitus: mockery was added; Nero lent his gardens and held circus games. Sulpicius: "new kinds of death were invented" (SUL-E 10661).
- **Circus.** "circense ludicrum" (MIR 2562): games at the chariot-racing ground. Plain: "chariot races"; the modern circus misleads.
- **The pity** (C7). Tacitus's reason: it seemed they died not for the public good but for one man's cruelty.
- **Tertullian's Apology.** A defence of the Christians written to Rome's governors (CEN `tertullian-s-voice`; TERT-E 960–967). Plain: "his defence of the Christians".
- **The Histories.** Another history by Tacitus, whose fifth book began the Jewish war with the origin of the Jews (TERT-E 2571–2573).
- **Sulpicius's Chronicle.** "a compendious history of the world from the Creation down to the year A.D. 400" (SUL-E 441–444). Plain: "a short history of the world".
- **The 1876 debate.** A public discussion over six nights on whether the Bible is divine (RB title), between Roberts, who held it was, and Bradlaugh, an atheist (RB 6612).
