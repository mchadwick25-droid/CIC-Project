In 257 the governor at Carthage, in Roman Africa, sent the city's bishop, Cyprian, into exile at Curubis, a small town on the coast, because he would not take part in the ceremonies of Rome's gods. One of the men who went with him wrote later that Cyprian had chosen him from among the men of his household as "a willing exile." In the same breath, he wished he had been allowed to share Cyprian's death as a martyr too.

His book never gives his name. Jerome, writing more than a century later, calls him Pontius, one of Cyprian's deacons, and beyond that one sentence almost all we know of him comes from his own book, written by a man who loved and praised his bishop. Pontius went into exile by choice, heard Cyprian tell a dream there, and was with him on his last night in 258. After Cyprian was put to death, Pontius wrote The Life and Passion of Cyprian, the story of his life and of his suffering and death as a martyr, so that those who came after would have his example.

On the day they settled at Curubis, Cyprian told his companions what he had seen. Before he fell asleep, he said, a young man of more than human size seemed to lead him to the governor's court. The governor wrote a sentence, his judgment, on his tablet, and the young man standing behind him read it and showed it by a sign. Pontius gives what followed in Cyprian's own words, and the "he" is the young man: "For he held his hand open and flat, like a sword blade, and copied the stroke of the usual execution. What he wanted understood, he showed as clearly as plain speech. I understood the sentence of martyrdom that was to come."

Cyprian begged a delay of one day, and the young man signalled that it was granted until the next day. Pontius read the one day as one year, and wrote that Cyprian would live a year after the vision.

Cyprian's friends later urged him to withdraw, and Pontius says he turned down their offers of places to go. Cyprian's own last letter tells it differently. When officers were sent to take him to another city, it says, he left his gardens for a while on his friends' advice, so that he would be tried in his own city, where he led the church. He was arrested all the same and held overnight in an officer's house, and Pontius writes that he and the others who shared Cyprian's table were with him there. Next day the governor sentenced him to the sword as an enemy of the Roman gods. He died on 14 September 258, on the very day of the year, Pontius says, on which his sentence had been shown him.

Afterward Pontius set out to write. The church's forebears, he says, had honored ordinary believers who died as martyrs and written down their sufferings. It would be hard, he writes, if the death of so great a bishop were passed over, and if his deeds in life stayed hidden. "I have decided to write down a few things, briefly. It is not that the life of so great a man is hidden from anyone, even among the pagans. It is so that this great and matchless example may be passed on to those after us too, into undying memory."

He began at Cyprian's baptism, because the deeds of a man of God, he wrote, should be counted only from the day he was born of God. Of the studies that had filled Cyprian's mind before, he says, "these things I pass over." For what Cyprian told the governor at the hearing that sent him into exile, he sends his readers to the court record.

The book was copied with Cyprian's own writings, and a list of his works made about a hundred years after his death already ends with it. In his catalog of Christian writers, Jerome called it "a notable volume," and without his sentence the book would carry no name at all.

The deacon who had wished at Curubis to share the martyr's death closes his book with his own grief. "Shall I grieve that I was not his companion? But his victory must be celebrated. Shall I celebrate the victory? But I grieve that I am not his companion. Yet I must confess it to you plainly, and you know it too: this was how I felt. I rejoice much, far too much, in his glory. Yet I grieve more that I remained behind."
