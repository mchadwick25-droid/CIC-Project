In 257 the governor at Carthage, in Roman Africa, sent its bishop, Cyprian, into exile at Curubis, a small town on the coast, because he would not take part in the Roman rites, the ceremonies of Rome's gods. One of the men who went with him wrote later that Cyprian had chosen him from his household as "a willing exile," and wished he had been allowed to share the bishop's death as a martyr too.

That man's book never gives his name, but Jerome, in his catalog of Christian writers, calls him Pontius, one of Cyprian's deacons. Beyond that one sentence, all we know of Pontius is in his own book, written by a man who loved and praised his bishop. He shared the exile, heard Cyprian tell a dream that, Cyprian said, showed him his death sentence, and was with him on his last night. After Cyprian was put to death in 258, Pontius wrote The Life and Passion of Cyprian, the story of his life and of his suffering and death as a martyr, so that those who came after would have his example.

On the day they settled at Curubis, Cyprian told them, a young man of more than human size seemed to lead him to the governor's court before he fell asleep. The governor wrote a sentence, his written judgment, on his tablet without the usual questions. The young man behind him read it, and since he could not say it aloud, he showed it by a sign. Pontius gives Cyprian's own words: "For he held his hand open and flat, like a sword blade, and copied the stroke of the usual execution. What he wanted understood, he showed as clearly as plain speech. I understood the sentence of martyrdom that was to come." Cyprian begged a delay of one day to put his affairs in order, and the young man signaled that it was granted. Cyprian woke glad of it, yet trembling at what it might mean.

Pontius read the day as a year, and writes that Cyprian was put to death on the very day of the year on which it had been shown him. The delay, he adds, was asked for the church's business, above all the care of the poor, and Cyprian spent it that way.

In 258, Pontius says, friends of high rank offered Cyprian places to go, and he would not agree. Cyprian's own last letter says that, on their advice, he left his gardens for a while, so that he would be tried in his own city, where he led the church. After his arrest, Pontius writes, he and the others who shared Cyprian's table spent the last night with him as usual. Next day the governor sentenced him to the sword as an enemy of the Roman gods, and he died on 14 September 258.

Pontius says why he wrote. The church's forebears had written down nearly every detail of the sufferings of ordinary believers who died as martyrs, even people still learning the faith before baptism, so that people not yet born would know them. It would be hard if so great a bishop's death were passed over, when he had much to teach apart from it, and his deeds in life stayed hidden. "I have decided to write down a few things, briefly. It is not that the life of so great a man is hidden from anyone, even among the pagans. It is so that this great and matchless example may be passed on to those after us too, into undying memory."

So he began at Cyprian's baptism, because the deeds of a man of God, he wrote, count only from the day he was born of God. Of the studies that had filled Cyprian's mind before, he says, "these things I pass over." For what Cyprian told the governor at his first hearing, he sends readers to the court record.

The book was copied with Cyprian's own writings, and a list of his works made about a hundred years after his death already ends with it. More than a century after the death, Jerome called it "a notable volume" and wrote that its author shared Cyprian's exile until the day of his bishop's death.

The book ends by weighing joy against grief. "Shall I grieve that I was not his companion? But his victory must be celebrated. Shall I celebrate the victory? But I grieve that I am not his companion. Yet I must confess it to you plainly, and you know it too: this was how I felt. I rejoice much, far too much, in his glory. Yet I grieve more that I remained behind."
