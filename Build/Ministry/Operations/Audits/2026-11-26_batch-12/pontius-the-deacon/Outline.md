# Outline: Pontius the Deacon

Kind: person (`kinds/person.md`). Items S, E, I, D are in `Source_Sheet.md`, which gives each passage verbatim with file and line; short names are listed at its head (VE, VL, HAR, AASS, JE, JL, KNOPF, GEB, EP, BEDE, VSB and the modern works). CEN = the census.

## The heart

- **The essence:** Pontius, one of Cyprian of Carthage's deacons, went with his bishop into exile by choice in 257, heard him tell a dream in which, Cyprian said, his death sentence was shown him, and was with him on his last night in 258 (E2, E4, E6; Documented, the book's own claims; his name and office are Jerome's, E1, Widely Accepted). After Cyprian was put to death, Pontius wrote *The Life and Passion of Cyprian*. Christians had already written down the sufferings of ordinary believers who died as martyrs, he says, and he wanted the bishop's deeds in life told as well as his death, for those who came after (E8; VL 40266–40310; Documented, his own words). The book never names him; Jerome does (E1; Documented). It was copied with Cyprian's own writings (I1; Widely Accepted).
- **Also told:** what the book says and why. Why he wrote, in his own words (Quote 1). What he chose: to begin at Cyprian's baptism and pass over all before it, to send readers to the court record for the first hearing (E9). The dream at Curubis, in Cyprian's words as he reports them (Quote 2), and his own reading of it, that one day meant one year (E5). Where his account and Cyprian's own last letter differ (D2). His grief, in the book's last lines, that he was left behind (Quote 3).
- **What came of him:** below, ranked. Defended: that a bishop's whole life, not his death alone, deserved writing down for those to come (E8; Quote 1).

## Setting

- **Time:** the exile, from late August 257 (S1; KNOPF 4823–4825, 4835–4836; the year from the consuls, Widely Accepted). Cyprian's death, 14 September 258 (S1; KNOPF 4955–4956; Widely Accepted). The book: after the death, for people who had heard Cyprian (S1; VE 27692–27695; Documented). No year for the book: 259 is a scholars' reckoning (Dominant Modern Reconstruction). Plain words: "in 257", "in 258", "after Cyprian's death".
- **Place:** Carthage, in Roman Africa, today in Tunisia (S2; CEN). Curubis, the town of the exile (S2; KNOPF 4835–4836; VL 41011–41012). Plain words: "Curubis, a small town on the coast". No distance.
- **Tradition:** the tree's spoken name is "the Latin Pastoral Congregations" (S3; CEN `informalName`). Plain words: "the Latin-speaking church of Carthage".

## Words to explain

- **passion** (in the title, and in his own lines): the suffering and death of a martyr (E4, E8). Gloss the title once: "the story of his life, and of his suffering and death as a martyr". Never "passion" bare.
- **catechumens:** "people still learning the faith before baptism" (E8; VL 40272). Never the word bare.
- **the Acts** (VE 28002–28004, "there are Acts which relate"): "the court record of the hearing" (E9). Never "the Acts" bare, which a listener will hear as the book of the Bible.
- **the Roman rites:** the ceremonies of Rome's gods, which the emperors ordered all who did not keep the Roman religion to take part in (E2; KNOPF 4826–4827). One clause, at the exile.
- **proconsul:** "the governor" (E4).
- **household companions** (*domesticos comites*): "the men of his household" (E2; VL 41013).
- **Jerome's book:** "his catalog of Christian writers" (E1; CEN `jerome` legacy, "His catalog, On Illustrious Men"). The Latin title is not needed.
- **Utica:** "another city", where the governor meant to try him (E6; EP 17370–17371, 17378–17380).

## Shape

**Teacher or thinker** (a biographer). The question he was trying to answer: how should the church remember a bishop who died a martyr? Ordinary believers who died as martyrs had their sufferings written down in detail; it would be hard, he says, if this bishop's death were passed over, when he had much to teach even apart from it, and if his deeds in life stayed hidden (E8). What he taught is in his book's choices: begin at the new birth, pass over what came before, tell the deeds as an example for later readers, and read the dream and the sentence as signs fulfilled (E5, E7, E9). Who learned it: the copyists who kept it with Cyprian's writings, and Jerome, who named its author (I1, I2). The trap is a list of books; with one book, the trap is a list of its contents. Show the idea through his own words (Quote 1) and one scene (the dream). The record decides the order.

## Openings

1. **Chosen: the willing exile** (E2; VL 41013–41015; VE 28055–28059). In 257 the governor at Carthage sent Cyprian, its bishop, into exile at Curubis, a small town on the coast (KNOPF 4835–4836). One of the men who went with him wrote later that Cyprian had chosen him, from among the men of his household, as "a willing exile". In the same breath he wished he had been allowed to share the martyrdom too (VL 41015, "quod utinam et in passione licuisset"; told in the narrator's words). Then the name: Jerome calls him Pontius, one of Cyprian's deacons (JL 57618). This is the first event in time; nothing later goes back before it.
2. Not chosen: his hearers' longing, that they wanted to hear everything of Cyprian now his living voice was silent (E8; VE 27692–27695). It comes after the death; opening there would send the beats back in time.

## Story beats

(The opening and the essence fill the first minute, about 135 words.)

1. **The exile, 257** (E2, E3). Why: the emperors had ordered all who did not keep the Roman religion to take part in the Roman rites; Cyprian would not, and the governor sent him to Curubis (KNOPF 4826–4827, 4835–4836; Documented, one clause). Pontius's own judgment, in his voice if told: wickedness always pays back good with worse (VL 40897–40900). Explain it: the Roman rites, glossed above. He says God gave Cyprian a sunny, fit place, a dwelling as hidden as he wished, many visiting Christians and kind townspeople (VE 28046–28051; VL 40972–40976; Pontius's account). To a Christian, he writes, the whole world is one home (VL 40912–40913, "Christiano totus hic mundus una domus est"; in the narrator's words, not quoted). Confidence: Documented as the record and as his account.
2. **The dream** (E4). On the day they settled at Curubis, Cyprian told them, before he fell asleep a young man of more than human size seemed to lead him to the governor's court (VL 41012–41018; VE 28055–28065). The governor wrote a sentence on his tablet without the usual questions. The young man behind him read it, and since he could not say it aloud, he showed it by a sign (VL 41019–41024). Then Quote 2. Cyprian begged a delay of one day to put his affairs in order; the judge's face seemed moved, and the young man signalled with his fingers twisted one behind the other that the delay was granted until the next day. Cyprian woke glad of it, yet trembling at what it might mean (VL 41029–41073; VE 28074–28089). Explain it: the sentence is the governor's written judgment; the stroke is the sword stroke of execution. Say once that these are Cyprian's words as Pontius gives them. Confidence: Documented as Pontius's report; a wonder, kept in their voice.
3. **How Pontius read it** (E5, E6). The one day, he writes, meant one year: Cyprian would live a year after the vision, and he was put to death, Pontius says, on the very day of the year on which it had been shown him (VL 41080–41083, 41090–41092; Pontius's claim, never the narrator's). He adds that the delay was asked for church business, above all the care of the poor, and that Cyprian spent it so (HAR 1408–1411; VE 28125–28129; his reading). Everyone knew his death was near, he says, though no one spoke of the day (VL 41092–41093). The court record agrees that something had been shown him: back at his gardens after Curubis, it says, he expected every day that they would come for him, as had been shown to him (KNOPF 4855–4857; GEB 6558–6559; Documented; in the narrator's words, not quoted). Confidence: Documented as each witness's words.
4. **The last days, 258** (E6, E7, D2). A messenger came from Rome, from (or about) its bishop, whom Pontius calls a most blessed martyr (VE 28137–28139; the bishop by role). Friends of high rank urged Cyprian again and again to withdraw and offered him places to go; Pontius says he would not agree (VE 28142–28149; VL 41184–41185; Pontius's account). Cyprian's own last letter says that when he heard officers were sent to take him to Utica, he did leave his gardens for a while on his dearest friends' advice, because a bishop should confess the Lord in the city where he leads the church (EP 17370–17375; Documented, his own words). Tell both, side by side, credited, and no verdict. Then the arrest, and the night in the officer's house: "we, his table companions and dear ones," Pontius writes, were with him as usual (VL 41246–41248, "conuiuae eius et cari in coutubernio ex more fuerimus"; told in the narrator's words, or the short phrase "as usual"). Next day the governor sentenced him to the sword, and he died on 14 September 258 (KNOPF 4910, 4955–4956; one clause; the sibling tells the trial and death). Explain it (the sentence): it called him an enemy of the Roman gods, who had gathered men into a wicked conspiracy (KNOPF 4902–4904). Pontius reads the sentence as a prophecy fulfilled (VL 41315–41319; card if room is short).
5. **Why he wrote** (E8). He wrote for people who had heard Cyprian, who wanted to hear everything about him, now that his living voice was silent (VL 40315–40319; VE 27692–27695). He felt pressed on both sides, he says, by the bishop's greatness and by their asking (VE 27698–27700). The church's forebears, he writes, had honored ordinary believers and people still learning the faith who died as martyrs, and wrote down nearly every detail of their sufferings, so that people not yet born would know them. It would be hard if the death of so great a bishop were passed over, when he had much to teach even apart from it, and if what he did in life stayed hidden (VL 40271–40310; VE 27677–27685; paraphrase). Then Quote 1. Explain it: catechumens glossed above. Which earlier accounts he meant is a scholars' reading: say only "accounts of martyrs". Confidence: Documented, his own words.
6. **What he chose to tell** (E9). He began at Cyprian's baptism: the deeds of a man of God, he wrote, should be counted only from the day he was born of God. Whatever studies had filled his mind before, "these things I pass over" (VL 40326–40332; VE 27703–27708; the short phrase may stand). He would tell what he was there for, and what he learned of earlier things (VL 40333, 40364; paraphrase only, D5). So he tells Cyprian giving away the price of whole estates to the poor while still learning the faith (VE 27730–27732), his withdrawal in an earlier persecution, which he says was obedience to God and not fear, since Cyprian later suffered after all (VE 27905–27908; one clause), and his care in a plague for everyone, not only Christians (VE 27991–27992; VL 40855–40856; one clause). He says he leaves out many other things, great ones too, to keep the book in measure (VL 40844–40845). For what Cyprian told the governor at the first hearing, he sends readers to the court record (VL 40900–40901; "the court record tells it"). Confidence: Documented, his own words.

## What came of him (heaviest first)

1. **It was kept with Cyprian's own writings** (I1). It was copied with them, and a list of Cyprian's works made about a hundred years after his death already ends with it (HAR 260–263; VSB 3320–3321, 4159–4161; Widely Accepted; the list's own text is not held). Say "a list of his works made about a hundred years after his death", no year. Defended by him: the whole life written down, for those to come.
2. **Jerome named its author** (I2, E1). More than a century later, in his catalog of Christian writers, Jerome wrote that Pontius, Cyprian's deacon, shared his exile until the day of his death and left "a notable volume" on his life and death (JL 57618–57622; JE 40833–40835; Documented; "a notable volume" may stand as a short phrase). Without that sentence the book would carry no name. This is the connection: tell it here.
3. **Later writers and calendars kept the line** (I3, I4). Bede copied Jerome's sentence into his chronicle (BEDE 23904–23909). Later calendars of saints kept Pontius's day at Carthage, on 8 March, and the Roman one adds that he too earned the crown of life (AASS 196819–196824; late legend). Card.

Who keeps him today: no held source or census tradition entry says; leave out.

2. **The Life, chapter 12: the dream, in Cyprian's words.** Rendered from VL 41025–41029: "mauu enim expansa et complanata ad spatae modum ictum solitae auimaduersiouis imitatus, quod uolebat iutellegi, ad iustar liquidi sermouis expressit. iutellexi sententiam passionis futuram." (that is, *manu ... animaduersionis ... intellegi, ad instar liquidi sermonis ... intellexi*). HAR 1309–1313 reads the same.
   - Rendering: **"For he held his hand open and flat, like a sword blade, and copied the stroke of the usual execution. What he wanted understood, he showed as clearly as plain speech. I understood the sentence of martyrdom that was to come."** (41 words.)
   - Stands behind it: an original in a damaged print that a second held print confirms (VL, HAR), with VE 28071–28074. *Spata* is a sword, so "sword blade"; *animaduersio* is the punishment of execution; *passio* is the martyr's death, so "martyrdom", where VE's "passion" would mislead.
   - Job: Beat 2. The dream's turn, in the voice of the man who dreamed it: the sentence nobody spoke. The narrator says first that "he" is the young man and that Cyprian is speaking.
3. **The Life, chapter 19: the book's last lines.** Rendered from VL 41405–41410: "dolebo quod non comes fuerim? sed illius uictoria triumphanda est. de uictoria triumphabo ? sed doleo quod comes non sim. uerum uobis tamen et simpliciter confitendum est, quod et uos scitis, in hac me fuisse sententia. multum ac nimis multum de gloria eius exulto, plus tamen doleo quod remansi." HAR 1808–1813 and AASS 196784–196790 read the same words.

Yet I must confess it to you plainly, and you know it too: this was how I felt. I rejoice much, far too much, in his glory. Yet I grieve more that I remained behind."** (64 words.)

A companion here is one who shares the death; the opening's wish sets this up.

## Connection to carry into the story

What passed from Pontius was one book, and with it Cyprian's exile, his dream and his last night as told by someone beside him (E2–E6). It passed on in two ways. It was copied with Cyprian's own writings, so that a list of his works made about a hundred years after his death already ends with it (I1). And Jerome read it and put Pontius's name and office to it, which the book itself never gives (I2, E1). The listener must understand what he set out to do, in his own words: martyrs' sufferings had been written down before, and he set out to tell this bishop's life as well, from his baptism, for later readers (E8, E9; Quote 1). The listener must also hear that Pontius chose what to tell, and that on one point Cyprian's own letter tells it differently (D2); that is the record's conflict, and it shows his book as praise by a man who loved its subject. Tell it through what was done with the book, the copying and the naming, never as a "this matters because" sentence.

## Ending

1. **Chosen: the book's last lines, Quote 3** (E11; VL 41381–41410). Tell it last, after What came of him, framed as the close of his own book ("His book ends ..."), so the listener hears a return to the book, not a new event after Jerome. It answers the opening: the man who wished at Curubis to share the martyrdom ends by grieving that he remained behind. Keep "What shall I do now? Between joy at his passion and grief at still remaining, my mind is divided" (VE 28294–28296) as narration only, if at all.
2. Not chosen: Jerome's sentence. It is the connection, and ending on a later writer is right only when that is the story's point; here the point is Pontius's own grief.
