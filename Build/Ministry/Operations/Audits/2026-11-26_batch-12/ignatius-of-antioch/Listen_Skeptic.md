## 1. The test (person)

- **Who:** filled. A bishop of Antioch in Syria, taken as a prisoner to Rome.
- **What happened:** filled. The arrest under Trajan, the road through Asia, the seven letters, the death by the beasts as Eusebius reports it.
- **What it was about:** filled. The three messages are unity around the bishop, Christ's real suffering, and "do not save me".
- **Why the connection matters:** filled, but thinly. Polycarp gathers the letters, and Irenaeus quotes the wheat line. "Later bishops quoted them for centuries" is never shown beyond those two (see 4).

## 2. The opening

The first three sentences are all quotation: "From Syria all the way to Rome ... Even when they are treated well, they grow all the worse." After them the listener does not know **who** is speaking or **when**, and does not know **why** he is on the road. They know only "Syria to Rome". The name comes in sentence 4, after the quote, which makes it a held-back reveal (Part 1: "never as a held-back reveal"). Sentence 4 also ends on "and the beasts on his road were his guards". That repeats the gloss the quote has already given ("I mean a band of soldiers").

BLOCKING (who). Exact change: replace paragraph 1 with:
> Ignatius, bishop of the church at Antioch in Syria, was a prisoner bound for death in the arena at Rome when he wrote: "From Syria all the way to Rome I fight with beasts, by land and by sea, by night and by day. I am bound to ten leopards, I mean a band of soldiers. Even when they are treated well, they grow all the worse."

The change uses only the brief's own words and cuts 11 words.

## 3. Sentences that fail on one hearing (MINOR)

1. "They thought the same of his own sufferings, and he charged..." The meaning lost is whose sufferings these are, Christ's or Ignatius's. Name the person instead of "his": either "of Christ's sufferings" or "of Ignatius's own chains", whichever the outline holds.
2. "If they spared him, it would be hard for him to reach God; if they kept silent, he would become God's." The meaning lost is what "kept silent" means: that Rome's Christians should not plead for him. The listener keeps the thread only because paragraph 2 said "do not save me". Give the plain sense alongside, as Part 1 allows, in words the outline holds.
3. "Two centuries on, Eusebius wrote that report said Ignatius was sent..." The meaning lost is who is reporting what. "Wrote that report said" stacks two layers of reporting and garbles the line. "Two centuries on" also follows "seventy years later", so the listener can count from Irenaeus, not from Ignatius. Exact change: "Two centuries after Ignatius, the church historian Eusebius wrote that, by report, he became food for wild beasts at Rome for his testimony to Christ." This also drops the second telling of "sent from Syria to Rome".

## 4. Bare statements (BLOCKING)

1. "News had come that the church at Antioch was at peace." Peace after what? The story never says the church was troubled, so the news and the messenger sent "to rejoice with it" carry no meaning. Exact change: say what it was at peace from, as the outline gives it. If the outline does not hold that, cut this sentence and the messenger clause.

Not counted: "later bishops quoted them for centuries". The quoting is partly shown later, through Irenaeus. "For centuries" is not shown, and that is left to the trust read.

## 5. Citations read aloud

- "...in the reign of the emperor Trajan, Eusebius says." This is the line the craft check flags. By Part 1 the credit itself is called for, because the source wrote long after. But the listener is never told who Eusebius is or when he wrote until the last sentence, so here it sounds like a bare footnote. MINOR. Exact change: "...in the reign of the emperor Trajan, as the church historian Eusebius wrote two centuries later." If this is applied, the last sentence in 3.3 can open "Eusebius also wrote that, by report, ...".
- "The bread was an offering, as the letter says next: he would be found a sacrifice to God." The phrase "as the letter says next" is a report about the record, and Part 1 does not call for it. MINOR. In the narrator's voice: "The bread was an offering: he would be found a sacrifice to God."

## 6. Tells and machine-made lines

Tells Part 1 names: none. There is no moral close, no "not X but Y", no dash for drama, no stock word, and no narrator question. The rhetorical questions are Ignatius's own.

Lines that sound machine-made (advisory, not BLOCKING):
1. **The numbered scaffold.** "Three messages run through them" is followed by "His first message ... His second message ... His third message". It is an outline read aloud, the habit of a model filling slots, and it sounds like a script rather than one person telling it. Suggested change: "To the churches of Asia he wrote about unity: ...", "He wrote against teachers who said ...", "To Rome he wrote in fear of that church's love: ...".
2. "and the beasts on his road were his guards". This is a tidy reveal tag after a quote that has already explained itself (fixed in 2).
3. "it came from fear of that church's love". This is a crafted paradox, held for one sentence before it is explained. It is understood, so it is not counted in 3, but it is the most "written" line in the piece.
4. "as the letter says next". This is narration of the document rather than of the man (fixed in 5).

## 7. Unexplained words, runs, talking down

Unexplained words:
1. **Eusebius.** He is named twice and never placed until the end (fixed in 5).
2. **Irenaeus of Lyon.** He gets no role, so the listener cannot tell whether he is a bishop or a writer.
3. **Neapolis.** A place the listener cannot locate.
4. **Philippi.** It gets no gloss as a city.
5. **"for the name of Christ".** For a listener in a second language this idiom needs its plain sense: for being a Christian.

The narration has no runs of short one-act sentences. The short sentences belong to Ignatius's quoted words. Nothing talks down.

## 8. Pull and drift

The opening pulls hard: the leopards are the best image in the piece. Attention first drifts at "The churches of Ephesus, Magnesia and Tralles sent their bishops to him there, and he wrote to those three churches and to Rome." That sentence has four place names and no act, and it follows a sentence of two more places. Paragraph 4 then turns to abstract church order with a chain of "one bread and one cup, at one altar, as there is one bishop". The quarrel with the teachers in paragraph 5 brings the listener back, and I would listen to the end. The close sags: Troas logistics, then Polycarp's letter, then a reported death told through a later writer.

## Length (642 against 500: what the heart does not need)

- **Polycarp appears three times** (paras 2, 3, 8). The slate gives him "a sentence here" as `polycarp-of-smyrna`'s sibling. Paragraph 8's two sentences on Polycarp's letter to Philippi tell the sibling's story.
- **Retold detail:** "sent from Syria to Rome" in the last sentence, and "the beasts on his road were his guards".

The heart is told by about word 135, inside the first minute.
