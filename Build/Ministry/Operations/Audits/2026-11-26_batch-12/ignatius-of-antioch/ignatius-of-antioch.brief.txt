"From Syria all the way to Rome I fight with beasts, by land and by sea, by night and by day. I am bound to ten leopards, I mean a band of soldiers. Even when they are treated well, they grow all the worse." Ignatius, bishop of the church at Antioch in Syria, wrote that as a prisoner bound for death in the arena at Rome. The beasts on his road were his guards.

On that road he wrote seven letters to the churches that came out to meet him, and they tell nearly all we know of him. Three messages run through them: keep together around your bishop, hold that Christ truly suffered, and, to Rome, do not save me. Polycarp of Smyrna gathered the letters and sent them on, and later bishops quoted them for centuries.

He was taken in chains for the name of Christ, in the reign of the emperor Trajan, Eusebius says. Across Asia, the Roman province that is now western Turkey, churches came out city by city to greet him, even those off his route. At Smyrna he met its bishop, Polycarp, and the churches of Ephesus, Magnesia and Tralles sent their bishops to him. He wrote to those three churches, and to Rome.

His first message was unity. Each church should act as one with its bishop, its council of elders who led it with him, and its deacons, the elders fitted to the bishop like strings to a harp so the whole church sings with one voice. Nothing should be done without the bishop: one thanksgiving meal of one bread and one cup, at one altar, as there is one bishop. Where there is division, he warned, God does not live.

His second message was aimed at teachers who said Christ only seemed to have a body and to suffer: "Some who are without God, that is, unbelievers, say that he only seemed to suffer. They themselves only seem to exist. If that is so, why am I in chains? Why do I long to fight the wild beasts? Do I die for nothing, then?" They shunned the thanksgiving meal, he wrote, because they would not confess it as Christ's flesh, and they cared nothing for the widow, the orphan or the hungry.

His third message, to Rome, came from fear of that church's love. If they spared him, it would be hard for him to reach God; if they kept silent, he would become God's. "I am God's wheat. I am ground by the teeth of wild beasts, so that I may be found pure bread." The bread was an offering, as the letter says next: he would be found a sacrifice to God.

At Troas, a port on the coast, he wrote to Philadelphia, to Smyrna and to Polycarp. News had come that the church at Antioch was at peace, and he asked them to send a messenger to rejoice with it. He could not write to every church, he told Polycarp, because he had to sail suddenly for Neapolis.

Later Polycarp wrote to the church at Philippi, which had seen Ignatius pass, and counted him among those whose endurance they had witnessed. At their request he sent on the letters of Ignatius he held, because they spoke of faith and patience, and asked for surer news of him. About seventy years later, Irenaeus of Lyon quoted the wheat line, unnamed, as the words of one of their own condemned to the beasts.

Two centuries on, Eusebius wrote that report said Ignatius was sent from Syria to Rome and became food for wild beasts, for his testimony to Christ.
