Ignatius, bishop of the church at Antioch in Syria, was a prisoner bound for death in the arena at Rome when he wrote: "From Syria all the way to Rome I fight with beasts, by land and by sea, by night and by day. I am bound to ten leopards, I mean a band of soldiers. Even when they are treated well, they grow all the worse."

On that road he wrote seven letters, six to churches and one to a fellow bishop, and they tell nearly all we know of him. Three messages run through them: keep together around your bishop, hold that Christ truly suffered, and, to Rome, do not save me. Polycarp of Smyrna gathered the letters he held and sent them on, and later bishops quoted them for centuries.

He was taken in chains as a Christian, for the name of Christ. It was the reign of the emperor Trajan, as the church historian Eusebius wrote two centuries later. Across Asia, the Roman province that is now western Turkey, churches came out city by city to greet him, even those off his route. At Smyrna he met its bishop, Polycarp. The churches of Ephesus, Magnesia and Tralles sent their bishops to him there, and he wrote to those three churches and to Rome.

His first message was unity: each church should act as one with its bishop, its elders and its deacons. At Ephesus, he wrote, the elders, the council who led a church with its bishop, were fitted to him like strings to a harp, and he urged the whole church to sing with one voice. Nothing should be done without the bishop: one thanksgiving meal of one bread and one cup, at one altar, as there is one bishop. He warned that God does not live where there is division and anger.

His second message was aimed at teachers who said Christ only seemed to have a body and to suffer: "Some who are without God, that is, unbelievers, say that he only seemed to suffer. They themselves only seem to exist. If that is so, why am I in chains? Why do I long to fight the wild beasts? Do I die for nothing, then?" They shunned the thanksgiving meal, he wrote, because they would not say it was Christ's flesh. They thought his own sufferings only seemed real too, he wrote, and he charged that they cared nothing for the widow, the orphan or the hungry.

His third message went to Rome. He was afraid, he wrote, that the love of the church there would try to spare him. If they spared him, it would be hard for him to reach God; if they kept silent about him, he would become God's. "I am God's wheat. I am ground by the teeth of wild beasts, so that I may be found pure bread." The bread was an offering: by the teeth of the beasts, he wrote, he would be found a sacrifice to God.

At Troas, a port on the coast, he wrote to the churches of Philadelphia and Smyrna, and to Polycarp. News had come that the church at Antioch, which he had left in chains, was at peace and whole again, and he asked them to send a messenger to Syria to rejoice with it.

Later Polycarp wrote to the church in the city of Philippi, which had seen Ignatius pass, and counted him among those whose endurance they had witnessed. At their request he sent on the letters of Ignatius he held, because they spoke of faith and patience, and asked for surer news of him and those with him. Later still, Irenaeus, writing at Lyon in Gaul, quoted the wheat line, without naming him, as the words of a fellow believer condemned to the beasts.

Eusebius also wrote that, by report, Ignatius was sent from Syria to Rome and became food for wild beasts, for his testimony to Christ.
