# Outline: Lactantius

Kind: person (`kinds/person.md`). Items S, E, I, D and Q-src are in `Source_Sheet.md`, which gives each passage verbatim with file and line. Short names as there: AN (ANF 7 English), DI-L, DI-L2, DI-L3 (Brandt's Latin of the Institutes), TE (Brandt, CSEL 27.1, ancient witnesses), JE, JPL (Jerome's Lives, English and Latin), JC (Jerome's Chronicle, Latin), JL, JLL (Jerome's letters, English and Latin), EU (Eusebius, English), AU (Augustine, English), LU (Lucifer, Latin), MO, SH (modern), CEN (the census).

## The heart

- **The essence:** Lactantius, a teacher of Latin public speaking from Africa, was working at Nicomedia, the city where the emperor Diocletian kept his palace, when the Great Persecution began there in 303 (S2, S3, S5, S6, E1; AN 4487; JE 41035–41041; EU 43460–43461; Documented, the year Widely Accepted). He answered it with the *Divine Institutes*, seven books arguing the faith to educated pagans from their own philosophers and historians (E6, E7; AN 556, 4581; Documented). They hold that religion cannot be forced, since it lives only in the will (E8; AN 5140, 5158; DI-L 26133–26135, 26247–26248; Documented). Quote 3 gives the rest in his words. Late in life, Jerome says, he taught Constantine's son Crispus in Gaul (E12; JE 41051–41054; Documented as Jerome's).
- **Also told:** what the book says, with its weight. Why he wrote: two men published books against the Christians while the Christians were under attack, in his presence and to his grief, and he set out to answer them and all like them (E4, E6). How: not from Scripture, which such readers thought false, but from their own authorities, as milk before solid food (E6; AN 4573–4581). What: the gods were once men; the philosophers refute one another; wisdom and religion are one; the world runs six thousand years (E7). The center: religion is a thing of the will, so force destroys it (E8; Quote 3).
- **What came of him:** Jerome read and judged him; Lucifer of Cagliari took his words on dying, not killing, and turned them on a Christian emperor (DEFENDED); Augustine gathered his Sibyl testimonies (I1–I3).

## Setting

- **Time:** the reign of the emperor Diocletian; the Great Persecution began in 303 (S6; EU 43460–43461 "the nineteenth year of the reign of Diocletian"; AN 10161 the consulship; Widely Accepted). Spoken: "in 303". No day; no year for his birth, arrival, leaving, teaching of Crispus or death.
- **Place:** Nicomedia in Bithynia, north-west Asia Minor, a Greek-speaking city where the emperor had his palace (S3, S5; JE 41037 "since it was a Greek city"; EU 43982–43983, 70323–70325). Later Gaul (E12; JE 41053). Spoken: "Nicomedia, the emperor's city in Bithynia, in what is now north-west Turkey" (the modern country is a gloss for the listener).
- **Tradition:** The Latin Apologists, "The Case Made in Latin" (S7; CEN). Spoken: the Christians who made the case for the faith in Latin, to the educated who scorned it. He is the last of them on the tree.

## Words to explain

- **rhetoric / oratory:** "the art of public speaking", taught to young men for the law courts and public life (S4; AN 556, 2487, 4468). Say "a teacher of public speaking", "in Latin", in a Greek city.
- **Bithynia:** "a Roman province on the Black Sea, in north-west Asia Minor" (S5). Say it once with Nicomedia.
- **the Great Persecution / the edict:** an emperor's order. Eusebius: churches to be leveled, Scriptures burned, Christians of rank degraded, Christian household servants stripped of freedom (E1; EU 43487–43491). Say "an imperial order" with its content, once.
- **governor:** for *praeses* (Q-src 1); Fletcher's "præfect" would mislead.
- ***Divine Institutes*:** his own gloss: lawyers had written "Institutions" of the civil law to settle quarrels; he writes "divine Institutions" about hope, life, salvation, immortality and God (E7; AN 569; DI-L 262–265). Say: a title taken from the law handbooks, a full course in the things of God.
- **Sibyls:** prophetesses whose verses the pagans kept (AN 700, as he reports the Roman scholar Varro). Only if the Sibyls stay in (Cut first 4).
- **a thousand-year reign:** after six thousand years, "righteousness reign for a thousand years" on earth (AN 6650). Say it in those plain terms.
- Not glossed: bishop, Scripture, church, emperor, apostles.

## Shape

**Teacher or thinker.** The question he was answering: how to answer, in their own terms, educated men who were cheering the torture of Christians (E4, E5, E6). What he taught: the *Institutes*, and above all that religion must be willing (E7, E8). Who learned from it: Jerome, who read and judged him; Lucifer, who took over his words; Augustine; and, by Jerome's report, a prince he taught (I1–I3, E12). The trap, a list of books, is met by showing one idea through his own words (Quote 3) set against the scene he saw (Quote 1). The record decides the order: life in time order, the book's case at its writing, What came after.

## Openings

- **(a), recommended:** his own words as an eyewitness (E5; Q-src 1). "I myself saw in Bithynia a governor astonishingly elated with joy, as if he had conquered some nation of barbarians. It was because one man, who had held out for two years with great courage, seemed at last to give way." Lactantius wrote that during the persecution, among people boasting that they had killed no one. The judges, he said, knew it was a contest. The name comes in the sentence after the quote; then the essence.
- **(b):** the two books (E4). While Christians were being tortured, a philosopher who praised poverty, and dined better at home than at the palace, published three books urging them back to the gods, and a judge who had pressed for the persecution wrote two more. Lactantius, a teacher in the same city, says he read them with grief. Risk: slower to reach the persecution itself.

## Story beats

(The opening and the essence fill the first minute, about 140 words.)

1. **Called to Nicomedia.** He came from Africa, Jerome says, and wrote a verse account of his journey from Africa to Nicomedia (S2; JE 41038–41041; TE 11187; Documented as Jerome's). He was "called there" to teach Latin speaking, he says, and Jerome puts it in Diocletian's reign (S3; AN 4487; DI-L 22774 "accitus"; JE 41035–41036; Documented; who called him is not said). Pupils were few, since it was a Greek city, and he turned to writing (JE 41036–41038; TE 11183–11186; Documented as Jerome's). He taught the art, but says he never pleaded a case himself (S4; AN 2487; DI-L 12295–12296; Documented).
2. **303: the persecution, and two men who mocked.** He was teaching there, he writes, when "the temple of God was overthrown" (E1; AN 4487; DI-L 22774–22775; Documented). *Explain it:* the imperial order, in Eusebius's words, leveled the churches, burned the Scriptures, stripped Christians who held rank of their honors, and took freedom from Christian servants who would not give up the faith (EU 43487–43491; Documented). *Why:* to make Christians worship the gods "by force and tortures", as Lactantius puts it (AN 5136; Documented as his). Then two men published books against the Christians "in my presence, and to my grief" (E4; AN 4487, 4567; Documented). One was a philosopher who praised poverty and dined better at home than at the palace; his three books said a philosopher's duty was to call people back to the gods (AN 4489, 4497). The other was a judge who had pressed for the persecution; he wrote two books "to" the Christians, to prove their Scripture contradicted itself, and called Peter and Paul deceivers (AN 4505, 4513). Both portraits are his, told as his ("he says"); both by role, no names (Do not say).
3. **Why and how he wrote.** He took up the task, he says, stirred by their arrogance, by the truth, and, as he believed, by God, to answer not just these two but all like them (Q-src 5; AN 4567; DI-L 23206–23209; narrated, not quoted; Documented). The learned scorned Scripture for its plain language, so truth needed skilled defenders (AN 4460, 4468). Of those before him, he found Tertullian learned but rough and very obscure, and Cyprian the most renowned, yet Cyprian had answered a pagan with Scripture the man thought false (AN 4472–4474, 4573; one clause, Sibling). A beginner, he says, needs milk before solid food: he must be refuted by his own authorities, the philosophers and historians (AN 4581). His aim: to lead the learned to true wisdom and the unlearned to true religion (AN 556; DI-L 191, 204–205). Then Quote 2: that calling, against his old one.
4. **What the *Divine Institutes* say.** The title, his own: lawyers wrote Institutes of the civil law; his are divine, of hope, life, salvation, immortality and God (E7; AN 569; DI-L 262–265; Documented). The pagan gods were once men, named gods after death (AN 1007–1009), and the Sibyls, prophetesses the pagans honored, bear witness against them (AN 692, 700). The philosophers disagree and refute one another, so philosophy destroys itself (AN 575, 2300). No religion without wisdom, no wisdom without religion (AN 584). The last book: the world lasts six thousand years, then righteousness reigns on earth for a thousand (AN 6648–6650). All Documented, his words.
5. **Religion cannot be forced.** In the book on justice he turns to the persecution itself. There is no need for force, he says, since religion cannot be compelled; it must be argued with words rather than blows, so that it is willing (E8; AN 5140; DI-L 26133–26135; Documented). The pagans say their public rites must be defended (AN 5156–5158; their side). He agrees religion must be defended with all one's strength, and then Quote 3. For nothing is so much a matter of the will as religion: if the worshipper's mind turns away, it is gone (AN 5158; DI-L 26247–26248; Documented).
6. **Need, a disputed book, and a prince's teacher.** He writes of himself in "the greatest necessities" (E9; AN 9310; TE 3608; Documented, his words). Jerome lists among his books one on persecution (D1; JE 41047; TE 11193; Documented as Jerome's). A book on how the persecutors died, which survives in one copy under the name of a Lucius Caecilius, tells the fall of the church at Nicomedia, the deaths of the emperors who persecuted, and Constantine's dream; whether it is his is disputed (E11, D1; AN 9970–10736, 10620; MO 28682; Contested). In extreme old age, Jerome says, he taught Constantine's son Crispus in Gaul, the son his father afterwards put to death (E12; JE 41051–41054; TE 11223–11226; Documented as Jerome's; no reason given for the killing, and none added). Jerome's *Chronicle* sets it at the year Crispus was made Caesar, and says he taught him Latin letters (JC 44979–44984; no year spoken).

## What came of him (heaviest first)

1. **Jerome read and judged him** (I1). He gave Lactantius a river of eloquence like Cicero's, and wished he had been as ready to build up the faith as to tear down others' (JL 14112–14114; JLL 27050–27052; narrated, the census `legacy` quotes it). He charged that Lactantius denied the Holy Spirit was a person in his own right, yet asked who could forbid him to read the Institutes, written against the pagans with much ability (JL 19203–19212). Documented as Jerome's; the charge is Jerome's, told as his. *Explain the charge:* Jerome says Lactantius took the passages on the Holy Spirit to mean the Father or the Son, and the words "holy spirit" only to show their holiness (JL 19204–19208).
2. **DEFENDED: Lucifer of Cagliari carried his words against a Christian emperor** (I2). Exiled for refusing to condemn Athanasius (JE 41266–41271; if kept, gloss the quarrel as "the faith of the council of Nicaea, which Athanasius stood for"), Lucifer wrote to the emperor Constantius, Constantine's son, a pamphlet titled *We must die for the Son of God* (LU 15818–15820). You say the Arian teaching must be defended, he wrote; you defend it with the sword, but we know religion is defended not by killing but by dying for God (LU 16934–16942). These are Lactantius's own sentences, taken over without his name (DI-L3 35546–35550). Documented. *Why it matters to the people in it:* the words written against pagan persecutors were turned on Constantine's own son, an emperor of the church's own (the emperor's side, as Lucifer gives it: the Arian teaching "must be defended", LU 16934–16936). Arian: "the teaching of Arius, that there was a time when the Son did not exist" (Socrates, *Church History*, `npnf202_socrates-sozomen-ecclesiastical-histories.xml` 2096: "there was a time when the Son was not"); the live `lucifer-of-cagliari` gives the quarrel, so a short gloss only.
3. **Augustine gathered his Sibyl testimonies** into one series in the *City of God*, where Lactantius had brought them in bit by bit (I3; AU 35552–35555; Documented). Card if cut.
- To the card: the edicts kept in the disputed book (I4); the list later credited to Gelasius (I5); Calvin and the Reformers (I6); Augustine's gold of Egypt (I3).
- Who keeps him today: no held source says. Left out.

## Quotes (3)

Each rendered by me from Brandt's Latin (CSEL 19, 1890), in the machine-corrected TEI, with a second scan of the same print confirming every word. None is used in another story on the tree (CEN, every `longDescription` searched for each), none is barred by the slate, and none appears in the Tertullian or Cyprian outlines.

Only these three are spoken as quotes. Five short phrases of his own stand inside the narrator's sentences, each rendered from the Latin: "the temple of God was overthrown" (Beat 2; "dei templum euerteretur", DI-L 22775); "in my presence, and to my grief" (Beat 2; "praesente me ac dolente", DI-L 23206); "by force and tortures" (Beat 2; "ui atque tormentis", DI-L 26072); "to" the Christians, not "against" them (Beat 2; "non contra Christianos … sed ad Christianos", DI-L 22917–22918); "the greatest necessities" (Beat 6; "in summis necessitatibus", TE 3608). Each is in a clean or readable held print and agrees with Fletcher's English (AN 4487, 4567, 5136, 4505, 9310). None is used in another story on the tree.

1. ***Divine Institutes* V.11.15.** Latin (DI-L 24576–24579; DI-L3 33611–33614): "uidi ego in Bithynia praesidem gaudio mirabiliter elatum tamquam barbarorum gentem aliquam subegisset, quod unus qui per biennium magna uirtute restiterat, postremo cedere uisus esset."
   - Rendering: **"I myself saw in Bithynia a governor astonishingly elated with joy, as if he had conquered some nation of barbarians. It was because one man, who had held out for two years with great courage, seemed at last to give way."**
   - Stands behind it: an original in a clean held print (Brandt 1890), a second scan agreeing; Fletcher's English printed 1886 (AN 4899) confirms. *Uidi ego* is "I myself saw". *Praeses* is a provincial governor: Fletcher's "præfect" would mislead. *Mirabiliter* is "astonishingly": "wonderfully" now sounds like praise. "It was because" renders *quod*; nothing added.
   - Job: the opening. What the persecution looked like to him, in his own eyes: torture as a contest the judges meant to win. It sets up the claim of Quote 3.
2. ***Divine Institutes* I.1.8.** Latin (DI-L 207–209; DI-L3 5903–5905): "quae professio multo melior utilior gloriosior putanda est quam illa oratoria, in qua diu uersati non ad uirtutem, sed plane ad argutam malitiam iuuenes erudiebamus."
   - Rendering: **"This calling must be thought far better, more useful and more glorious than that of public speaking. We were long engaged in that, and we trained young men not in virtue, but plainly in clever wickedness."**
   - Stands behind it: an original in a clean held print (Brandt 1890), a second scan agreeing; Fletcher's English printed 1886 (AN 556) confirms. *Oratoria* is the calling of the orator: "public speaking" keeps it plain. *Argutam malitiam* is "clever wickedness". "This calling" is the one named just before, to lead the learned to true wisdom and the unlearned to true religion; the narrator says so just before the quote (Beat 3).
   - Job: the teacher judging his own trade, in his own voice, at the turn from teaching speech to teaching the faith.
3. ***Divine Institutes* V.19.22** (chapter 20 in the English). Latin (DI-L 26240–26242; DI-L2 19163–19165): "defendenda enim religio est non occidendo sed moriendo, non saeuitia sed patientia, non scelere sed fide."
   - Rendering: **"For religion must be defended not by killing but by dying, not by cruelty but by endurance, not by crime but by faithfulness."**
   - Stands behind it: an original in a clean held print (Brandt 1890), with a second scan agreeing (DI-L2; DI-L3 35549–35551 reads the same apart from a scan fault); Fletcher's English printed 1886 (AN 5158) confirms. *Patientia* is endurance under suffering; *fides* here is keeping faith, as a soldier with his king in the next lines, so "faithfulness", not "belief".
   - Job: the book's center, in his own words, the idea What came 2 shows carried on.

## Connection to carry into the story

What passed from him: a full Latin case for the faith, made to the educated from their own books, and the claim that religion must be freely chosen and defended by dying, not by killing. The listener must hear it pass in two acts. Jerome read the Institutes, praised the style and faulted the teaching, and would not stop reading them (JL 14112–14114, 19203–19212). Lucifer, writing to a Christian emperor who backed one side by force, took over Lactantius's own sentences without naming him, recast to speak to the emperor: 'you feel … you defend it with the sword, but we know religion is defended not by killing but by dying for God' (LU 16934–16942; DI-L3 35546–35550). The writer must make plain, through the two texts side by side, that words written against pagan persecutors were turned on a Christian ruler: say whom Lucifer wrote to, and what that emperor was defending with the sword. No sentence saying why it matters.
