"I myself saw in Bithynia a governor astonishingly elated with joy, as if he had conquered some nation of barbarians. It was because one man, who had held out for two years with great courage, seemed at last to give way." Lactantius wrote this during the persecution, among governors who boasted that they had shed no blood. The judges, he said, knew it was a contest.

Lactantius was teaching Latin public speaking at Nicomedia, in what is now north-west Turkey, the city where the emperor Diocletian kept his palace, when the Great Persecution began in 303. He answered with the Divine Institutes, seven books arguing the faith to educated pagans from their own philosophers and historians. Religion, they hold, cannot be forced, since it lives only in the will. He is the last on the tree of the Latin Apologists, who made the case for the faith in Latin.

He came from Africa, says Jerome, writing later that century, on whom most of his life outside his books rests. In his own words, he was called to Nicomedia to teach, and was teaching when "the temple of God was overthrown." The imperial order, says a bishop who lived through it, leveled churches, burned Scriptures, stripped Christians of rank of their honors, and took freedom from Christian servants who would not give up the faith. Its aim, Lactantius writes, was to make Christians worship the gods "by force and tortures."

Then two men published books against the Christians "in my presence, and to my grief," he says. One, as he tells it, a philosopher who praised poverty, wrote three books saying a philosopher's duty was to call people back to the gods. The other, a judge who had pressed for the persecution, wrote two books "to" the Christians, to prove their Scripture contradicted itself.

He set out to answer them and all like them. Of the Latin writers before him, he judged Tertullian learned in every kind of letters, but rough and very obscure. Cyprian, the most renowned, had answered a pagan with Scripture the man thought false. A beginner needs milk before solid food, Lactantius says, and must be refuted by his own authorities.

He took his title from the lawyers' Institutes of the civil law, written to settle quarrels, and his were divine, a full course on hope, life, salvation, immortality and God. The pagan gods, he argued, had been men named gods after death. The philosophers refuted one another, and there could be no religion without wisdom, nor wisdom without religion.

Religion cannot be compelled, he wrote, so it must be argued with words rather than blows. The pagans said their rites must be defended. He agreed religion must be defended with all one's strength, and he said how. "For religion must be defended not by killing but by dying, not by cruelty but by endurance, not by crime but by faithfulness."

Jerome lists among his books one on persecution. A book on how the persecutors died, surviving in one copy under the name of a Lucius Caecilius, tells of Constantine's dream, but whether it is his is disputed. In extreme old age, Jerome says, Lactantius taught Constantine's son Crispus in Gaul.

Jerome wished Lactantius had been as ready to build up the faith as to tear down other people's. Yet he asked who could forbid him to read the Institutes, written against the pagans with much ability.

Lucifer of Cagliari, a bishop, wrote to Constantine's son, the emperor Constantius, who backed the Arian teaching that there was a time when the Son of God did not exist. You say this teaching must be defended, Lucifer told him, and you defend it with the sword, but we know religion is defended not by killing but by dying for God. Lucifer had taken over Lactantius's sentences, recast to address the emperor, without naming him.

In his history of the world, Jerome called Lactantius the most eloquent man of his time, yet so poor in this life that he often lacked even what he needed.
