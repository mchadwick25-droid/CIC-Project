"I myself saw in Bithynia a governor astonishingly elated with joy, as if he had conquered some nation of barbarians. It was because one man, who had held out for two years with great courage, seemed at last to give way." Lactantius wrote those lines during the persecution, among governors who boasted that they had shed no blood. The judges, he said, knew it was a contest.

Lactantius taught public speaking in Latin, the art young men learned for the law courts and public life. He was working at Nicomedia in Bithynia, in what is now north-west Turkey, the city where the emperor Diocletian kept his palace, when the Great Persecution began there in 303. He answered it with the Divine Institutes, seven books that argued the faith to educated pagans from their own philosophers and historians. At their center is the claim that religion cannot be forced, since it lives only in the will. Late in life he taught Constantine's son Crispus, says Jerome, a scholar writing later in the same century, on whose word most of his life outside his own books rests. Lactantius is the last on the tree of the Latin Apologists, the Christians who made the case for the faith in Latin to the educated people who scorned it.

He came from Africa, Jerome says, and he writes that he was called to Nicomedia to teach Latin speaking in a Greek-speaking city. He was teaching there, he writes, when "the temple of God was overthrown." The imperial order behind it, as a bishop who lived through it describes, leveled the churches and burned the Scriptures. It stripped Christians who held rank of their honors, and took freedom from Christian servants who would not give up the faith. Its aim, as Lactantius puts it, was to make Christians worship the gods "by force and tortures."

Then two men published books against the Christians, he says, "in my presence, and to my grief." One, in his account, was a philosopher who praised poverty, and whose three books said a philosopher's duty was to call people back to the gods. The other was a judge who had pressed for the persecution. He addressed his two books "to" the Christians, rather than against them, and set out to prove that their Scripture contradicted itself.

Lactantius took up the task of answering these two men and all who were like them. He weighed the Christians who had written in Latin before him. Tertullian, he judged, was learned in every kind of letters, but rough and very obscure. Cyprian was the most renowned, yet he had answered a pagan with Scripture, which the man thought false. A beginner, Lactantius says, needs milk before solid food, so such a reader must be refuted by his own authorities, the philosophers and historians.

He took his title from the law. Lawyers had written handbooks called Institutes of the civil law, to settle quarrels, and his were divine Institutes, a full course on hope, life, salvation, immortality and God. Across seven books he argued that the pagan gods had once been men, named gods after their deaths. The philosophers disagreed and refuted one another, he wrote, and there could be no religion without wisdom, and no wisdom without religion.

There is no need for force, he wrote, since religion cannot be compelled. It must be argued with words rather than blows, so that it is willing. The pagans said that their public rites must be defended. Lactantius agreed that religion must be defended with all one's strength, and then he said how: "For religion must be defended not by killing but by dying, not by cruelty but by endurance, not by crime but by faithfulness."

Jerome lists among his books one on persecution. Another book, on how the persecutors died, survives in one copy under the name of a Lucius Caecilius, and it tells of Constantine's dream, but whether Lactantius wrote it is disputed. In extreme old age, Jerome says, he taught Crispus in Gaul.

Jerome read him closely. He wished Lactantius had been as ready to build up the faith as he was to tear down other people's. Yet who, he asked, could forbid him to read the Institutes, written against the pagans with much ability?

Lucifer of Cagliari, a bishop, wrote to the emperor Constantius, Constantine's son, who backed the Arian teaching, which held that there was a time when the Son of God did not exist. You say the Arian teaching must be defended, Lucifer told him, and you defend it with the sword, but we know that religion is defended not by killing but by dying for God. Those sentences were Lactantius's own, recast to speak to the emperor, and Lucifer took them over without naming him.

In his Chronicle, a history of the world set out year by year, Jerome called Lactantius the most eloquent man of his time. Yet he was so poor in this life, Jerome wrote, that he often lacked even what he needed.
