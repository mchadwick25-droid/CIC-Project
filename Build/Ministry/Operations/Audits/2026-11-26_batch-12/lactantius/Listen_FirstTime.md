## 1. The test (person kit)

- **Who was he:** a teacher of Latin public speaking from Africa, at the emperor's city of Nicomedia, who argued the faith in writing. Filled. ("The faith" is never called the Christian faith, but "Christians" in paragraph 3 makes it clear.)
- **What happened:** the Great Persecution began in 303; two pagan writers attacked the Christians in front of him; he answered with the Divine Institutes; in old age he taught Constantine's son. Filled.
- **What it was about:** religion cannot be forced; it must be argued with words, from the pagans' own authorities, and defended "not by killing but by dying". Filled.
- **Why the connection matters:** a later bishop took his sentences and turned them on an emperor who used the sword for his teaching. Filled, but thin on "what it changed": the Lucifer paragraph carries it alone, and the Constantine link cannot land because the listener is never told Constantine was the emperor.

## 2. The opening

First three sentences: the governor quote; "Lactantius wrote this during the persecution, among governors who boasted that they had shed no blood."; "The judges, he said, knew it was a contest."

Missing after them:
- **Who:** the name comes, but not what Lactantius was. Who the "one man" is, and what he held out against, is not said. Christians are not named at all.
- **Where:** "Bithynia" is a place I cannot put on any map. It is never tied to Nicomedia or north-west Turkey later either.
- **When:** "the persecution" with no century, no year and no persecuted group. 303 comes only in paragraph 2.
- **Why there:** nothing yet.

The quote itself pulls me in: a governor celebrating like a conqueror over one man giving way is strong. But sentences 2 and 3 lose me. Who is being persecuted? Why does shedding no blood matter? What contest? BLOCKING: the opening leaves me lost about who suffers and where. Exact change for the reviser, from what the outline holds: say in sentence 2 or 3 that the man was a Christian, the persecution was of Christians, and when it was. Place Bithynia in a clause ("Bithynia, the province around Nicomedia" or as the outline glosses it), and say plainly what the judges' "contest" was: to make Christians give way rather than kill them, if the outline holds that.

## 3. Sentences that fail on one hearing

1. "The judges, he said, knew it was a contest." Lost: what contest, between whom, and what the boast of shedding no blood means. (Part of the opening block.)
2. "He came from Africa, says Jerome, writing later that century, on whom most of his life outside his books rests." Lost: who Jerome is, and "on whom ... rests" folds back on itself. I heard "Africa" and nothing else.
3. "The other, a judge who had pressed for the persecution, wrote two books "to" the Christians, to prove their Scripture contradicted itself." The quote marks around "to" cannot be heard. Why the word matters is lost.
4. "He took his title from the lawyers' Institutes of the civil law, written to settle quarrels, and his were divine, a full course on hope, life, salvation, immortality and God." Lost: what an "Institutes" is, and "his were divine" does not attach to anything I can follow. Second drift point.
5. "A book on how the persecutors died, surviving in one copy under the name of a Lucius Caecilius, tells of Constantine's dream, but whether it is his is disputed." Lost: "his" could be Lucius Caecilius, Constantine or Lactantius; who Lucius Caecilius is; what the dream was.
6. "Yet he asked who could forbid him to read the Institutes, written against the pagans with much ability." Mostly understood, but "him" sounds like Lactantius until I work out it is Jerome. MINOR.
7. "...there could be no religion without wisdom, nor wisdom without religion." The words are clear; the meaning (what wisdom he means, why it answers the philosophers) is not. MINOR.

None loses the main thread except no. 1 (opening) and no. 5 (see bare statements).

## 4. Bare statements (Part 1, Explain it)

1. "tells of Constantine's dream" — BLOCKING. The dream is named but I am not told what he dreamed or why it mattered. Exact change: give the dream in one plain clause from the outline, or cut the clause.
2. "Jerome lists among his books one on persecution." — BLOCKING. A book named with nothing on what it said. Exact change: say in a clause what it was, if the outline holds it, or fold it into the next sentence ("Jerome lists a book of his on persecution; a book on how the persecutors died...") so it is plainly the reason the disputed book comes up, or cut it.

(The Divine Institutes title is explained, if unclearly: see failing sentence 4.)

## 5. Citations read aloud not called for

- "In extreme old age, Jerome says, Lactantius taught Constantine's son Crispus in Gaul." Paragraph 3 already says once that his life rests on Jerome; Part 1 asks for that once. MINOR. Narrator's voice: "In extreme old age, Lactantius taught Constantine's son Crispus in Gaul."
- To confirm with the trust read: "The imperial order, says a bishop who lived through it, leveled churches..." If the old sources agree on the order, the credit is not called for. Narrator's voice: "The imperial order leveled churches, burned Scriptures, stripped Christians of rank of their honors, and took freedom from Christian servants who would not give up the faith."

The check tool's other lines: "Its aim, Lactantius writes" and "Lactantius says" (sentences 12 and 19) are the subject speaking, part of the story, not citations. The Jerome credit at sentence 9 is called for (one late witness, said once), but its wording fails (above).

## 6. Tells

None counted. The check tool flags "not X but Y" in the Lucifer sentence; it is Lucifer's own recast of the Lactantius quote, and the repeat is the point of the paragraph, so it is the record's turn, not the narrator's. "argued with words rather than blows" is paraphrase of Lactantius, not a narrator flourish. No dash, no stock word, no moral close, no question asked only to answer it. The ending is Jerome's line on the subject, which is allowed.

## 7. Words, rhythm, tone

Unexplained words (MINOR each):
- **Bithynia** — never placed.
- **Jerome** — the main witness, heard five times, never given a role (who he was, that he was a church writer).
- **Constantine** — never said to be the emperor; "Constantine's son" and "Constantine's dream" mean nothing without it. Constantius is called emperor; his father is not.
- **Institutes** — see failing sentence 4.
- **Gaul** — an old name; a clause ("in Gaul, now France") would help a second-language listener.
- **Lucius Caecilius** — a name that neither acts nor links. MINOR.
- **Lucifer of Cagliari** — "Lucifer" heard cold sounds like the devil to a newcomer, and Cagliari is unplaced. A clause ("Lucifer, bishop of Cagliari in Sardinia") would steady it.

No run of short one-act sentences. Nothing talks down.

## 8. Attention

- **Pull:** yes. The governor quote is a real hook.
- **First drift:** sentence 3, "The judges, he said, knew it was a contest." Paragraph 2 pulls me back at once with place, year and the book.
- **Second drift:** paragraph 6, "He took his title from the lawyers' Institutes...": title history in place of the story's question, with a word I don't know.
- **Third:** paragraph 8 (the persecution books and the dream), where three new names and an unexplained dream arrive in two sentences.
- **To the end:** yes. "Defended not by killing but by dying" is clear and moving, and hearing a later bishop throw those words back at an emperor is the payoff.
