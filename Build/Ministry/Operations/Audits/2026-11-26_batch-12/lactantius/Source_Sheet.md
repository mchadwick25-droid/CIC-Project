## 1. Setting

S1. **His name.** Jerome: "Firmianus, … known also as Lactantius, a disciple of Arnobius" (JE 41033–41034); Latin "Pirmianua [Firmianus] qui et Lactaiitius [Lactantius] Arnobü [Arnobii] discipulus" (TE 11177–11179; JPL 59045–59046 "Firmianus !, qui et Lactantius, Arnobii di-scipulus"). The NPNF "Died 325" printed inside the chapter is the editor's insertion (JE 41033), not Jerome. Census name "Lactantius" (CEN). Later witness, Documented as Jerome's. Monceaux's full name "L. Caecilius Firmianus qui et Lactantius" rests on two African inscriptions (MO 28686–28688): his reconstruction.

S2. **From Africa.** Jerome: "We have a Banquet of his which he wrote as a young man in Africa and an Itinerary of a journey from Africa to Nicomedia written in hexameters" (JE 41038–41041). TE 11186–11187 reads "quod aduleßcentuluB scripait Africae et hodoeporicum Africa uaque [usque] Nicomediam"; JPL 59051–59052 has "quod adolescentulus scripsit; [hodoeporicum] de Africa usque Nicomediam", without "Africae". The two Latin prints differ on "in Africa" for the Banquet; both have the journey "from Africa to Nicomedia". Documented as Jerome's: he came from Africa. Arnobius taught rhetoric at Sicca in Africa (Jerome; CEN `latin-apologists`). Born in Africa: Monceaux, "il y est né presque sûrement" (MO 24812–24813), his reconstruction; Fletcher's older view of Firmum in Italy (AN-ed) is set aside.

S3. **Called to Nicomedia to teach Latin speaking.** Own words: "When I was teaching rhetorical learning in Bithynia, having been called thither" (AN 4487); Latin "ego cum in Bithynia oratorias litteras accitus docerem" (DI-L 22774). "accitus" names no one who called him. Jerome: "during the reign of Diocletian summoned to Nicomedia with Flavius the Grammarian … taught rhetoric there and on account of his lack of pupils (since it was a Greek city) he betook himself to writing" (JE 41035–41038); Latin "sub Diocletiano principe accitus … Nicomediae rhetoricam docuit ac penuria discipulorum ob Graecam uidelicet ciuitatem ad scribendum se contulit" (TE 11179–11186, OCR read; JPL 59046–59050). OWN and LATER agree. Documented. Schanz's "Diocletian called him" (SH 38941–38942) goes past Jerome's "under Diocletian": not carried.

S4. **What rhetoric was, in his own words.** "oratory, in which being long engaged, we trained young men not to virtue, but altogether to cunning wickedness" (AN 556); Latin Q-src 2. "yet I have never been eloquent, inasmuch as I never even engaged in public speaking" (AN 2487); Latin "eloquens numquam fui, quippe qui forum ne adtigerim quidem" (DI-L 12295–12296): "I never so much as set foot in the forum", the public square where cases were pleaded. He taught the art; he never pleaded. Eloquence, he says, "seeks wealth, desires honours" and "desires to display itself to the people" (AN 4468). OWN, Documented. Plain word: rhetoric = the art of public speaking, taught here in Latin in a Greek-speaking city.

S5. **Nicomedia, the emperor's city.** Eusebius places "the palace at Nicomedia" in the first days of the persecution (EU 43982–43983). Constantine: "Nicomedia attests the fact … The palace, and the emperor's private chamber were destroyed" (EU 70323–70325). *Persecutors* 7: Diocletian built endlessly, "endeavouring to equal Nicomedia with the city Rome in magnificence" (AN 10083) (disputed book, D1). "Capital of the East" is McGiffert's (EU-ed 43824–43826) and Monceaux's (MO 28765–28766) phrase: not carried as such. Plain: the city in Bithynia, in north-west Asia Minor, where the emperor Diocletian had his palace. Documented.

S6. **The year 303.** Eusebius: "It was in the nineteenth year of the reign of Diocletian … that royal edicts were published everywhere" (EU 43460–43461, 43487–43488). *Persecutors* 12: "in the eighth consulship of Diocletian and seventh of Maximian" (AN 10161), on the feast of Terminus (AN 10152), which the ANF note converts to "23d of February" (AN-ed 10154). The year 303 is the editors' conversion of two old datings that agree (EU-ed 43461–43464): Widely Accepted. The day rests on the disputed book and an editor's conversion: not carried.

S7. **Tradition.** Told within `latin-apologists`, "The Latin Apologists", informal name "The Case Made in Latin" (CEN). Spoken: the Christians who made the case for the faith in Latin. Era 1.

S8. **When he became a Christian: not known.** Schanz: "Ob noch in Afrika oder schon in Nikomedien, wissen wir nicht" ("whether still in Africa or already at Nicomedia, we do not know", SH 38940–38941). Monceaux: almost surely at Nicomedia, between 290 and 303 (MO 25021–25034), his inference. He was a Christian when the persecution began: the two men's books were set out "in my presence, and to my grief" (AN 4567). Documented that he grieved; the conversion's time and place are not held.

## 2. Events (time order)

E1. **The persecution begins while he teaches there.** Own words: "When I was teaching rhetorical learning in Bithynia, having been called thither, and it had happened that at the same time the temple of God was overthrown" (AN 4487); Latin "contigissetque ut eodem tempore dei templum euerteretur" (DI-L 22774–22775). "The temple of God" may be the church building or the church at large; he does not say he watched it fall. Eusebius gives the first edict: "commanding that the churches be leveled to the ground and the Scriptures be destroyed by fire, and ordering that those who held places of honor be degraded, and that the household servants, if they persisted in the profession of Christianity, be deprived of freedom" (EU 43487–43491). OWN and CONTEMPORARY. Documented. Monceaux's "il y vit détruire l'église" ("he saw the church destroyed there", MO 28768) is his reading: not carried.
- *Explain the edict, from each side.* Eusebius says what it ordered (above). *Persecutors* 11 gives the persecutors' side as its author tells it: some advisers held Christians "enemies of the gods and adversaries of the established religious ceremonies", and Diocletian wanted it done "without bloodshed", while Galerius "would have had all persons burnt alive who refused to sacrifice" (AN 10141). In the *Institutes* the aim is to make Christians worship the gods "by force and tortures" (AN 5136). OWN (Institutes), Documented; the *Persecutors* account, Contested authorship.

E2. **The church at Nicomedia leveled** (*Persecutors* 12, disputed book). "suddenly, while it was yet hardly light, the prefect, together with chief commanders, tribunes, and officers of the treasury, came to the church in Nicomedia, and the gates having been forced open, they searched everywhere for an image of the Divinity. The books of the Holy Scriptures were found, and they were committed to the flames … That church, situated on rising ground, was within view of the palace; and Diocletian and Galerius stood, as if on a watch-tower, disputing long whether it ought to be set on fire. The sentiment of Diocletian prevailed, who dreaded lest, so great a fire being once kindled, some part of the city might he [be] burnt … Then the Pretorian Guards came in battle array, with axes and other iron instruments, and … they in a few hours levelled that very lofty edifice with the ground" (AN 10161). English only. Documented as the book's text; its author Contested (D1). The live `latin-apologists` already tells this scene.

E3. **The edict torn down; the palace fires** (*Persecutors* 13–15, disputed book). Next day's edict: Christians deprived "of all honours and dignities", subject to tortures "without any distinction of rank or degree" (AN 10174). "A certain person tore down this edict … saying in scorn, 'These are the triumphs of Goths and Sarmatians.' … he was not only tortured, but burnt alive" (AN 10174). Eusebius agrees on the man: "a certain man, not obscure but very highly honored … seized the edict … and tore it to pieces" (EU 43835–43838). The palace fire: Galerius "employed private emissaries to set the palace on fire … the blame was laid on the Christians" (AN 10185); "Presbyters and other officers of the Church were seized … and together with their families led to execution … servants, having millstones tied about their necks, were cast into the sea" (AN 10198); RU 4458–4461 gives the Latin of the last ("domestici alligatis ad collum molaribus mergebantur", a modern editor's quotation, damaged OCR). The fire's cause: D2. McGiffert's "That he deserved death is clear enough" (EU-ed 43844–43845): an editor's judgment, never carried.

E4. **The two writers who mocked** (*Institutes* V.2–3, own). "there were living at the same place two men who insulted the truth as it lay prostrate and overthrown" (AN 4487). One "professed himself the high priest of philosophy" (AN 4487); "though in his school he was the maintainer of virtue, the praiser of parsimony and poverty, he dined less sumptuously in a palace than at his own house" (AN 4489); "at the very same time in which a righteous people were impiously assailed, vomited forth three books against the Christian religion and name; professing … that it was the office of a philosopher to remedy the errors of men, and to recall them to the true way, that is, to the worship of the gods" (AN 4497). The other "was then of the number of the judges, and who was especially the adviser of enacting persecution"; he wrote "two books, not against the Christians … but to the Christians", trying to prove Scripture "altogether contradictory to itself" (AN 4505), and "chiefly … assailed Paul and Peter … as disseminators of deceit" (AN 4513). OWN, Documented. Names: Brandt, "ignoto nomine ut uid." for the philosopher, Baronius's Porphyry refuted (DI-L 22781–22783). The judge as Hierocles is the editors' identification (AN-ed 4505; Hierocles named in *Persecutors* 16, AN 10223, as "the author and adviser of the persecution"): Dominant Modern Reconstruction, not carried.
- *Explain the two books, from their side as he gives it:* the philosopher said a philosopher's duty was to call men back to the worship of the gods, by whose power the world is ruled, so that simple people would not be preyed on by crafty ones (AN 4497); the judge wrote "to" the Christians as if out of kindness (AN 4505).

E5. **The governor's joy, and the contest** (*Institutes* V.11, own, eyewitness). Q-src 1. Before it: "I myself have heard some boasting that their administration has been in this respect without bloodshed" (AN 4899); judges who would kill no one devised slow tortures, "For they know that this is a contest and a battle" (AN 4899); Latin "sciunt enim certamen esse illud et pugnam" (DI-L3 33611). Also "an individual in Phrygia, who burnt a whole assembly of people, together with their place of meeting" (AN 4897; not said to be seen). Documented.
- *Explain the act (torture to make them give way), from his side:* the governors wanted to boast they had killed no innocent, and to win (AN 4899). From the persecutors' side, as he reports their argument: "the public rites of religion must be defended" (AN 5156–5158).

E6. **Why he wrote, and how** (*Institutes* I.1, V.1, V.4, own). Q-src 3 for the task (V.4.1, narrated only). "I have believed that these errors should be encountered, that both the learned may be directed to true wisdom, and the unlearned to true religion" (AN 556). Q-src 2 for his old trade. The learned scorn Scripture "because the prophets spoke in common and simple language" (AN 4460); "wisdom and truth need suitable heralds" (AN 4468). On Tertullian: "skilled in literature of every kind; but in eloquence he had little readiness, and was not sufficiently polished, and very obscure" (AN 4472). On Cyprian: "above all others distinguished and renowned" (AN 4472–4474), yet "derided by the learned men of this age", one of whom called him "Coprianus" (AN 4474). "Although Tertullian fully pleaded the same cause in that treatise which is entitled the Apology" (AN 4569), answering accusers differs from teaching (the sentence goes on past this line; the live `latin-apologists` tells it). Cyprian answered Demetrianus "by the testimonies of Scripture, which he plainly considered vain, fictitious, and false" instead of "by arguments and reason" (AN 4573); "as an infant is unable … to receive the nourishment of solid and strong food, but is supported by liquid and soft milk … so also it was befitting that this man … should be presented with human testimonies—that is, of philosophers and historians—in order that he might especially be refuted by his own authorities" (AN 4581). OWN, Documented.

E7. **What the *Divine Institutes* say** (seven books, AN 538–8478, own). The title, his own: "if some skilful men and arbiters of justice composed and published Institutions of civil law … how much better and more rightly shall we follow up in writing the divine Institutions, in which we shall not speak about rain-droppings, or the turning of waters, or the preferring of claims, but we shall speak of hope, of life, of salvation, of immortality, and of God" (AN 569); Latin "institutiones ciuilis iuris … diuinas institutiones" (DI-L 262–265). Book I, the gods: "how they who were men obtained the name of gods" (AN 1007; "it is evident from these things that they were men", AN 1009), with "the sibyls and their predictions" (AN 692). Books II–III, the philosophers: "they disagree among one another with great contention, and are for the most part at variance with themselves" (AN 575); "philosophy consumes and destroys itself" (AN 2300). Wisdom and religion joined: "neither is any religion to be undertaken without wisdom, nor any wisdom to be approved of without religion" (AN 584). Book V, justice and the persecution; Book VI, true worship; Book VII: "the world must continue in its present state through six ages, that is, six thousand years … at the end of the six thousandth year all wickedness must be abolished from the earth, and righteousness reign for a thousand years" (AN 6648–6650). OWN, Documented.

E8. **Religion cannot be forced** (*Institutes* V.19–20; Brandt's chapter 19 is ANF chapter 20). "There is no occasion for violence and injury, for religion cannot be imposed by force; the matter must be carried on by words rather than by blows, that the will may be affected" (AN 5140); Latin "non est opus ui et iniuria, quia religio cogi non potest, uerbis potius quam uerberibus res agenda est, ut sit uoluntas" (DI-L 26133–26135; DI-L2 19033; DI-L3 35409). "And thus no one is detained by us against his will, for he is unserviceable to God who is destitute of faith and devotedness" (AN 5140). Q-src 3. "For nothing is so much a matter of free-will as religion; in which, if the mind of the worshipper is disinclined to it, religion is at once taken away, and ceases to exist" (AN 5158); Latin "nihil est enim tam uoluntarium quam religio" (DI-L 26247–26248). Their religion "has the mind itself for a sacrifice" (AN 5162). OWN, Documented. Brandt sets beside it Tertullian, *To Scapula* 2, "sed nec religionis est cogere religionem" (DI-L 26140–26141): the editor's parallel, not a citation by Lactantius; not carried.
- *Explain it, from the other side as he gives it:* "they say, the public rites of religion must be defended" (AN 5156–5158). He agrees religion must be defended "with the whole of our power", and differs on the manner (AN 5158).

E9. **Need.** *On the Workmanship of God* opens: "How disturbed I am, and in the greatest necessities, you will be able to judge from this little book which I have written to you, Demetrianus" (AN 9310); Latin "Quam minime sim quietus etiam in summis necessi[tatibus]" (TE 3608). OWN. Jerome's *Chronicle*: Q-src 4 (poverty). *Persecutors* 22, under Galerius: "eloquence was extinguished, pleaders cut off, and the learned in the laws either exiled or slain" (AN 10313; disputed book). Monceaux joins the closing of the schools to his poverty and his leaving Bithynia "probablement … à la fin de 305 ou en 306" (MO 25162–25165): his inference, not carried.

E10. **The address to Constantine.** "And we now commence this work under the auspices of your name, O mighty Emperor Constantine, who were the first of the Roman princes to repudiate errors, and to acknowledge and honour the majesty of the one and only true God" (AN 571); Brandt prints it in the apparatus, "in RSg locus hic" (DI-L 286–289): in some manuscripts only. Contested (D3).

E11. ***On the Deaths of the Persecutors*** (AN 9970–10736; disputed book, D1). Title and address: "Addressed to Donatus" (AN 9975); "The Lord has heard those supplications which you, my best beloved Donatus" (AN 9981). Purpose: "God delayed to punish them, that, by great and marvellous examples, He might teach posterity that He alone is God" (AN 9983); "Of the end of those men I have thought good to publish a narrative, that all who are afar off, and all who shall arise hereafter, may learn" (AN 9987). Donatus "nine times exposed to racks and diversified torments" (AN 10223), freed "from a jail, which had been your residence for six years" (AN 10505). Galerius's disease (AN 10462) and his edict letting Christians be "again … Christians" (AN 10486), posted at Nicomedia (AN 10501). Constantine "was directed in a dream to cause the heavenly sign to be delineated on the shields of his soldiers" (AN 10620): the sibling's (Outline, Sibling). The edict of Milan, posted at Nicomedia (AN 10680–10684): "that it was proper that the Christians and all others should have liberty to follow that mode of religion which to each of them appeared best" (AN 10684); Latin "ut daremus et Cliristianis [Christianis] et omnibus liberam potestatem sequendi religio-nem, quam quisque voluisset" (MI 4795–4796). "from the overthrow of the Church until its restoration, there was a space of ten years and about four months" (AN 10690). The end of the families, among them "Maximus, the son of Daia, a boy eight years old, and a daughter of Daia, who was seven years old … So, by the unerring and just judgment of God, all the impious received according to th[e deeds]" (AN 10712). Close: "I relate all those things on the authority of well-informed persons" (AN 10736). Documented as the book's text.

E12. **Teacher of Crispus, late in life.** Jerome: "In his extreme old age he was tutor to Crispus Cæsar a son of Constantine in Gaul, the same one who was afterwards put to death by his father" (JE 41051–41054); Latin "hic extrema senectute magister Caesaris Crispi filii Constantini in Gallia fuit, qui postea a patre interfectus est" (TE 11223–11226; JPL 59062 ff.). *Chronicle*, at the year Crispus and others "Caesares appellantur" ("are named Caesars"): "quorum Crispum Lactantius Latinis litteris erudiuit" ("of whom Lactantius taught Crispus in Latin letters", JC 44979–44984; TE 11238–11241). Crispus "crudelissime interficitur" ("is most cruelly put to death", JC 45056–45057). LATER. Documented as Jerome's. Jerome names Gaul, not Trier. The year of the teaching (316 or 317) is Monceaux's and Schanz's (MO 25185–25192; SH 39040–39041): not carried. His death: not held (MO 25248–25249, "On ne sait absolument rien sur les dernières années de Lactance, ni sur la date de sa mort").

## 3. What came of him (ranked, heaviest first)

I1. **Read as the Latin case for the faith, praised for its style and faulted for its teaching** (Jerome, most often of the old witnesses: MO 29871–29874). Letter 58: "Lactantius has a flow of eloquence worthy of Tully: would that he had been as ready to teach our doctrines as he was to pull down those of others!" (JL 14112–14114; Latin "Lactantius, quasi quidam fluuius eloquentiae Tullianae, utinam tam nostra adfirmare potuisset, quam facile aliena destruxit!", JLL 27050–27052). This line stands in the census `legacy` and `floorNote`: narrate, never quote. Letter 70: "If you read any of these you will find in them an epitome of Cicero's dialogues" (JL 16719–16721). Letter 84: "Lactantius in his books and particularly in his letters to Demetrian altogether denies the subsistence of the Holy Spirit … But who can forbid me to read his Institutes—in which he has written against the Gentiles with much ability—simply because this opinion of his is to be abhorred?" (JL 19203–19212). Jerome counts him among those who held that "after the resurrection the Lord will reign in the flesh with the saints" (JE 39882–39885). LATER. Documented as Jerome's.

I2. **DEFENDED: religion freely chosen, defended by dying and not by killing; carried on against a Christian emperor.** Lucifer of Cagliari, in his pamphlet *Moriendum esse pro Dei Filio* ("We must die for the Son of God", LU 15818), addressed "Constanti imperator" (LU 15820), takes over Lactantius's sentences without naming him: "dicis talia honesta uoluntate errans. sentis enim nihil esse in rebus humanis religione praestantius eamque summa ui oportere defendi; defendis gladio, at nos defendendam nouimus religionem non occidendo, sed moriendo pro deo uniuersaque mala haec quae tu ingeris sustinendo" ("You say such things, erring with an honourable will. For you feel that nothing in human affairs is more excellent than religion and that it must be defended with all force; you defend it with the sword, but we know that religion is to be defended not by killing but by dying for God and by bearing all these evils you heap on us", LU 16936–16942, my translation). What he defends with it, in the lines before: "defendendum est, inquis, … Arrianum dogma" ("it must be defended, you say, … the Arian teaching", LU 16934–16936). Lactantius's own words: DI-L3 35546–35550 ("o quam honesta uoluntate miseri errant! sentiunt enim nihil esse in rebus humanis religione praestantius eamque summa ui oportere defendi … defendenda enim religio est non oecidendo [occidendo] sed moriendo"). Brandt's apparatus finds it: "EXPILATORES 11—15 (o quam — moriendo)] Lucifer pag. 307" (DI-L3 35563–35564); the verbal identity is in both old texts. LATER (Lucifer wrote under Constantius, Constantine's son). Documented.

I3. **Augustine set his Sibyl testimonies in order.** "Lactantius made use of these sibylline testimonies, introducing them bit by bit in the course of his discussion as the things he intended to prove seemed to require, and we have set them down in one connected series" (AU 35552–35555, *City of God* XVIII.23). And: "How much Lactantius brought with him?" among those who came out of Egypt loaded with its gold, that is, pagan learning put to Christian use (AU 51718–51722, *On Christian Teaching* II.40). LATER. Documented.

I4. ***Persecutors* keeps the edicts.** The book prints Galerius's edict (AN 10486) and the edict of Milan (AN 10684), whose Latin survives there (MI 4790 ff.). McGiffert: "The greater part of the edict … is extant in its original Latin form in Lactantius' De mort. pers. chap. 48. The Greek translation is still less accurate" (EU-ed 50239–50242): a modern note that clarifies an old text. Monceaux's "le récit le plus exact et le plus complet que nous possédions" and "très partial" (MO 29274–29279): his judgments, not carried. Card.

I5. **A late list sets his little works aside.** "Opuscula Lactantii apocrypha" ("the little works of Lactantius, not received"), in the list later credited to Pope Gelasius (TE 11465–11472) and repeated under Hormisdas (TE 11481–11486); Brandt's note points to a study against the ascription (TE 11488–11491). LATE. Documented as the list's text; who made the list, Contested. Card.

I6. **Read by the Reformers.** Calvin: "to conclude with Lactantius … 'No religion is genuine that is not in accordance with truth'" (CA 7254–7256); "let Lactantius and Eusebius be read on this subject" (CA 10123). Monceaux: "beaucoup lu au Moyen-Age et à la Renaissance" (MO 29876–29878), and his influence on his own time "presque nulle" (MO 29893–29894): his judgments. Card.

## 5. Where the sources differ, or come late

D1. **Who wrote *On the Deaths of the Persecutors*.** Jerome lists among Lactantius's books "one book On persecution" (JE 41047; TE 11193 "De perseeutione libnirti [librum] unum"; JPL 59057–59058). No old source says this is the book on the persecutors' deaths. Its one manuscript names the author "L(ucius) Cecilius" (MO 28682; Gibbon, as Coxe quotes him, "the Colbert ms.", AN-ed 9924). Gibbon doubted it was Lactantius's (AN-ed 9918–9924); Monceaux: "Tout bien pesé, nous jugeons plus vraisemblable la thèse de l'authenticité" (MO 28624–28625); Schanz: "deren Echtheit allerdings bestritten ist" (SH 39061–39062). Contested. The story says only what the old sources hold: Jerome lists a book of his on persecution; a book on the persecutors' deaths survives in one copy under the name of a Lucius Caecilius; whether it is his is disputed.

D2. **The palace fire.** *Persecutors*: set by Galerius's agents (AN 10185). Eusebius: "a conflagration having broken out in those very days in the palace at Nicomedia, I know not how, which through a false suspicion was laid to our people" (EU 43982–43984). Constantine: "consumed by lightning, devoured by the fire of heaven", with "eyewitnesses, of whom I myself am one" (EU 70323–70325). Three witnesses, three accounts. McGiffert's view that "the case against them is pretty strong" (EU-ed 43999–44000): an editor's. Card.

D3. **The address to Constantine.** In some manuscripts only (DI-L 286). Brandt set it in his apparatus; Schanz reports that Brandt later gave up that view, and holds the addresses Lactantius's own, from a second edition (SH 39679–39691); Monceaux calls them a forger's (MO 25246–25247). Contested. Card.

D4. **"In Africa" for the Banquet.** TE reads "scripsit Africae"; JPL lacks "Africae" (S2). The NPNF English has "in Africa". Card, if at all.

D5. **Editors' claims not carried.** Trier as his last home and death about 325 (Fletcher; the NPNF insertion "Died 325", JE 41033). "The Christian Tully" (AN-ed 375): an editor's name. Monceaux's sketch of a timid man without a call to martyrdom. Born at Firmum.

## 6. Quote sources (renderings in the Outline)

Q-src 1. *Institutes* V.11.15. Latin: "uidi ego in Bithynia praesidem gaudio mirabiliter elatum tamquam barbarorum gentem aliquam subegisset, quod unus qui per biennium magna uirtute restiterat, postremo cedere uisus esset" (DI-L 24576–24579; DI-L3 33611–33614, same reading). Fletcher: "I saw in Bithynia the præfect wonderfully elated with joy, as though he had subdued some nation of barbarians, because one who had resisted for two years with great spirit appeared at length to yield" (AN 4899).

Q-src 2. *Institutes* I.1.8. Latin: "quae professio multo melior utilior gloriosior putanda est quam illa oratoria, in qua diu uersati non ad uirtutem, sed plane ad argutam malitiam iuuenes erudiebamus" (DI-L 207–209; DI-L3 5903–5905, same reading). Fletcher: "And this profession is to be thought much better, more useful and glorious, than that of oratory, in which being long engaged, we trained young men not to virtue, but altogether to cunning wickedness" (AN 556). "quae professio" is the calling just named: to direct the learned to true wisdom and the unlearned to true religion (DI-L 191, 204–205; AN 556).

Q-src 3. *Institutes* V.19.22 (ANF ch. XX). Latin: "defendenda enim religio est non occidendo sed moriendo, non saeuitia sed patientia, non scelere sed fide" (DI-L 26240–26242; DI-L2 19163–19165, same reading; DI-L3 35549–35551, "oecidendo" a scan fault). Fletcher: "For religion is to be defended, not by putting to death, but by dying; not by cruelty, but by patient endurance; not by guilt, but by good faith" (AN 5158).

Q-src 4 (narrated, not quoted). Jerome, *Chronicle*: "uir omnium suo tempore eloquentissimus sed adeo in hac uita pauper ut plerumque etiam necessariis indiguerit" ("the most eloquent man of all in his time, but so poor in this life that he often lacked even necessities", JC 44984–44986; TE 11241–11243, same reading).

Q-src 5 (narrated, not quoted). *Institutes* V.4.1, why he took up the task: "et illorum superba inpietate stimulatus et ueritatis ipsius conscientia et, ut ego arbitror, deo, suscepi hoc munus" (DI-L 23206–23209); Fletcher AN 4567.
