"I myself saw in Bithynia a governor astonishingly elated with joy, as if he had conquered some nation of barbarians. It was because one man, who had held out for two years with great courage, seemed at last to give way." Lactantius wrote that among governors who boasted that they had shed no blood. The judges, he said, knew it was a contest.

Lactantius taught Latin public speaking at Nicomedia in Bithynia, now north-west Turkey, the city where the emperor Diocletian kept his palace, when the Great Persecution began there in 303. He answered with the Divine Institutes, seven books arguing the faith to educated pagans from their own philosophers and historians. Religion, they hold, cannot be forced, since it lives only in the will. Late in life, Jerome says, he taught Constantine's son.

Most of his life outside his own books rests on Jerome, writing later that century. Lactantius came from Africa, Jerome says. He was called to Nicomedia, he says himself, and Jerome puts it in Diocletian's reign. In a Greek city his pupils were few, so he turned to writing.

He was teaching there, he writes, when "the temple of God was overthrown." Eusebius, a bishop who lived through it, gives the imperial order: churches leveled, Scriptures burned, Christians of rank stripped of honors, and Christian servants who held to the faith stripped of freedom. The aim, Lactantius says, was to make Christians worship the gods "by force and tortures."

Two men then wrote against the Christians "in my presence, and to my grief," he writes. One was a philosopher who praised poverty, and whose three books said a philosopher's duty was to call people back to the gods. The other, a judge who had pressed for the persecution, addressed two books "to" the Christians, to prove that their Scripture contradicted itself.

Stirred by their arrogance, by the truth and, as he believed, by God, Lactantius set out to answer them and all like them. He found Tertullian learned in every kind of letters, yet rough and very obscure, and Cyprian the most renowned, though Cyprian had answered a pagan with Scripture the man thought false. A beginner needs milk before solid food, he says, so must be refuted by his own authorities. The aim was to lead the learned to true wisdom and the unlearned to true religion, and he set that calling against his old one: "This calling must be thought far better, more useful and more glorious than that of public speaking. We were long engaged in that, and we trained young men not in virtue, but plainly in clever wickedness."

He took his title from the law handbooks. Lawyers had written Institutes of the civil law, and his were divine Institutes, a full course in hope, life, salvation, immortality and God. The pagan gods, he argues, were once men, named gods after death, while the philosophers refute one another. There is no religion without wisdom, he holds, and no wisdom without religion.

In the book on justice he turns to the persecution itself. Religion cannot be compelled, he says, so it must be argued with words rather than blows, and be willing. The pagans said their rites must be defended. He agrees that religion must be defended with all one's strength, and goes on: "For religion must be defended not by killing but by dying, not by cruelty but by endurance, not by crime but by faithfulness." Nothing is so much a matter of the will as religion, and if the worshipper's mind turns away, it is gone.

Jerome lists a book of his on persecution. One on how the persecutors died, kept in one copy under the name of a Lucius Caecilius, tells of the fall of the church at Nicomedia, the deaths of the persecuting emperors, and Constantine's dream, but whether it is his is disputed. In extreme old age, Jerome says, Lactantius taught Constantine's son Crispus in Gaul, the son his father afterwards put to death.

Jerome gave Lactantius a river of eloquence like Cicero's, and wished he had been as ready to build up the faith as to tear down the beliefs of others. He charged that Lactantius denied the Holy Spirit was a person in his own right. Yet who, Jerome asked, could forbid him to read the Institutes, written against the pagans with much ability?

The bishop Lucifer of Cagliari wrote to another of Constantine's sons, the emperor Constantius, in a pamphlet titled We Must Die for the Son of God. You say, he told the emperor, that the Arian teaching must be defended, the teaching that there was a time when the Son did not exist. You defend it with the sword, Lucifer went on, while we know that religion is defended by dying for God, and never by killing. These were Lactantius's own sentences, recast to address the emperor, and Lucifer did not name him.

In his Chronicle, Jerome calls Lactantius the most eloquent man of his time, yet so poor in this life that he often lacked even what he needed.
