## 1. The test (person kit)

- **Who:** filled. A presbyter at Caesarea who built a library and copied Origen's books by hand.
- **What happened:** filled. Tortured, two years in prison, Defense written, put to death about 310 with eleven others.
- **What it was about:** filled. Keeping and sharing books, and defending Origen against named charges.
- **Why the connection matters:** filled. The catalog was used by Eusebius and Jerome, Rufinus translated the Defense, and Jerome complains that a martyr's praise shielded Origen. What he defended (Origen) is plain.

Note for the trust reader: the "what it changed" clause ("the books Pamphilus kept were read for generations after him") ends at about word 181, roughly 70 seconds in. That is past 150 words. Whether it is late is for the trust read to rule.

## 2. The opening

The first three sentences are: "Eusebius of Caesarea wrote a Life..."; "It is lost, but the scholar Jerome kept a passage... 'He handed out the holy Scriptures as well...'"; "He gave them to men, and to women too..."

- **Who:** partial. He is named, and he is a friend of Eusebius who gives out Scriptures. What he was (a priest, a rich layman?) is not told.
- **Where:** missing. "Caesarea" is heard only as part of Eusebius's name, not as a place.
- **When:** missing. No century until "about 310" in paragraph 2.
- **Why there:** implied (he gave books to readers who were poor).

All of it arrives in the next sentence, so the listener is not lost. MINOR. The bigger fault is the frame. The first thing heard is a chain of sources: a lost Life, a later scholar, a kept passage. That is a report about the record (Part 1, Craft) before it is a moment. **Change (MINOR):** open on the act in a single clause of sourcing, e.g. "In a passage from Eusebius's lost Life of his friend, kept by the scholar Jerome, Pamphilus helps poor readers who love books:" followed by the quote.

## 3. Sentences that fail on one hearing

2. "He handed out the holy Scriptures as well" — "as well" as what? Nothing before it is heard. MINOR. The quote stands; a plain lead-in clause ("besides his other gifts," only if the source holds it) or the quote's start trimmed by the trust reader.
3. "It answered charges that Origen taught the soul lived before the body, and made Christ either a mere man or one whose deeds happened only in seeming." — On one hearing the subject of "made" slips: it sounds as if the Defense made Christ a mere man. "Only in seeming" is lost too (see 7). MINOR. Change: "...charges that Origen taught the soul lived before the body, and that he made Christ either a mere man or one who only seemed to do what he did."
4. "Each gave the same answer, and he sentenced them as he had the others." — Which others? No earlier group is told. Sentenced to what? Death arrives only in the next sentence. MINOR. Change: "Each gave the same answer, and he sentenced them to death, as he had other confessors before them" (as the source holds).
5. "Origen's accusers, it says, read pagan books and even those of heretics, whose teaching the church condemns, keeping the good like a money-changer who tests each coin and throws out the false." — 33 words with two nested clauses. The point is a double standard: they let themselves read anything, but forbid Origen's readers. It is not stated, so the next quote lands without its sting. MINOR. Change: "Origen's accusers, it says, read pagan books and even heretics' books themselves. They keep the good, like a money-changer who tests each coin and throws out the false." The Defense's own quote then carries the charge of a double standard.

### 6b. Lines that sound machine-made

1. **The "says" metronome.** Paragraphs 2 to 5 carry about nine attributions: "Jerome says", "Eusebius says", "Jerome says", "Eusebius says", "Photius... says", "it says", "he says". Heard aloud, it sounds stitched from source notes, not like one narrator who knows the record. MINOR. Keep the ones Part 1 requires, and tell the rest plainly in the narrator's voice.
2. "...and the books Pamphilus kept were read for generations after him." A tidy summary clause bolted on as a signpost, in the cadence of a generated abstract. MINOR. Make it concrete: name who read them (the catalog, Jerome), or move it to where it is shown.
3. "He refused, heedless of threats." Translationese: "heedless" is an archaic word carried over from a translation. MINOR. "He refused, whatever they threatened."
4. "It is written to confessors condemned to forced labor in the copper mines of Palestine." The present tense in a past-tense story reads like a pasted catalog note. MINOR. "Pamphilus wrote it to confessors..." or "It was addressed to..."
5. "The Defense set out Origen's own words with replies between." Tacked on after the quote. Form comes after argument, like a stray note. MINOR. Move it to just after "It answered charges...".
6. "...says he shared his possessions with the needy, was devoted above all to the Scriptures, and gathered Origen's works and other church writers'." A list of three virtues, which reads as a catalog. MINOR. Tie the clauses to each other with cause or time.

## 7. Unexplained words, short runs, talking down

- "a Life" (sentence 1): the genre (a biography) is not plain to every ear. MINOR. "a book on the life of his friend".
- "only in seeming": opaque. MINOR. See 3.3.
- "the command to become approved money-changers": whose command, and that it is a saying the readers knew, is not told. Its sense is given by the sentence before it, so MINOR. If the outline holds its origin, add it in a clause.
- "Byzantine" (Photius): a period label. MINOR. "a Greek scholar of Constantinople", if the outline holds it.
- **Short one-act run, paragraph 6 (the climax):** "He knew they had held firm... / Each gave the same answer... / Pamphilus died with eleven others, the only presbyter among them. / Eusebius took his name as a surname." The death lands as a list of facts, and the last line is a non-sequitur. On one hearing, "his name" could also be the governor's. MINOR. Join the death and the surname with a time link: "...the only presbyter among them; afterward Eusebius took Pamphilus's name as his own surname."
