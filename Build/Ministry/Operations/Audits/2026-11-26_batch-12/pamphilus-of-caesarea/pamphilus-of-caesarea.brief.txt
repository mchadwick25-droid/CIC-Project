Eusebius of Caesarea wrote a Life of his friend Pamphilus. It is lost, but the scholar Jerome kept one passage, in which Pamphilus helps any lover of books who lacks the means to live: "He handed out the holy Scriptures as well, and most readily, to keep and not only to read. He gave them to men, and to women too, when he saw they were devoted to reading. So he kept many books prepared, to give freely to anyone who wished whenever need called for it."

Pamphilus of Caesarea was a presbyter, an elder and priest of the church, at Caesarea, a port on the coast of Roman Palestine. He built a library for his church and, Jerome says, copied most of the works of Origen with his own hand. He never met Origen, the Bible scholar who had taught at Alexandria and then at Caesarea in the century before. In the persecution Pamphilus was tortured, held two years in prison and put to death about 310. Eusebius says the two friends wrote a Defense of Origen together, and the books Pamphilus kept were read for generations after him.

Eusebius, whose pages hold nearly all of his life, says he shared his possessions with the needy, was devoted above all to the Scriptures, and gathered Origen's works and other church writers' for the church. Jerome says he searched the world for books and bought all of Origen's at great cost.

Then the emperors' orders reached Palestine, first to tear down the churches and burn the Scriptures, then for everyone to sacrifice to the Roman gods. In the fifth year of the persecution, the governor tested Pamphilus in rhetoric and philosophy, then tried to force him to sacrifice. He refused, heedless of threats. In anger the governor had his sides scraped in long torture and put him in prison with the confessors, Christians who had held to the faith under trial.

The friends wrote the Defense with care, Eusebius says, because of those who looked for faults. Photius, a Byzantine scholar five centuries later, says Pamphilus wrote most of it in prison. It answered the charges that Origen taught the soul lived before the body, and made Christ either a mere man or one whose deeds happened only in seeming. It is written to confessors condemned to forced labor in the copper mines of Palestine. Origen's accusers, it says, read pagan books and even those of heretics, whose teaching the church condemns, keeping the good like a money-changer who tests each coin and throws out the false. Yet they gave Origen's readers no such pardon. "They think the command to become approved money-changers is wholly shut out for those who read Origen's books. Such money-changers know how to keep what is good and hold back from every kind of evil. If anyone is only seen reading his books, at once they drench him in the shame of the heretics." The Defense set out Origen's own words with replies between.

After two whole years in prison, Pamphilus and his companions came before a new governor. He knew they had held firm under earlier tortures, and asked only whether they would now obey. Each gave the same answer, and he sentenced them as he had the others. Pamphilus died with eleven others, the only presbyter among them. Eusebius took his name as a surname.

Eusebius later sent readers to the library's catalogue to learn which of Origen's works survived, and Jerome used it in his day. Rufinus of Aquileia put the Defense's first book, the only one that survives, into Latin under Pamphilus's name. Some ninety years after Pamphilus died, Jerome complained that readers ignored the bishops who had condemned Origen as a heretic, because they thought a martyr had praised him.

Jerome had also found twenty-five volumes of Origen's commentaries on the twelve prophets, copied in Pamphilus's hand, and guarded them, he says, as if he had a king's riches. "If it is a joy to own one letter from a martyr, how much greater to own so many thousand lines! It seems to me he marked them with the traces of his own blood."
