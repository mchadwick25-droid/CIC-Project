- **The essence:** Pamphilus, a presbyter at Caesarea on the coast of Roman Palestine, gathered a library of sacred books for his church and, Jerome says, copied most of Origen's works with his own hand. In the persecution he was tortured, held two years in prison and put to death about 310. His friend Eusebius says the two of them wrote a Defense of Origen together, and the books Pamphilus kept were read for generations after him. (S1, E2, E3, E5, E7, E10, I1)
- **Also told:**
  - The life, given room before the end: he shared his possessions with the needy and gave away copies of Scripture to keep, to women as well as men (E1, E4, Quote 1). Documented, Eusebius.
  - What the book says: it was written to confessors condemned to the mines, against men who condemned Origen and shamed anyone seen reading him as a heretic (E8, E9, Quote 2); it set out Origen's own words with replies between (E9). Documented, the Defense in Rufinus's Latin; whose words, disputed (D1, card).
  - The stand: tested in learning, ordered to sacrifice, refused, tortured, imprisoned; two years later asked again if he would obey, with the same answer from all twelve (E5, E10). Documented, Eusebius.
  - Checked against the Library: all held, with three limits. "Copied Origen's works by hand" is Jerome's ("the greater part", E3). "From prison" is held only in Photius, about 550 years later (E7); Eusebius says only that the two worked together, and the Defense's heading shows it was written in the persecution, to confessors in the mines (E8). "Taught Eusebius" is held only in a seventh-century chronicle (I5); Eusebius calls him "my dearest friend" (E5).

- **Setting:**
  - Time: the persecution that began with orders to tear down churches and burn the Scriptures, then to make everyone sacrifice (S3). Arrested "in the fifth year" of it (S3, E5); put to death after "two entire years" in prison, on a day in February (S4, E10). Year: "about 310" only (S3, D3; M1).
  - Place: Caesarea, a port on the coast of Roman Palestine (S1). The copper mines of Phaeno, in Palestine (E6), only if the writer needs the place.
  - Tradition: the census tells him within "Alexandrian Christianity" (S6); the story tells Caesarea, where the record puts him, and carries the Alexandrian line through Origen's books. "Caesarea and Its Library" (S6) only as a link.
  - Power: the governor Urbanus, then a new governor (S5); by role.
- **Words to explain:**
  - presbyter: "an elder, a priest of the church" (X1).
  - the order to sacrifice: "to offer to the Roman gods, as the emperors' orders commanded everyone" (S3, X3).
  - confessors: "Christians who had held to the faith under trial" (X5); the mines: "forced labor in the copper mines" (E6).
  - the Defense: "a book answering the charges against Origen, that he taught the soul lived before the body and made Christ either a mere man or one whose deeds happened only in seeming, by setting out his own words with replies between" (X6, E9, E9a). "Apology" never alone.
  - money-changers, in Quote 2: "like a money-changer who tests each coin, keeping the good and throwing out the false", in the setup (X8).
  - heretic: "one whose teaching the church condemns" (X7), where Quote 2 first uses it.
  - Origen: "the Bible scholar who had taught at Alexandria and then at Caesarea, in the century before" (X11).
  - martyr: known to a grown listener; no gloss.
- **Shape:** Martyr. The life comes to a stand: tested, ordered to sacrifice, refused, two years in prison, the same answer at the end. What the stand cost: torture and death, with eleven others. The impact is in how the church remembered it and used the memory: a martyr's name on the Defense gave it weight in the quarrel over Origen (I2), and Jerome treasured lines in his hand as if marked in blood (Quote 3). Trap: the death swallows the story. The library, the copying, the giving of books and the Defense get as much room as the end (Beats 1, 2 and 4 against Beats 3 and 5).

- **Openings:**
  1. The giver of books (E4, Quote 1). Eusebius, his friend, wrote his Life; it is lost, but Jerome kept one passage. In it Pamphilus helps any lover of books who lacks the means to live. Quote 1. Leads into the essence: the man who gave books away also gathered them. Preferred.
  2. The test (E5): a Roman governor first tests a presbyter in rhetoric and philosophy, then orders him to sacrifice to the gods; he refuses. Use only if Opening 1 is not; Beat 3 then tells only the torture and prison.
- **Story beats:**
  1. **Caesarea and the library.** Eusebius, whose pages hold nearly all of his life (say so once, here or in Opening 1, in a sentence about Eusebius), knew him as a presbyter at Caesarea who lived as a true philosopher, Eusebius says, and set up a school there (S1, S2; narrate, not quoted). He shared his possessions with the needy (MP's wider giving; drop it if Opening 1 has told the need, card) and was devoted above all to the Scriptures (E1). He gathered a library for the church: Origen's works and other church writers' (E2, Eusebius). Jerome says he searched the world for books and bought all of Origen's at great cost, and gave them to the church at Caesarea (E2). Confidence: Documented.
  2. **The copying.** Jerome says he so loved the holy books that he copied the greater part of Origen's works with his own hand (E3). Origen in one clause (X11). Confidence: Documented as Jerome's report, "the greater part".
  3. **The stand.** The emperors' orders reached Palestine: first to tear down the churches and burn the Scriptures, then for everyone to sacrifice to the gods (S3). In the fifth year of the persecution the governor tested Pamphilus in rhetoric and philosophy, then tried to force him to sacrifice; he refused and paid no heed to threats. In anger the governor had his sides scraped in long torture, and put him in prison with the confessors (E5). About the same time the governor sent other confessors to the copper mines, their ankles first disabled with hot irons (E6), one clause. Reason: his refusal to sacrifice, as given; the testing in learning has no reason given (X4). Confidence: Documented, Eusebius.
  4. **The Defense.** Eusebius says he and Pamphilus wrote a Defense of Origen together, with care, because of those who looked for faults (E7; narrate, not quoted). A Byzantine scholar who read all six books, Photius, some five centuries later, says Pamphilus wrote five of them in prison, with Eusebius beside him (E7), credited. It is written to confessors condemned to the mines of Palestine (E8). It says some men condemned Origen out of ignorance, others out of malice, and turned on anyone who read him (E9). Its accusers, as the Defense reports them, charged that Origen taught the soul lived before the body, and that he made Christ a mere man, and also the opposite, that he made all the Saviour's deeds happen only in seeming, not in history (E9a, APL 17661-17670, 20291-20293). Confidence: Documented as the Defense's report. These men, the Defense says, read pagan and even heretics' books themselves, keeping what was good, and refused Origen's readers the same pardon (APL 14118-14153, with notes between; APM 41976-42033). Quote 2, with the money-changer glossed in its setup. It set out Origen's own words, under his name, with the defender's replies between (E9). Only the first book survives, in a Latin translation (I2), one clause. Confidence: Documented (Eusebius's word "jointly"; the Defense's heading and text); "in prison": Documented as Photius's report, about 550 years later; never the narrator's.
  5. **The death.** After two whole years in prison, on a day in February, Pamphilus and his companions came before a new governor (E10, S4). He knew they had held firm under earlier tortures, and asked only whether they would now obey; each gave the same answer, and he sentenced them as he had the others (E10). Twelve died, and Eusebius counts them like the apostles; Pamphilus was the only presbyter (E10). A young servant of his household, taught in his master's way of life, cried out from the crowd for the bodies to be buried, and was burned before his master died (E10). Reason for the sentence: they would not obey, as given. Confidence: Documented, Eusebius.
  6. **His friend's name.** Eusebius called himself after him: Eusebius Pamphili, and Jerome says the surname came from his friendship for the martyr (JER 41076-41077; narrate), and wrote his Life in three books, now lost (E11). One or two clauses. Confidence: Documented as Jerome's report.
- **What came of it** (ranked; about a fifth of the story):
  1. **The library, used and rebuilt.** Eusebius sent readers to its catalogue to learn which of Origen's works survived (E2, I1). Jerome used it in his day (E2, DVI 3). It was later partly destroyed, and two later bishops of Caesarea tried to restore it on parchment, one leaving an inventory (I1, Jerome). Isidore of Seville, three centuries on, still counted him first among Christian founders of libraries (I1; "nearly thirty thousand volumes" is Isidore's alone, card).
  2. **The Defense in the Latin quarrel (defended: Origen's faith and good name).** Rufinus put its first book into Latin under Pamphilus's name, and about ninety years after Pamphilus's death Jerome complained that readers ignored the bishops who condemned Origen as a heretic (JER 51456-51458), because they thought a martyr had praised him (I2; narrated, not quoted, credited, "thought" kept). The martyr's name carried the book.
  2a. Jerome says the churches of Palestine read the copies of the Greek Old Testament that Origen prepared and Eusebius and Pamphilus published (I6a, JERL 35093-35098); Documented as Jerome's report. Tell it in one clause if the recount allows, else card.
  3. Socrates called Pamphilus and Eusebius trustworthy witnesses for Origen (I3; **defended**; narrate). Card, or one clause.
  4. Card: the Maronite Chronicle and Theophanes keep him among the martyrs and as Eusebius's teacher (I5); the colophons, editors only (I6).

- **Connection to carry into the story:** What passed from Pamphilus was Origen's work, kept in two forms. First the books: he gathered them for the church at Caesarea and copied most of them himself, and later readers worked from them: Eusebius, who sent readers to his catalogue to learn which of Origen's works survived (E2), and Jerome, who found volumes there in Pamphilus's hand (E3, I1). Second the Defense, which set Origen's own words against the charges made against him (E9) and, carried into Latin under a martyr's name, made readers trust Origen against bishops who condemned him, by Jerome's complaint (I2). The writer must make plain that Pamphilus never met Origen; that the line to Alexandria runs through Origen's books, not through Pamphilus's own schooling (D4); and that "the martyr's name carried the book" is shown by Jerome's complaint, never said by the narrator as a judgment. Carry it through Beats 1, 2 and 4, the impact and the ending, never as a "this matters because" sentence.
