- **The essence:** Origen taught the faith at Alexandria, in Egypt, and then at Caesarea, on the coast of Palestine, and spent his life on one question: what Scripture means beneath its plain words. He set the Hebrew text beside the Greek translations, and bishops and scholars after him read Scripture through his books and fought over them for centuries. (E3, E10, T2, I1, I7)
- **Also told:**
  - The question, in his own words: Jews, heretics and simple believers all went wrong about God because they took Scripture only by its bare letter (T1-T3, Quote 1); God put impossibilities in it so readers would look for a meaning worthy of him (T7, T8). Documented, his own words.
  - The other side: Porphyry, a pagan who had met him, said he took the Greeks' figurative way of explaining their sacred rites and applied it to the Jewish scriptures (T11, Quote 2). Documented as Porphyry's charge.
  - The columns: he learned Hebrew, hunted out translations and set them side by side with the Hebrew, keeping the church's Greek while learning the Jews' readings (E10, T13). Documented.
  - The break with his bishop: Demetrius first praised him, then wrote against him to bishops everywhere; the old sources differ on why and in what order (E14, E17, E18, D1), and Origen himself speaks only of a storm (E20, Quote 3). Contested as to order and cause; the letters Documented.
  - The torture under Decius: chains, the iron collar, the stocks, the threat of fire, and a judge who would not let him die (E27, E28). Documented, Eusebius from Origen's letters.
  - Checked against the Library: all held. "Head of the catechetical school" is held only as Eusebius's "the catechetical school had been entrusted to him alone by Demetrius" (E7). "Condemned as a heretic in 553" is held only as a council text naming him in a list (I7). No old source says the "storm" was Demetrius (E20).

- **Shape:** Teacher or thinker. The question he worked at: what Scripture means beneath its plain words, and why readers who stop at the letter go wrong about God. What he taught: Scripture read on more than one level, with God's own stumbling blocks to push the reader deeper. Who learned it: his pupils at Caesarea, and later Basil of Caesarea and Gregory of Nazianzus. The one idea is shown through a moment: the boy pressing his father for the meaning of Scripture (Opening 1), answered by his own words grown up (Quote 1).

- **Openings:**
  1. The boy and his father (E3). Eusebius, writing from letters and people who had known him (S1), tells the kiss as "they say". His father taught him Scripture every day, before any Greek learning. The boy was not content with the simple, obvious meaning and kept asking what the inspired words really meant, until his father, for appearance's sake, scolded him to his face not to search beyond his age or past the plain sense; yet in private the father thanked God for such a son, and often stood by the sleeping boy, uncovered his chest and kissed it (EUS "seemingly to his face"; EUSG τῷ μὲν δοκεῖν, "for appearance's sake"). Leads straight into the essence: that question became his life's work. Preferred.
  2. The stocks (E28), told as a reach ahead: an old man, his feet stretched four spaces in the stocks for many days, and a judge who would not let him die. Use only if Opening 1 is not; Beat 7 then tells only what the opening did not.
- **Story beats:**
  1. **The teaching entrusted.** He earned his keep teaching grammar (E5). Demetrius, the bishop, entrusted the teaching of the faith to him alone (E7). He sold his books of old Greek literature for four obols a day, a few coins, and slept on the ground (E7). Confidence: Documented as Eusebius's account.
  2. **The deed.** Eusebius says he took Jesus' saying about men who "made themselves eunuchs for the kingdom of heaven's sake" too literally, and castrated himself: to keep the saying, and to give unbelievers no ground for scandal, since he taught women as well as men (E9). Demetrius, when he learned of it, admired his daring and urged him on (E9). Reason: Eusebius gives it (E9); "too literal" is Eusebius's judgment, credited. Confidence: Documented as Eusebius's account (D3).
  3. **The Hebrew beside the Greek.** He learned Hebrew, got the Hebrew Scriptures from the Jews, and searched out other Greek translations besides the Seventy (E10). He set them side by side in columns with the Hebrew (E10). His reason, in his letter to a fellow scholar: to keep the churches' Greek, so as not to bring a forgery on the churches, and still know the Jews' readings, so as not to quote to them what their copies lacked (T13, narrate). Confidence: Documented.
  4. **The bare letter.** He wrote of why readers go wrong in a book, On First Principles, in the part that tells how Scripture is to be read (T14; PHIL 44). Narrate T1 and T3 briefly: the unbelieving Jews followed the bare letter of the prophecies and did not receive Jesus; heretics, reading of God's anger and jealousy, took the God of the Jewish Scriptures for an imperfect, lesser maker of the world, and said Jesus had proclaimed a more perfect God above him; simple believers in the church imagined things of God they would not believe of the most savage and unjust man (T3, Crombie). Then Quote 1. The Word of God, he wrote, had set stumbling blocks in Scripture, things impossible if read by the letter, so readers would look for a meaning worthy of God: a first, second and third day with no sun, moon or stars (T7, T8, narrate). The three levels in one clause at most, with whom each level serves (T4): as a human being is body, soul and spirit, so is Scripture; its body, the plain sense, helps simple believers, its soul those further on, and its spirit the perfect. Then the other side: Porphyry, a pagan philosopher who later wrote against the Christians. Porphyry met him when Porphyry himself was young (T11). He used the books, Porphyry wrote, of Chaeremon, a Stoic, and of Cornutus (T11). Then Quote 2, with the mystery rites glossed in its setup. Confidence: Documented (his words; Porphyry's charge as Porphyry's).
  5. **The break.** Years later, when war broke out in Alexandria, he went to Caesarea, and its bishops asked him to preach, though he was not yet a presbyter (S5, E14). Demetrius wrote that laymen preaching before bishops had never been heard of; the bishops of Jerusalem and Caesarea answered that holy bishops did ask able men to preach, and named cases. Demetrius called him home, and he went (E14). Later, on a church errand to Greece, he passed through Palestine and was ordained presbyter at Caesarea (E16). Then the sources part. Eusebius says Demetrius, seeing him grow famous, had already written to bishops everywhere that the deed of his youth was most foolish; after the ordination, with nothing else to say against him, he accused him bitterly, and those who had ordained him too (E17). ("Overcome by human weakness" is Eusebius's judgment, credited if used.) Jerome says it was the ordination that so enraged Demetrius that he wrote everywhere against him (E18). He left Alexandria for Caesarea (S4). Origen's own words: Quote 3 (E20). Confidence: the letters Documented; the order and cause Contested (D1).
  6. **Caesarea.** The bishops of Jerusalem and Caesarea sat under him as their only teacher (E21). Two brothers came to study with him, and he found them keen on Greek and Roman learning; he gave them a love of philosophy and turned them to the study of God, and after five years both became bishops in Pontus (E22; link `anatolian-church-third-century`). Confidence: Documented.
  7. **Under Decius.** Eusebius says the emperor Decius, out of hatred for his predecessor Philip, persecuted the churches (E27). Origen endured for the word of Christ chains, tortures of the body, the iron collar and the dungeon; for many days his feet were stretched four spaces in the stocks, and he was threatened with fire; and his judge did all he could not to let him die (E28). He died in the time of Gallus, at sixty-nine (S6). Reason for keeping him alive: none given; tell as given. Confidence: Documented, Eusebius from Origen's own letters.
- **What came of it** (ranked; about a fifth of the story):
  1. **His way of reading.** In the century after Origen's death, Socrates says, Basil of Caesarea and Gregory of Nazianzus got his books and drew from them the right reading of Scripture, and when Arians quoted him, they showed the Arians did not understand him (I1; **defended**: Origen's reasoning). Gregory sent a friend "the Philocalia of Origen", passages chosen from him (I2).
  2. **The fight.** Pamphilus of Caesarea wrote a Defense of him, answering from his own words those who said his books differed from the church's teaching (I12, I4; **defended**: his books). Athanasius called him "labour-loving" and cited him for the Son, who never was not (I3; **defended**; the word "labour-loving" is in the live Origen text and is not quoted). About three centuries after his death, a council at Constantinople named him, with his writings, among the heretics (I7; Ending 1).
  3. **The columns used.** Jerome, attacked for a new Latin translation of the Old Testament, answered that Origen himself had marked where the old Greek fell short of the Hebrew (I8; **defended**: Jerome's translation); Jerome had the extra translations from Origen's own library (I9).
  4. His pupils (told in Beat 6); Dionysius of Alexandria his pupil (I11, link; card).
  5. Card: Socrates's four critics (I6), Eusebius's share in the Defense, written, Eusebius says, "on account of faultfinders" (EUS 36974-36976), with its five books from prison (I4), Pamphilus's library (I10), Methodius's charge that the body was given to the soul as a fetter after its fall (I5, T16, in its own content).

- **Connection to carry into the story:** What passed from Origen was a way of reading Scripture on more than one level, a text set beside its Hebrew, and the books that held both. Two brothers studied with him at Caesarea for five years and then became bishops in the churches of Pontus (E22). In the next century, by Socrates's account, Basil of Caesarea and Gregory of Nazianzus took their reading of Scripture from his books and defended him against the Arians' use of him (I1), and Gregory sent on a book of his chosen passages (I2). Jerome defended his own translation by Origen's marks (I8). Pamphilus of Caesarea defended his books from his own words against those who said they differed from the church's teaching (I12); a council named him among heretics three centuries on (I7). The writer must make plain that "the right reading" is Socrates's judgment, credited; that Porphyry's charge (Quote 2) is his own; and that no old source here says he invented reading Scripture this way (his own model is Paul, T6). Carry it through Beat 6, the impact and the ending, never a "this matters because" sentence.
- **Ending:**
