In Alexandria, in Egypt, a father taught his young son Scripture every day, before any Greek learning. The boy, Origen, was not content with the plain meaning and kept asking what the inspired words really meant, until his father, for appearance's sake, scolded him to his face not to search beyond his age. Eusebius of Caesarea, who wrote of Origen's life from his letters and from people who had known him, says that in private the father thanked God for such a son, and that, they say, he often stood by the sleeping boy, uncovered his chest and kissed it.

That question, what Scripture means beneath its plain words, became Origen's life's work. He taught the faith at Alexandria and later at Caesarea, on the coast of Palestine. He set the Hebrew text beside the Greek translations, and for centuries after him bishops and scholars read Scripture through his books and fought over them.

Under the emperor Severus the churches were persecuted, and the boy longed to die a martyr. His mother begged him to stay, then hid all his clothes so he could not go out. His father was beheaded and the family's property was seized, and Origen, not quite seventeen, was left with his mother and six younger brothers. Pagans came to him to hear the word of God, and in his eighteenth year Demetrius, the bishop, entrusted to him alone the teaching of the faith to those who came to learn it.

Eusebius says he took one of Jesus' sayings too literally, that some "have made themselves eunuchs for the kingdom of heaven's sake." Origen castrated himself, Eusebius reports, to keep the saying, and to give unbelievers no ground for scandal, since he taught women as well as men. When Demetrius learned of it, he admired his daring and urged him on.

Origen learned Hebrew, got the Hebrew Scriptures from the Jews, and searched out other Greek translations besides the Seventy, the old Greek translation of the Hebrew Scriptures, which the churches read. He set them side by side in columns with the Hebrew. In a letter to a fellow scholar he gave his reason. He would keep the churches' Greek, so as not to bring a forgery on them, and still know the Jews' readings, so as not to quote to Jews what their own copies lacked.

At Alexandria he wrote a book, On First Principles, and in the part on how Scripture is to be read he asked why readers go wrong about God. The unbelieving Jews, he wrote, followed the bare letter of the prophecies and did not receive Jesus. Heretics read of God's anger and jealousy, took the God of the Jewish Scriptures for a lesser maker of the world, and set a more perfect God above him. Simple believers in the church imagined things of God that they would not believe of the most savage and unjust man. "All these I have spoken of hold false and impious opinions about God, or ignorant ones. The only reason, it seems, is that Scripture is not understood on its spiritual side, but is taken in its bare, literal sense." God himself, he wrote, had set stumbling blocks in Scripture, things impossible if read by the letter, so that readers would look for a meaning worthy of God.

Porphyry, a pagan philosopher who later wrote against the Christians, had met Origen when Porphyry himself was young. Origen was always with Plato, Porphyry wrote, and used the books of Chaeremon, a Stoic, and of Cornutus. Greek writers like these read the secret rites of Greek religion as figures for hidden truths, and Porphyry charged that Origen took their method for the Bible: "From these writers he learned the figurative way of explaining the Greek mystery rites, and he applied it to the Jewish scriptures."

Later, on a church errand to Greece, Origen passed through Palestine and was ordained at Caesarea as a presbyter, an elder and priest of the church. Eusebius and Jerome tell what followed differently. Eusebius says that Demetrius, seeing him grow famous, had already written to bishops everywhere that the deed of his youth was most foolish, and that after the ordination, with nothing else to say against him, he accused him bitterly, along with those who had ordained him. Jerome, writing more than a century later, says it was the ordination itself that so enraged Demetrius that he wrote everywhere against him. Origen left Alexandria for Caesarea, and in a commentary on John's Gospel he wrote: "We came out of the storm; we were brought out of Egypt. The God who led his people out from there set us free."

At Caesarea two brothers came to him for Greek and Roman learning. He gave them a love of philosophy and turned them to the study of God, and after five years both became bishops in the churches of Pontus.

Under the emperor Decius, who, Eusebius says, persecuted the churches out of hatred for Philip, the emperor before him, Origen endured chains, tortures of the body, the iron collar and the dungeon for the word of Christ. For many days he lay in the stocks, a wooden frame that held a prisoner's feet apart, with his feet stretched four spaces, and he was threatened with fire. Eusebius, who had this from Origen's own letters, says his judge did all he could not to let him die. Origen died in the time of the emperor Gallus, at sixty-nine.

Pamphilus of Caesarea and Eusebius wrote a Defense of him. Others attacked his books, and about three centuries after his death a council at Constantinople named him, with his writings, among the heretics. Yet his way of reading had already passed to the church's teachers. In the century after him, the historian Socrates says, Basil of Caesarea and Gregory of Nazianzus got his books and drew from them the right reading of Scripture. When the Arians, who taught that the Son of God had a beginning, quoted Origen for their side, the two showed that the Arians did not understand him.

Gregory sent a friend the Philocalia of Origen, a book of passages chosen from his writings. Its first chapter tells how Scripture is to be read and understood, and why, taken by the letter, it sometimes seems impossible.
