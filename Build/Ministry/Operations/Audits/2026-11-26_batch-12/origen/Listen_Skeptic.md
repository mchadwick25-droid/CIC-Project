## 1. The test (person kit)

- **Who this was:** filled. He was a teacher of the faith and a Bible scholar at Alexandria and then Caesarea.
- **What happened:** filled. The story gives his teaching, the castration, the Hebrew and Greek columns, his ordination and the break with Demetrius, the move to Caesarea, his torture under Decius and his death.
- **What it was about:** filled. It was about reading Scripture beneath its plain words. The opening scene and paragraphs 5 and 6 carry it.
- **Why the connection matters:** filled, but weakly. Later readers come through: Basil and Gregory, the Philocalia, the Arians, the defense and the council. The pupils come through only as "two brothers" who "became bishops in Pontus", and a listener cannot tell why that matters or where Pontus is (see 7).

## 2. The opening (first three sentences)

- **Who:** Origen is named, but the listener does not learn what he became until paragraph 2. That is acceptable, since the essence lands by word 150.
- **Where:** Alexandria, in Egypt. Clear.
- **When:** missing. No century or year appears anywhere in the story. The only anchors are "two generations after Origen's death", the emperors Decius, Philip and Gallus, and later counts ("in the next century", "about three centuries after"). A listener with no church background cannot place him at all. **Change:** add a time clause in the opening or the essence, from the outline's Setting (for example "in the 200s", if the outline holds it).
- **Why there:** missing for three sentences, then given in sentence 4. It is not blocking.

## 3. Sentences that fail on one hearing

1. **BLOCKING.** The close jumps back in time. It runs Basil and Gregory (next century), then the Arians, then "Pamphilus of Caesarea and Eusebius ... wrote a book in his defense" (earlier than Basil), then the council "about three centuries after his death", and then back to "Gregory of Nazianzus sent a friend the Philocalia". On one hearing Gregory comes back after the council, and the order is lost. The bar treats a jump back as a failure to understand. **Change:** put the paragraphs in time order. First the Pamphilus and Eusebius defense, then the Socrates sentences on Basil, Gregory and the Arians, then the Philocalia sentences, and the council last. Or keep the Philocalia as the close and move the council sentence before it, with a time word showing the return ("Long before that, Gregory ..."). Either way, nothing goes back without a marker.
2. "He kept the churches' Greek, he explained in a letter to a fellow scholar, so as not to bring a forgery on them." It is not clear what "bring a forgery on them" means. Did he fear forging a text, or seeming to? And in the next sentence "them" shifts from the churches to the Jews. MINOR. **Change:** say it plainly, in the outline's sense (for example "so that he would not seem to hand the churches a forged Bible"), and name "the Jews" in place of the second "them".
3. "Years later ... was ordained at Caesarea as a presbyter, an elder, a priest of the church." Three names for one office, stacked, blur the sentence. MINOR. **Change:** "was ordained a presbyter, an elder of the church, at Caesarea."
4. "Eusebius says Demetrius ... had already written to bishops everywhere that the deed of his youth was most foolish." "The deed of his youth" relies on the listener linking it back four paragraphs to the castration. MINOR. **Change:** "that his castration as a young man had been most foolish."
5. "For many days his feet were stretched four spaces in the stocks ..." "Four spaces" means nothing on one hearing. MINOR. **Change:** "four holes apart in the stocks", if the outline's gloss supports it. Otherwise give the outline's plain sense.
6. "His judge did all he could not to let him die." The listener hears mercy and cannot tell why a torturer would keep him alive. MINOR. **Change:** give the reason if the outline holds one. If it does not, leave the sentence as the record gives it.
7. "In the next century, the historian Socrates says, Basil of Caesarea ..." A second-language listener hears "Socrates" and thinks of the Athenian philosopher. "Basil of Caesarea" also sounds like Origen's own Caesarea in Palestine. MINOR. **Change:** "the church historian Socrates" and "Basil, bishop of Caesarea in Cappadocia" (if the outline spells it so).
8. "The other side comes from Porphyry ..." The other side of what? No argument has been set up, so the listener has to guess that a charge is coming. MINOR (see also 6). **Change:** "Porphyry, a pagan philosopher who had met Origen when young and later wrote against the Christians, made a charge against this way of reading."

## 4. Bare statements (Part 1, Explain it)

1. **BLOCKING.** "Scripture, he taught, has a body, a soul and a spirit, as a person does, and its plain sense helps many readers." The body is linked to the plain sense. Soul and spirit are named but never explained, so the listener cannot say what the second and third levels are. This is the heart's "Scripture read on more than one level". **Change:** give the soul and the spirit each a plain clause from the outline (what each level teaches the reader). If the outline does not hold that, recast as "has a plain sense, like a body, and deeper senses beneath it, like a soul and a spirit".
2. **BLOCKING.** "Pamphilus of Caesarea and Eusebius ... wrote a book in his defense." A defense against what? At this point no charge against his teaching has been told except Porphyry's and the Arians' use of him. **Change:** say in one clause what the book answered, from the outline. If the outline holds no such line, cut the sentence.
3. **BLOCKING unless the trust read shows the record gives no reason.** "About three centuries after his death, a council of bishops at Constantinople named him ... among the heretics." This is a condemnation with no reason given. Part 1 asks for the reason wherever the old sources give one. **Change:** add the reason the council's record gives. If the record gives none, keep the sentence as it stands.

Not counted: "Origen left Alexandria for Caesarea." It is an ordinary journey, and the break just told gives its cause.

## 5. Citations read aloud that Part 1 does not call for

1. "Eusebius says the emperor Decius persecuted the churches out of hatred for Philip, the emperor before him, and tells what followed from Origen's own letters." Crediting the motive is fine, since it is blame. The second half is a how-we-know aside, and the opening already says Eusebius drew on Origen's letters. MINOR. **Narrator's voice:** "The emperor Decius, Eusebius says, persecuted the churches out of hatred for Philip, the emperor before him. For the word of Christ, Origen endured ..."

The other credits are called for. "Eusebius says" on the castration credits a judgment ("too literally"). "Here the old sources part", with Eusebius and Jerome, marks a real difference. Socrates is a later witness to what came of him, and Porphyry is an enemy's charge.

## 6. Tells, and lines that sound machine-made

Keep sentence 1 as the one early sentence about the single witness.

- "His charge turns on the secret rites of Greek religion." "Turns on" is abstract framing from a summarizer, not a narrator. **Change:** fold it into the Porphyry sentence: "... charged that he had borrowed his method from the way Greeks explained their secret rites."

## 8. Pull and attention

- **Does the opening pull?** Yes, once the scene starts. The father kissing the sleeping boy's chest is the strongest image in the piece. Sentence 1 delays it with a source note, and sentence 2's "He begins with" frames it as a document (see 6).
- **First drift:** "In a book called On First Principles, he wrote of why readers go wrong." From here to the end of the Porphyry paragraph the story stops moving through events and becomes a reading of texts: three groups of misreaders, a long quote, a doctrine and an enemy's quote. **Why, for the reviser:** the ideas are sound, but no person acts. The threat from Demetrius, set up in paragraph 3, stalls for about 230 words. Tying the doctrine to a moment (his teaching, the letter, a student) would keep the thread.
- **Would I listen to the end?** Yes. The ordination quarrel, the "out of Egypt" quote and the torture pull it back. The out-of-order close (3.1) is where a listener is most likely to lose the thread.
