His deacon, Pontius, who writes as an admirer, says that soon after this man's baptism the whole people of Carthage wanted him as their bishop. A crowd, he says, besieged the doors of Cyprian's house, while he stepped back to give place to older men. He was still a new Christian when they made him bishop.

Jerome says he had once been a teacher of public speaking. In the middle of the third century Cyprian led the Latin-speaking church of Carthage, in Roman Africa, and the councils of Africa's bishops through persecution, plague and division. He taught that the church is one, a mother outside whom no one has God as Father, and that each bishop answers to Christ alone. He would not accept the ruling of Stephen, bishop of Rome, on baptism, and in 258 he was put to the sword. Later the Donatists and Augustine both claimed him.

In 250 the emperor Decius ordered Christians before the magistrates to sacrifice to the gods. At Carthage, Cyprian wrote later, the crowds kept demanding him with violent shouting. So he withdrew for a while, thinking less of his own safety than of the brothers' peace, so that his presence would not stir the riot further, and from hiding he led his church by letter.

His going brought a charge against him: a shepherd had left his flock. The clergy at Rome wrote to Carthage's clergy that he had withdrawn for a set reason, and had done rightly. In the same letter they spoke of the gospel's good shepherd, who lays down his life for the sheep, and the hired man who runs from the wolf. They asked Carthage's clergy not to be hired men.

At Rome a second bishop, Novatian, was set up against the one already chosen, and as that rival rose, Cyprian wrote a book on the church's unity. In it the church is a single mother, from whose womb Christians are born and by whose milk they are fed. Of anyone who leaves the church of Christ, he writes: "He is a stranger, he is unholy, he is an enemy. The one who does not have the Church as mother cannot have God as Father." By the church he meant the one body joined to its lawful bishops, while Novatian's side held a bishop and a church of its own.

A few years later a dreadful plague swept the city. Some Christians were troubled that it struck them as hard as it struck the pagans, and Cyprian answered them in a sermon.

"This plague and pestilence, which seems so dreadful and deadly, searches out how just each person is. It tests the minds of the human race. Do the healthy serve the sick? Do relatives love their kin as they ought? Do masters pity their sick slaves? Do doctors not abandon the sick who plead with them?"

Then came a quarrel with Rome over baptism, the washing that makes a Christian, which is given once. But when someone washed by a group outside the church came over to it, did that washing count? Cyprian counted as heretics all who stood outside the one church. With councils of African bishops he held that there is one baptism because the church is one, and none outside it, so those who came over were to be baptized in the church.

Stephen, bishop of Rome, answered otherwise, as Cyprian copies his letter: let nothing new be done beyond what has been handed down. Those who came over were to have hands laid on them for repentance, without a new washing, since the heretics themselves do not wash again those who come over to them.

In the months of that quarrel, eighty-seven African bishops met in a council, and Cyprian spoke first. Communion meant belonging to the church and sharing its bread and cup. He began: "It remains for each of us to say what we think on this matter. We judge no one, and we cut no one off from communion if he thinks otherwise. For none of us sets himself up as a bishop of bishops. None of us drives his colleagues to obey by a tyrant's terror."

In 258, at his gardens, he learned he was to be taken from the city. In his last letter he says he left the gardens for a while, because a bishop should confess his faith in the city where he leads the church, before all his people.

He was taken at his gardens, and next morning, 14 September 258, he stood before the governor. The emperors had ordered him to perform the rites, the sacrifices to Rome's gods, and he said he would not. The sentence, in Rome's own terms, named him an enemy of the Roman gods whose blood would make the law hold, and ordered him put to the sword. Cyprian said, "Thanks be to God." In the field of Sextus he covered his eyes with his own hand. When he could not tie the cloth, a presbyter and a subdeacon, both named Julian, tied it, and there he was put to death.

From the 310s a rival church in Africa, the Donatists, baptized again all who came over to it, and quoted his letters and council in its defense. Augustine of Hippo, a later African bishop, answered them about 140 years after Cyprian's death. Stop citing Cyprian's authority for repeating baptism, he told them, and hold with us to his example of keeping unity.

Augustine also preached on Cyprian's birthday, the martyr's feast on the day he died, kept, he said, through all Africa. Where Cyprian was put to death, he said, a table for God's altar now stood, called Cyprian's table because there he was offered up. Worshippers now ringed the place where persecutors had ringed him.
