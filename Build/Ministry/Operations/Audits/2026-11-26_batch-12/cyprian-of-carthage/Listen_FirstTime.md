## 1. The test

- **Who or what this was:** filled. A teacher of public speaking, made bishop of Carthage soon after becoming a Christian, mid-third century, killed in 258.
- **What happened:** filled. Hiding under Decius, the plague sermon, the book on unity, the baptism quarrel with Stephen, the trial and death.
- **What it was about:** filled. The church is one; a bishop answers to Christ, not to another bishop.
- **Why the connection matters:** filled, but only at the end. Paragraph 2 says "Later the Donatists and Augustine both claimed him", which I could not explain until the last two paragraphs. Acceptable, since the end fills it.
- Weak spot: the charge that he ran from his flock is the story's question, and I lost it twice (see 3a and 3f). I could not say, on one hearing, whether Rome backed him or whether his death answered the charge.

## 2. The opening

After the first three sentences I know: a man was made bishop by a crowd at Carthage, soon after his baptism, while he tried to step back. His name, Cyprian, arrives only as "Cyprian's house" in sentence 2, after "this man" in sentence 1; I nearly missed that this was him.
- Missing: **when** (no century until sentence 5), and **where Carthage is** (a city in Roman Africa comes only in sentence 5). Not lost: I had a place name and a person. MINOR.
- Suggested change (MINOR, held-back name): "His deacon, Pontius, who writes as an admirer, says that soon after Cyprian's baptism the whole people of Carthage wanted him as their bishop. A crowd, he says, besieged the doors of his house, while he stepped back to give place to older men."

## 3. Sentences that fail on one hearing

a. **BLOCKING (loses the thread of the charge).** Paragraph 4: "The clergy at Rome wrote to Carthage's clergy that he had withdrawn for a set reason, and had done rightly. In the same letter they spoke of the gospel's good shepherd, who lays down his life for the sheep, and the hired man who runs from the wolf. They asked Carthage's clergy not to be hired men." Meaning lost: first Rome says he did right, then it talks about a hired man who runs. I could not tell whether Rome was defending him or quietly blaming him. "For a set reason" says nothing to me. I also do not know who the good shepherd is. For the reviser: say plainly what the letter does (praise, warning, or both, as the source gives it), and give the good shepherd as Jesus' picture of himself in the gospel.

b. MINOR. Paragraph 2: "He taught that the church is one, a mother outside whom no one has God as Father, and that each bishop answers to Christ alone." "A mother outside whom no one has God as Father" is too folded for one hearing in a second language. The later quote makes it clear, so the thread is not lost.

c. MINOR. Paragraph 3: "...thinking less of his own safety than of the brothers' peace..." I did not know "the brothers" meant the Christians of Carthage.

d. MINOR. Paragraph 8: "Stephen, bishop of Rome, answered otherwise, as Cyprian copies his letter: let nothing new be done beyond what has been handed down." "As Cyprian copies his letter" stopped me; I had to work out that Stephen's words survive only in Cyprian's copy. Narrator's voice: "Stephen, bishop of Rome, answered otherwise. His letter survives only in Cyprian's copy, and it says: let nothing new be done beyond what has been handed down."

e. MINOR. Paragraph 11: "The sentence, in Rome's own terms, named him an enemy of the Roman gods whose blood would make the law hold..." "Whose blood would make the law hold" is half understood: I guessed his death was meant as a warning, but was not sure.

f. **BLOCKING (loses the thread).** Paragraph 10 into 11: "In 258, at his gardens, he learned he was to be taken from the city. In his last letter he says he left the gardens for a while, because a bishop should confess his faith in the city where he leads the church, before all his people. He was taken at his gardens..." Meaning lost: taken from the city to where, and why? Did he hide again? Then he is taken at the gardens he left, so did he come back? This looks like the answer to the old charge (this time he stays to face death among his people), but on one hearing I heard only confusing comings and goings. For the reviser: say, as the source gives it, where he was to be taken and that he came back to be taken in his own city.

## 4. Bare statements

a. **BLOCKING.** "Cyprian counted as heretics all who stood outside the one church." "Heretics" is the label Part 1 names, and it is never explained. I do not know what a heretic is or why it matters for baptism. Give its plain sense as the outline holds it, in the sentence.

b. **BLOCKING.** "Those who came over were to have hands laid on them for repentance, without a new washing..." The custom is named but not explained: I do not know what laying on hands does, or why it could stand in for a washing. Give its plain sense as the outline holds it.

c. **BLOCKING, if the old sources give a reason.** "At Rome a second bishop, Novatian, was set up against the one already chosen..." A break is named with no reason. Part 1 asks for why where the sources give it; if they give only the act, this stands as told. The trust readers decide.

## 5. Citations read aloud

- MINOR. "Jerome says he had once been a teacher of public speaking." Jerome is a name I do not know, and he does nothing in the story. If he is credited because he wrote long after, say that, so the name has a reason: "Jerome, writing more than a century later, says he had once been a teacher of public speaking." If no credit is needed: "He had once been a teacher of public speaking."
- Not counted: Pontius (praise, and a sibling story), Cyprian's own letters, Augustine (acts in the story). The craft tool's flag on "Cyprian said, 'Thanks be to God.'" is a false hit: he speaks in the scene.

## 6. Tells

Change to a statement: "But people washed by groups outside the church sometimes came over to it, and the question was whether that washing counted."

## 8. Attention

- The opening pulls me in: the crowd at his doors and the man stepping back is a real scene.
- Paragraph 2 is heavy: Jerome, Stephen, the Donatists and Augustine all come in a few sentences before I know any of them. I held on.
- **First drift:** paragraph 4, the Rome letter (3a). Why: praise and warning sit side by side without being told which is which, so I lost what the charge came to.
- Second drift: paragraphs 8 to 9, Stephen's letter and the laying on of hands (3d, 4b), because the custom at the centre of the quarrel is unexplained.
- Attention comes back at the trial: "Thanks be to God" and the two men named Julian tying the cloth are strong.
- I would listen to the end. The last image, worshippers ringing the place where persecutors had ringed him, lands.
