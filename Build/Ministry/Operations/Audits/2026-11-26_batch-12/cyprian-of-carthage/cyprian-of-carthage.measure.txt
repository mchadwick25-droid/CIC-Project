His deacon, Pontius, who wrote his life as an open admirer, says that soon after this man's baptism the whole people of Carthage wanted him as their bishop. A crowd besieged the doors of Cyprian's house, while he, still a new Christian, stepped back to make way for older men.

Before that, Jerome says, Cyprian had been a teacher of public speaking. As bishop of Carthage, the great city of Roman Africa, in the middle of the third century, he led its Latin-speaking church and the councils of Africa's bishops through persecution, plague and division. He taught that the church is one, a mother outside whom no one has God as Father, and that each bishop answers to Christ alone. He would not accept the ruling of Stephen, bishop of Rome, on baptism, and in 258 he was put to the sword. Long after his death, both sides of a split in Africa's church claimed him.

In 250 the emperor Decius ordered Christians to come before the magistrates and sacrifice to the gods. At Carthage, he wrote later, the people kept demanding him with violent shouting, so he left for a while. In his telling, he thought less of his own safety than of the brothers' peace, and did not want his presence to stir the riot further. Absent in body, he led his church by letter.

The clergy at Rome, whose own bishop had been killed in the same persecution, wrote to the clergy at Carthage. A subdeacon from Carthage had told them, they wrote, that Cyprian had withdrawn for a set reason, and that he had done rightly. In the same letter they spoke of the good shepherd in the gospel, who lays down his life for the sheep, and of the hired man who runs from the wolf. They asked Carthage's clergy not to be hired men. Cyprian wrote back that his acts had been reported to them less than plainly and faithfully, and gave his own account.

Many Christians had given way and sacrificed, and Cyprian would not settle their return to the church on his own. Then, at Rome, a second bishop, Novatian, was set up against the one already chosen, and Cyprian refused his envoys. As that rival bishop rose, Cyprian wrote a book, On the Unity of the Church. The church, it says, is one mother: from her womb Christians are born, and by her milk they are fed. Whoever leaves her for a rival church, he writes, is outside. "He is a stranger, he is unholy, he is an enemy. The one who does not have the Church as mother cannot have God as Father." The church he meant was the one body that held together with its lawful bishops, while Novatian's side kept a bishop and a church of its own.

A few years later a dreadful plague swept the city. Some Christians were troubled that it struck them as hard as the pagans, and Cyprian answered them in a sermon, On the Mortality, a title that means "on the deaths". In it he made the dying city a test of who would serve whom. "This plague and pestilence, which seems so dreadful and deadly, searches out how just each person is. It tests the minds of the human race. Do the healthy serve the sick? Do relatives love their kin as they ought? Do masters pity their sick slaves? Do doctors not abandon the sick who plead with them?"

Then came a quarrel over baptism, the washing that makes a Christian, which is given only once. The question was whether a washing counted when it had been given by a group outside the one church, people Cyprian called heretics, and someone from that group came over. With councils of African bishops, Cyprian answered that there is one baptism because the church is one, and none outside it. Those who come over are not baptized again, he wrote, but baptized, for they received nothing where there was nothing. Stephen, bishop of Rome, answered otherwise, in a letter known only as Cyprian copies it. Let nothing new be done beyond what was handed down, it said, and hands should be laid on them for repentance, since the heretics themselves did not wash again those who came over to them.

In those months Cyprian opened a council of eighty-seven bishops from across Roman Africa. To cut a man off from communion was to bar him from the church and from its shared bread and cup, and Cyprian set out how this meeting would run. "It remains for each of us to say what we think on this matter. We judge no one, and we cut no one off from communion if he thinks otherwise. For none of us sets himself up as a bishop of bishops. None of us drives his colleagues to obey by a tyrant's terror."

In 257 the governor of the province told Cyprian that the emperors had ordered everyone who did not keep the Roman religion to take part in its rites, the sacrifices to Rome's gods. He answered, "I am a Christian and a bishop," and he was sent away to Curubis, a town of the province. In 258, back at his gardens, he learned that he was to be taken from the city. In his last letter he says he left his gardens for a while, because a bishop should confess his faith in the city where he leads the church, before all his people. Pontius says he refused friends' offers of places to hide.

He was taken at his gardens and held for one night in an officer's house. Next morning the governor told him that the emperors had ordered him to perform the rites. "I will not," Cyprian answered, and the sentence called him an enemy of the Roman gods, whose blood would make the law hold, and ordered him put to the sword. "Thanks be to God," he said. In the field of Sextus he covered his eyes with his own hand, and a presbyter and a subdeacon, both named Julian, tied the cloth when he could not. He died there on the fourteenth of September, 258.

From the 310s a rival church in Africa, the Donatists, baptized again all who came over to it, and they quoted Cyprian's letters and council to defend it. More than a century after his death, Augustine of Hippo, a later bishop in the same Africa, answered them. Stop citing Cyprian's authority for repeating baptism, he told them, and hold with us to his example of keeping unity. Cyprian held a different view on baptism, Augustine said, yet he never left the church's unity.

Augustine also preached on Cyprian's "birthday", the day of his death, kept as his feast, he said, through all Africa. At the very place where Cyprian was put to death, Augustine said, a table for God's altar now stood. It was called Cyprian's table because there he was offered up, and worshippers now ringed the place where persecutors had once ringed him.
