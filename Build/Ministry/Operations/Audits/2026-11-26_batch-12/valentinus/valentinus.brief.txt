In a letter, Valentinus, who taught at Rome in the middle of the second century, compared the heart to an inn for travelers. It has holes and ruts and is often full of dung, and the men who stay there live filthily and take no care of a place that belongs to others.

At Rome he founded a school, a circle of students around a teacher. By his opponents' accounts, it taught that heavenly powers came forth in pairs from a first pair beyond any name, and that the world came from the fall of one of them. Of the writers near his time, Tertullian alone says he broke with the church. His students carried the school on in two branches, while the church's writers answered it with the faith in one God who made the world. For eighteen centuries his school was known almost only through their books, until writings from these circles were found in Egypt in 1945.

In the same letter he went on: "So it is with the heart, while no one gives it any thought. It is unclean, a home for many demons. But when the Father, who alone is good, visits it, it is made holy, and it shines with light." Those words survive because Clement of Alexandria quoted them to argue against him, and almost everything known of Valentinus comes from men who wrote against him.

Irenaeus says Valentinus came to Rome under one bishop and stayed until the time of a third. Tertullian, writing at Carthage early in the next century, tells why he left. "Valentinus had hoped to be made a bishop, because he had the talent and the gift of speech for it. But another man won the place, by the claim his suffering for the faith gave him. Angry at this, he broke away from the church of the true rule." By the true rule Tertullian meant that faith in one God, the maker of the world. He puts the break down to ambition, and to the wish for revenge that men who want first place feel. In another book he gives a second reason, a restless curiosity that spread to other believers, for which Valentinus was put out of the church at Rome more than once.

In Irenaeus's account, a pair beyond any name, Unspeakable and Silence, gave rise to Father and Truth, then to Word and Life, and Man and Church, and to more powers after them. One of the last broke away and fell, and from its fall came the world below and a lesser maker who made it. Tertullian says Valentinus held these powers inside God, as his thoughts, feelings and movements.

In a sermon Clement also keeps, Valentinus told his hearers they had come into death only to use it up and end it. "You are immortal from the beginning, and children of eternal life. You wanted death shared out to you, to spend it and squander it, so that death might die in you and through you." Clement answers that this makes some people saved by nature, by what they were born as, a different race come down from above. Then the commandments would be useless, he says, and salvation would come without the Savior.

Tertullian says Valentinus's follower Ptolemy made the powers into beings outside God. Hippolytus says the school split into an Italian branch, Ptolemy's, and an Eastern one.

Irenaeus wrote five books against them, aimed above all at the school of Ptolemy, which he calls a bud of Valentinus's. Tertullian wrote a book against the Valentinians, and Hippolytus a long book answering their teaching.

So far had his students moved from him, Tertullian says, that Valentinus himself was now nowhere. Yet there were still Valentinians, named after him, and only one man, Axionicus at Antioch, kept his teaching whole.
