## 1. The test (person kit)

- **Who this was:** filled. A Christian teacher and elder (presbyter) at Alexandria in Egypt, late second and early third century.
- **What happened:** filled. He taught there, answered believers who feared Greek philosophy and Valentinus's followers, wrote the Miscellanies, lived through persecution under Severus, carried a bishop's letter, and had died by the time Alexander wrote to Origen.
- **What it was about:** filled. Whether Christians may use Greek philosophy: he said it may have been God's gift to guide the Greeks to Christ, as the Law guided the Hebrews.
- **Why the connection matters:** filled, but thin for this listener. Origen is named three times and never said to be anyone more than a boy pupil who later wrote ten books of Miscellanies. I understood that Clement taught Origen and that Jerome used Clement to defend quoting pagan books. I did not understand why Origen matters, so the Origen link lands as a name. Not blocking: the link comes through acts (pupil, imitated his book). Origen has his own page in the batch.

## 2. The opening

First three sentences: "Clement of Alexandria went looking for teachers, he wrote, and he found them in Greece, in southern Italy and in the East." / "I came upon the last, who was the first in power." / "I tracked him down where he was hidden in Egypt, and I found rest."

- **Who:** the name, and that he sought teachers. Missing: that he is a Christian, and who the "hidden" teacher is. I guessed at first the hidden one might be Christ.
- **Where:** Egypt. Clear.
- **When:** missing. It comes in the next sentence ("late in the second century and into the third").
- **Why there:** the teacher he found. Clear.

Not lost on who or where. Not blocking. One thread left loose: the hidden teacher is never tied to "his teacher" in paragraph 3, so I could not tell whether they are the same man.

## 3. Sentences that fail on one hearing (MINOR)

1. "I came upon the last, who was the first in power." Meaning lost: "first in power" (the greatest of them). The quote is in the opening, where a stumble costs the most.
2. "Both sides of the quarrel Clement stepped into speak only in his telling." Meaning lost: that we know both sides only through Clement's writing. Heard once, "speak only in his telling" did not resolve. (Also counted under 6.)
3. "They left faith to 'us, the simple,' Clement wrote, and claimed knowledge for themselves as men saved by their very nature." Meaning lost: who "us" is. I could not tell that "us, the simple" is the ordinary church, spoken of by Valentinus's followers.
4. "By philosophy he meant no single school, but whatever each Greek school had said well, and what men had cut away and falsified he would never call divine." Meaning lost in the second half: the object comes first, and "cut away" from what was unclear. (Also counted under 6.)
5. "Martyrs, Clement wrote, were burned, impaled and beheaded before their eyes every day." Meaning lost: whose eyes. "Their" seems to point to the martyrs.
6. "Later, writing to Origen, Alexander counted his own teacher and 'the holy Clement,' his master and helper, among the blessed men who had gone on ahead." Meaning lost: whose teacher, whose master, and that "gone on ahead" means they had died. This is the only place Clement's death is told, so the end of his life slipped past me. Closest to losing the thread; MINOR because the following paragraph does not depend on it.

Also noticed, not counted as a failure: Jerome's charge is heard twice, in paragraph 2 ("charged with defiling the church by quoting pagan writers") and again in full in paragraph 8, both introduced by "two centuries later". Kit Part 2 lets the essence reach ahead, so I leave it to the reviewer whether the second telling counts as an event told twice. On hearing it, the last paragraph felt like a repeat at first.

## 8. Attention

- **Pull:** yes. A man travelling the world for teachers and finding rest in Egypt is a good first image. The riddle of the hidden teacher is never resolved, though.
- **First drift:** paragraph 2, last sentence: "Eusebius says the boy Origen was among his pupils, and two centuries later Jerome, charged with defiling the church by quoting pagan writers, pointed to Clement's books." Two new names, a jump of two centuries and a charge arrive in one sentence, before I know who any of them are. It drifts again at paragraph 3's opening sentence about where the record comes from, which is about sources, not about Clement.
- **Back in:** the quarrel (children afraid of masks) and the persecution paragraph hold well.
- **Drifts again:** the Alexander paragraph: three unnamed or half-named men (Alexander's teacher, "his master") and no place.
- **To the end:** yes. The ending is on Jerome, a later writer; it does show what came of Clement, so it may be the story's point, but the last voice I hear is Jerome's, not Clement's.
