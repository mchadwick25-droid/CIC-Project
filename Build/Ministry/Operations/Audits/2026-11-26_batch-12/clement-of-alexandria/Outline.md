# Outline: Clement of Alexandria (`clement-of-alexandria`)

Batch 12, Part B. Row codes (S, E, T, I, L, D, M) are rows of `Source_Sheet.md` in this folder, each with its file and lines. Files are under `cic/texts/`. ANF2 = `anf02_hermas-tatian-athenagoras-theophilus-clement-alexandria.xml`; EUS = `npnf201_eusebius-church-history-life-of-constantine.xml`; JERL = `jerome_epistulae-1-70-lat_hilberg-csel54-1910.txt`.

- **The essence:** Clement was a teacher and an elder of the church at Alexandria, in Egypt, who answered Christians afraid of Greek philosophy: God had given it to the Greeks to bring them to Christ, as the Law brought the Hebrews. Eusebius says the boy Origen was among his pupils, and two centuries later Jerome pointed to his books when he was charged with defiling the church by quoting pagan writers. (S1, S3, T1, T3, E7, I2)
- **Also told:**
  - The search: he sought teachers in Greece, southern Italy and the East, and the last, hidden in Egypt, gave him rest. (E1-E3; Documented, his own words)
  - The quarrel: on one side Christians who feared philosophy like children fearing masks and wanted "bare faith alone"; on the other, the followers of Valentinus, who left faith to the simple and claimed knowledge for themselves as men saved by nature. Their own words survive only in his account. (T1, T2, T17; Documented as Clement's account)
  - What his book held: notes patched together, Scripture beside the Greek writers, keeping whatever each school said well and never calling divine what men had falsified. (T5, T8, T9; Documented)
  - The cost of the faith around him: martyrs burned and beheaded before their eyes every day, and his rule that one who gives himself up to the judge shares the guilt of his own death. (E11-E13; Documented)
  - The last act the record holds: with Alexander, a bishop in prison, carrying his letter to the church at Antioch. (E14, E15; Documented)
  - Checked against the Library: all held. "Head of the catechetical school" is held only as Eusebius's "had charge … of the catechetical instruction" (S3) and Jerome's "led the theological school" (S4). His leaving Alexandria is not held (D3).

- **Setting:**
  - Time: the late second century into the early third. His first book of Miscellanies counts the years to the death of Commodus, so Eusebius dates the work to the reign of Severus (S6; Commodus died in 192, Widely Accepted). Jerome: he flourished under Severus and his son (S8). No birth or death year is held (E17).
  - Place: Alexandria, in Egypt (S1-S3). Antioch, in Syria, receives the letter he carries (E14). Alexander's prison: no place given.
  - Tradition: "Alexandrian Christianity" (S9).
  - Power: the emperor Severus persecuted the churches (E9); no reason is given in the old sources.
- **Words to explain:**
  - presbyter: "an elder of the church" (S1).
  - catechetical instruction: "the teaching of the faith to learners at Alexandria" (S3, E10). Eusebius's word "school" may stand, credited to him (S5).
  - the Miscellanies (Stromata): his own sense, "notes patched together, passing from one thing to another" (T8); Eusebius adds that it sets Scripture beside the Greek writers (T9).
  - philosophy, in his sense: "whatever each Greek school had said well" (T5).
  - the "guide" in Quote 2 (Wilson's "schoolmaster"): the word Paul uses in his letter to the Galatians for the one who leads a child; Clement's own sense, "the one who leads the children" (T3, T12). Say it inside the setup to Quote 2, never as a line after it.
  - the Exhortation to the Greeks (only if I3 is kept): "a call to the Greeks to leave their idols for Christ" (T11).
- **Shape:** Teacher or thinker. The question he answered: may a Christian learn from Greek philosophy? What he taught: philosophy as the Greeks' guide to Christ. Who learned it: Origen and Alexander as pupils, and Jerome as a later reader. The idea is shown through his own words (Quotes 1 and 2), not a list of books.
- **Sibling:**
  - `origen` (this batch, researched after this outline): tells Origen's own life and teaching. Here one clause, credited to Eusebius: the boy Origen was among his pupils (E7). Alexander's line that he came to know Origen through Pantaenus and Clement (E16), and Jerome's claim that Origen imitated his Miscellanies (I2), are told here as Clement's connection; Origen's story need not repeat them. Quotes used here, closed to the Origen story: Stromata I.1, Stromata I.5, Jerome Letter 70.4's question.
  - `valentinus` (this batch, live beside): tells Valentinus. Here a link only: Clement kept words of Valentinus's letters in his Miscellanies to answer them (T16), and charged his followers with claiming to be saved by nature (T17). No Valentinus quote here; all are barred.

- **Openings:**
  1. His own words (E1-E3, Quote 1). He stored up his notes against old age, he wrote, an image of the words of blessed men he had been allowed to hear. He names teachers in Greece, in southern Italy, and others in the East. Then Quote 1. Leads straight into the essence: the city where that teacher hid became his. Preferred.
  2. The masks (T1). Many Christians, Clement wrote, were frightened of Greek philosophy, as children are frightened of masks, afraid it would lead them astray. A faith that clever speech could undo, he added, should be undone. Use only if Opening 1 is not; Beat 2 then does not tell the masks again.
- **Story beats:**
  1. **The hidden teacher and the city.** Eusebius, writing about a century later, is the source of most of what is told of his life; say so once, inside this sentence. Eusebius thinks the teacher hidden in Egypt was Pantaenus, whom Clement names as his teacher in another book (E4, D1). At Alexandria he was trained in Scripture with Pantaenus and became well known (S2), then took over from him the teaching of the faith to learners (S3). He was a presbyter (S1). Among his pupils, Eusebius says, was Origen, then still a boy (E7). Reason: Clement gives his own, that he found rest (E3). Confidence: Documented as Eusebius's account; the identification is Eusebius's judgment.
  2. **The quarrel.** Many believers, he wrote, feared Greek philosophy as children fear masks, afraid it would lead them astray (T1); some wanted "bare faith alone", like a man who wants grapes without tending the vine (T2, his own words, kept as narration). On the other side stood the followers of Valentinus: they left faith to "us, the simple", he wrote, and claimed knowledge for themselves as men saved by nature (T17). He kept words of Valentinus's own letters in his book in order to answer them (T16; link). Say once, inside a sentence, that the fearful believers' side survives only in his telling. Confidence: Documented as Clement's account.
  3. **His answer.** God is the cause of all good things, he wrote, of Scripture first, and of philosophy by consequence (T3). Then Quote 2, with the "guide" glossed in its setup. By philosophy he meant no one school but whatever each had said well, and what men had cut away and falsified he would never call divine (T5). Knowledge, he held, is never without faith, nor faith without knowledge (T6, narrated). Confidence: Documented.
  4. **The Miscellanies.** He called his book notes patched together, passing from one thing to another (T8). Eusebius says it sets Scripture beside the Greek writers, quoting them wherever they seemed useful, and answers false teachers (T9). It counts the years to an emperor's death, so Eusebius dates it to the reign of Severus (S6). Confidence: Documented.
  5. **Martyrs every day.** Under Severus the churches were persecuted, and believers from all Egypt were brought to Alexandria (E9). Clement wrote that martyrs were burned, impaled and beheaded before their eyes every day (E11). Their enemies said Christians were criminals, justly punished; Clement answered that they were punished unjustly, for doing right (E13). Christ's word to flee, he taught, was no command to dread death, but whoever gave himself up to the judge shared the guilt of his own death (E12). Do not tie this teaching to his own life. Tell Eusebius's account of the persecution under Severus as Eusebius's, and Clement's "every day" as Clement's words of his own time; do not say Clement was describing that persecution. Confidence: Documented.
  6. **With Alexander in prison.** Alexander, a bishop, wrote from prison to the church at Antioch, glad that it had a new bishop, and sent the letter by Clement, "the blessed presbyter", who had strengthened and built up the church where Alexander was (E14, E15; narrate; the phrase "the blessed presbyter" may stand as two words of Alexander's in the narration). No reason for the prison and no place is given: tell it as given. Confidence: Documented, a contemporary.
  7. **Gone ahead.** Later, writing to Origen, Alexander counted Pantaenus and "the holy Clement", his master and helper, among the blessed men who had gone on ahead; through them, he wrote, he had come to know Origen (E16; narrate). This is the last word of him; no death year or place is held (E17). Confidence: Documented. (Used as the ending in Ending 2.)
- **What came of it** (ranked; about a quarter of the story):
  1. **His pupils.** Origen as a boy, by Eusebius's account (E7), and Alexander, who calls him master and came to know Origen through him (E16). (I1)
  2. **Defended: a Christian may learn from Greek writers.** About two centuries later a critic asked Jerome why he defiled the whiteness of the church with the filth of the heathen by quoting secular books (I2, the charge in its own content). Jerome answered with a roll of Christian writers who had done the same. Among them he named Clement, in his judgment the most learned of all (credited praise), and asked Quote 3 of his books. Origen, Jerome adds, imitated him in ten books of Miscellanies that set Christian and philosophers' views side by side (I2; Jerome's claim, credited). (Ending 1.)
  3. **His Exhortation copied.** In his Preparation for the Gospel, Eusebius copied long passages of Clement's Exhortation to the Greeks on the pagan mysteries, calling him "the admirable Clement" (I3). Told before Item 2, in time order.
  4. Card: Theodoret counts "Clement the Stromateus" among those who fought for the truth against Valentinus and others (I4); Jerome's Chronicle calls him and Pantaenus most eloquent in defending the faith (I6); Photius's mixed verdict on three of his books (I5).
- **Quotes** (3):
  1. "I came upon the last, who was the first in power. I tracked him down where he was hidden in Egypt, and I found rest." Source: Stromata I.1 (E3), ANF2 27489-27492, tr. Wilson: "When I came upon the last (he was the first in power), having tracked him out concealed in Egypt, I found rest." Rendered from the English printed before 1931; Clement's Greek is not held. McGiffert's English confirms the sense (EUS 28751-28758). Job: the opening; his search, in his words, ending where the story's city begins.
  2. "Perhaps philosophy was given to the Greeks directly and in the first place, until the Lord should call the Greeks. For it was a guide to bring the Greek mind to Christ, as the Law brought the Hebrews." Source: Stromata I.5 (T3), ANF2 27890-27893, tr. Wilson: "Perchance, too, philosophy was given to the Greeks directly and primarily, till the Lord should call the Greeks. For this was a schoolmaster to bring "the Hellenic mind," as the law, the Hebrews, "to Christ."" Rendered from the English printed before 1931; Greek not held. "Guide" stands for Wilson's "schoolmaster", whose modern sense misleads: Paul's word (Galatians 3:24) for the one who leads a child, in Clement's own sense "who leads the children" (T12). Job: the essence, his answer in his words.
  3. "What in them is unlearned? Or rather, what in them does not come from the heart of philosophy?" Source: Jerome, Letter 70.4 (I2), rendered from the Latin, JERL 35367-35368: "quid in illis indoctum, immo quid non de media philosophia est?" Clean critical print (Hilberg, 1910); Fremantle's English confirms (`npnf206_jerome-principal-works.xml` 16634-16636). Job: the story's question resolved by a later reader, in the ending.

- **Connection to carry into the story:** What passed from Clement was a way of teaching the faith that used Greek learning, and the people who carried it. Origen was his pupil as a boy (Eusebius, E7). Alexander, his pupil and friend, came to know Origen through Pantaenus and Clement (E16). Two centuries later Jerome, charged with defiling the church by quoting pagan writers, defended himself by Clement's example, and says Origen imitated his Miscellanies (I2). The writer must make plain that Jerome was answering a charge, so the listener hears the quarrel still alive two centuries on; that "imitated" is Jerome's word; and that no old source says Origen learned his way of reading from Clement. Carry it through Beat 1's clause, Beat 7's letter, and Jerome's scene, never a "this matters because" sentence.
- **Ending:**
  1. Jerome's defence (I2, Quote 3). The critic's charge, Jerome's roll of writers, Clement "in my judgment the most learned of all", Quote 3, and Origen imitating him. The story's question, whether a Christian may learn from the Greeks, resolves where it began, in Clement's books. Preferred. Beat 7 is then told in its place before the impact.
  2. Alexander's letter (Beat 7, E16): the blessed men who had gone on ahead, the holy Clement among them, through whom Alexander came to know Origen. Use only if Jerome's defence and Eusebius's copying are carried as a reach ahead in the essence; Quote 3 then goes to the card.
- **Links:** Origen: taught him (Eusebius). Valentinus: quoted his letters to answer them. Alexandrian Christianity: his home. Jerome: praised him and defended Greek learning by his example. Eusebius of Caesarea: wrote of his life; copied his Exhortation. Theodoret of Cyrrhus: counted him among those who fought false teaching (card). Photius of Constantinople: read and judged three of his books (card). Tatian: Clement names Tatian's Discourse to the Greeks, as Eusebius reports (card).
