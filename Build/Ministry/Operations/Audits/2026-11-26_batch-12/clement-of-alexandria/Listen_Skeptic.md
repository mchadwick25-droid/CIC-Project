## 1. The kind kit's test

- **Who:** filled. A Christian teacher and presbyter at Alexandria, late in the second century and into the third.
- **What happened:** filled thinly. I hear that he taught, answered two camps, lived through a persecution, and carried a bishop's letter. The life has no clear shape: I never learn how his teaching there ended, or whether he stayed. I cannot tell where the Alexander paragraph fits in his life.
- **What it was about:** filled. Greek philosophy as God's "guide" bringing the Greeks to Christ, as the Law brought the Hebrews. I could explain this after one hearing.
- **Why the connection matters:** filled thinly. Origen as his pupil (credited to Eusebius) and Jerome's defense come through. The Alexander paragraph (S24 to S26) is offered as a connection, but I cannot say what it adds. "Through them, he wrote, he had come to know Origen" leaves me guessing what passed from Clement to Origen through Alexander.

## 2. The opening (S1 to S3)

- **Who:** Clement, named at once. Good. But after three sentences I do not know he was a Christian, or what kind of teacher he went looking for. That comes in S5 ("presbyter, an elder of the church").
- **Where:** Egypt, S3; the city, Alexandria, comes in S4. Close enough.
- **When:** missing after three sentences; "late in the second century and into the third" comes in S4, at once after. Not blocking.
- **Why there:** half missing. "I tracked him down where he was hidden in Egypt" gives a reason for coming to Alexandria, but the hidden teacher is never named or explained. I cannot tell whether he means a man or Christ. The listener is not lost about who or where, so this is not blocking, but see S2 below.

## 3. Sentences that fail on one hearing

1. S2: "I came upon the last, who was the first in power." Meaning lost: the last of what, the first in what power? The quote has no plain sense beside it (Part 1 allows one). MINOR. Change: add the plain sense in the narrator's voice right after, from the outline, for example "the last teacher he found, and the greatest", if the outline holds that reading.
2. S9: "Clement was trained in the Scriptures at Alexandria under his teacher and then took over from him the teaching of the faith to learners there." Meaning lost: which teacher? The brief has three unnamed teachers: the "hidden" teacher (S2 to S3), "his teacher" (S9) and Alexander's "own teacher" (S25). I could not tell whether they are one man, two or three. This loses the thread of who taught whom, which is the story's connection. BLOCKING. Change: name the teacher once, from the outline, where he first appears, and say plainly in S3 or S9 whether he is the hidden teacher. If the outline does not let us say they are the same man, give each by a distinct role.
3. S10: "Both sides of the quarrel Clement stepped into speak only in his telling." Meaning lost on one hearing: "speak only in his telling" is abstract. I had to replay it to see it means "we know both sides only from Clement." MINOR here; also counted under item 6b.
4. S18: "...and what men had cut away and falsified he would never call divine." Meaning lost: cut away from what? The inverted order and the missing object lose it. MINOR. Change: "and he would never call divine the parts that men had cut away and falsified," with the outline's object for "cut away" if it gives one.
5. S21: "Martyrs, Clement wrote, were burned, impaled and beheaded before their eyes every day." Meaning lost: whose eyes? No group has been named in S20 except "the churches." MINOR. Change: "before the eyes of Clement's own readers every day," or the outline's wording for whose eyes.

## 4. Bare statements (Part 1, Explain it)

1. S12: "On the other side stood the followers of Valentinus, who had taught under the emperor Hadrian, by Clement's reckoning." Valentinus is named with a date but never said to be what he was (a teacher who called himself Christian? his own school?). S13 tells what his followers claimed, but not who he was or why Christians in Alexandria dealt with his followers. BLOCKING. Change: say in plain words who Valentinus was, from the outline, in place of the Hadrian clause or alongside it.
2. S24: "Alexander, a bishop, wrote from prison to the church at Antioch and sent the letter by Clement, 'the blessed presbyter.'" An imprisonment is told with no reason, and no city for his see. I assumed the Severus persecution, but the story does not say so. Part 1 asks why for each punishment where the old sources give it. BLOCKING if the outline holds a reason; if the sources give only the act, it stands as told and this falls away. Change: add the reason and Alexander's place from the outline, in a clause.

## 5. Citations read aloud

The craft check lists two.

1. S20: "Eusebius writes that under the emperor Severus the churches were persecuted." Nothing in the story suggests the old sources differ on a persecution under Severus. The credit reads as a footnote. MINOR. Narrator's voice: "Under the emperor Severus the churches were persecuted."
2. S7: "Eusebius says the boy Origen was among his pupils..." The batch header requires this to be told as Eusebius says it. Called for; not a finding.

Also heard but not counted, because they credit Clement as a speaker in his own story: "he wrote" or "Clement wrote" seven times (S1, S6, S11, S13, S21, S26 for Alexander). Together with "in his telling," "by Clement's reckoning" and "in his judgment," the brief often sounds like a report about the record rather than a story told from it (Part 1, Craft, first line). This is a texture note for the reviser, not a count.

## 6. Tells Part 1 names

A "not X but Y" turn, and the two halves do not join on one hearing. BLOCKING. Change: give his teaching as positive claims, in the outline's wording, for example "He taught that Christ's word to flee when persecuted was meant to be obeyed, and that anyone who gave himself up to the judge shared the guilt of his own death," if the outline holds that sense; the trust reader checks it.

## 8. Pull and attention

- **Pull:** yes. The search for a hidden teacher across Greece, Italy and the East is a real hook, and the essence comes by about the 100th word.
- **First drift:** S8 to S9. After the essence has reached forward to Origen and Jerome, the story stops for a sourcing note and then steps back to an unnamed teacher. I lost track of whether this was the hidden teacher. It is the same cause as 3.2 and 6.4.
- **Second drift:** S24 to S26, the Alexander paragraph. Three men, two of them unnamed ("his own teacher"), and a letter whose point for Clement is not said.
- **Would I listen to the end:** yes. The philosophy paragraph (S15 to S19) and the persecution paragraph hold well. The Jerome ending is strong if it is heard only once.
- **Ending:** it closes on Jerome's words about Clement's books. Part 1 allows a later writer only when that is the story's point; here the point is what Clement's books were. I note it and do not count it.
