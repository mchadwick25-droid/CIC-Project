Clement of Alexandria went looking for teachers, he wrote, and he found them in Greece, in southern Italy and in the East. "I came upon the last, who was the first in power. I tracked him down where he was hidden in Egypt, and I found rest."

That hidden teacher's city, Alexandria in Egypt, became Clement's own, late in the second century and into the third. There he taught, and he served as a presbyter, an elder of the church. He answered Christians who, he wrote, feared Greek philosophy, and his answer was that it might have been God's gift to lead the Greeks to Christ. Eusebius says the boy Origen was among his pupils, and two centuries later Jerome, charged with defiling the church by quoting pagan writers, pointed to Clement's books.

Most of what is told of his life comes from Eusebius of Caesarea, who wrote about a century later. Clement was trained in the Scriptures at Alexandria under his teacher and then took over from him the teaching of the faith to learners there.

Both sides of the quarrel Clement stepped into speak only in his telling. Many believers, he wrote, feared philosophy as children fear masks, afraid it would lead them astray. On the other side stood the followers of Valentinus, who had taught under the emperor Hadrian, by Clement's reckoning. They left faith to "us, the simple," Clement wrote, and claimed knowledge for themselves as men saved by their very nature. He copied words from Valentinus's own letters into his book in order to answer them.

For his own answer he borrowed a word from Paul's letter to the Galatians, the guide who leads a child, and gave it to philosophy. "Perhaps philosophy too was given to the Greeks directly and in the first place, until the Lord should call the Greeks. For it was a guide to bring the Greek mind to Christ, as the Law brought the Hebrews." By philosophy he meant no single school, but whatever each Greek school had said well, and what men had cut away and falsified he would never call divine. He called his book the Miscellanies: notes, he said, patched together, passing from one thing to another.

Eusebius writes that under the emperor Severus the churches were persecuted. Martyrs, Clement wrote, were burned, impaled and beheaded before their eyes every day. Their enemies said that Christians were criminals, justly punished, and Clement answered that they suffered unjustly, for doing right. Christ's word to flee when persecuted, he taught, was no command to dread death, but anyone who gave himself up to the judge shared the guilt of his own death.

Alexander, a bishop, wrote from prison to the church at Antioch and sent the letter by Clement, "the blessed presbyter." Later, writing to Origen, Alexander counted his own teacher and "the holy Clement," his master and helper, among the blessed men who had gone on ahead. Through them, he wrote, he had come to know Origen.

About two centuries later a critic asked Jerome why he stained the whiteness of the church with the filth of the pagans by quoting their books. Jerome answered with a roll of Christian writers who had done the same. Among them he named Clement, in his judgment the most learned of all, and he added that Origen imitated him in ten books of Miscellanies that set Christian and philosophers' views side by side. Of Clement's books Jerome asked, "What in them is unlearned? Or rather, what in them does not come from the heart of philosophy?"
