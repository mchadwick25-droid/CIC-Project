Clement of Alexandria went looking for teachers, he wrote, and he found them in Greece, in southern Italy and in the East. The last of them was the first of all in ability. "I came upon the last, who was the first in power. I tracked him down where he was hidden in Egypt, and I found rest."

Alexandria in Egypt became Clement's own city, late in the second century and into the third. Many believers, he wrote, feared Greek philosophy as children fear masks, afraid it would lead them astray. His answer was that it might have been God's gift to lead the Greeks to Christ. About two centuries after Clement, Jerome, charged with defiling the church by quoting pagan writers, pointed to Clement's books.

Eusebius of Caesarea, who wrote about a century later and keeps most of what is told of his life, says Clement was trained in the Scriptures there under his teacher. Then he took over from that teacher the teaching of the faith to learners, and Origen, Eusebius says, was among his pupils. Clement also served as a presbyter, an elder of the church.

His answer took the word Paul uses in his letter to the Galatians for the guide who leads a child, and gave it to philosophy. He set philosophy beside the law that God gave the Hebrews through Moses. "Perhaps philosophy too was given to the Greeks directly and in the first place, until the Lord should call the Greeks. For it was a guide to bring the Greek mind to Christ, as the Law brought the Hebrews." By philosophy he meant whatever each Greek school had said well. But he would never call divine the human reasoning that men had mangled and falsified.

He called his book the Miscellanies: notes, he said, patched together, passing from one thing to another. In it he also answered the followers of Valentinus, a teacher he opposed. They left faith, Clement wrote, to ordinary believers like himself, "us, the simple," and claimed knowledge for themselves as men saved by their very nature. He copied words from Valentinus's own letters into the book in order to answer them.

Eusebius writes that under the Roman emperor Severus the churches were persecuted. Every day, Clement wrote, he and his fellow believers saw martyrs burned, impaled and beheaded before their own eyes. Some asked, he wrote, why Christians were persecuted and put to death if God cared for them. He answered that Christ had foretold it, and that Christians suffered unjustly, for doing right. Christ's word to flee when persecuted, he taught, was meant to keep believers from bringing harm on themselves or on the persecutor, and anyone who gave himself up to the judge shared the guilt of his own death.

Alexander, a bishop who later led the church at Jerusalem, wrote from prison to the church at Antioch and sent the letter by Clement, "the blessed presbyter." Later, writing to Origen, Alexander recalled the blessed men who had gone on ahead, through whom he had come to know Origen. One of them was "the holy Clement," his master and helper.
