## BLOCKING findings

**1. ASSUMPTION ADDED.** "That hidden teacher's city, Alexandria in Egypt, became Clement's own, late in the second century and into the third."
- Clement names only Egypt: "having tracked him out concealed in Egypt, I found rest" (ANF2 27490-27491). Taking the hidden teacher to Alexandria needs Eusebius's judgment that he was Pantaenus: "It seems to me that he alludes to the same person also in the first book of his Stromata" (EUS 28698-28700). The brief cuts that credit (Cut first 2), so the identification now stands in the narrator's voice. The Do not say list warns against exactly this ("Pantaenus, his teacher hidden in Egypt" as fact → "Eusebius thinks…").
- Change: "Alexandria in Egypt became Clement's own city, late in the second century and into the third." Paragraph 3's "under his teacher" then reads as Eusebius's account (S2-S3), with no tie to the hidden teacher.

**2. SOFTENED OR CHANGED.** "Their enemies said that Christians were criminals, justly punished, and Clement answered that they suffered unjustly, for doing right."
- Source (ANF2 38903-38914): "But, say they, if God cares for you, why are you persecuted and put to death? … although we are punished not alone, but along with many. But those, it is said, being malefactors, are righteously punished. Accordingly, they unwillingly bear testimony to our righteousness, we being unjustly punished for righteousness' sake."
- In context, "those … malefactors" are most naturally the many others punished alongside the Christians. The objectors set those criminals apart, and so "unwillingly bear testimony to our righteousness". The text does not clearly have the objectors calling Christians criminals. The objectors' plain charge is the question "if God cares for you, why are you persecuted and put to death?" Clement does not call them "enemies" here either ("say they").
- Change: "Some asked, he wrote, why Christians were persecuted and put to death if God cared for them, and Clement answered that they suffered unjustly, for doing right." This keeps the other side's charge in its own content (Part 1) and Clement's answer as he gives it.

**3. SOFTENED OR CHANGED.** "Later, writing to Origen, Alexander counted his own teacher and "the holy Clement," his master and helper, among the blessed men who had gone on ahead."
- Source (EUS 34343-34355): "those blessed fathers who have trodden the way before us … Pantænus, the truly blessed man and master, and the holy Clement, my master and benefactor". Greek (EUSG 3046-3049): "Πάνταινον, τὸν μακάριον ἀληθῶς καὶ κύριον, καὶ τὸν ἱερὸν Κλήμεντα, κύριόν μου γενόμενον καὶ ὠφελήσαντά με".
- Alexander calls Pantaenus "master" (κύριον), with no "my". He uses the same title for Origen ("my master and brother"), so it does not mean teacher. "His own teacher" turns it into a claim that Pantaenus taught Alexander, which the letter does not make. Eusebius's frame, "among his familiar acquaintances" (EUS 34330-34331), does not make it either.
- Change: "Later, writing to Origen, Alexander counted "the holy Clement," his master and helper, among the blessed men who had gone on ahead." The next sentence becomes: "Through such men, he wrote, he had come to know Origen." Pantaenus stays unnamed (Cut first 2).

**4. LIVE-TEXT ECHO.** "For his own answer he borrowed a word from Paul's letter to the Galatians, the guide who leads a child, and gave it to philosophy."
- Live text: "The word Clement borrowed from Paul meant the household servant…". The outline never uses "borrowed". It gives "the word Paul uses in his letter to the Galatians" and "Paul's word (Galatians 3:24)" (Words to explain; Quote 2 note).
- The sense holds: Wilson marks "to Christ" as Paul's words, with Gal. iii. 24 in the footnote (ANF2 27892-27894), and Clement's own sense of the word is "the Instructor, who leads the children" (T12). Only the wording is the finding.
- Change: "For his own answer he took the word Paul uses in his letter to the Galatians for the guide who leads a child, and gave it to philosophy."

## Notes (not findings)

- "Eusebius writes that under the emperor Severus…" is followed straight away by Clement's "every day". Each is credited separately, as Beat 5 and the Do not say list require, so it holds. Neither sentence says Clement was describing Severus's persecution.
- "Clement was trained in the Scriptures at Alexandria under his teacher". McGiffert prints the Greek as συνασκούμενος, "being trained with him" (EUS 28691-28694). "Under his teacher" is the outline's wording (Beat 1). It is a fair compression, given Eusebius's report that Clement names Pantaenus as his teacher (EUS 28697-28698). Marked SUMMARY HOLDS.
