At the fourth Easter of the persecution, Peter, the bishop of Alexandria, wrote about Christians who had been jailed and tortured until they gave way and offered sacrifice to the gods. Some were already in a third year of mourning their fall. "They did not come to this by deliberate choice. They were betrayed by the weakness of the flesh, since they show the marks of Jesus in their bodies." The marks were the scars of their torture.

Peter led the churches of all Egypt and Libya through the persecution that began in 303. He set out how the church should take back the fallen, weighing what each had suffered and chosen. In those years one of his own bishops, Melitius, broke from him, and in 311 Peter was beheaded.

He had become bishop about 300, less than three years before the emperors ordered the churches pulled down and the Scriptures burned, and bishops of Egypt were thrown into prison.

Those who had given way asked to share communion again: a place in the prayers and in the body and blood of Christ. In a sermon on repentance, Peter gave each a time of fasting and prayer, measured by what they had suffered and chosen. These rules are known as his canons. The tortured would fast forty more days, the days Christ fasted. Those jailed without torture would wait a year more. For those who fell from fear alone, he pointed to the parable of the fig tree, spared one more year to bear fruit. Those who paid to be left alone, or left everything and fled, were not to be blamed. Clergy who fell could share communion again, but never return to their office.

Meanwhile four bishops of Egypt, writing from prison, rebuked Melitius, a bishop of Upper Egypt, for making priests and deacons in churches that were not his. By the law of their fathers, they wrote, no bishop could ordain outside his own church, so that those ordained were known and confusion avoided. He should have waited for the judgment of Peter, whom they called their great bishop and father.

An account kept with that letter says Melitius did not answer. After the four bishops were martyred, he went to Alexandria. There he set aside the priests Peter had appointed to visit the churches, who were then in hiding, and put men of his own in their place. Peter wrote to his people that Melitius was invading his parish and trying to pull its priests from his authority. "Take heed of this. Do not share communion with him until I meet him with some wise and sensible men, and see what he has in mind."

Later writers tell the break otherwise. Athanasius, a later bishop of Alexandria and an enemy of the Melitians, says a council of bishops under Peter removed Melitius for many crimes, among them sacrificing to the gods. Epiphanius of Salamis, writing some sixty years later, says the two were in prison together and split over the fallen. Peter, he says, pleaded to receive them as they repented, while Melitius would wait until the persecution ended and would not restore fallen clergy to their rank.

His end came after an order from the emperors had been posted letting Christians worship again, which the eastern emperor Maximinus would not accept. Eusebius of Caesarea, who lived through those years, wrote of it: "He was seized for no cause at all, without warning, and suddenly and without reason, as if Maximinus had ordered it, he was beheaded." It was 311, the ninth year of the persecution, and many other bishops of Egypt died with him.

The split outlived him, and in 325 the Council of Nicaea let Melitius keep his city but took away his power to ordain. Athanasius remembered Peter as bishop and martyr. His rules were copied into the collections of church law, and in 692 a council set its seal on the canons of the fathers, naming among them those of Peter, archbishop of Alexandria and martyr.
