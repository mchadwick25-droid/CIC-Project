One morning, Justin writes, as he walked in a colonnade, a man with friends came up and greeted him as a philosopher. He had been taught to greet anyone in the cloak philosophers wore, the man said, in case some good came of it. He was Trypho, a Jew who had fled the recent war.

Justin, a Greek-speaking philosopher, became a Christian, kept the cloak and taught at Rome in the middle of the second century. He wrote to the emperor that Christians were punished for their name alone, and that Christ is the Word in whom every people has a share. Later, at Rome, he refused to sacrifice to the gods and was beheaded with six other Christians.

He tells his turn himself, in his Dialogue, his written argument with Trypho that Jesus is the Christ the prophets foretold. To Justin, philosophy was the search for God. Teachers of several schools left him empty, and a follower of Plato gave him a hope, foolish as he later judged it, of soon seeing God. Then, walking alone near the sea, he met a gentle, dignified old man who told him of the prophets, far older than the philosophers, who alone saw the truth and spoke it. Justin never saw him again. "But at once a fire was kindled in my soul. A love took hold of me for the prophets, and for those men who are friends of Christ. As I turned his words over in my mind, I found this philosophy alone to be sure and useful. That is how, and why, I am a philosopher."

His family came from a Roman town in the province of Palestine. Under the emperor Antoninus Pius he wrote a petition, a formal written defense, to the emperor, his sons and the Senate, for people of every nation who were unjustly hated, himself among them. Christians, called godless for refusing the gods, were punished for the name alone. A man who denied it went free and one who confessed it was punished, though justice, Justin says, called for weighing the lives of both. A name, he argued, is neither good nor bad apart from deeds, so judge us by what we do, and punish us if the charges are proved. No reply is recorded.

He also took up what his readers might ask about everyone who lived before Christ, born, by his count, only a hundred and fifty years earlier. His answer was the Word, in Greek logos, which also means reason. "We have been taught that Christ is God's firstborn, and we have pointed out before that he is the Word. Every people of humankind has had a share in him. And those who lived with the Word are Christians, even though they were held to be godless." Among them he named Socrates and Heraclitus of the Greeks, and Abraham and Elijah.

Events at Rome made him write again. A Christian teacher, accused out of a husband's spite, Justin says, was asked by the judge only whether he was a Christian and was led away to punishment, and so was a man who protested that he was punished for the name alone. Meanwhile Crescens, a Cynic philosopher, was calling Christians godless and impious in public, to please the crowd. Justin questioned him and, he says, proved that he knew nothing, and he expected to be plotted against and fixed to the stake, perhaps by Crescens. Tatian, writing at the same time, says Crescens claimed to despise death but feared it so much that he tried to bring death on Justin and on Tatian, because Justin showed the philosophers to be gluttons and cheats.

The account of his trial, by a writer we do not know, says that orders had gone out to force Christians to pour offerings to idols, and so Justin and six others were arrested and brought before Rusticus, the prefect, the city's governor. Told to obey the gods and the emperors, Justin said that obeying Christ deserved no blame. Asked what teaching he followed, the account has him say: "I tried to learn every teaching. But I gave my agreement to the true teachings, those of the Christians, even if they do not please people who hold false beliefs." On his second stay in Rome, he said, he lodged above a bath-house and shared the words of truth with anyone who came. Told to sacrifice, he said no one in his right mind falls from true worship into impiety. Rusticus sentenced all who would not sacrifice or obey the emperor to be scourged and put to death according to the laws. They were beheaded, and believers secretly took the bodies and laid them in a fitting place.

The account was copied and kept, and so were his books. Irenaeus of Lyon quoted his lost book against Marcion, who taught a God greater than the world's maker, to argue that the maker is the Father of Jesus: Justin, he wrote, would have believed no one, even the Lord, who announced any God but our maker. Irenaeus also calls Tatian Justin's hearer. Tertullian named him among those who refuted the founders of rival teachings in their own day, and gave him the name the church kept: Justin, philosopher and martyr.
