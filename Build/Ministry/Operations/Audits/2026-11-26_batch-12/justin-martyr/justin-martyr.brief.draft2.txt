One morning, Justin writes, a stranger in a colonnade greeted him as a philosopher. He greeted anyone in the cloak philosophers wore, the man said, in case some good came of it. He was Trypho, a Jew.

Justin, a Greek-speaking philosopher, became a Christian, kept the cloak and taught at Rome. Writing to the emperor in the middle of the second century, he said Christians were punished for their name alone, and that Christ is the Word, in whom every people has had a share. Later, at Rome, he refused to sacrifice to the gods and was beheaded with six other Christians.

He tells the story himself, in his written argument with Trypho over the prophets. To Justin, philosophy was the search for God. He tried teachers of several schools, and at last a follower of Plato, hoping soon to see God. Then, walking alone near the sea, he met a gentle, dignified old man who told him of the prophets, far older than the philosophers, who alone saw the truth and spoke it. "But at once a fire was kindled in my soul. A love took hold of me for the prophets, and for those men who are friends of Christ. As I turned his words over in my mind, I found this philosophy alone to be sure and useful. That is how, and why, I am a philosopher."

Like other Christians who defended their faith in Greek to the emperors, he sent a petition, a formal written defense, to the emperor Antoninus Pius. Christians, he wrote, were punished for the name alone: a man who denied it went free, while one who confessed it was punished. Yet a name is neither good nor bad apart from the deeds behind it, so he asked that Christians be judged by what they did. They were called godless for refusing the gods.

He also asked what became of everyone who lived before Christ. His answer was the Word, in Greek logos, which also means reason. "We have been taught that Christ is God's firstborn, and we have pointed out before that he is the Word. Every people of humankind has had a share in him. And those who lived with the Word are Christians, even though they were held to be godless."

Then a case at Rome made him write again. By his account, a husband's spite brought a Christian teacher before a judge, who asked only whether he was a Christian, and when he said he was, he was led away to punishment.

A Cynic philosopher, Crescens, was also calling Christians godless in public. Justin says he questioned him and proved he knew nothing, and he expected one of the men he had named, or perhaps Crescens, to plot against him and have him fixed to the stake. Tatian, who lived then, says Crescens tried to bring death on Justin and on Tatian himself, because Justin showed the philosophers to be gluttons and cheats.

Eusebius, writing much later, blames Crescens's plot for Justin's death. The account of his trial, by a writer we do not know, says orders had gone out in town and country to force Christians to pour offerings to idols. So Justin and six others were arrested and brought before Rusticus, the prefect, the city's governor.

In that account, asked what teaching he followed, Justin answered: "I tried to learn every teaching. But I gave my agreement to the true teachings, those of the Christians, even if they do not please people who hold false beliefs." Asked where he gathered his disciples, he said he lodged above a bath-house and shared the words of truth with anyone who came. Told to sacrifice, he said no one in his right mind falls away from true worship into impiety. Rusticus sentenced all who would not sacrifice to the gods or obey the emperor's command to be whipped and put to death according to the laws, and they were beheaded.

His books outlived him, among them books against all the heresies and against Marcion of Pontus, who in Justin's day taught that a higher god stood above the maker of the world. Irenaeus of Lyon later used that book to argue that the world's maker is the Father of Jesus. Tertullian named Justin among those who set out the heresy-founders' teachings and refuted them. The account of his trial was kept too, and Irenaeus says Tatian had heard him.

Tertullian called him Justin, philosopher and martyr.
