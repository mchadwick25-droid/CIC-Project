# Outline: Justin Martyr

Kind: person (`kinds/person.md`). Census id `justin-martyr`, told within `greek-apologists-second-century` ("The Second-Century Greek Apologists"). File letters (R1, R2, AR, ANF1, GEB, KK, EUS, TAT, TERT, HIP, JVI, JCH, EPI, THD, MET, PHO) and the tags S, E, I, D are those of `Source_Sheet.md` in this folder.

- **The heart** (set first, from the census entry and the slate's job, then checked against the Library):
  - **The essence:** Justin was a Greek-speaking philosopher who became a Christian, kept the philosopher's cloak, and taught at Rome. In a petition to the emperor he wrote that Christians were punished for their name alone, and that Christ is the Word in whom every people has had a share; later, at Rome, he refused to sacrifice to the gods and was beheaded with six other Christians. Own words (R1 68-75, 124-126, 1230-1233; AR 428-435), Documented; Rome, the trial and the beheading from the trial account (GEB 1300-1442), Widely Accepted.
  - **Also told:** what he was looking for (God, which he held to be philosophy's work, AR 92-94), how he says he found it (AR 139-180, 399-435; R2 368-370), what he taught at Rome and to whom (GEB 1346-1353, 1386-1388), what the petition asked (R1 93-101, 124-126, 141-145), the one idea, the Word, and the question it answered (R1 1226-1233; R2 332-339), and the stand before the prefect (GEB 1309-1442). Documented as his own account; the trial in the account's own words.
  - **What came of him:** his books were kept and used against rival teachers, from Irenaeus and Tertullian to Eusebius and Theodoret (I1); *defended: the one God who made the world, against Marcion; the church's teaching against the heresies.* His name, philosopher and martyr, and the account of his trial were kept (I2). A hearer, Tatian (I3). Documented.

- **Setting:**
  - Time: the reign of Antoninus Pius, to whom the petition is addressed (R1 68-71), "a hundred and fifty years" after Christ's birth by his own count (R1 1227); Jerome's Chronicle puts the book in Antoninus's fourth year (JCH 42754-42757). The old sources differ, so no year. His death: under Rusticus, prefect of Rome (GEB 1304-1306); the old sources differ on the emperor (D2) and the year rests on modern reckoning. Spoken: "in the middle of the second century", "under the emperor Antoninus Pius", "later".
  - Place: his family were "from Flavia Neapolis in Syria Palaestina" (R1 73-74); he calls the Samaritans "my people" (AR 5691). He taught at Rome on his second stay there (GEB 1349-1350). Spoken: "Rome". His family's town goes to the card (Cut first 14).
  - Tradition: the census's "The Second-Century Greek Apologists". Spoken: "the Christians who wrote defenses of their faith, in Greek, to the emperors".
  - The danger: being a Christian was itself the charge (R1 141-145; R2 136-158; S4).

- **Words to explain:**
  - *The Word (logos):* the Greek word means both "word" and "reason" (ANF1 15686-15688, translator's note). Say it before Quote 2: "the Word, in Greek logos, which also means reason".
  - *The philosopher's cloak:* the dress that marked a philosopher (AR 77-81; EUS 22443-22446, editor's note on the word). Say "the cloak philosophers wore", in the essence (Cut first 13).
  - *Petition, apology:* a formal written defense addressed to the emperor (R1 75, "τὴν προσφώνησιν καὶ ἔντευξιν"). Say "a petition", or "a written defense"; if "apology" is used, gloss it as a defense.
  - *Godless (ἄθεοι):* what Christians were called for refusing the gods (R1 186-188; R2 276-277). Say "godless", never "atheists".
  - *Prefect of Rome:* the city's governor and judge (GEB 1304-1306). Say "the prefect, the city's governor".
  - *Cynic:* a philosopher of a school that, Justin says, made indifference its goal (R2 292-293). Say "a Cynic philosopher".
  - *The schools:* Stoic, Peripatetic, Pythagorean, Platonist (AR 139-169). Say "teachers of four schools of philosophy", naming only the Platonist ("a follower of Plato").
  - *Sacrifice:* to make an offering to the gods (GEB 1422-1423); no gloss needed beyond "offer sacrifice to the gods".

- **Shape:** Teacher or thinker, his death told in its beats. The question: what is true about God (AR 92-94), and, once he was a Christian, why his people were hated for a name (R1 124-126). The impact is in his readers and hearers (I1-I3).

- **Openings:**
  The story opens on the essence (Cut first 13); Opening 1 is left out and Opening 2 is not used, since beat 3 tells the petition.
  1. (Left out, Cut first 13.) He writes that one morning, as he walked in a colonnade, a man with friends came up and said, "Philosopher, greetings!" (AR 72-73). The man explained that he had been taught to greet anyone he saw in that dress, in case some good might come of it (AR 77-82). The man was a Jew, Trypho, who had fled the recent war (AR 87-89). Then the name: Justin, and the essence. Paraphrase the greeting, or keep the two words "Philosopher, greetings" as its sense; it is not one of the three quotes. Leads straight into a philosopher who became a Christian.
  2. The petition's opening: Justin, son of Priscus, of Flavia Neapolis, writes to the emperor, his sons and the Senate on behalf of people of every nation who are unjustly hated and abused, "myself being one of them" (R1 72-75; ANF1 13963-13969). Paraphrase only; do not use the barred wording "in every race of people".

- **Story beats** (after the opening and the essence, in time order):
  1. **The search.** He tells it himself, in his Dialogue (Documented as his account). He went to a Stoic, who taught him nothing about God and said it did not matter; to a Peripatetic, who wanted his fee agreed first, so Justin thought him no philosopher; to a Pythagorean, who sent him away because he had not studied music, astronomy and geometry; and to a follower of Plato, with whom he hoped, "in my stupidity", soon to see God (AR 139-169). Walking alone near the sea, he met an old man, gentle and dignified, who told him of the prophets, men far older than the philosophers who alone saw and spoke the truth; the old man left and Justin never saw him again (AR 175-180, 399-403, 428-429). Then Quote 1. He gives a second reason elsewhere: he had heard Christians slandered, but saw them unafraid of death, and saw that they could not be living in vice (R2 368-370). Trypho's side, at once: his friends laughed, and Trypho said he would have done better to stay with Plato than follow men of no account (AR 443-447). Explain it: philosophy, to Justin, was the search for God (AR 92-94).
  2. **The teacher at Rome.** He kept the philosopher's cloak (AR 72-81); Eusebius, later, says that in a philosopher's guise he preached the divine word and fought for the faith in his writings (EUS 22442, 22455-22456). He taught at Rome (Jerome, later, says he held his discussions there, JVI 52490-52491; the trial account, beat 7; Widely Accepted). Where and how he taught is told once, in beat 7, as his answer to the prefect; not here. Explain it: the cloak, as above.
  3. **The petition.** Under Antoninus Pius he wrote to the emperor, his sons and the Senate, on behalf of people of every nation unjustly hated, himself one of them (R1 68-75). Why: Christians were punished for the name alone. A man who denied the name went free; one who confessed it was punished for confessing, though justice, he says, called for looking into the lives of both (R1 141-145). Justin's answer: a name is neither good nor bad apart from the deeds behind it, so judge us by what we do (R1 124-126); if the charges are proved, punish us (R1 99-101); "you can kill us, but you cannot harm us" (R1 95-96, may be paraphrased). Explain it: the petition, a formal written defense (R1 75); "godless", the name given to Christians who refused the gods (R1 186-188). No reply is recorded. Documented.
  4. **The idea: the Word.** In the same petition he answered a question his readers might raise: if Christ came only a hundred and fifty years ago, what of everyone who lived before him (R1 1226-1230)? Gloss logos first, then Quote 2. After it, the narrator gives his examples: Socrates and Heraclitus among the Greeks, Abraham and Elijah among the peoples who were not Greek (R1 1233-1235). He saw Socrates' death in the same light: Socrates was put to death as godless for trying to lead people away from the demons they honored as gods (R1 172-176). Documented. Explain it: the Word, as above.
  5. **The argument with Trypho.** He wrote up his long argument with Trypho, that the prophets spoke of Jesus (AR 6603-6607, Justin's closing prayer that they come to see that Jesus is the Christ of God; EUS 24330-24332, Eusebius on its use of the prophets); in it he refers back to writing to Caesar about Simon, the magician of the Samaritans (AR 5691-5693), as the First Apology does (R1 711-718), so the Dialogue was written after the petition. It has a hard edge, for Justin tells Trypho the Jews' present sufferings are just (AR 788-791), yet they parted as friends, each praying for the other (AR 6589-6604). Tell the edge and the friendship together. Explain it: the Dialogue, his written argument with a Jew over the prophets (E5).
  6. **He wrote again: the teacher led away, and Crescens.** Something that had just happened at Rome also made him write again, he says (R2 97-100): before a judge at Rome, a Christian teacher was asked only whether he was a Christian and was led away; a man who protested that he had been punished for no crime, only the name, was led away too, and a third after them (R2 136-158; tell by role). In the same writing he says Socrates had been charged with the same things as the Christians, but no one died for Socrates' teaching, while for Christ even craftsmen and quite unlearned people despised fear and death (R2 323-326, 332-339). And a Cynic philosopher, Crescens, was saying in public that Christians were godless and impious, to please the crowd; Justin questioned him and, he says, proved that he knew nothing, and wrote that he expected to be plotted against and fixed to the stake by one of those he had named, "or perhaps by Crescens" (R2 273-285; Documented that he expected it). Tatian, his contemporary, says Crescens, who claimed to despise death, so feared it that he tried to bring death on Justin and on Tatian, because Justin showed the philosophers to be gluttons and cheats (TAT 8105-8111; ANF2 6550-6556). Explain it: the teacher's case from each side held (Justin: a husband's spite and the name, R2 115-139; the court: no reason but the name, R2 154-155); the quarrel from each side held (Crescens's own words are not held). Whether Crescens caused his death: Contested, see Do not say. Told once only.
  7. **The trial and the death.** By the account of his trial, by a writer we do not know, it was a time when orders had gone out in town and country to force Christians to pour offerings to idols, and so Justin and six others were arrested and brought before Rusticus, the prefect of Rome (GEB 1300-1306, the six at 1355-1400; ANF1 29266-29269). The reason is told as the account's words; the account does not name Crescens. Told to obey the gods and submit to the emperors, Justin said obeying Christ's commands deserved no blame (GEB 1309-1313). Asked what teaching he followed: Quote 3 (GEB 1314-1317). Asked where he gathered his disciples, he said he lodged above a bath-house, on his second stay in Rome, and shared the words of truth with anyone who came to him (GEB 1346-1353). Told here only. Of the others, an imperial slave, Euelpistus, said he had gladly heard Justin's words but had the faith from his parents; others too had it from their parents (GEB 1373-1375, 1385-1388). Rusticus mocked him as a man said to be learned, who thought he knew the true teachings (GEB 1402-1405). Told to sacrifice, Justin said no one in his right mind falls away from true worship into impiety (GEB 1422-1425). Rusticus sentenced all who would not sacrifice to the gods or yield to the emperor's command to be scourged and put to death "according to the laws"; they were beheaded, and some believers secretly took the bodies and laid them in a fitting place (GEB 1433-1442; ANF1 29396-29406). Widely Accepted; the words belong to the account. Explain it: the arrest and sentence, in the account's terms (refusing to sacrifice and to obey the emperor's command); the beheading, the penalty "according to the laws".

- **What came of it** (heaviest first, told after beat 7):
  1. **His books were kept and used against rival teachers.** Irenaeus of Lyon, writing after his death, quoted Justin's lost book against Marcion: paraphrase only, that Justin would not have believed the Lord himself had he announced any God but our maker (ANF1 44907-44917; the same passage is paraphrased in `marcion-of-pontus`, so here one clause). Tertullian named him among those who, in the heresy-founders' own day, set out their teachings and refuted them (TERT 9705-9709; ANF3 43159-43163); the name he gives him is kept for the ending. Eusebius, in the early 300s, says many of his works were still in the hands of many Christians (EUS 24332-24333). Theodoret, in the 400s, named him among the church's old teachers from whom he gathered the stories of the old heresies (THD 27072-27075). *Defended: the one God who made the world, against Marcion's other, greater God (R1 729-731, narrated only); the church's teaching against the heresies.*
  2. **The account of his trial was copied and kept** (GEB, KK). One clause. Tertullian's name for him, philosopher and martyr, is told only at the ending.
  3. **His hearer.** Irenaeus says Tatian was his hearer (ANF1 33663-33664; Greek at HIP 17441). One clause; the sibling tells the rest.
  4. **His Word idea.** No old held witness carries it by his name (I5): do not claim a later reception. Card only: a missionary in 1922 made Justin's words on the Word in every writer his own (I5).

- **Connection to carry into the story:** Justin's writings, above all his lost books against Marcion and against all the heresies, passed to the church's later teachers, who used them against rival teaching. What the listener must understand, in order:
  1. What he left: books, kept and read after his death; he had also written against all the heresies and against Marcion (R1 743-745; ANF1 44907-44917).
  2. Who took it up: Irenaeus quoted his book against Marcion to argue that the God who made the world is the Father of Jesus (ANF1 44907-44917); Tertullian named him among the writers against the heresy-founders in their own day (TERT 9705-9709); Theodoret, three centuries on, still named him among his sources against the old heresies (THD 27072-27075).
  3. What he defended: the one God who made the world; his own words against Marcion's "other god, greater than the maker" (R1 729-731) stay narrated, since that line is quoted in `marcion-of-pontus`.
  4. And a hearer: Irenaeus says Tatian heard him (ANF1 33663-33664). One clause, no more; no place or school.
  Carry it through what each later writer did with his books, never a "this mattered because" sentence.
