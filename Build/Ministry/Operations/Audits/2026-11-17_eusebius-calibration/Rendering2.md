**Q1. Church History I.1.1: what he set out to do**
- Rendering 2: My purpose is to write an account of the successions from the holy apostles. I will also cover the times that have passed from our Saviour's day to our own. I will tell the many great events said to have happened in the history of the Church. I will name those who governed and led the Church in the most famous local churches. And I will name those who, in each generation, proclaimed the divine word by speech or in writing.
- Note: "Successions" means the lines of bishops handed down from the apostles. The printed "parishes" (Greek paroikiai) means local church communities led by bishops, not parishes in the modern sense.

**Q2. Church History I.1.3–4: the martyrs of his own day, and the untrodden path**
- Rendering 2: I will also describe the character of those who, at various times, fought for the divine word in the face of blood and torture. I will record the confessions made in our own day. And last, I will tell of the gracious and kindly help our Saviour has given them all. I admit it is beyond me to produce a perfect and complete history. I am the first to take up this subject, and I am trying to travel, as it were, a lonely and untrodden path. I pray to have God as my guide and the power of the Lord as my help. For I cannot find even the bare footprints of those who went this way before me. There are only small fragments. In them, some in one way and some in another have handed down to us accounts of the times they lived in. From far off they raise their voices like torches. They call out as from a high watchtower that all can see. They tell us where to walk, and how to steer our work steadily and safely.
- Note: The printed English has "contended for it"; "it" refers back to the divine word in the sentence before. Greek not held.

**Q3. Church History I.1.5: "like flowers from a meadow"**
- Rendering 2: So we have gathered whatever we think important for this work from what they mention here and there. We have plucked the right passages from ancient writers, like flowers from a meadow. We will try to bring it all together in one historical narrative. We will be content if we keep the memory of the successions from our Saviour's apostles. If not of all of them, then of the most renowned, in the most famous churches, which are still held in honour today.
- Note: Greek not held. "Most renowned" refers to the successors; "still held in honour" refers to the churches.

**Q4. Church History VIII.2.1: "we saw with our own eyes"**
- Rendering 2: All these things were fulfilled in our time. With our own eyes we saw the houses of prayer thrown down from top to bottom, to their very foundations. We saw the inspired and holy Scriptures handed over to the fire in the middle of the market-places. We saw the shepherds of the churches shamefully hiding here and there. Others were captured in disgrace and mocked by their enemies.

**Q5. Church History VIII.2.2–3: what he chose to record, and why**
- Rendering 2: But it is not our task to describe the grim disasters that came on them in the end. Nor is it fitting for us to hand down the memory of their quarrels and misconduct toward each other before the persecution. So we decided to record nothing more about them than what would show the divine judgment to be just. So we have not chosen to mention those who were put to the test by the persecution. Nor will we mention those who were wholly shipwrecked in their salvation and, by their own choice, sank into the depths of the flood. We will add to our general history only what may help, first ourselves, and then those who come after us.
- Note: Greek pepeiramenōn is "those who were tested", which the printed English gives as "shaken"; it may mean those who failed under the test.

**Q6. Church History VIII.7.2: "we were present ourselves" (Tyre)**
- Rendering 2: We ourselves were there when these things happened. We saw the divine power of the one to whom the martyrs bore witness, our Saviour Jesus Christ himself. That power was present and plainly showed itself in the martyrs. For a long time the man-eating beasts did not dare to touch or come near the bodies of those dear to God. They rushed instead at the others who goaded and urged them on from outside. They would not touch the holy contestants at all, though these stood alone and naked. The martyrs waved their hands to draw the beasts toward them, for they had been ordered to do this. Sometimes the beasts did rush at them. But they were driven back, as if by some more divine power, and retreated again.
- Note: The printed "our martyred Saviour" misreads tou martyroumenou sōtēros, "the Saviour to whom witness was borne". Historēsamen here means "we observed", not "we put on record". The sentences about the martyrs waving their hands come from the printed English; the Greek copy skips them.

**Q7. Church History VIII.9.4–5: "being on the spot ourselves" (the Thebaid)**
- Rendering 2: We too were on the spot, and we saw many put to death together on a single day. Some were beheaded, others punished by fire. The killing sword grew blunt, weakened and broke apart. The executioners themselves grew tired and took turns relieving one another. Then we saw the most wonderful eagerness, and the truly divine power and zeal, of those who believed in the Christ of God. As soon as sentence was passed on the first, others ran up one after another to the judge's seat. They confessed themselves Christians. They took no notice of the terrors and the many kinds of torture. They spoke out boldly and without fear for the worship of the God of all. They received the final sentence of death with joy, laughter and gladness. They sang and offered hymns and thanks to the God of all until their very last breath.
- Note: "Historēsamen" means "we observed". The Greek copy ends after "believed in the Christ of God"; the rest follows the printed English.

**Q8. Church History VI.20.1: the library at Aelia (Jerusalem), his source**
- Rendering 2: At that time many learned churchmen flourished. Their letters to one another are still preserved and are easy to find. They have been kept down to our time in the library at Aelia. Alexander, who then led the church there, had furnished that library. From it we ourselves have been able to gather the material for the work in hand.
- Note: Aelia is the Roman name for Jerusalem at this time. The Greek episkeuastheisēi may mean "furnished" or "set up"; the printed English has "established".

**Q9. Church History VI.32.3: the catalogue of Pamphilus's library**
- Rendering 2: Why should we now give an exact list of the man's writings? That would need a separate study. We have already set it down in our account of the life of Pamphilus, a holy martyr of our own time. There we showed how great Pamphilus's zeal for divine things was. And we set out the catalogues of the library he gathered, of the works of Origen and of other church writers. Anyone who wishes can easily learn from these which of Origen's works have reached us. But we must now go on with our history.
- Note: "The man" is Origen. The last two sentences follow the printed English; the Greek copy stops before them.

**Q10. Church History VII.32.25: Pamphilus**
- Rendering 2: In his time we came to know Pamphilus. He was a most eloquent man and a true philosopher in the way he lived. He was thought worthy of the rank of presbyter in the church there. It would be no small task to show what kind of man he was and where he came from. But we have set out the details in a separate work about him. It covers his life and the school he founded. It covers his struggles in many confessions during the persecution, and the martyr's crown he won at the last. Of all who were there, he was the most admirable.
- Note: "In his time" refers to Agapius, then bishop of Caesarea; "there" is Caesarea. The printed "parish" (paroikia) means the local church.

**Q11. Martyrs of Palestine 7.4: "my dearest friend Pamphilus"**
- Rendering 2: Others again, after harsh tortures, he shut up in prison. Among them was Pamphilus, to me the dearest of all my companions. Of the martyrs of our time, he was the most illustrious for every kind of virtue.
- Note: The Greek copy breaks off before "most illustrious"; that word follows the printed English. The Greek says "dearest of all my companions", where the printed English has "dearest friend".

**Q12. Martyrs of Palestine 11.1–2: Pamphilus and the twelve**
- Rendering 2: Now is the time to tell the great and famous spectacle of those who died in martyrdom with Pamphilus, a name three times dear to me. There were twelve of them in all. They were counted worthy of a prophetic, or even an apostolic, gift and number. Their leader was Pamphilus. He alone among them held the rank of presbyter at Caesarea. All through his life he stood out for every virtue. He renounced and looked down on worldly life. He shared his property with the needy and set little store by worldly hopes. He lived the disciplined life of a philosopher. Above all, he outshone everyone in our time in his most genuine devotion to the divine Scriptures. He worked tirelessly at whatever he set out to do. And he was a help to his relatives and to all who came near him.
- Note: "Died in martyrdom" renders teleiōthentōn, literally "made perfect". The printed English drops "prophetic or". "Philosopher" here means one who lives an ascetic life, not an academic thinker.

**Q13. Martyrs of Palestine 4.14–15: the sea at the gates of Caesarea (wonder)**
- Rendering 2: What happened at once after this, if told, will very likely not be believed by those who did not see it. We know that well. Still, reason does not let us fail to hand down the whole truth in our history. To put it plainly, all the people of Caesarea were witnesses of the event. Indeed, people of every age saw this marvel. They had thrown this truly holy and thrice-blessed young man into the bottomless depths of the sea. At once a strange upheaval stirred the sea and all the shore around it. The land and the whole city were shaken by it. At the same moment as this wondrous and sudden upheaval, the sea threw out the divine martyr's body before the city gates. It was as if the sea could not bear it.
- Note: The Greek copy ends at "witnesses of what happened"; the rest follows the printed English. The young man is Apphianus, named earlier in the chapter.

**Q14. Martyrs of Palestine 9.12: the pillars that wept (wonder)**
- Rendering 2: These things went on for many days, and then something extraordinary happened. The air was clear and bright, and the sky was perfectly calm. Then suddenly most of the pillars across the city that held up the public colonnades began to drip drops, like tears. The market-places and streets grew wet, sprinkled with water from I know not where. Yet no drizzle had fallen from the sky. At once the word spread to everyone that the earth had wept in a way beyond words. It could not bear the godless deeds done then. To rebuke the hard and unfeeling nature of human beings, stones and lifeless matter had wept over what had happened. I know well that this account may seem idle talk and a fable to those who come after us. But it will not seem so to those for whom that moment confirmed the truth.
- Note: The Greek says most of the pillars dripped; the printed English has "many drops fell". "These things" are the exposure of the martyrs' unburied bodies, described just before.

**Q15. Church History X.1.2 and X.2.2: the last book, and documents kept as a memorial**
- Rendering 2: (a) With prayers, we now add the tenth volume of the Church History to the ones already completed. We will dedicate it to you, my most holy Paulinus, and hail you as the seal of the whole work. It is fitting that, in a perfect number, we should set here the perfect speech of celebration on the renewal of the churches. (b) The supreme emperors also kept passing laws on behalf of Christians. By these they made God's generosity toward us firmer and greater still, far into the future. Personal letters from the emperor reached the bishops, along with honours and gifts of money. At the right point in this account, it would not be out of place to inscribe their words in this book, as on a sacred stone. They are translated from Latin into Greek. So they may be carried in memory by all who come after us.
- Note: (a) Greek hama de euchais, "with prayers", may mean "in answer to your prayers"; the printed English has "in accordance with thy wishes". Ten counted as a perfect number. (b) "Sacred stone" renders hiera stēlē, an inscribed public slab.

**Q16. Life of Constantine I.10: why he wrote the *Life***
- Rendering 2: Is it not shameful that the memory of Nero, and of certain godless tyrants far worse than he, found tireless writers? Those writers dressed up the stories of worthless deeds in elegant language, and set them down in histories of many volumes. Yet we would stay silent. God himself has granted us to meet an emperor like no other that all of history has recorded. He has thought us worthy to see him, to know him and to keep his company.

**Q17. Life of Constantine I.11: he will write only of the emperor's religious life**
- Rendering 2: So I mean to pass over most of the royal deeds of this thrice-blessed man. I will leave aside his campaigns, his battles and his lines of war. I will leave aside his feats of valour, his victories, his trophies over the enemy and all the triumphs he led. I will leave aside what he settled in peacetime to set public affairs right and for each person's good. I will leave aside his laws and many other imperial labours, fresh in everyone's memory. The aim of this work is to speak and write only of what bears on his life of devotion to God. Even these things are countless. From what has reached us, I will choose the most fitting, and those worth remembering for later generations. I will set them out as briefly as I can.
- Note: The Greek skips part of the list; "many other imperial labours, fresh in everyone's memory" follows the printed English. "Trophies" here are victory monuments.

**Q18. Life of Constantine I.19: "we have ourselves seen" the young Constantine**
- Rendering 2: As he passed from boyhood to young manhood, he was already thought worthy of the highest honour among them. We ourselves saw him like this. He was passing through the province of Palestine with the senior emperor. He stood at that emperor's right hand and was most conspicuous to anyone who wished to see him. Even then he gave signs of a royal spirit.
- Note: The printed "the very earliest period of his youth" loosens the Greek "from child to young man". The senior emperor is usually taken to be Diocletian.

**Q19. Life of Constantine I.28: the vision, as Constantine told it (wonder)**
- Rendering 2: So he called on this God in prayer. He begged and pleaded that God would show him who he was, and stretch out his right hand to help him in what lay ahead. As the emperor prayed and pleaded earnestly, a most extraordinary sign from God appeared to him. If anyone else had told of it, it might not have been easy to accept. But the victorious emperor himself told it to us, the writers of this account, long afterwards. That was when we were thought worthy to know him and to keep his company. He confirmed his account with oaths. So who could hesitate to believe the story? All the more, since the time that followed has borne witness that it was true. He said that around midday, as the day was already turning, he saw it with his own eyes. In the sky itself, above the sun, was a victory sign in the shape of a cross, made of light. Words were joined to it, saying: "By this, conquer." Amazement seized him at the sight. It seized the whole army too, which was following him on a march and saw the wonder.
- Note: The printed "from heaven" is not in the Greek; theosēmia means "a sign from God". "Victory sign" renders tropaion (printed "trophy"), a battlefield monument of victory.

**Q20. Life of Constantine III.11: the opening speech at Nicaea (speaker unnamed)**
- Rendering 2: Then the bishop who held first place in the right-hand row rose. He gave a measured speech, addressing the emperor. In it he offered a hymn of thanks to God Almighty for the emperor. When he too had sat down, silence fell, and all looked steadily at the emperor. He looked round at them all calmly, with bright eyes. Then he gathered his thoughts and, in a quiet and gentle voice, gave the following speech.
- Note: Eusebius does not name the speaker. Later writers name Eusebius himself (Q55) or Eustathius (Q62).

The first is the statement of faith that we put forward. The second is the one they issued with additions to our words. Our own statement was read in the presence of our most devout emperor. It was declared good and beyond objection. It ran like this:

The two printed English copies differ on the reception: Socrates's has "seemed to meet with universal approbation", Athanasius's has "declared to be good and unexceptionable". The Greek does not say who "they" are; Athanasius's printed English adds "[the Fathers]".

- Rendering 2: We received it from the bishops before us, both when we were first taught and when we were baptised. We learned it from the divine Scriptures. We believed it and taught it as a presbyter, and as a bishop too. So now we believe it still, and we set out our faith before you plainly. It is this:
- Note: Greek not located. Athanasius's copy has "the Holy Laver" for baptism and stops before "so now we believe". The bracketed words in Socrates's copy are dropped.

**Q23. The letter: "peace being the object which we set before us"**
- Rendering 2: When they put forward this statement of faith, we did not neglect to examine the exact sense of two phrases. These were "of the substance of the Father" and "of one substance with the Father". Questions and answers followed, and the meaning of these terms was clearly tested. It was agreed that "of the substance" shows the Son is from the Father. But it shows he does not exist as a part of the Father. We too thought it right to agree with this meaning. For godly teaching says the Son is from the Father, but is not a part of his substance. So we ourselves agree with this meaning. We do not refuse even the word homoousios, "of one substance". We keep peace before our eyes as our aim, and we do not want to fall away from the right meaning.
- Note: Ousia is "substance" or "essence"; homoousios is "of one substance". The Greek copy begins mid-passage, so the opening follows Socrates's printed English.

**Q24. The letter: "we resisted even to the last minute"**
- Rendering 2: We have had to send you this, beloved, to show you how carefully we judged our examination and our agreement. We had good reason to resist even to the last hour, as long as the different wording offended us. But we accepted without dispute what no longer troubled us. That was when we examined the sense of the words fairly. Then they seemed to agree with what we ourselves had confessed in the statement of faith we had already put forward.

**Q25. The letter: the paragraph only some copies have**
- Rendering 2: Further, we did not think it absurd to condemn the phrase "Before he was born, he was not". For all confess that the Son of God existed before his birth in the flesh. Indeed, our most devout emperor argued at the time, in a speech, that he existed even in his divine birth, which is before all ages. Even before he was begotten in actuality, he was in the Father in potential, without being begotten. The Father is always Father, just as he is always King and always Saviour. He is all things in potential, and he always remains the same, in the same way.
- Note: Only Athanasius's copy has this paragraph; Socrates's does not. The printed "energy" and "virtue" stand for actuality and potential (energeia, dynamis); "virtue" misleads in modern English. The Greek has "in the Father", where the printed English has "with the Father". The closing phrases describe the Father.

**Q26. Life of Constantine III.61: Constantine's letter to Eusebius on Antioch (CONTEMP document, kept by Eusebius)**
- Rendering 2: The emperor's letter to us, on our refusal of the bishopric of Antioch. "Victor Constantine, Maximus Augustus, to Eusebius. I have read with great pleasure the letter that your Wisdom wrote. I saw that the rule of church discipline has been kept exactly. Hold fast, then, to what is pleasing to God and agrees with the apostles' tradition. Count yourself blessed in this very thing. By the witness of the whole world, so to speak, you have been judged worthy to be bishop of any church. For if everyone longs to have you with them, they add to your good fortune beyond dispute. Yet your Wisdom is resolved to keep the commands of God, the apostolic rule and the rule of the Church. You have done extremely well in refusing the bishopric of the church at Antioch. You have done well to want to stay in the church whose oversight you first received by God's will."
- Note: "Your Wisdom" (printed "your Prudence") is a courtly form of address. The Greek says "I read with great pleasure"; the printed English has "most carefully perused". The printed "a proof of true piety" is not in the Greek. The sentence the Greek copy skips is rendered from the full Greek in Heikel.

**Q27. Life of Constantine III.60: Constantine to the people of Antioch**
- Rendering 2: I confess that when I read your records, I saw how highly they praise Eusebius, bishop of Caesarea. I have long known him well and valued him for his learning and his moderation. I saw that you are strongly attached to him, and wish to claim him as your own.

**Q28. Life of Constantine IV.33: the emperor stood to listen**
- Rendering 2: Another thing, I think, should not be left out of the record. This admirable man did it in our own presence. Once, made bold by his reverence for God, we asked to give a speech about the Saviour's tomb for him to hear. He listened most eagerly. A crowd of listeners stood around him, inside the palace itself. He stood upright and listened with the rest. We begged him to rest on the royal throne that stood nearby. But he would not be persuaded at all. He weighed what we said with close attention, and bore witness to the truth of its teaching about God. Much time passed, and the speech grew long. We wanted to stop, but he would not let us. He urged us to go on to the end. When we begged him to sit, he pressed us in turn. At one point he said it was not right to listen carelessly when teaching about God was being discussed. At another, he said this was good and helpful for him. It is holy, he said, to stand while listening to divine things. When this too came to an end, we went back home and took up our usual work.
- Note: The sentence on weighing the teaching follows the printed English; the Greek copy skips it. The Greek antedysōpei is "he pressed us back" or "he shamed us in turn"; the printed English has "he was displeased". The Saviour's tomb is the Holy Sepulchre in Jerusalem.

**Q29. Life of Constantine IV.36–37: fifty copies of the Scriptures**
- Rendering 2: I have thought it right to instruct your Wisdom to have fifty volumes of the sacred Scriptures written on prepared parchment. You know that providing them and using them is most needed for teaching the Church. They should be easy to read and easy to carry. They should be written by trained copyists who are fully skilled in their craft. Such were the emperor's orders. The work itself followed at once. We sent him the volumes, costly and richly made, in threes and fours.
- Note: "In threes and fours" (trissa kai tetrassa) is uncertain. It may mean volumes sent in sets of three or four, pages laid out in three or four columns, or gatherings of three or four sheets. The Greek verb in the letter is garbled in the OCR.

**Q30. Oration XI.2: "our own province of Palestine"**
- Rendering 2: It is true that everyone praises your noble efforts, throughout the inhabited world, for the daily worship and honour of the Supreme God. As for what you have dedicated in our own home, I mean the province of Palestine, the reason is not plain to everyone. You have dedicated it in this very city, from which the saving Word poured out to all people, as from a spring. Here you have set up thank-offerings to your Guardian and Saviour. You have raised trophies of his victory over death, in houses of prayer and consecrated temples. Around the Saviour's shrine of undying memory, you have built lofty and beautiful works. They are royal works of a royal mind.
- Note: The first sentence follows the printed English; the Greek copy starts after it. The long Greek sentence is split, and its closing clause ("the cause is not plain to all") is moved up. "This city" is Jerusalem. "Shrine" renders martyrion, the church built at the tomb. "Trophies" are victory monuments.

**Q31. Church History VI.33.4: the defence of Origen, written with Pamphilus**
- Rendering 2: The elders of our time hand down many other things about Origen. I think I will leave them out, since they do not belong to this work. Whatever needed to be known about him can be gathered from the defence we wrote for him. We wrote it with Pamphilus, the holy martyr of our time. We worked on it together with care, because of those who like to find fault.
- Note: Presbyteroi can mean "elders" in age or "presbyters" by office.

**Q32. Arius to Eusebius of Nicomedia: "Eusebius, your brother bishop of Cæsarea"**
- Rendering 2: He has driven us out of the city as godless men, because we do not agree with what he preaches in public. He preaches: "God always, the Son always. As the Father, so the Son. The Son exists with God, unbegotten. He is everlasting. God does not come before the Son, either in thought or by any moment of time. Always God, always Son. He is begotten of the unbegotten. The Son is from God himself." Eusebius, your brother bishop of Caesarea, has been condemned. So have Theodotus, Paulinus, Athanasius, Gregory, Aetius and all the bishops of the East. That is because they say that God existed before his Son.
- Note: "He" is Bishop Alexander of Alexandria. This Athanasius is Athanasius of Anazarbus, not Athanasius of Alexandria. "Godless" renders the printed "atheists", a charge of impiety, not unbelief in the modern sense.

**Q33. Eusebius of Nicomedia to Paulinus of Tyre: "the zeal of my lord Eusebius"**
- Rendering 2: The zeal of my lord Eusebius for the truth has reached our ears. So has your silence about it. So, though we rejoiced at my lord Eusebius's zeal, we are grieved at you. For even the silence of a man like you looks like a defeat for our cause.
- Note: "The truth" here is the Arian position as the writer saw it.

**Q34. Athanasius, De Decretis 3: "who had denied the day before, but afterwards subscribed"**
- Rendering 2: And here is something strange indeed. Eusebius of Caesarea in Palestine refused the day before, but afterwards signed. He then sent his church a letter saying that this was the Church's faith and the tradition of the Fathers. He openly confessed that they had been wrong before, and had been rashly fighting against the truth. At the time he was ashamed to use these phrases, and he excused himself to his church in his own way. Yet in his letter he clearly means all this, since he does not reject "one in essence" and "of the essence". And so he got himself into a difficulty. While excusing himself, he went on to attack the Arians for saying "the Son was not before his birth". By this they deny that he existed before his birth in the flesh. Acacius knows this too, though from fear he may pretend otherwise because of the times, and deny the facts. So I have added Eusebius's letter at the end.
- Note: "Refused" renders arnoumenos, "denying" or "refusing"; what he refused is not stated. "They had been wrong" may mean Eusebius's own party. This is Athanasius's hostile reading of the letter in Q21 to Q24.

**Q35. Athanasius, Defence against the Arians 8: the charge of sacrificing**
- Rendering 2: What kind of council of bishops was held then? Was it a gathering that kept to the truth? Was not nearly every one of them our enemy? Did not Eusebius and his party attack us out of zeal for the Arian madness? Did they not stir up the others who thought as they did? Have we not always written against them as holding the views of Arius? Was not Eusebius of Caesarea in Palestine accused of sacrificing, by the confessors who were with us?
- Note: The first Eusebius named is Eusebius of Nicomedia; the council is Tyre in 335. The Greek says only "on a charge of sacrifice"; the printed "to idols" is the translator's addition, though pagan sacrifice is meant.

**Q36. The Egyptian bishops at Tyre (Defence against the Arians 77)**
- Rendering 2: For you know, as we said before, that they are our enemies. And you know why Eusebius of Caesarea has been our enemy since last year.

**Q37. Athanasius, De Synodis 17: the letter to Euphration**
- Rendering 2: And Eusebius of Caesarea in Palestine, writing to Bishop Euphration, did not hesitate to say plainly that Christ is not true God.
- Note: This is Athanasius's summary, not a quotation. Fragments of the letter itself appear in Q66.

**Q38. Athanasius, Letter to the Bishops of Africa 6: "at first an accomplice"**
- Rendering 2: Eusebius knew this. He was bishop of Caesarea, and at first a partner in the Arian heresy. But afterwards he signed at the Council of Nicaea. Then he wrote to his own people, saying this: "We know that certain learned and distinguished bishops and writers, even from early times, used the word 'of one substance' for the Godhead of the Father and the Son."
- Note: "Of one substance" renders the printed "coessential" (homoousios). Greek not located.

**Q39. Epiphanius, Panarion 68: Tyre and Potamon (about forty years after Tyre)**
- Rendering 2: He orders a council to be gathered in Phoenicia, in the city of Tyre. He ordered Eusebius of Caesarea and certain others to sit as judges. These men leaned somewhat toward the vulgar babble of the Arians. The blessed Potamon, mentioned earlier, was zealous for truth and right belief. He spoke freely and never favoured anyone. In the persecution he had been blinded in one eye for the truth. He saw Eusebius sitting as judge, and Athanasius standing. He was overcome with grief and wept, as true men do. He cried out to Eusebius in a loud voice: "Eusebius, do you sit there while Athanasius, an innocent man, is judged by you? Who could bear such things? Tell me, were you not with me in prison during the persecution? I lost an eye for the truth. But you show no maimed part of your body. Nor did you bear witness unto death. You stand here alive, with nothing cut off. How did you get out of prison? Only by promising our persecutors to do the forbidden thing, or by doing it." Hearing this, Eusebius flared up in anger. He stood up and broke up the court, saying: "If you come here and make such charges against us, then your accusers are telling the truth. For if you play the tyrant like this here, how much more in your own country."
- Note: "He" is Constantine. Potamon was an Egyptian bishop. "The forbidden thing" (to athemiton) is left unnamed; sacrifice is implied. The Greek "how did you get out" sentence is a question. It is split here, and its "unless" clause is given as an answer. "Bear witness unto death" renders emartyrēsas, which can also mean "confess". No printed English is held.

**Q41. Jerome, Lives of Illustrious Men 75: Pamphilus and the library at Caesarea (about fifty years after)**

**Q42. Jerome, Lives of Illustrious Men 81: Eusebius**
- Rendering 2: Eusebius, bishop of Caesarea in Palestine, was devoted to the study of the divine Scriptures. With Pamphilus the martyr, he searched the Holy Bible with great care. **Q43. Jerome, Letter 84.2: "the most open champion of the Arian impiety" (about sixty years after)**
- Rendering 2: Apollinaris wrote a most weighty book against Porphyry. Eusebius wrote a fine history of the Church. Yet the first of these taught a halved version of Christ's taking flesh. The second is the most open champion of the Arian heresy.

**Q44. Jerome, Apology against Rufinus I.11: "Am I to be called an Arian because Eusebius ... is an Arian?"**

**Q45. Jerome, Letter 84.10: Eusebius and Pamphilus "one soul"**
- Rendering 2: Eusebius and Pamphilus were in such complete harmony that they seemed to share one soul. One of them even took the other's name. So how could they disagree so deeply on this point? - Note: Jerome is arguing against Rufinus that the Defence of Origen is really Eusebius's work. The anachronisms are part of his point.

**Q46. Rufinus, preface to his Latin *Church History*: why he translated it, and what he changed (about sixty-five years after)**
- Rendering 2: You charge me to translate into Latin the Church History that the most learned Eusebius of Caesarea had written in Greek. Reading it would hold the minds of its hearers fast. As they eagerly sought to know past events, they might somehow forget the evils now taking place. It should be known that the tenth book of this work, in the Greek, had very little history in it. All the rest of it was taken up with speeches praising bishops, which add nothing to knowledge of events. So we left out what seemed superfluous. Whatever history it held, we joined to the ninth book, and we brought Eusebius's account to an end there. We ourselves wrote a tenth and an eleventh book. We drew partly on the traditions of our elders and partly on what our own memory had already grasped.
- Note: "You" is Bishop Chromatius of Aquileia. "The evils now taking place" are generally taken as the Gothic invasion of Italy. Vel in "decimum vel undecimum" means "and" here. No printed English is held.

**Q47. Rufinus, at the start of his own books: "Up to here Eusebius has handed down ..."**
- Rendering 2: Up to here Eusebius has handed down to us the record of what was done in the Church. The rest followed in order down to the present time. We have found it in the writings of our elders, or it has come within our own memory.
- Note: The Latin sentence breaks off in the copy before its main verb, so the second and third sentences are an incomplete thought. No printed English is held.

**Q48. Socrates, Church History I.1: where the next history began (about a hundred years after)**
- Rendering 2: Eusebius Pamphili set out the history of the Church in ten books in all. He ended it in the times of the emperor Constantine, when the persecution Diocletian had begun against the Christians also came to an end. Writing on the life of Constantine, the same author mentions the affairs of Arius only in part. He cared more for the emperor's praises and for the grand language of a festive speech than for an exact account of what happened, as in a speech of praise. We have set out to write what happened in the churches from his time to ours. We will begin our work from what he left out. We will not care for weighty phrasing. We will set down what we found in writing, or heard from those who told what they knew.
- Note: Greek ex hōn ekeinos apelipe can mean "from what he left out" or "from where he left off". The printed English takes the first sense. "As in a speech of praise" renders hōs en enkōmiōi.

**Q49. Socrates, Church History I.8: Eusebius's account used "as an authority"**
- Rendering 2: Such is the testimony on these matters that Eusebius has left us in writing, in his own words. We have rightly used it. Yet Eusebius made the same confession after the closest testing. In fact, he has left out some things on purpose and twisted others. And he has put his own construction on everything. Yet he praises Eusebius Pamphili as a trustworthy witness. He also praises the emperor as able to teach Christian doctrine. But he still brands the faith declared at Nicaea as set out by ignorant men who had no understanding of the matter. So he willingly scorns the words of a man he himself calls a wise and true witness.
- Note: The critic here ("he") is Sabinus, a Macedonian bishop whose collection of council documents Socrates is attacking.

**Q50. Socrates, Church History I.8: "who had held aloof for a short time"**
- Rendering 2: At that time, during the council, Eusebius paused for a little while. He bore the surname Pamphili and held the bishopric of Caesarea in Palestine. He also sent the definition of faith in writing to the people in his charge. He explained the word homoousios, so that no one would suspect him at all because of his pause.

**Q51. Socrates, Church History I.23: Eusebius and Eustathius**
- Rendering 2: Eusebius Pamphili says that, right after the council, Egypt was stirred up by divisions within. But he does not give the reason for this. So he has gained a name for lack of candour. - Note: Sabellius taught that Father, Son and Spirit are three modes of one divine person.

**Q52. Socrates, Church History I.24: the riot at Antioch, and his refusal**
- Rendering 2: When they came to choose a successor, so fierce a quarrel broke out that it threatened the whole city with ruin. The people split into two factions. One fought hard to move Eusebius Pamphili from Caesarea in Palestine to Antioch. The other insisted just as hard that Eustathius be restored. The city's people were caught up in the party spirit of this quarrel among the Christians. Soldiers were drawn up on both sides, ready to fight. Blood would have been shed if God and fear of the emperor had not held back the crowd. The emperor, by his letters, and Eusebius, by refusing the bishopric, calmed the unrest. For this the emperor admired Eusebius greatly. He wrote to praise his wise decision. He congratulated him as one thought worthy to be bishop not of one city only, but of almost the whole world.

**Q53. Socrates, Church History II.21: "Defense of Eusebius Pamphilus"**
- Rendering 2: Some have tried to slander even Eusebius Pamphili himself, as leaning to Arian views in the works he published. So I think it not out of place to say a little about him. First, he was present at the council of Nicaea, which defined the homoousion, and he agreed to it. In the third book of his Life of Constantine he also says this, word for word. "The emperor drove them all toward agreement. In the end, as far as it lay with him, he made them of one mind and one belief on all the disputed points. So the faith of Nicaea won out with one voice." In speaking of the council of Nicaea, then, Eusebius says that all differences were removed and all came to one mind. So what ground is there for assuming he was himself an Arian? The Arians are certainly wrong, too, in thinking he favoured their views. Eusebius uses such words in his work against Marcellus. We have quoted them because of those who have tried to slander and accuse him. Nor can they prove that Eusebius gives the Son of God a beginning of existence. They may find him often using such phrases, but loosely, as a concession. This is all the more so because he followed and admired the works of Origen. Those able to grasp the depth of Origen's writings will see that they everywhere say the Son was begotten of the Father.
- Note: "As far as it lay with him" is in the Greek but not the printed English. "Loosely, as a concession" renders the printed "by accommodation". Homoousion is the doctrine that the Son is of one substance with the Father.

**Q64. Gelasius of Cyzicus: "the most trustworthy" (about a hundred and thirty-five years after)**
- Rendering 2: This much, then, has Eusebius Pamphili left us, the most trustworthy of the ancient church writers. He took on very many struggles and searched things out.
