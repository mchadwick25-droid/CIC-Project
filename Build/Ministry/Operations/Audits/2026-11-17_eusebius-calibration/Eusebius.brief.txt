In Caesarea, a city by the sea in Palestine, a presbyter named Pamphilus gathered a library. It held the works of Origen and of other church writers. His friend Eusebius wrote out the list of its books.

Eusebius served the church at Caesarea, first as a presbyter and then as its bishop. He lived in the years when the emperor Diocletian ordered the churches pulled down, and then in the reign of Constantine. From libraries like this one, he set out to do a new thing.

"My purpose is to write an account of the successions from the holy apostles," he wrote. He added, "I am the first to take up this subject, and I am trying to travel, as it were, a lonely and untrodden path." He picked passages from older writers, he said, "like flowers from a meadow."

Then his own time became part of the story. He wrote, "With our own eyes we saw the houses of prayer thrown down from top to ground, foundations and all." In Upper Egypt he saw many put to death on a single day. Pamphilus was tortured and shut up in prison, and later died as a martyr with eleven others. Eusebius called him "to me at least the dearest of all my companions."

Was Eusebius in prison too? His own works that we hold do not say. A later writer, Epiphanius, tells of a man who had lost an eye in the persecution. At a council, he asked Eusebius how he got out of prison, unless he promised "to do the forbidden thing, or did it." Epiphanius names no source.

When peace came, the bishops met in a great council at Nicaea, with the emperor Constantine present. Eusebius put forward the creed of his own church. The emperor approved it, but asked for the word "of one substance" to be added. The council issued a fuller creed. Eusebius questioned its terms, then signed. He wrote home to explain: "We keep before our eyes the aim of peace, and of not falling away from the right understanding."

The council had met over the teaching of Arius. Athanasius, who wrote against Eusebius, called him "at first an accomplice of the Arian heresy." About a hundred years later the historian Socrates asked, "What ground is there, then, for assuming he was himself an Arian?"

The historians after Eusebius began where he stopped. Theodoret wrote, "I will begin my history where his ends." Socrates began from what Eusebius had left out. He said that in his life of Constantine, Eusebius "cared more for the emperor's praises and the festive grandeur of his words than for setting down exactly what happened." Eusebius had said he would write "only of what bears on his life as one dear to God."

He copied the emperor's letters into his books so that they would be "carried in memory by all who come after us." About four hundred and fifty years later, a council rejected his writings. Yet the same council heard it read that "no work of those older authors escaped his notice."
