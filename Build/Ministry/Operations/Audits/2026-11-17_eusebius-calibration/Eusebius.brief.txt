Early in the fourth century, the church in Caesarea, a city by the sea in Palestine, had a presbyter, or elder, named Pamphilus. He gathered a library of the works of Origen and of other church writers. His friend Eusebius wrote out the list of its books.

Eusebius was a presbyter, and then bishop of Caesarea. From the library at Aelia, in Jerusalem, he gathered material for his history of the church. "My purpose is to write an account of the successions from the holy apostles," he said. He meant the line of leaders in the leading churches, from the apostles to his own day. He wrote, "I am the first to take up this subject, and I am trying to travel, as it were, a lonely and untrodden path." He gathered older writers' words, he said, "like flowers from a meadow."

In the reign of the emperor Diocletian, royal edicts ordered the churches pulled down. Eusebius wrote, "With our own eyes we saw the houses of prayer thrown down from top to ground, foundations and all." Pamphilus was tortured and shut up in prison, and later died as a martyr with eleven others. Eusebius called him "to me at least the dearest of all my companions."

Was Eusebius in prison too? His surviving works do not say. A later writer, Epiphanius, tells of a council at Tyre where Eusebius sat as a judge. A man named Potamon, who had lost an eye in the persecution, accused him. They had been in prison together, Potamon said. How had Eusebius got out, unless he had promised "to do the forbidden thing, or did it?" Epiphanius names no source.

When peace came, the bishops met at Nicaea, before the emperor Constantine. They met chiefly over Arius. In his own letter, Arius says he was driven out of his city for refusing the teaching "God always, the Son always." Arius named Eusebius among those who said that God existed before his Son. Eusebius put forward the creed of his own church. By his own account, the emperor approved it but asked for one added word: the Son is "of one substance" with the Father. The council issued a fuller creed. Eusebius asked how its terms were to be understood, then signed. He wrote home to explain: "We keep before our eyes the aim of peace, and of not falling away from the right understanding."

Athanasius said that Eusebius was "at first an accomplice of the Arian heresy", but afterwards signed. About a hundred years after Eusebius, the historian Socrates asked, "What ground is there, then, for assuming he was himself an Arian?"

His history became the one others built on. Antipater of Bostra, who wrote against him, admitted that "no work of those older authors escaped his notice." The historians after him began where he stopped. Theodoret wrote, "I will begin my history where his ends." Rufinus put the history into Latin, shortened its last book, and added two of his own. He began them: "Up to here Eusebius has handed down to us the memory of what was done in the church."
