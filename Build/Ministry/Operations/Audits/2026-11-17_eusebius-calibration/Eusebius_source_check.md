## Sentence by sentence

**1. In Caesarea, a city by the sea in Palestine, a presbyter named Pamphilus gathered a library.**
SUPPORTED WITH CAUTION.
- Caesarea in Palestine: EUS 42996–42997.
- The sea at the city gates: EUS 46329–46331 and 46445–46451. "A city by the sea" rests on these lines.
- Pamphilus a presbyter: EUS 43040–43041 (VII.32.25, "presbyter in that parish"); SCH 24800–24802 (MP 11.2, the only presbyter at Caesarea).
- "Gathered": SCH 5155, τῆς συναχθείσης αὐτῷ … βιβλιοθήκης ("the library collected by him").
- Caution: Eusebius does not say where the library was (EUS 36889–36892; SCH 5151–5156). The library is placed at Caesarea only by Jerome, *Lives* 75 (NP3 40959–40961), a later witness. The sentence is sound, but "in Caesarea" rests on Jerome.

**2. It held the works of Origen and of other church writers.**
SUPPORTED. SCH 5155–5156: τῶν τε Ὠριγένους καὶ τῶν ἄλλων ἐκκλησιαστικῶν συγγραφέων βιβλιοθήκης. Printed English: EUS 36890–36891.

**3. His friend Eusebius wrote out the list of its books.**
SUPPORTED.
- The list: SCH 5153–5156 says Eusebius set out its catalogues (τοὺς πίνακας) in his account of the life of Pamphilus (EUS 36886–36891). That work is lost.
- "Friend": SCH 24044 (ἑταίρων, "companions"); Jerome, "friendship" (*Lives* 81).

**4. Eusebius served the church at Caesarea, first as a presbyter and then as its bishop.**
SUPPORTED WITH CAUTION.
- Presbyter, then bishop: his letter on Nicaea, NP4 19595–19598 ("in the presbytery, and in the Episcopate itself"); NP2 2998–3003.
- Bishop of Caesarea: NP2 2972–2973; Constantine's letter, HK 12895–12896 ("the church whose oversight you received at the first").
- Caution: the letter does not say where he served as a presbyter. That it was Caesarea is a strong inference, since the creed came "from the Bishops who preceded us", but no held text states it. The sheet's S7 makes the same inference. The source fully supports "He was a presbyter, and then bishop of Caesarea."

**5. He lived in the years when the emperor Diocletian ordered the churches pulled down, and then in the reign of Constantine.**
SUPPORTED WITH CAUTION.
- The edict: EUS 43460–43489 and SCH 13716–13720. Eusebius dates it to "the nineteenth year of the reign of Diocletian", when "royal edicts" (βασιλικὰ γράμματα) commanded the churches to be levelled.
- Caution: Eusebius dates the edict by Diocletian's reign. He does not say Diocletian issued it. Socrates names him: "the persecution that Diocletian began" (NP2 1861–1863; HUS 3549–3555).
- The sentence holds on Socrates's witness, or as "in the reign of Diocletian, when imperial orders had the churches pulled down."

**6. From libraries like this one, he set out to do a new thing.**
SUPPORTED WITH CAUTION.
- The only library he names as a source for the work is the one at Aelia (Jerusalem): "From that library we too have been able to gather the material for the work in hand" (EUS 35438–35443; SCH 3959–3961).
- He does not say he drew the *History* from Pamphilus's library. That claim is in McGiffert's note (card only).
- "A new thing" turns his own claim into the narrator's statement. "I am the first" is his claim (EUS 6937–6938). Sozomen names earlier compilers (NP2 26359–26376, per I1).
- Fix the source requires: name the Aelia library as his stated source, or keep "libraries like this one" but let "the first" stay his claim ("he set out to do what he called a new thing").

**7. "My purpose is to write an account of the successions from the holy apostles," he wrote.**
SUPPORTED. EUS 6878–6879 (printed: "successions of the holy apostles"). The brief uses Q1's settled "from".

**8. He added, "I am the first to take up this subject, and I am trying to travel, as it were, a lonely and untrodden path."**
SUPPORTED. EUS 6935–6938 (I.1.4). "Added" is loose, since the sentence comes two sections later, but it is not wrong.

**9. He picked passages from older writers, he said, "like flowers from a meadow."**
SUPPORTED. EUS 6975 onward (I.1.5: "having plucked like flowers from a meadow the appropriate passages from ancient writers").

**10. Then his own time became part of the story.**
SUPPORTED. EUS 6896–6898 ("the confessions which have been made in our own days"); HE VIII Introduction (EUS 43296–43299).

**11. He wrote, "With our own eyes we saw the houses of prayer thrown down from top to ground, foundations and all."**
SUPPORTED. EUS 43431–43433; SCH 13615–13618.

**12. In Upper Egypt he saw many put to death on a single day.**
SUPPORTED.
- EUS 44268–44271: "We, also being on the spot ourselves, have observed large crowds in one day; some suffering decapitation, others torture by fire".
- SCH 14540–14542: ἱστορήσαμεν … ἐπὶ τῶν τόπων γενόμενοι πλείους ἀθρόως κατὰ μίαν ἡμέραν.
- The chapter is "Those in Thebais" (EUS 44236). "Upper Egypt" is a plain geographic gloss for the Thebaid. It comes from the editor's note (card), and it is accurate.

**13. Pamphilus was tortured and shut up in prison, and later died as a martyr with eleven others.**
SUPPORTED.
- Tortured, then imprisoned: EUS 46690–46712 (MP 7.4–6: "after severe tortures, he cast into prison. Among these was … Pamphilus"; Urbanus "put him also with the confessors in prison"); SCH 24043–24044.
- The martyrs: EUS 47066–47070 and SCH 24600–24802. "Twelve in all", with Pamphilus their leader, makes eleven others.

**14. Eusebius called him "to me at least the dearest of all my companions."**
SUPPORTED. SCH 24044; EUS 46694–46695 (printed: "my dearest friend").

**15. Was Eusebius in prison too?**
SUPPORTED. The question is raised by Epiphanius (OEH 44591–44593) and by Photius's "in the company of Eusebius" (PHO 11437–11439). It is contested (D4).

**16. His own works that we hold do not say.**
SUPPORTED.
- A search of EUS for first-person imprisonment finds none in his own text.
- McGiffert's prolegomena (card, EUS 1483–1490) also speaks of "Eusebius' own silence".
- "That we hold" carries the right hedge.

**17. A later writer, Epiphanius, tells of a man who had lost an eye in the persecution.**
SUPPORTED. OEH 44582–44583: τὸν ὀφθαλμὸν γὰρ ἐν τῷ διωγμῷ διὰ τὴν ἀλήθειαν ἐτυφλώθη. Also 44594 (ὀφθαλμὸν ἀπεβαλόμην). Petavius's Latin, 44665–44666: "alterum in persecutione oculum amiserat".

**18. At a council, he asked Eusebius how he got out of prison, unless he promised "to do the forbidden thing, or did it."**
SUPPORTED WITH CAUTION.
- Who asked: the man who lost an eye (Potamon, unnamed in the brief).
- Where: the council at Tyre (OEH 44476–44478, "a council … in Tyre"). There Eusebius was sitting as judge and Athanasius was standing trial (44583–44585). "At a council" is accurate.
- The question: OEH 44596–44599, Πῶς ἀνεχώρησας ἀπὸ τῆς φυλακῆς, εἰ μὴ ὅτι ὑπέσχου … τὸ ἀθέμιτον πρᾶξαι, ἢ ἔπραξας; Latin 44673–44674.
- Caution 1, the end mark: the quote ends "." where the source and Q39 have "?". The words are exact, and the indirect form is fair. Restore "?" or move the full stop outside the quotation marks to meet the character-for-character rule.
- Caution 2, a dropped question: Potamon first asks "were you not with me in prison?" (44592–44593). The brief drops it, so "how he got out of prison" carries his assumption without saying so. That is acceptable in his reported voice.

**19. Epiphanius names no source.**
SUPPORTED. OEH 44440–44610 gives no source for the Potamon scene. The only hearsay marker nearby is ὡς ἔχει ὁ λόγος ("as the story goes", about 44449), and it belongs to the earlier lamp episode. It names no one.

**20. When peace came, the bishops met in a great council at Nicaea, with the emperor Constantine present.**
SUPPORTED.
- "Great council gathered at Nicaea": NP4 19561–19562.
- The emperor present: NP4 19568 ("in the presence of our most pious Emperor"); NP2 3046–3048.
- The persecution's end comes before it (NP2 1861–1863). "When peace came" compresses the time between: Nicaea fell in Constantine's twentieth year (EUS 67414–67416), but nothing in the sentence is false.

**21. Eusebius put forward the creed of his own church.**
SUPPORTED. NP4 19564–19566 ("the formula of faith presented by ourselves"); NP4 19595–19598 (received from "the Bishops who preceded us", taught "in the presbytery, and in the Episcopate").

**22. The emperor approved it, but asked for the word "of one substance" to be added.**
SUPPORTED WITH CAUTION.
- NP2 3046–3052: "our most pious emperor himself was the first to admit that they were perfectly correct … exhorting all present … with the insertion, however, of that single word *homoousios*". NP4 19568–19569 has "declared to be good and unexceptionable".
- Caution: this is Eusebius's own account. Robertson's note (card, NP4 19517–19523) says it is "not altogether easy to reconcile" with Athanasius's account of how the word came in. The sheet flags this at D2(e), but those Athanasius chapters were not read (N12).
- Fix the source requires: attribute it ("By his own account, the emperor approved it…").

**23. The council issued a fuller creed.**
SUPPORTED. NP4 19566–19567 ("the second, which [the Fathers] put forth with some additions to our words"); NP2 2988–2990.

**24. Eusebius questioned its terms, then signed.**
SUPPORTED.
- He examined the terms: NP2 3087–3092; HUS 7290–7296.
- He resisted, then accepted: NP2 3149–3151 ("resisted to the last moment … received them without dispute").
- He signed: Socrates, NP2 2971–2975 ("at length acquiesced … and subscribed it"); Sozomen, Q56.

**25. He wrote home to explain: "We keep before our eyes the aim of peace, and of not falling away from the right understanding."**
SUPPORTED. HUS 7405–7407: σκοποῦ πρὸ ὀφθαλμῶν ἡμῖν κειμένου, καὶ τοῦ μὴ τῆς ὀρθῆς ἐκπεσεῖν διανοίας. NP2 3101–3103. In the source this clause gives his reason for not refusing "of one substance". The quote is a whole sentence of Q23.

**26. The council had met over the teaching of Arius.**
SUPPORTED WITH CAUTION.
- Socrates I.8 gives two causes: the strife over Arius and the Easter dispute. "When, therefore, the emperor beheld the Church agitated on account of both of these causes, he convoked a General Council" (NP2 2683–2684).
- Photius, codex 127: the "dispute" between Arius and Alexander led the emperor to call the synod (PHO 11794–11802).
- Fix the source requires: "had met chiefly over the teaching of Arius", or say there was also a dispute over the date of Easter.

**27. Athanasius, who wrote against Eusebius, called him "at first an accomplice of the Arian heresy."**
SUPPORTED WITH CAUTION.
- The quote: NP4 60746–60749, exact.
- "Wrote against" holds only in this sense: Athanasius wrote hostile passages about him (*De Decretis* 3, NP4 26780–26794; *De Synodis* 17, NP4 57219–57222; *Defence* 8, NP4 22301–22309). No held work of Athanasius is written against Eusebius.
- In this passage Athanasius uses Eusebius as a witness. The same sentence goes on: "but afterwards, having signed at the Council of Nicæa, wrote to his own people affirming" the old use of "of one substance" (NP4 60749–60753). The quote stops at that turn.
- Fix the source requires: "who was hostile to him", and, if room allows, the rest of the sentence ("…but afterwards signed at Nicaea").

**28. About a hundred years later the historian Socrates asked, "What ground is there, then, for assuming he was himself an Arian?"**
SUPPORTED WITH CAUTION.
- The quote: NP2 6930–6933; HUS 19106–19121, *Defence of Eusebius Pamphilus*.
- "About a hundred years" rests on the file-header dates (C1): Socrates "c. 380–after 439", Eusebius "c. 260–339".
- Caution: the sentence comes right after Athanasius, so "later" can be heard as "after Athanasius". That would not be true.
- Fix the source requires: "About a hundred years after Eusebius".

**29. The historians after Eusebius began where he stopped.**
SUPPORTED. Theodoret (NP3 5219–5223); Socrates (NP2 1861–1862, "closed it with that period of the emperor Constantine"); Rufinus's continuation and Jerome's *Chronicle* (I1).

**30. Theodoret wrote, "I will begin my history where his ends."**
SUPPORTED. NP3 5222–5224 (printed: "I shall begin my history from the period at which his terminates").

**31. Socrates began from what Eusebius had left out.**
SUPPORTED WITH CAUTION.
- NP2 1868–1869: "we begin with the narration of the particulars which he has left out". This follows the settled Q48 reading.
- Renderings_Compared marks ἐξ ὧν ἐκεῖνος ἀπέλιπε "MEANING DIFFERS": it may also mean "from where he left off".
- No change is required. The sentence rests on the printed English and the settled reading, and sentence 29 already covers the other sense.

**32. He said that in his life of Constantine, Eusebius "cared more for the emperor's praises and the festive grandeur of his words than for setting down exactly what happened."**
SUPPORTED. NP2 1863–1866; HUS 3555 onward. The quote is unbroken.

**33. Eusebius had said he would write "only of what bears on his life as one dear to God."**
SUPPORTED. EUS 60661–60663; HK 6561–6563.

**34. He copied the emperor's letters into his books so that they would be "carried in memory by all who come after us."**
SUPPORTED WITH CAUTION.
- The purpose clause: EUS 49189–49196 and SCH 20031–20037. It is stated in *Church History* X.2.2 for documents placed "in this book" (τῇδε τῇ βίβλῳ). The letters are "of the emperor" (βασιλέως γράμματα), unnamed there, among ordinances of "the supreme rulers" (plural).
- He also copied Constantine's letters into the *Life* (HK 12878–12896, 14376–14385). So "his books" is fair, but the stated purpose belongs to Book X.
- No change is required. For strict fit: "into his history".

**35. About four hundred and fifty years later, a council rejected his writings.**
SUPPORTED WITH CAUTION.
- MEN 20801–20806: after his letter to Euphration was read, the papal legates said "his inclination was Arian". Tarasius said, "We reject the writings of this man". The council said, "We reject and anathematize them".
- The context is the iconoclasts' appeal to his letter to Constantia (MEN 20711–20714).
- "450 years" is modern dating: 787 against 339 (C1, C12).
- The rejection stands as said, but the listener is given no reason for it. The source's reason is the charge of Arian views.

**36. Yet the same council heard it read that "no work of those older authors escaped his notice."**
SUPPORTED WITH CAUTION.
- MEN 20806–20826. Stephen the monk read from Antipater of Bostra's *Confutation of Eusebius's Defence of Origen*. The quote is exact.
- Caution: the words are a concession in a book written against him. They are followed at once by "Still, I affirm that he never attained to any accuracy of doctrine" (20824–20826). Without that, the line can be heard as the council's praise.
- Fix the source requires: name the speaker ("…heard a writer against him admit…"), or keep it and accept the narrowing.

## Century

The brief names no century. The sheet supports a plain spoken century in the same way "second century" was used for Polycarp, from modern dating of the reigns.
- Every datable point in his own record falls in the 300s by modern dating: Diocletian's nineteenth year (S5(b)), Constantine's reign, and Nicaea and Tyre in Constantine's twentieth and thirtieth years (S5(c)). C12 gives these as 325 and 335.
- His birth, "c. 260" (C1), is only a file-header date. It does not need a century of its own.
- Supported form: **"in the fourth century"**, or "early in the fourth century". Join it to the Diocletian/Constantine sentence or to the Caesarea opening.
- Not supported in the narrator's voice: "the Great Persecution" (C12), or any exact year.
- Note: the sheet now lists "fourth century" as card only (S5, C12). The Polycarp standard asks for "their century". So using it is a sheet-level call to align the two.

## Heard once through

- **Where a global listener gets lost.**
  - "Presbyter" and "the successions from the holy apostles" go unexplained.
  - The Nicaea paragraph turns on "of one substance" before anyone has said what was in dispute. Arius first appears afterwards, in sentence 26.
  - In sentence 18, "he … he … he" passes between Potamon and Eusebius.
  - In sentence 33, "his life" can be heard as Eusebius's own.
  - Three councils go by unnamed or barely named.
- **Story or report.** The first half is a story: the library, the edict, the Thebaid, Pamphilus, the Tyre question. From Nicaea on it becomes a report. Athanasius, Socrates, Theodoret, Socrates again, then a council each give a judgment in turn, with no scene.
- **What came of him.** The weight of I1 (the first church history, which others continued) and I2 (he gathered and kept the record) is there. But it is spread over citations, and the brief ends on a hostile council's rejection softened by a hostile writer's concession. It ends faintly, not with how far his work reached.
