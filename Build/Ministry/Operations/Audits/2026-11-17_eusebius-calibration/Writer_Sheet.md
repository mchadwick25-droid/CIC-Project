# Source sheet: Eusebius of Caesarea (writer's copy)

## 1. Setting

S1. **His city: Caesarea in Palestine.** He names it in his own list of its bishops: "In Cæsarea in Palestine, Agapius succeeded Theotecnus" (HE VII.32.24, EUS 42996–43004; Greek Καισαρείᾳ δὲ τῆς Παλαιστίνης, SCH 12918). Its gates stood by the sea (MP 4.2, 4.15, EUS 46329–46331, 46445–46451). He calls the province home: "our own province of Palestine" (*Oration* XI.2, EUS 71923; Greek ἐπὶ τῆς ἡμεδαπῆς ἑστίας, φημὶ δὴ τοῦ Παλαιστινῶν ἔθνους, "at our own hearth, I mean the people of Palestine", HK 20095). Jerome: "Eusebius bishop of Cæsarea in Palestine" (Lives 81, NP3 41061–41062). OWN; LATER. Documented.

S2. **His church and his office.** (a) He tells his own church at Caesarea that his creed is the one he received "from the Bishops who preceded us, and in our first catechisings, and when we received the Holy Laver", and that he "believed and taught in the presbytery, and in the Episcopate" (letter on Nicaea, NP4 19595–19598; Socrates's text, NP2 2993–3005). So he was first a presbyter there, then its bishop. (b) The church at Caesarea before him: Theotecnus, who "most zealously performed the duties of his episcopate", then Agapius, who cared "for all the poor with liberal hand" (HE VII.32.24, EUS 43003–43007; SCH 12918–12921). (c) Constantine writes that he wished "to continue in that church of which you first received the oversight" (VC III.61, EUS 65967–65969; Greek εἰς ἣν ἐκ πρώτης θεοῦ βουλήσει τὴν ἐπισκοπίαν ὑπεδέξατο, HK 12895–12896). OWN; CONTEMP. Documented.

S3. **Pamphilus, in Eusebius's own words.** (a) "In his time we became acquainted with Pamphilus, that most eloquent man, of truly philosophical life, who was esteemed worthy of the office of presbyter in that parish" (HE VII.32.25, Q10). (b) "My dearest friend Pamphilus" (MP 7.4, Q11). (c) "A man thrice dear to me", "the only one honored with the position of presbyter at Cæsarea" among the twelve who died with him (MP 11.1–2, Q12). (d) "The great glory of the parish of Cæsarea" (HE VIII.13.6, EUS 44790–44792; τὸ μέγα δὲ κλέος τῆς Καισαρέων παροικίας, SCH 15464–15466). (e) He wrote a life of Pamphilus in three books, now lost (MP 11.3, EUS 47082–47085; HE VI.32.3, Q9). OWN. Documented. **He does not call Pamphilus his teacher in any held text** (N3). Others: Jerome calls Pamphilus his "patron" (Lives 75, Q41) and "contubernalis", "companion", of Eusebius (*Chronicle*, Q40); Photius says Pamphilus wrote in prison "in the company of Eusebius" (PHO 11437–11439).

S4. **Pamphilus's school and library, and the city's learning.** (a) Eusebius wrote "all the particulars of his life, and of the school which he established" (HE VII.32.25, EUS 43042–43045; Greek διατριβῆς, "school, place of study", SCH 12925). (b) In the life of Pamphilus "we give ... a catalogue of the library which he collected of the works of Origen and of other ecclesiastical writers" (HE VI.32.3, Q9). (c) Jerome saw that library: Pamphilus "transcribed the greater part of the works of Origen with his own hand and these are still preserved in the library at Cæsarea" (Lives 75, Q41). (d) In the longer *Martyrs of Palestine* (Greek only), Apphianus comes "to this our city", lives "with us ourselves", and is trained in "divine studies and sacred words under Pamphilus the great martyr" (SCH 23293–23297: ἐπὶ τήνδε τὴν ἡμετέραν πόλιν … συγγενόμενος δὲ ἡμῖν αὐτοῖς καὶ τοῖς θείοις συγκροτηθεὶς μαθήμασιν λόγοις τε ἱεροῖς ὑπὸ Παμφίλῳ τῷ μεγάλῳ μάρτυρι). Aedesius too "shared for a longer time in the school of Pamphilus" (τῆς τε Παμφίλου διατριβῆς πλείονι χρόνῳ μετέσχηκεν, SCH 23752–23753). OWN; LATER. Documented.

S5. **The time, in his own markers.** He does not count centuries. He dates by emperors' years and by "our own times": (a) "a few of the most important occurrences of our own times" (HE VIII, Introduction, EUS 43296–43299). (b) "It was in the nineteenth year of the reign of Diocletian, in the month Dystrus, called March by the Romans, when the feast of the Saviour's passion was near at hand, that royal edicts were published everywhere, commanding that the churches be leveled to the ground and the Scriptures be destroyed by fire" (HE VIII.2.4, EUS 43460–43489; SCH 13717–13721). (c) The council at Nicaea "in the Twentieth", the Jerusalem dedication "in the Thirtieth, Year of Constantine's Reign" (VC IV.47 heading, EUS 67414–67416; IV.45, EUS 67386–67388). (d) "The persecution in our day" (HE VIII.1.1, EUS 43311–43312). OWN. Documented. "Fourth century" and "the Great Persecution" are modern labels (card C12).

S6. **The church before the persecution, as he saw it.** "Those vast assemblies, and the multitude that crowded together in every city, and the famous gatherings in the houses of prayer; on whose account not being satisfied with the ancient buildings they erected from the foundation large churches in all the cities" (HE VIII.1.5, EUS 43373–43378). And its quarrels: "rulers assailing rulers with words like spears, and people forming parties against people" (VIII.1.7, EUS 43386–43394). OWN. Documented as his account.

S7. **Plain spoken forms for his place, church and time**, from his own record:
- "the bishop of Caesarea, a port city of Palestine" — support: S1; the sea at the city gates (MP 4.15, EUS 46445–46451). "Port" rests on the sea at the gates only; "a city by the sea" is safer.
- "who had served his church there first as a presbyter, then as its bishop" — support: S2(a).
- "a friend and fellow worker of Pamphilus, a presbyter of that church who gathered a great library" — support: S3, S4. Not "his teacher" (N3).
- "in the years when the emperor Diocletian ordered the churches pulled down and the Scriptures burned, and then in the reign of Constantine" — support: S5.
- "he lived through the persecution in his own day, and saw the peace that followed" — support: HE VIII.2.1 (Q4); X.2.1 (EUS 49180–49187).


## 2. Recorded moments

In time order as far as the record allows. Headings quote the record.

M1. **"In his time we became acquainted with Pamphilus."** Under bishop Agapius of Caesarea, Eusebius came to know Pamphilus (HE VII.32.25, EUS 43009–43046; SCH 12921–12928; Q10). OWN. Documented. When, and how old Eusebius was, is not said (N1).

M2. **"We have heard him expound the Scriptures wisely in the Church."** In the time of bishop Cyril of Antioch, "we became acquainted with Dorotheus", a presbyter of Antioch who read the Hebrew Scriptures (HE VII.32.2–3, EUS 42583–42623). OWN. Documented as his account. Where Eusebius heard him is not said.

M3. **"An instance of this we have ourselves seen."** Eusebius saw the young Constantine pass through Palestine "with the senior emperor, at whose right hand he stood" (VC I.19, EUS 60952–60959; HK 6873–6880; Q18). OWN. Documented as his account. The year is not given; the "senior emperor" is not named in this sentence.

M4. **"When we saw with our own eyes the houses of prayer thrown down."** The first edict: churches razed, Scriptures burned in the market-places, pastors hiding or seized (HE VIII.2.1–4, EUS 43431–43491; SCH 13614–13621, 13716–13724; Q4, Q5). OWN. Documented as his account. Which city he watched this in is not said.

M5. **"We were present ourselves when these things occurred."** Martyrs from Egypt at Tyre in Phoenicia, scourged, then set before leopards, bears, wild boars and a bull goaded with fire (HE VIII.7.1–5, EUS 44140–44200; SCH 14265–14286; Q6). OWN. Documented as his account. **Wonder attributed:** the beasts would not touch the martyrs, "restrained as if by some divine power" (Eusebius, as eyewitness).

M6. **"We, also being on the spot ourselves."** In the Thebaid, in Upper Egypt, he saw many killed in a single day, "so that the murderous sword was blunted", and saw them run to confess (HE VIII.9.4–5, EUS 44268–44283; SCH 14540–14546; Q7). OWN. Documented as his account. Why he was in Egypt is not said (N5). No wonder attributed.

M7. **"Before the gates of Cæsarea."** Apphianus, a young man "not twenty years old", educated at Berytus, confessed at Caesarea, was tortured and thrown into the sea (MP 4, EUS 46311–46458; SCH 23571–23581; Q13). OWN. Documented as his account. **Wonder attributed:** "an uncommon commotion and disturbance agitated the sea and all the shore", and the sea "threw out before the gates of the city the body" — Eusebius says "all the inhabitants of Cæsarea were witnesses".

M8. **"Many drops fell like tears."** At Caesarea, while the bodies of martyrs lay unburied by the gates, the pillars of the public porches dripped on a clear day (MP 9.12, EUS 46995–47007; SCH 24517–24528; Q14). OWN. Documented as his account. **Wonder attributed:** the dripping is told as Eusebius's own description; the meaning ("the earth ... had shed tears") is what "was reported everywhere". He adds that later readers may think it "idle and fabulous".

M9. **"Among these was my dearest friend Pamphilus."** Pamphilus was tortured and imprisoned by the governor Urbanus (MP 7.4–6, EUS 46690–46720; SCH 24043–24046; Q11), and later died with eleven others (MP 11, EUS 47064–47315; Q12). OWN. Documented as his account. Whether Eusebius was in prison with him: D4.

M10. **"The Apology in his behalf written by us and Pamphilus."** Eusebius and Pamphilus wrote a defence of Origen together, "on account of faultfinders" (HE VI.33.4, EUS 36967–36976; SCH 5257–5262; Q31). OWN. Documented. How the work was split: D8.

M11. **"My most holy Paulinus."** He dedicated the tenth book to Paulinus, bishop of Tyre, and set in it a "panegyric upon the restoration of the churches" (HE X.1.2, EUS 49051–49079; SCH 19817–19823; Q15a). At the dedication of the new church at Tyre "a certain one of those of moderate talent" gave the oration (X.4.1, EUS 49258–49267). OWN. Documented as text. The speaker is not named in the text; that it was Eusebius is McGiffert's note (card C2).

M12. **"You have probably had some intimation, beloved."** At Nicaea he put forward the creed of his church; Constantine approved it but asked that "one in essence" (*homoousios*) be added; the council issued a fuller creed; Eusebius questioned its terms, then signed, and wrote home to explain (letter on Nicaea: NP2 2981–3153; NP4 19560–19937; HUS 6907–7687; Q21–Q25). OWN. Documented. Socrates and Sozomen say he held back a little, then signed (Q50, Q56). How others read his conduct: D2.

M13. **"The bishop who occupied the chief place in the right division of the assembly."** At the opening of the council, after Constantine entered, that bishop "delivered a concise speech, in a strain of thanksgiving to Almighty God on his behalf" (VC III.11, EUS 64437–64445; HK 11151–11157; Q20). OWN. Documented that someone spoke; who spoke is Contested (D3).

M14. **"Declining the bishopric of the church at Antioch."** After Eustathius was deposed at Antioch, a party there sought Eusebius as bishop. He refused, and Constantine praised the refusal (VC III.60–62, EUS 65828–66000; HK 12878–12900; Q26, Q27). Socrates I.24, Sozomen II.19, Theodoret I.21 agree that he refused (Q52, Q57, Q63). OWN; CONTEMP (Constantine's letters); LATER (about 100 years). Documented.

M15. **"Speech on our Saviour's sepulchre" before Constantine.** In the palace Constantine "stood and listened with the rest", would not sit, and told him to go on to the end (VC IV.33, EUS 66986–67005; HK 14296–14313; Q28). OWN. Documented as his account. Place and year not given.

M16. **"Fifty copies of the sacred Scriptures."** Constantine wrote asking him to have fifty copies made on parchment for the churches of his new city; Eusebius sent them "in magnificent and elaborately bound volumes" (VC IV.34–37, EUS 67010–67124; HK 14313–14315, 14385, 14431–14432; Q29). OWN; CONTEMP (the letter). Documented. Socrates and Theodoret copy the letter (I8).

M17. **"A Council to be convened at Tyre."** Constantine called bishops to Tyre over "contentions" in "the Egyptian churches", and then to Jerusalem (VC IV.41–43, EUS 67213–67323). OWN. Documented that the council met. Eusebius's own account names no accused bishop and no verdict (N7). What others say he did there: D5.

M18. **"I myself too ... pronounced various public orations."** At the dedication of the church at Jerusalem, in Constantine's thirtieth year, he spoke several times, explaining the building and the prophets' visions (VC IV.45, EUS 67351–67388; Q30 for his words on Palestine). Soon after he travelled to Constantine's city and gave the oration for the thirtieth year "in the emperor's own presence" (VC IV.46, EUS 67398–67409). OWN. Documented.

M19. **"The victorious emperor himself long afterwards declared it to the writer of this history."** Eusebius tells Constantine's vision of a cross of light above the sun, with the words "Conquer by this", and Christ's appearing to him in sleep (VC I.28–29, EUS 61187–61208; HK 7118–7138; Q19). OWN, reporting Constantine. **Wonder attributed:** to Constantine's own account, "confirmed ... by an oath"; the whole army "witnessed the miracle". When and where Constantine told him is not said beyond "long afterwards". See D7.

M20. **His death.** "At this time Eusebius, who was bishop of Cæsarea in Palestine, and had the surname of Pamphilus, having died, Acacius, his disciple, succeeded him in the bishopric" (Socrates II.4, NP2 5802–5806; Sozomen III.2, NP2 30818–30826; Q59). LATER (about 100 years). Documented by later witnesses; no date, place or manner given (N1).


## 3. Quotes (final readings)

Each quote is in our modern English, settled from two independent renderings. Use the final reading exactly. Where a quote carries a warning, the warning says how it may be used.

**Q1. Church History I.1.1: what he set out to do**
- Source: EUS 6878–6885 (McGiffert). Greek not held (Book I).
- Final reading: My purpose is to write an account of the successions from the holy apostles. It will cover the times that have passed from our Saviour's days to our own. I will tell the many great events said to have happened in the history of the church. I will name those who governed and presided over the church in the most prominent local churches. And I will name those who, in each generation, proclaimed the divine word by speech or in writing.
- Use: USABLE

**Q2. Church History I.1.3–4: the martyrs of his own day, and the untrodden path**
- Source: EUS 6895–6902, 6934–6937, 6952–6960. Greek not held.
- Final reading: I will also describe the character of those who, at different times, fought for the divine word in the face of blood and torture. I will tell of the confessions made in our own days. And last, I will tell of the gracious and kindly help our Saviour has given them all. … I admit it is beyond my power to produce a perfect and complete history. I am the first to take up this subject, and I am trying to travel, as it were, a lonely and untrodden path. … I pray to have God as my guide and the power of the Lord as my help. For I cannot find even the bare footprints of those who went this way before me, except in brief fragments. In them, some in one way and some in another, have handed down to us accounts of the times they each lived in. From far off they raise their voices like torches. They call out as from a high watchtower that all can see. They tell us where to walk, and how to steer our work steadily and safely.
- Use: USABLE

**Q3. Church History I.1.5: "like flowers from a meadow"**
- Source: EUS 6962–6975. Greek not held.
- Final reading: So we have gathered, from what they mention here and there, whatever we think important for this work. We have plucked the fitting passages from ancient writers, like flowers from a meadow. We will try to bring the whole into one historical narrative. We will be content if we keep the memory of the successions from our Saviour's apostles. If not of all, then of the most renowned of them, in the most noted churches, which are still held in honour today.
- Use: USABLE

**Q4. Church History VIII.2.1: "we saw with our own eyes"**
- Source: EUS 43431–43437; SCH 13614–13620.
- Final reading: All these things were fulfilled in our time. With our own eyes we saw the houses of prayer thrown down from top to ground, foundations and all. We saw the inspired and holy Scriptures handed over to the fire in the middle of the market squares. We saw the shepherds of the churches hiding shamefully here and there, and others caught in disgrace and mocked by their enemies.
- Use: USABLE

**Q5. Church History VIII.2.2–3: what he chose to record, and why**
- Source: EUS 43441–43453; SCH 13622–13625, 13708–13714.
- Final reading: But it is not our task to describe the grim misfortunes that came on them in the end. Nor do we think it fitting to hand down to memory their quarrels and wrongdoing toward one another before the persecution. So we decided to tell nothing more about them than what would show God's judgment to be just. So we were not moved to mention those who were put to the test by the persecution. Nor did we choose to mention those who were wholly shipwrecked from salvation, and of their own will were thrown into the depths of the waves. We will add to the general history only what may be of help, first to ourselves, and then to those who come after us.
- Use: USE WITH WARNING: "put to the test" (πεπειραμένων, printed "shaken") may mean those who fell under the test; do not say which.

**Q6. Church History VIII.7.2: "we were present ourselves" (Tyre)**
- Source: EUS 44161–44171; SCH 14273–14287 (the full passage; the sheet's ellipsis left out 14283–14287).
- Final reading: We ourselves were present when these things happened. We saw the divine power of our Saviour, to whom they bore witness, Jesus Christ himself, present and plainly showing itself in the martyrs. For a long time the man-eating beasts did not dare to touch the bodies of those dear to God, or even come near them. They rushed instead at the others, who goaded them on from outside. The holy athletes stood alone and naked, waving their hands to draw the beasts toward themselves, for they had been ordered to do this. Yet the beasts would not touch them at all. Sometimes they did rush at them, but they were driven back as if by some more divine power, and drew back again.
- Use: USE WITH WARNING: a wonder told by Eusebius as eyewitness; keep it in his voice ("as if by some more divine power").

**Q7. Church History VIII.9.4–5: "being on the spot ourselves" (the Thebaid)**
- Source: EUS 44268–44283; SCH 14540–14546 (first half only).
- Final reading: When we were in those places ourselves, we saw many put to death together on a single day. Some endured beheading, others punishment by fire. The killing blade grew blunt, lost its strength and broke. The executioners themselves grew weary and took turns relieving one another. Then we saw the most wonderful eagerness, a truly divine power and zeal, in those who had believed in the Christ of God. As soon as sentence was passed on the first, others rushed one after another to the judge's seat and confessed themselves Christians. They cared nothing for the terrors and the many kinds of torture. They spoke out boldly and without fear for the worship of the God of all. They received the final sentence of death with joy, laughter and good cheer. They sang and offered hymns and thanks to the God of all until their very last breath.
- Use: USABLE

**Q8. Church History VI.20.1: the library at Aelia (Jerusalem), his source**
- Source: EUS 35434–35443; SCH 3957–3962.
- Final reading: At that time many learned churchmen flourished. The letters they wrote to one another are still preserved now and are easy to find. They have been kept down to our time in the library at Aelia, which Alexander, then leader of the church there, had furnished. From that library we too have been able to gather together the material for the work in hand.
- Use: USE WITH WARNING: do not say Alexander founded the library; the verb may mean he equipped or restored it.

**Q9. Church History VI.32.3: the catalogue of Pamphilus's library**
- Source: EUS 36883–36895; SCH 5151–5156 (Greek ends at τοὺς πίνακας).
- Final reading: Why should we make an exact catalogue of the man's writings here? That would need a separate work. We have recorded it in our account of the life of Pamphilus, a holy martyr of our own time. There we showed how great Pamphilus's zeal for divine things was. And we set out the catalogues of the library he gathered, of the works of Origen and of other church writers. Anyone who wishes can easily learn from them which of Origen's works have reached us. But we must now go on with our history.
- Use: USABLE

**Q10. Church History VII.32.25: Pamphilus**
- Source: EUS 43009–43046; SCH 12921–12928.
- Final reading: In his time we came to know Pamphilus. He was a most learned man, a true lover of wisdom in his very way of life, and had been thought worthy of the office of presbyter in the church there. To show what kind of man he was, and where he came from, would be no small task. But we have set out each part of his life in a work of its own about him. There we told of the school he founded, his struggles in many confessions during the persecution, and the martyr's crown he won at the last. But of all who were there, he was the most admirable.
- Use: USE WITH WARNING: "most learned" may also be read "most eloquent" or "most distinguished".

**Q11. Martyrs of Palestine 7.4: "my dearest friend Pamphilus"**
- Source: EUS 46690–46698; SCH 24043–24046, continued at SCH 24081 (ἐπιδοξότατος).
- Final reading: Others again, after harsh tortures, he shut up in prison. Among them was Pamphilus, to me at least the dearest of all my companions. Of the martyrs of our time, he was the most glorious for every virtue.
- Use: USABLE

**Q12. Martyrs of Palestine 11.1–2: Pamphilus and the twelve**
- Source: EUS 47067–47080; SCH 24600–24602, 24800–24808.
- Final reading: Now is the time to tell of the great and famous spectacle of those who died as martyrs around Pamphilus, a name thrice dear to me. There were twelve in all. They were counted worthy of a prophetic, or even an apostolic, gift and number. Their leader, and the only one honoured with the rank of presbyter at Caesarea, was Pamphilus. All his life he stood out for every virtue. He renounced and despised worldly life. He shared his goods with the needy. He thought little of worldly hopes. He lived the disciplined life of a lover of wisdom. Above all, he stood out beyond everyone in our time for his most genuine zeal for the divine Scriptures. He worked tirelessly at whatever he set out to do. And he helped his relatives and all who came near him.
- Use: USABLE

**Q13. Martyrs of Palestine 4.14–15: the sea at the gates of Caesarea (wonder)**
- Source: EUS 46438–46451; SCH 23571–23581 (opening only).
- Final reading: What happened at once after this will likely not be believed, when told, by those who did not see it. We know that well. Even so, reason will not let us fail to hand down the truth, by every means, in this history. To put it plainly, all the people of Caesarea were witnesses of what happened. Indeed, people of every age saw this marvel. They threw this truly holy and thrice-blessed young man into the bottomless depths of the sea. At once a strange upheaval stirred the sea and all the shore around it. The land and the whole city were shaken by it. At the same moment as this wonderful and sudden upheaval, the sea threw the body of the divine martyr out before the city gates, as if it could not bear it.
- Use: USE WITH WARNING: a wonder in Eusebius's voice; he names the whole city as witness, not himself.

**Q14. Martyrs of Palestine 9.12: the pillars that wept (wonder)**
- Source: EUS 46995–47007; SCH 24517–24528.
- Final reading: These things went on for many days, and then something strange happened. The sky was clear, the air bright, the weather perfectly calm. Then suddenly most of the pillars across the city that held up the public colonnades dripped drops, in a way like tears. The market squares and streets grew wet, sprinkled with water from I know not where, though no drizzle had fallen from the air. At once the word spread to everyone that the earth had wept in a way past telling. It could not bear the wickedness done then. To rebuke the hard and unfeeling nature of human beings, stones and lifeless matter had wept over what had happened. I know well that this account may perhaps seem idle talk and a fable to those after us. But not to those for whom the time itself confirmed the truth.
- Use: USE WITH WARNING: the dripping is Eusebius's own description; its meaning (the earth weeping) is what "spread to everyone", and must stay reported.

**Q15. Church History X.1.2 and X.2.2: the last book, and documents kept as a memorial**
- Source: (a) EUS 49051–49079; SCH 19812–19822. (b) EUS 49189–49196; SCH 20029–20037.
- Final reading: (a) Along with these prayers, we now add this tenth volume of the Church History to those already set out. We dedicate it to you, my most holy Paulinus, and hail you as the seal of the whole work. Fittingly, in a perfect number, we will set here the perfect speech of celebration on the renewal of the churches. (b) The highest emperors, too, by law after law in favour of Christians, made God's great gifts to us firmer still, further and greater. Letters from the emperor came to the bishops in person, with honours and gifts of money. At the fitting point in our account, it would not be out of place to carve their words into this book, as on a sacred stone. They were translated from the Romans' language into Greek. So they may be carried in memory by all who come after us.
- Use: USABLE

**Q16. Life of Constantine I.10: why he wrote the *Life***
- Source: EUS 60615–60622; HK 6531–6537.
- Final reading: Would it not be shameful if the memory of Nero, and of certain godless and impious tyrants far worse than he, had the good fortune of tireless writers? They dressed up the stories of worthless deeds in elegant language, and set them in histories of many books. And should we be silent? God himself has thought us worthy to meet such an emperor as all the ages have never recorded. He has let us come into his sight, and know him, and keep his company.
- Use: USABLE

**Q17. Life of Constantine I.11: he will write only of the emperor's religious life**
- Source: EUS 60653–60668; HK 6552–6569 (full; the sheet's ellipsis left out HK 6556–6558).
- Final reading: So I think I will pass over most of the thrice-blessed emperor's royal deeds. I mean his campaigns, his battles and lines of war, his feats and victories, his victory monuments over enemies, and all the triumphs he led. I mean too what he settled in peacetime to set public affairs right and for each person's good. I mean the laws he drew up for the benefit of his subjects' common life, and very many other royal labours that everyone remembers. The aim of the work before us leads me to speak and write only of what bears on his life as one dear to God. Even these things are countless. From what has come down to us, I will pick the most fitting, and those worth remembering for people after us. I will set them out as briefly as I can.
- Use: USABLE

**Q18. Life of Constantine I.19: "we have ourselves seen" the young Constantine**
- Source: EUS 60953–60959; HK 6875–6880.
- Final reading: Already, as he passed from boyhood to young manhood, he was thought worthy of the highest honour among them. Nor can they prove that Eusebius gives the Son of God a beginning of existence, even if they find him in his books using loosely the language of the divine plan. All the more since he followed and admired the works of Origen. Those able to grasp the depth of Origen's books will find in them, everywhere, that the Son was begotten of the Father.
- Use: USE WITH WARNING: "the language of the divine plan" (τοῖς τῆς οἰκονομίας λέξεσιν, Latin *dispensationis vocabulis*) names words used of God's plan, often the Son's coming in the flesh; Socrates does not spell out which, and the card should not.

## 4. Impact as the record gives it

Ranked heaviest first. Weight is what later witnesses carried on and used, not what is easiest to tell. [DEFENDED] marks what he defended.

I1. **"It is my purpose to write an account."** He wrote the church's first history, and the historians after him began where he stopped. Socrates opens on him and begins "with the narration of the particulars which he has left out" (Q48). Theodoret: "I shall begin my history from the period at which his terminates" (Q60). Sozomen names him with Clement, Hegesippus and Africanus as those who had already "compiled" the records "up to their own time" (Sozomen I.1, NP2 26359–26376). Rufinus turned it into Latin, shortened Book X, and wrote two more books, "Hucusque nobis Eusebius ... memoriam tradidit" (Q46, Q47). Jerome's *Chronicle*: "Huc usque historiam scribit Eusebius" (Q40). Gelasius calls him "the most trustworthy of the old church writers" and the only one who "travelled the highway of truth without turning" from the Lord's coming to Constantine (Q64). Photius read the ten books (codex 27, PHO 2371–2375) and calls Socrates's history "a continuation of that of Eusebius" (codex 28, PHO 2379–2381). OWN; LATER. — Rank 1. Carrying quote: Q1 (with Q2 for "a lonely and untrodden path").

I2. **"Like flowers from a meadow."** [DEFENDED] He gathered and kept the words of earlier writers and the documents of his own time, so that they would "remain as a memorial to all who shall come after us" (Q3, Q15b). He names his sources: the library at Aelia (Q8) and the library of Pamphilus, whose catalogue he wrote (Q9). He copied Constantine's letters into the *Life* and into Book X (Q15b, Q26, Q29). Later witnesses carried both the gathering and the copies: Antipater, read at the Second Council of Nicaea, grants "no work of those older authors escaped his notice" (Q66); Socrates and Theodoret copy Constantine's letter to him on the Scriptures from his *Life* (Socrates I.9, NP2 3541–3545; Theodoret I.14–15, NP3 7213–7231); Basil cites him as a witness to the old form of prayer (Q65). OWN; LATER. — Rank 2. Carrying quote: Q3. (Defended: he defends the worth of keeping the record, VC I.10, Q16.)

I3. **"Peace being the object which we set before us."** [DEFENDED] He is the main eyewitness of Nicaea who wrote at the time, and his account was used by both sides after him. He defended to his own church why he signed, and how he read "of one substance" (Q21–Q24). Socrates sets his letter and his *Life* III into his own history "as an authority ... for the fidelity of this history" (Q49) and uses them to defend the council against Sabinus. Athanasius appended the letter to his *De Decretis* and quoted it in his letter to the bishops of Africa, to show that even Eusebius signed (Q34, Q38). Theodoret copied the letter and the *Life* III (Q61; Theodoret I.12, NP3 7008–7033). OWN; CONTEMP; LATER. — Rank 3. Carrying quote: Q23 (or Q24).

I4. **"We saw with our own eyes."** He was a witness of the persecution in his own day, and of the martyrs of Palestine, many of whom he knew (Q4–Q7, Q11–Q14). Socrates marks the end of his history by it: "when the persecution which Diocletian had begun against the Christians came to an end" (Q48). Jerome lists "other brief works On the martyrs" (Q42) and tells of Pamphilus's death "in the persecution of Maximinus" (Q41). Rufinus carried Book VIII into Latin (SCH 13657–13680 prints his Latin of VIII.1–2, "pro nefas! ipsi haec nostris vidimus oculis", "Shame! We ourselves saw these things with our own eyes", SCH 13676). OWN; LATER. — Rank 4. Carrying quote: Q4.

I5. **"The victorious emperor himself long afterwards declared it to the writer."** His *Life of Constantine* became the source for the emperor's turning and for the council, and it was judged as well as used. Sozomen tells the vision on his word (Q54). Socrates and Theodoret quote *Life* III for Nicaea (Q53, Socrates I.8 NP2 2691–2864; Theodoret I.12). Socrates and Photius fault it as praise more than history (Q48, Q67). OWN; LATER. — Rank 5. Carrying quote: Q19.

I6. **"A man thrice dear to me."** [DEFENDED] He kept the memory of Pamphilus, of his library at Caesarea, and of their joint defence of Origen. His own words: Q9–Q12, Q31. Later: Jerome saw the library at Caesarea and held Pamphilus's copies of Origen (Q41); Jerome explains his name by "his friendship for Pamphilus" (Q42) and calls him Pamphilus's "companion" (Q40); Photius read the six-book *Defence* (Q67); Socrates calls him "an emulator and admirer of the works of Origen" (Q53). The defence of Origen also drew attack: Jerome (Q45, D8) and Antipater's *Confutation*, read and endorsed at the Second Council of Nicaea (Q66). OWN; LATER. — Rank 6. Carrying quote: Q12.

I7. **"Declining the bishopric of the church at Antioch."** He stayed with his own church when Antioch wanted him, and the emperor praised him for it. His own copy of the letters (Q26, Q27); Socrates (Q52), Sozomen (Q57), Theodoret (Q63). OWN; CONTEMP; LATER. — Rank 7. Carrying quote: Q26.

I8. **"Fifty copies of the sacred Scriptures."** Constantine turned to him to have Bibles made for the churches of the new capital (Q29). Socrates and Theodoret copy the letter (I2 refs). OWN; CONTEMP; LATER. — Rank 8. Carrying quote: Q29.

I9. **"Against Porphyry ... twenty-five books."** [DEFENDED] He answered the faith's critics and wrote much besides. Jerome lists the works (Q42) and names him with Origen and Methodius among those who "ably answered" Celsus and Porphyry (Letter 70, NP6 16535–16549). Photius read the *Preparation* and *Proof of the Gospel* (codices 9–10, PHO 2066–2080). Sozomen: "a man intimately acquainted with the sacred Scriptures and the writings of the Greek poets and historians" (NP2 26373–26374). OWN; LATER. — Rank 9. Carrying quote: Q42.

I10. **"Acacius, his disciple."** He taught his successor at Caesarea: Socrates (Q59); Sozomen: Acacius "could boast of Eusebius Pamphilus as his teacher" (Sozomen IV.23, NP2 34481–34483). LATER. — Rank 10.

**Card only, not for the story**

I11. **"Father of Church History."** McGiffert's note at HE I.1.4 (EUS 6937–6951 in the raw file) calls him that. A modern editor's title, not held ancient witness.
I12. **"Inestimable worth" of the quotations.** McGiffert's note at HE I.1.5 says many earlier works "are known to us only through the extracts made by Eusebius" (EUS note at 6965–6970). The ancient witness for I2 is Antipater (Q66); "known only through him" is the modern claim and is not shown by the held ancient texts.
I13. **Constantine's letter on the Scriptures and the great codices.** Any link between the fifty copies (Q29) and surviving manuscripts is modern; nothing held says so.


## 5. Disputes and unclear points

D1. **What did he hold about Arius's teaching before Nicaea?**
- (a) Counted among those who agree with Arius: Arius names "Eusebius, your brother bishop of Cæsarea" among the bishops of the East "condemned because they say that God had an existence prior to that of His Son" (Q32). CONTEMP.
- (b) Sozomen: Arius sent to Paulinus of Tyre, "Eusebius Pamphilus, who presided over the church of Cæsarea", and Patrophilus, who with others "assembled in Palestine, granted the petition of Arius, and permitted him to assemble the people as before; but enjoined submission to Alexander" (Sozomen I.15, NP2 27637–27649). LATER.
- (c) Eusebius of Nicomedia praises "the zeal of my lord Eusebius in the cause of the truth" (Q33). The letter does not say which Eusebius; reading it as Caesarea is Inferential-Thin.
- (d) Athanasius: in a letter to Euphration he "did not scruple to say plainly that Christ was not true God" (Q37); "at first an accomplice of the Arian heresy" (Q38). The Second Council of Nicaea read the Euphration letter ("the Son was not co-existent with the Father") and the papal legates said it "proves that his inclination was Arian" (Q66). CONTEMP; LATER.
- (e) Jerome: "the most open champion of the Arian impiety" (Q43); "Eusebius ... is an Arian" (Q44); "the Chief of the Arians" (Apology II.15, NP3 53497–53498); "who is admitted by every body to have been an Arian" (Letter 133, NP6 27887–27893); and in the *Dialogue against the Luciferians* 20 he names "Eusebius, bishop of Cæsarea in Palestine" among the Arians received at Nicaea (NP6 32460–32486: "eight Arian bishops were welcomed"). Theodoret: the Arians "honour Eusebius, because he adopted their sentiments" (Q61). Photius: "In many passages he utters blasphemies against the Son" (Q67). LATER.
- (f) The other side. His own letter says the creed he brought was the one he was taught at baptism and taught as presbyter and bishop (Q22). Socrates writes a "Defense of Eusebius Pamphilus": he was at Nicaea and agreed; his work against Marcellus condemns calling the Son "a Creature made of nothing" (Q53; NP2 6959–6967). Gelasius: let no one think he "ever held anything of Arius's wicked blasphemy"; what sounded like it came "from a simple mind" (Q64), though a manuscript's margin note disagrees (GEL 4465–4467). Sabinus, by Socrates's report, called him "a trustworthy witness" (Q49).
- Contested. Do not settle it in the story.

D2. **His conduct at Nicaea.**
- (a) He hesitated, then signed: Socrates "held aloof for a short time ... at length acquiesced" (Q50); Sozomen "withheld his assent for a little while, but on further examination assented" (Q56); his own letter, "we resisted even to the last minute ... but received without contention" (Q24).
- (b) Athanasius: he "had denied the day before, but afterwards subscribed", and in his letter in effect admitted "they were before in error", while he "excused himself to the Church in his own way" (Q34). CONTEMP. Eusebius's letter says nothing of having been in error; Athanasius gives this as what Eusebius "means to imply".
- (c) To whom he wrote, and why. Socrates: "to the people under his charge ... that no one might impugn his motives on account of his previous hesitation" (Q50). Athanasius: "to his Church" (Q34). Theodoret: "to some of the Arians, who were accusing him, it seems, of treachery" (Q61).
- (d) The text of the letter differs. Athanasius's copy has a paragraph (on "Before His generation He was not", and on Constantine's speech) that Socrates's copy lacks; Hussey's apparatus says Theodoret, Gelasius and Nicephorus have nearly the same as Athanasius (Q25).
- (e) Who brought in "of one substance". His letter: Constantine himself, at the reading of Eusebius's creed, advised adding "that single word" (NP2 3046–3061; NP4 19655–19661). The NPNF editor sets this against Athanasius's own account in *De Decretis* 19–20 and *To the Africans* 5 (NP4 19517–19534); I did not read those chapters (N12). Card C5.

D3. **Who gave the opening speech at Nicaea?** His own *Life* says only "the bishop who occupied the chief place in the right division" (Q20). Sozomen names Eusebius (Q55). Theodoret names Eustathius of Antioch (Q62). The NPNF chapter heading names Eusebius (EUS 64434–64435); Heikel argues from Eusebius's own wording that he means himself (HK 3029–3037, card C3). Contested.

D4. **Was he in prison in the persecution, and how did he come out?** Epiphanius has Potamon at Tyre say "were you not in prison with me in the persecution? ... How did you get out of prison? Was it not that you promised the persecutors to do the forbidden thing, or did it?" (Q39). Athanasius: "accused by our confessors of sacrificing" (Q35). Photius: Pamphilus wrote five books "when in prison in the company of Eusebius" (Q67). His own held works tell of Pamphilus's imprisonment (Q11) but, in what I read and searched, say nothing of his own (N2). The NPNF note to Athanasius says the charge "was groundless", citing a modern dictionary (NP4 22309–22316, card C6). Contested. Do not script an answer.

D5. **His part at Tyre.** (a) Judge: Epiphanius says the emperor "ordered Eusebius of Caesarea and some others to judge", and that they "leaned somewhat more toward ... the Arians" (Q39). (b) Enemy of Athanasius: the Egyptian bishops' letter, "you are aware why Eusebius of Cæsarea became such [our enemy] last year" (Q36); Sozomen: Athanasius refused the council at Caesarea "fearing the artifices of Eusebius, bishop of the city" (Q58); Athanasius asks whether the council at Tyre can be trusted when Eusebius of Caesarea was among the accused (Q35). (c) His own account names no one and gives no verdict (VC IV.41–43, EUS 67213–67323). (d) Rufinus's account of Tyre tells a different confessor's rebuke: Paphnutius tells Maximus of Jerusalem not to sit "in concilio malignantium" (SCH 28008–28017). It does not name Eusebius of Caesarea. Do not merge it with Epiphanius's Potamon scene. Contested.

D6. **Did the *Life of Constantine* leave things out on purpose?** (a) Yes, or so it was judged: Socrates, "has but slightly treated of matters regarding Arius, being more intent on ... the praises of the emperor" (Q48), and gave no reason for the troubles in Egypt, "so that hence he has won the reputation of disingenuousness" (Q51); Photius, "as if ashamed and unwilling to make public the facts concerning Arius" (Q67). (b) His own stated plan: "to speak and write of those circumstances only which have reference to his religious character" (Q17). (c) In the held *Life*, Eusebius's own narration of the dispute names neither party; the name "Arius" appears in the NPNF chapter headings and in Constantine's letter to Alexander and Arius (EUS 63694–63696, 63783–63788, 63883–63893). The name "Athanasius" does not appear in the held *Life* or *Oration* text. Record both sides.

D7. **The vision.** (a) His *Life* gives it on Constantine's word and oath (Q19). (b) His *Church History* IX.9, telling the same war, has the statue with "the saving sign of the cross" and its inscription (EUS 48509–48520), but a keyword search of Book IX found no vision. (c) Sozomen repeats the *Life*'s account with different wording, "By this sign, conquer" (Q54). (d) Sozomen and the *Life* add Christ's appearing in sleep (Q19 HK 7133–7138; NP2 26540–26543). Record; do not reconcile.

D8. **Who wrote the *Defence of Origen*?** His own: "written by us and Pamphilus ... we ... did the work jointly" (Q31). Jerome, *Lives* 75: Pamphilus "wrote an Apology for Origen before Eusebius had written his" (Q41). Photius: five books by Pamphilus in prison, the sixth by Eusebius after Pamphilus's death (Q67). Jerome, Letter 84.10 and the *Apology*: the book Rufinus put out under Pamphilus's name is really Eusebius's sixth book, and Eusebius "the Chief of the Arians" (Q45; NP3 53497–53498); Letter 133 says Rufinus ascribed "to him the first of the six books in defence of Origen written by Eusebius of Cæsarea" (NP6 27886–27893). Contested.

D9. **His name.** The Greek form is "Eusebius of Pamphilus" (ὁ Παμφίλου, HUS 3549; Εὐσέβιος ὁ τὴν Παμφίλου προσωνυμίαν ἔχων, "who had Pamphilus's name as a surname", HUS 6860–6862). Jerome: from "his friendship for Pamphilus" (Q42); "one even went so far as to adopt the other's name" (Q45). Jerome's *Chronicle*: "Pamphili martyris contubernalis" (Q40). Whether it means son, pupil or friend is not settled by the held texts. In speech, "Eusebius" or "Eusebius of Caesarea".

D10. **Antioch: who wanted him?** VC III.60: the people of Antioch, by their "records" (Q27). Socrates: one faction of the people, against another that wanted Eustathius back (Q52). Sozomen: the bishops opposed to Eustathius, to set "one of their own opinion" there (Q57). Theodoret: "it was intended", without saying by whom (Q63). All agree he refused.

D11. **What Jerome translated.** Jerome says he gave "these works of his" (History, Chronicle, holy land) to Latin readers (Q44). The held record shows Jerome's Latin *Chronicle* (FOT) and Rufinus's Latin *History* (Q46); Jerome's own translation of the *History* is not held or attested elsewhere in what I read.

D12. **"Threefold and fourfold."** The form of the copies sent to Constantine (Q29) is unclear in the Greek and the English. Do not explain it.

D13. **The weeping pillars and the sea.** He tells both as seen at Caesarea, one by "all the inhabitants" (Q13), the other with his own "I know not whence" and the meaning given as what "was reported everywhere" (Q14). He expects later readers to doubt (Q14). Record as his; no other held witness tells either.


## 6. What the record does not tell us

N1. His birth year, birthplace, family and age at any point; when he became presbyter or bishop; and the date, place and manner of his death. Socrates and Sozomen say only that he died "at this time" in Constantius's reign (Q59).
N2. Whether he was himself imprisoned, or confessed, in the persecution. His own held works do not say (D4).
N3. That Pamphilus was his "teacher". Eusebius calls Pamphilus his dearest companion and "thrice dear to me" (Q11, Q12); Jerome says "patron", "friendship", "companion" (Q40–Q42); Photius "in the company of" (Q67). No held text has Eusebius call him teacher.
N4. The text of his three books *On the Life of Pamphilus* (lost; Q9, Q42), of Acacius's life of Eusebius (lost; Q59), and of Sabinus's collection (lost; Q49).
N5. Why and when he was in Tyre and in the Thebaid in the persecution (M5, M6), and in which city he saw the churches pulled down (M4).
N6. Who spoke first at Nicaea (D3).
N7. What he did, said or decided at Tyre, beyond Epiphanius's scene (D5); and why the Egyptian bishops call him an enemy "since last year" (Q36).
N8. Where and when Constantine told him of the vision (M19); where and when he gave the speech on the Sepulchre (M15).
N9. The full letter to Euphration; it is held only in Athanasius's summary and the excerpts read at the Second Council of Nicaea (Q37, Q66). His letter to Constantia is named at that council (MEN 20711–20714) but its text was not read in this session.
N10. Any account of his own teaching at Caesarea, or of his pupils besides Acacius.
N11. The Greek of *Church History* I–V and of Sozomen and Theodoret's histories (not in the Library); and the Latin of Jerome's *Lives*.
N12. Not read in this session, though held: the Greek of the Caesarean creed in Hussey (Q22), the Greek of Constantine's letter to Antioch (Q27), Athanasius *De Decretis* 19–20 and *To the Africans* 5 (D2(e)), the Greek of Athanasius *De Synodis* and *To the Africans* (Q37, Q38), Rufinus's Latin of Pamphilus's *Apology* (`pamphilus_apologia-pro-origene-rufinus-lat_lommatzsch1846.txt`), and the longer *Martyrs of Palestine* beyond the lines cited.
N13. Any held source that tells a wonder done by Eusebius himself. None does; the wonders are told by him, of others (see "Wonders").
