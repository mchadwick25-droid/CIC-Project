The church remembers him by two words. A later Christian writer, Tertullian, called him "Justin, philosopher and martyr." Justin's own books tell how he became a philosopher. The record of his trial tells how he became a martyr, one who died for Christ.

Justin was born in Flavia Neapolis, in Palestine, and he called the Samaritans his people. He lived in the second century, about a hundred and fifty years after Jesus was born, by his own count. He became a teacher among the Christians of Rome.

He tells how he went from one teacher of philosophy to another, until a follower of Plato gave his mind "wings." "In my foolishness I hoped to see God at once," he wrote.

Once he went to a field near the sea to be alone. A very old man followed him, and told him of the prophets, men more ancient than the philosophers. Then he went away, and Justin did not see him again. "At once a fire was lit in my soul," Justin wrote. "A love took hold of me for the prophets, and for those men who are friends of Christ." He ended his story, "That is how, and why, I am a philosopher."

He gives a second reason too. He heard the Christians slandered, but he saw them "unafraid of death."

In those days a Christian could be put to death for the name alone. So Justin wrote to the emperor Antoninus Pius, his sons and the Roman Senate, and asked that Christians be judged by their deeds. "But in our case you take the name itself as proof," he wrote. He told the rulers, "You can kill us, but you cannot harm us." The record does not say whether the emperor answered.

In Rome, Justin questioned a philosopher named Crescens in public and found, he says, that the man knew nothing. "So I too expect to be plotted against," he wrote, perhaps by Crescens. Two later writers, Eusebius and Jerome, say Crescens did bring about his death. The record of the trial does not name him.

It says that Justin and six others were arrested and brought before Rusticus, the prefect of Rome. "First, obey the gods, and submit to the emperors," Rusticus said. When he asked where the Christians met, Justin answered, "The God of the Christians is not bounded by place." Asked again, Justin named his own lodging in Rome. "If anyone wished to come to me, I shared the words of truth with him."

Rusticus said, "So then, you are a Christian?" Justin said, "Yes, I am a Christian."

Rusticus threatened him with flogging and beheading, and asked, "So you suppose you will go up to heaven, to receive some reward?" Justin said, "I do not suppose it. I know it, and I am fully sure of it." When Rusticus ordered them all to sacrifice to the gods, Justin refused, and the others said, "Do what you want. We are Christians, and we do not sacrifice to idols."

Rusticus sentenced them to be flogged and beheaded. They gave glory to God, and at the usual place their heads were cut off. The record adds, "Some of the faithful took their bodies in secret and laid them in a fitting place."

Irenaeus, another later writer, quoted Justin's book against Marcion, a book now lost. And Eusebius wrote that many of his works were "still in the hands of many fellow believers."
