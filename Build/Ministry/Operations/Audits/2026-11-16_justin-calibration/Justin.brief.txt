A later Christian writer, Tertullian, called him "Justin, philosopher and martyr." Justin's own books tell how he became a philosopher. The record of his trial tells how he became a martyr, one who died for Christ.

Justin's family came from Flavia Neapolis, a city of Palestine, and he called the Samaritans his people. He lived in the second century, about a hundred and fifty years after Jesus was born, by his own count. He became a teacher among the Christians of Rome.

He tells how he went from one teacher of philosophy to another. Then, in a field near the sea, a very old man told him of the prophets, men more ancient than the philosophers. He went away, and Justin never saw him again. "At once a fire was lit in my soul," Justin wrote. "A love took hold of me for the prophets, and for those men who are friends of Christ." He found this alone, he wrote, to be "a philosophy that is safe and useful. That is how, and why, I am a philosopher."

Justin says that in his day Christians were condemned for the name alone. So he wrote to the emperor Antoninus Pius, his sons, the Senate and the whole Roman people. He demanded a fair trial: judge Christians by their deeds, not by their name. "You can kill us, but you cannot harm us," he told them.

Later, Justin and six others were arrested and brought before Rusticus, the prefect who governed the city of Rome. Rusticus asked if he was a Christian. Justin said, "Yes, I am a Christian."

Rusticus asked whether he was sure he would go up to heaven if he were flogged and beheaded. Justin said he hoped so. Rusticus asked, "So you suppose you will go up to heaven, to receive some reward?" Justin answered, "I do not suppose it. I know it, and I am fully sure of it."

Rusticus ordered them all to sacrifice to the gods, and threatened to punish them without mercy. The others said, "Do what you want. We are Christians, and we do not sacrifice to idols." They were sentenced to be flogged and beheaded, and so they died.

Later writers, Eusebius and Jerome, blame his death on Crescens, a philosopher whom Eusebius says Justin had often refuted. The record of the trial does not name him.

Justin had taught from his own lodging. "If anyone wished to come to me, I shared the words of truth with him," he told Rusticus. Euelpistus, who died with him, said at the trial, "I gladly listened to Justin's words." Another hearer, Tatian, called him "the most admirable Justin."

Later teachers took up his books. Irenaeus used Justin's book against Marcion, whose teaching Justin opposed, to defend the one God who made the world. Eusebius quoted Justin's defense again and again in his history of the church. "Dressed as a philosopher, he preached the divine word," Eusebius wrote, "and in his writings he fought for the faith." He also wrote that many of his works were "still in the hands of many fellow believers."
