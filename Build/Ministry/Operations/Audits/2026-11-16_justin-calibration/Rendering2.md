**Q1. Martyrdom 2: "Yes, I am a Christian"**
- Rendering 2: Rusticus said, "So, in the end, you are a Christian?" Justin said, "Yes, I am a Christian."

**Q2. Martyrdom 1: the decrees, the order, and his answers**
- Rendering 2: It was the time of the lawless champions of idol worship. Ungodly orders were posted against the godly Christians in town and country. They were meant to force them to pour offerings to worthless idols. So the holy men were arrested and brought before the prefect of Rome, a man named Rusticus. When they had been brought before his judgment seat, Rusticus the prefect said to Justin, "First, obey the gods and submit to the emperors." Justin said, "To obey the commands of our Saviour Jesus Christ deserves no blame and no condemnation." Rusticus the prefect said, "What teachings do you follow?" Justin said, "I tried to learn every teaching. But I gave my assent to the true teachings, those of the Christians, even if they do not please people who hold false beliefs."
- Note: The Greek has "First, obey", not the printed English "at once"; "kings" in the Greek means the emperors; "at last" in the printed English has no Greek word behind it.

**Q3. Martyrdom 2: where they meet**
- Rendering 2: Rusticus the prefect said, "Where do you meet?" Justin said, "Wherever each person chooses and is able. Do you really think we all meet in the same place? Not so. The God of the Christians is not bounded by any place. He is unseen, yet he fills heaven and earth. Everywhere the faithful worship him and give him glory." Rusticus the prefect said, "Tell me, where do you meet? Or in what place do you gather your disciples?" Justin said, "I live above a certain Martinus, at the bath of Timothinus. This is my second stay in the city of Rome. For all this time I have known no other meeting except his. If anyone wanted to come to me, I shared the words of truth with him."
- Note: "Martinus of the bath of Timothinus" could also mean Martinus son of Timothinus, by the bath; the printed English "Timiotinian Bath" is one reading. The Greek name is Timothinus, not the English "Timiotinian".

**Q4. Martyrdom 3: "Did Justin make you Christians?"**
- Rendering 2: Rusticus the prefect said, "Did Justin make you Christians?" Hierax said, "I was a Christian, and I will be one." Paeon stood and said, "I too am a Christian." Rusticus the prefect said, "Who taught you?" Paeon said, "We received this good confession from our parents." Euelpistus said, "I gladly listened to Justin's words. But I too learned from my parents to be a Christian."
- Note: Paeon's line ("I too am a Christian") stands in the held Greek and English but is cut by the sheet's ellipsis; it is restored here.

**Q5. Martyrdom 4: "I do not suppose it, but I know"**
- Rendering 2: The prefect said to Justin, "Listen, you who are called learned, who think you know the true teachings. If you are flogged and beheaded, are you sure you will go up to heaven?" Justin said, "I hope to have his dwellings, if I endure these things. For I know that the divine gift stays with all who have lived this way, until the whole world comes to its completion." Rusticus the prefect said, "So you suppose you will go up to heaven to receive some reward?" Justin said, "I do not suppose it. I know it, and I am fully sure of it."
- Note: "His dwellings" rests on the editor's own correction (δώματα, "scripsi"); the manuscripts read δόγματα ("his teachings"), and the printed English "His gifts" follows a third reading, δόματα. The noun is uncertain; avoid building on it.

**Q6. Martyrdom 4: "No right-thinking person"**
- Rendering 2: Rusticus the prefect said, "Now let us come to the matter in front of us, which is pressing. Come together, then, and all of you sacrifice to the gods." Justin said, "No one in his right mind falls away from godliness into godlessness."

**Q7. Martyrdom 4: "Do what you will"**
- Rendering 2: Rusticus the prefect said, "If you do not obey, you will be punished without mercy." Justin said, "It is our prayer to be punished for the sake of our Lord Jesus Christ, and so to be saved. For this will be our salvation and our confidence before the more fearful judgment seat of our Master and Saviour, where the whole world is judged." The other martyrs said the same: "Do what you want. We are Christians, and we do not sacrifice to idols."
- Note: Δι᾽ εὐχῆς ἔχομεν is read as the idiom "we pray for, we long for"; the printed English takes it as "through prayer we can be saved", and its own footnote allows the other reading.

**Q8. Martyrdom 5: sentence, death and burial**
- Rendering 2: Rusticus the prefect gave sentence: "Those who have refused to sacrifice to the gods and to yield to the emperor's command are to be flogged and led away. They are to suffer the penalty of beheading, as the laws require." The holy martyrs gave glory to God and went out to the usual place. There they were beheaded. They completed their witness by confessing the Saviour. Some of the faithful secretly took their bodies and laid them in a fitting place. The grace of our Lord Jesus Christ worked with them. To him be glory for ever and ever. Amen.
- Note: The printed Greek has δοξασθέντες ("having been glorified"), not the sheet's δοξασ[αντες]; the object "God" requires the sense "giving glory", which is rendered.

**Q9. First Apology 1: the address**
- Rendering 2: To the Emperor Titus Aelius Hadrian Antoninus Pius Augustus Caesar, and to his son Verissimus the Philosopher. To Lucius, son of Caesar the Philosopher by birth and of Pius by adoption, a lover of learning. To the sacred Senate and the whole Roman people. I, Justin, son of Priscus, son of Bacchius, have written this address and petition. My family comes from Flavia Neapolis in Syria Palestine. I write for people of every nation who are unjustly hated and mistreated. I am one of them myself.
- Note: The Greek can mean Lucius is the natural son of "Caesar the Philosopher" (so read here) or, as the printed English has it, "Lucius the Philosopher, natural son of Caesar". The printed English drops "Syria" and adds "wantonly".

**Q10. First Apology 2: "You can kill, but not hurt us"**
- Rendering 2: We have not come to flatter you with this writing, or to speak to win your favour. We have come to ask you to judge by careful and searching reason. Do not let prejudice hold you, or a wish to please superstitious people. Do not let unthinking impulse or long-standing evil rumour lead you to cast a vote against yourselves. For we reckon that no one can do us any harm, unless we are shown to be doers of evil or judged to be wicked. You can kill us, but you cannot harm us.

**Q11. First Apology 4: punished for the name**
- Rendering 2: You punish none of those accused before you until they are proved guilty. But in our case you take the name itself as proof. Yet as far as the name goes, you ought rather to punish our accusers. We are accused of being Christians. But to hate what is good is unjust. Again, if one of the accused denies it aloud and says he is not one, you let him go. You have nothing to prove him a wrongdoer. But if anyone admits he is one, you punish him for that admission. You ought instead to examine the life of the one who confesses and of the one who denies. Then each one's deeds would show what kind of person he is.
- Note: "Good" is χρηστόν (chrēstos), a play on "Christian" (Christianos); the printed English marks it "(Chrestian)".

**Q12. Dialogue 1: "Hail, O philosopher!"**
- Rendering 2: One morning I was walking in the covered walks of the Xystus. A man met me, with others in his company, and said, "Greetings, philosopher!" As soon as he had said this, he turned and walked along with me. His friends turned with him too. I greeted him in turn and said, "What is it?" He said, "In Argos I was taught by Corinthus the Socratic not to despise or ignore people who wear this garb. Instead, I should show them every kindness and talk with them."
- Note: The Xystus is a colonnaded walk; the passage does not name its city.

**Q13. Dialogue 2: "I expected forthwith to look upon God"**
- Rendering 2: The grasp of bodiless things took hold of me strongly, and so did the contemplation of the Forms. It gave my mind wings. In a short time I thought I had become wise. In my foolishness I hoped to see God at once. For that is the goal of Plato's philosophy.
- Note: "Ideas" here are Plato's Forms; the modern word "ideas" would mislead.

**Q14. Dialogue 3: the old man by the sea**
- Rendering 2: That was my state of mind. Once I wanted to be filled with deep quiet and to keep off the beaten paths of men. So I made my way to a field not far from the sea. I was near that place, where I meant to be alone with myself. Then a very old man followed me, a little way behind. He was not at all someone to look down on. His manner was gentle and dignified.
- Note: "The path of men" echoes Homer (Iliad 6.202); the editor's bracketed reference is not part of the text.

**Q15. Dialogue 8: the flame**
- Rendering 2: He said these things and many more, which there is no time to tell now. Then he went away, telling me to pursue them. I have not seen him since. At once a fire was lit in my soul. A love took hold of me for the prophets, and for those men who are friends of Christ. As I turned his words over in my mind, I found this philosophy alone to be safe and useful. That is how, and why, I am a philosopher.

**Q16. Second Apology 12: "fearless of death"**
- Rendering 2: I myself once delighted in the teachings of Plato. I heard Christians slandered. Yet I saw them unafraid of death, and of everything else people count fearful. I realised it was impossible for them to be living in wickedness and the love of pleasure.

**Q17. Second Apology 13: "to be found a Christian"**
- Rendering 2: The evil demons had thrown a wicked cloak over the divine teachings of the Christians, to turn other people away. When I learned this, I laughed at those who spread these lies. I laughed at the cloak itself, and at what the crowd believed. I confess that I pray, and strive with all my strength, to be found a Christian. This is not because Plato's teachings are foreign to Christ's. It is because they are not alike in every way. Neither are those of the others: the Stoics, the poets and the prose writers. … So whatever has been said well among all people belongs to us Christians.
- Note: The held Greek reads παρὰ καλῶς with no object; the sheet's "[πᾶσι]" ("among all") is not in the file, but the sense needs it and the printed English has it. Εὐχόμενος can mean "praying" or "boasting"; the printed English takes "boast".

**Q18. First Apology 46: "those who lived reasonably are Christians"**
- Rendering 2: We have been taught that Christ is the firstborn of God. We have already shown that he is the Word, in whom every human race has shared. Those who lived with the Word are Christians, even if they were thought godless. Among the Greeks there were Socrates, Heraclitus and others like them. Among the non-Greeks there were Abraham, Ananias, Azarias, Mishael, Elijah and many others. We know it would take too long to list their deeds or their names, so we pass over them for now.
- Note: Μετὰ λόγου means both "with reason" and "with the Word"; the printed English "reasonably" keeps only the first. "Godless" (ἄθεοι) was the charge of rejecting the gods, not modern atheism. Ananias, Azarias and Mishael are Daniel's three companions.

**Q19. First Apology 67: Sunday**
- Rendering 2: On the day called Sunday, all who live in towns or in the country gather in one place. The memoirs of the apostles or the writings of the prophets are read, as long as time allows. When the reader has finished, the one who presides speaks. He warns us and calls us to imitate these good things. … Those who are well off, and willing, each give what they want, as each one chooses. What is collected is left with the one who presides. He helps orphans and widows, and those in need through sickness or any other cause. He helps those in prison, and the strangers staying among us. In short, he takes care of everyone in need.
- Note: "Sunday" renders "the day called the Sun's"; "the one who presides" replaces "president", whose modern sense misleads.

**Q20. First Apology 66: "not as common bread"**
- Rendering 2: We do not receive these as ordinary bread or ordinary drink. Jesus Christ our Saviour was made flesh through the Word of God. He had both flesh and blood for our salvation. In the same way, we have been taught about the food over which thanks has been given by a prayer of the word that comes from him. By its change, our blood and flesh are nourished. We have been taught that this food is the flesh and blood of that Jesus who was made flesh.
- Note: "A prayer of the word that comes from him" (δι᾽ εὐχῆς λόγου τοῦ παρ᾽ αὐτοῦ) is disputed: it may mean a prayer in his words or a prayer for the Word. "By its change" (κατὰ μεταβολήν) most likely means the body's digestion of it.

**Q21. Second Apology 3 [AP2 8]: Crescens**
- Rendering 2: So I too expect to be plotted against and fastened to the stake by one of those I have named. Or perhaps by Crescens, that lover of noise and boasting.
- Note: Φιλόψοφος ("lover of noise") is a pun on φιλόσοφος ("philosopher"). Eusebius's text reads "put in the stocks", so "stake" is not certain.

**Q22. Second Apology 2: Lucius**
- Rendering 2: Urbicus ordered him to be led away to punishment. A man named Lucius, himself also a Christian, saw how unreasonably the judgment had been given. He said to Urbicus, "What is the charge? This man has not been convicted of adultery, sexual immorality, murder, theft or robbery, or of any wrong at all. He only confesses that he bears the name of Christian. Why have you punished him? Your judgment, Urbicus, does not befit the Emperor Pius. Nor does it befit the Philosopher, Caesar's son, or the sacred Senate." Urbicus gave no other answer, but said to Lucius, "You seem to me to be one of them too." Lucius said, "Certainly." So Urbicus ordered that he too be led away. Lucius said he was grateful. He knew he was being freed from such wicked masters, and was going to the Father and King of heaven.

**Q23. Dialogue 142: the parting**
- Rendering 2: After a pause, Trypho said, "You see that it was not by design that we came to discuss these things. I admit that I have been especially pleased by our meeting. I think these friends feel as I do. We have found more than we expected, more than anyone could ever have expected. … But since you are about to set out, and expect to sail any day, do not hesitate to remember us as friends when you have gone." "For my part," I said, "if I had stayed, I would have wished to do the same every day. But now I expect to set sail soon, if God allows and helps. I urge you to take up this great struggle for your own salvation. Be eager to honour the Christ of God Almighty above your own teachers." After this they left. They wished me safety on the voyage and from every harm. I prayed for them and said, "Gentlemen, I can pray for nothing greater for you than this. May you recognise that by this way happiness is given to every person. Then you too may come to think as we do, that Jesus is the Christ of God."
- Note: The held Greek has εὐδαιμονεῖν ("to be happy, blessed"), not the printed English "intelligence", and ὅμοια ποιήσητε ("do as we do"), which does not cleanly govern "that Jesus is the Christ". The last two sentences are uncertain; hold them or say so.

**Q24. First Apology 68: "honour them ... or despise them"**
- Rendering 2: If these things seem reasonable and true to you, honour them. If they seem nonsense to you, despise them as nonsense. But do not decree death against people who have done no wrong, as if against enemies. We warn you in advance: you will not escape the coming judgment of God if you persist in injustice. For our part, we will cry out: "What pleases God, let that be done!"
- Note: The Greek's last clause is a cry, "What pleases God, let that be done!"; the printed English "we ourselves will invite you to do that which is pleasing to God" does not follow it.

**Q36. Dialogue 120: "my people, that is, the Samaritans"**
- Rendering 2: "I would say these things," I said, "because I care about nothing except telling the truth. I will not be shamed into silence by anyone, even if you tear me limb from limb on the spot. When I wrote to Caesar, I took no account of anyone of my own race, I mean the Samaritans. I said they were deceived in trusting the magician Simon, from their own race. They say he is God, above every rule, authority and power."
- Note: "Race" renders γένος (people, nation); how Justin belonged to the Samaritans is not settled by this line.

**Q37. Dialogue 9: the cloak held**
- Rendering 2: Then his companions laughed again and shouted rudely. I got up and was about to leave. But he took hold of my cloak. He said he would not let me go until I had done what I promised.
- Note: The Greek says "he would not let go", not the printed English "I should not accomplish that".

**Q38. First Apology 31: Barchochebas**
- Rendering 2: In the Jewish war that has just taken place, Barchochebas was the leader of the Jewish revolt. He ordered that only Christians be taken off to terrible punishments, unless they denied Jesus the Christ and blasphemed.
- Note: Barchochebas is Simon bar Kokhba.

**Q25. Irenaeus, Against Heresies IV.6.2: "I would not have believed the Lord Himself"**
- Rendering 2: In his book against Marcion, Justin rightly says: "I would not have believed the Lord himself, if he had proclaimed anyone other than the one who formed us, made us and feeds us. But the only-begotten Son came to us from the one God. That God made this world and formed us. He holds and governs all things. The Son gathered up his own handiwork in himself. So my faith in him is firm, and my love for the Father cannot be shaken. God gives us both."
- Note: Rendered from the printed English only; it is a translation of Irenaeus's Latin, itself quoting a lost Greek work.

**Q26. Irenaeus, Against Heresies I.28.1: Tatian, "a hearer of Justin's"**
- Rendering 2: A man named Tatian first brought in this blasphemy. He had been a hearer of Justin. As long as he stayed with him, he voiced no such views. But after Justin's martyrdom he broke away from the Church. He was carried away and puffed up by the thought of being a teacher, as if he stood above others. He put together his own distinct form of teaching.

**Q27. Tatian, Address 18: "the most admirable Justin"**
- Rendering 2: Demons do not heal. They use their skill to take people captive. And the most admirable Justin rightly said that the beings just named are like robbers.
- Note: The Greek says "like robbers", not the printed English "denounced them as robbers".

**Q28. Tatian, Address 19: Crescens**
- Rendering 2: Crescens made his nest in the great city. He outdid everyone in pederasty, and he was wholly given to the love of money. He was the one who despised death. Yet he feared death so much that he worked to bring it on Justin, and on me too, as if death were an evil. He did this because Justin, preaching the truth, had shown the philosophers to be gluttons and cheats.
- Note: The Greek leaves "preaching the truth" without a named subject; Justin is the natural reading, and Eusebius's version takes it so. The OCR is faulty here ("αὐτὸν ἐδεδέεε" in the file).

**Q29. Tertullian, Against the Valentinians 5: "Justin, philosopher and martyr"**
- Rendering 2: …in carefully written books, by so many outstandingly holy and excellent men. They include not only those who lived before us, but also those who lived at the same time as the founders of these heresies. One of them was Justin, philosopher and martyr.
- Note: Rendered from the printed English only; the Latin was not found.

**Q30. Eusebius IV.11.8: "In the guise of a philosopher"**
- Rendering 2: But Justin stood out above all in those days. Wearing the dress of a philosopher, he preached the divine word. In his writings he fought for the faith.
- Note: The Greek is ἐν σχήματι φιλοσόφου, "in a philosopher's garb"; "guise" now suggests pretence, which the Greek does not.

**Q31. Eusebius IV.16.1: "crowned with divine martyrdom"**
- Rendering 2: About this time Justin, whom we mentioned just above, addressed a second book defending our teachings to the rulers already named. He was then crowned with a divine martyrdom. This came from a plot laid against him by Crescens. Crescens was a philosopher who copied the life and ways of the Cynics, and bore their name. Justin had often refuted him in public debates. By his martyrdom Justin won the prize of victory, dying for the truth he preached.

**Q32. Eusebius IV.18.1, IV.18.8: his works kept**
- Rendering 2: This writer has left us a great many works from a mind trained and practised in the things of God. They are full of useful matter of every kind. … A great many other works of his are still in the hands of many fellow believers.

**Q33. Jerome, Lives 23**
- Rendering 2: Justin was a philosopher, and he wore a philosopher's dress. He was a citizen of Neapolis, a city of Palestine, and the son of Priscus Bacchius. He worked hard for the religion of Christ. He went so far as to hand a work written against the pagans to Antoninus Pius, his sons and the Senate. He did not shrink from the shame of the cross. … He held teaching sessions in the city of Rome. Crescens the Cynic said many blasphemous things against the Christians. Justin exposed him as a glutton and a man afraid of death. He proved him devoted to luxury and lust. At last he was accused of being a Christian, through Crescens' efforts and tricks. He shed his blood for Christ.
- Note: Rendered from the printed English; "Against the nations" may be a title or a description ("against the pagans"). Διατριβάς may mean discussions or a school.

**Q34. Jerome, Chronicle (Latin)**
- Rendering 2: Justin the philosopher handed Antoninus a book written in defence of our religion. … Crescens the Cynic comes to notice. Justin, a philosopher of our faith, kept exposing him as a glutton and a traitor to philosophy. So Crescens stirred up a persecution against Justin. In it Justin shed his blood for Christ in glory.
- Note: "Agnoscitur" is the chronicle's formula for "is known, flourishes". "Praeuaricator" is one who betrays the cause he claims to serve.

**Q35. Epiphanius, Panarion 46.1 (Greek, OCR)**
- Rendering 2: …was in his prime at the same time as Justin the philosopher. Justin was a holy man and a friend of God, who came from the Samaritans to faith in Christ. This Justin was a Samaritan by race, but he came to believe in Christ. He trained himself greatly and showed a life of virtue. In the end he bore witness for Christ and was found worthy of the perfect crown. This was in Rome, under the governor Rusticus and the emperor Hadrian. He was thirty years old, in the settled prime of life.
- Note: The subject of "was in his prime" falls before the excerpt. "Hadrian" and "thirty years old" are Epiphanius's claims and clash with the Rusticus date; tag them as contested if used.
