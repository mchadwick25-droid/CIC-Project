# The outline

The researcher fills this in for one person, from the source sheet. One page, no prose. Every line points to its place in a held source text (file and line). The writer gets this outline, `Shapes.md` and `Standard.md`, and nothing else.

- **The heart of their life:** the major contributions and the essence of their work, told as substance: what they did and made, what it held, and what it changed. For a writer, what the book says. For a founder, what was founded and how it lived. For a council, what it decided and what that did to the churches. Set it first, from the census entry and what the person is known for, then check every claim in it against the Library. Where the sources will not hold a claim, say what they do hold. It is opened in the first minute of the story and carries the rest.
- **Length:** set last, once the beats, Do not say and Leave out are fixed: as long as the heart and what must stay in need, and no longer. Up to about 3 minutes for a person and about 4 minutes 15 for a council; as short as 1 minute when the record is thin. Never fill to reach a length, and never cut the record's force to meet one. Words follow the voice as heard: about 150 a minute for a person (Neo), about 130 for a council (Daniel).
- **Setting:** the century or year, the place and the tradition, each with its supporting lines and the plainest spoken word for it. These are facts for the writer, not a sentence to paste.
- **Shape:** one of the shapes in `Shapes.md`.
- **Openings:** one or two things from the record that could draw a listener in and lead straight into the heart of their life: a moment, an image, their own words, someone else's words about them, or a question the record raises. It must make plain sense heard once.
- **Story beats:** 5 to 7 events, in plain paraphrase, arranged so the heart of their life is told in the first minute and the rest deepens it. Keep time order where it serves; step out of it where the heart would otherwise come late. Each beat gives the reason for the act, the meaning of any word or custom that would puzzle a newcomer, its source, and its confidence level.
- **What came of them:** ranked heaviest first, where weight is what later witnesses carried on and used, not what is easiest to tell. What they defended is marked. This part carries about a third of the story.
- **Quotes, 2 or 3 at most:** each with its modern rendering, its source, and the job it does in the story.
- **Connection to carry into the story:** what specifically passed from this person or event to another person, community, text, practice or decision. Give the supporting passage, and state what the listener needs to understand about that relationship. Naming a later person or event is not enough: say what the writer must explain where a listener would otherwise have to infer it. It should come through source-supported action, never a forced "this matters because" sentence or an appended moral.
- **Links:** each tie to another person on the tree, labelled by kind: met, heard him teach, wrote to, kept his letters, quoted his book, wrote his life, kept his feast.
- **Who tells it:** for each main source, who wrote it and whether in the person's own time or later; where it matters, what the listener must know about the witness, for the writer to place where the listener needs it. How-we-know gets a sentence or two in the story, unless the doubt is itself the story; name which.
- **Do not say:** the wordings a writer could easily get wrong from this outline, each with the right wording beside it. These include a wonder that must stay in its witness's voice, a qualifier that must not be dropped ("partly", "forty or more"), an outcome or motive the record does not give, and a detail a writer might add for colour. When a later check or review finds a writer's error that the outline left room for, it is added here.
- **Leave out:** what the record holds that this story should not carry, and why: doubtful claims, side stories. Never the record's force, coercion or conflict: those are told as plainly as its virtues.

Confidence uses the project's five levels: Documented, Widely Accepted, Dominant Modern Reconstruction, Contested, Inferential-Thin.
