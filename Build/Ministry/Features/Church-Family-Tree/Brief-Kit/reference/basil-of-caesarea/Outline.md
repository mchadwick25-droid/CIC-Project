# Outline: Basil of Caesarea (`basil-of-caesarea`)

Line references are to `Source_Sheet.md` rows (S, E, R, P, B, H, Sp, D, I), each of which gives the file and its lines. Files are under `cic/texts/`.

- **The essence:** Basil of Caesarea gave up teaching rhetoric for a life of prayer and shared work. He wrote the rules that taught monks to live together and serve one another, built a great lodging for the poor and the sick a little way outside Caesarea, and as its bishop from 370 defended the Holy Spirit's place beside the Father and the Son. He died in 378 or 379, before the council of 381 confessed it. (E3-E4, R3-R7, H1-H4, B1, Sp1-Sp7, D2, I5)
- **Also told:**
  - The rules: monks should live together, not alone, because love does not seek its own good and a man alone has no one to serve. They are answers to his brothers' questions. (R5-R7; Documented)
  - The house: a lodging for strangers, travellers and the sick, with doctors and transport, stocked by the rich at his urging; lepers driven from their towns were taken in. (H1, H3, H4; Documented, H4 in Gregory's voice)
  - The Spirit: he was attacked for closing prayer in two forms, 'with the Son together with the Spirit' and 'through the Son in the Spirit', as new words that contradicted each other. He answered from what the church already did: as we are baptized, so we believe and so we praise. His friend says he held back the bare words "the Spirit is God" in writing, from caution. (Sp1, Sp4, Sp6, Sp7; Documented, Sp7 in Gregory's voice)
  - The cost: an emperor's official threatened him with loss of goods, exile, torture and death; he faced a quarrel with his own bishop and a rival bishop who blocked his road. (B2-B4, P2; Documented as Gregory's account, B3 confirms)

- **Openings:**
  1. His own words (E4, Quote 1): years later, defending himself in a letter, Basil looked back. He had wasted nearly all his youth, he wrote, in the vain labour of learning a wisdom God had made foolish. Then Quote 1. Leads straight to the essence.
  2. Gregory's new city (H3): a little way out of Caesarea, Gregory of Nazianzus told the mourners, stood a "new city", filled by the rich at Basil's urging and open to the sick. Use only if Opening 1 is not used; Beat 5 then drops H3.
- **Story beats:**
  1. **Athens and the turn (c. 350-358).** He studied at Athens with Gregory of Nazianzus (E1), and came home to Caesarea, where the city welcomed him (E2). He taught rhetoric, the historian Rufinus says (E3). Who turned him away from it? In his own telling, he woke to the gospel: he read in it of selling goods and sharing with the poor, and went looking for monks in Egypt, Palestine, Syria and Mesopotamia (E4). If Opening 1 is used, do not tell the waking again. Go straight to 'he read in the gospel of selling goods and sharing with the poor, and went looking for monks'. His brother Gregory of Nyssa gives the credit to their sister Macrina (E5). Rufinus says his friend Gregory took him from the teacher's chair (E6). Reason: the gospel, in his own telling. Confidence: the turn Documented; who caused it Contested; teaching rhetoric Widely Accepted (Rufinus).
  2. **Pontus and the rules.** In Pontus, near the Black Sea, he founded a community and led it, Gregory says (R1). Rufinus says he went through the towns and countryside, teaching people to come together, build monasteries, pray, and care for the poor (R2). His friend calls his rules "legislation" for monks (R3), and says he brought hermits and communities together rather than walling them apart (R4). The rules answer his brothers' questions; one asks whether to live alone or with others (R5). Basil answers that love does not seek its own good (R6), and that Christ washed his disciples' feet: Quote 2 (R7). Plain sense: a man alone has no one to serve. Confidence: Documented; the Rules' date not held (R8).
  3. **Priest, quarrel and famine (c. 364-369).** The bishop of Caesarea made him a priest (P1). They quarrelled; Gregory will not say why. The monks wanted to break away with him, but Basil went back to Pontus rather than split the church (P2). When the emperor Valens moved against the churches that held the Son equal to the Father, he came back and served the bishop (P3, S4). In a great famine he moved the owners of grain to open their stores, gathered the hungry and, with his servants, fed them soup and meat and tended them himself, Gregory says (P4). Confidence: Documented; the famine's year 368 is the editors' (P4).
  4. **Bishop, and the emperor's official (370-372).** The old bishop died in Basil's arms, Gregory says, and Basil was chosen against the bishops of the province (B1). The emperor's highest official ordered him to accept the emperor's faith, and threatened him with loss of his goods, exile, torture and death. In Gregory's telling Basil answered that a man who owns nothing but rags and a few books cannot be robbed, every land is God's, and death would only send him sooner to God. The official said no one had spoken to him so boldly; perhaps, said Basil, he had never met a bishop (B2). A later historian, Sozomen, tells the same threat of death and refusal (B3). When the province was divided, a rival bishop claimed part of Basil's churches and once blocked his road with armed men (B4). Basil answered by making more bishops: his brother Gregory at Nyssa, his friend Gregory at Sasima, a hurt the friend says time never healed (B5). Confidence: Documented as Gregory's account; B3 Widely Accepted.
  5. **The new city (c. 372).** Accused before the governor, Basil wrote to him: whom does it harm to build a lodging for strangers, for travellers and for the sick, with doctors and transport? (H1, H2, paraphrase; the letter's date is the editors'.) Gregory says it stood a little way from the city, stocked by the rich at Basil's urging (H3). Lepers, once driven from their towns and families, were taken in. Basil greeted them as brothers and went to tend them himself, Gregory says (H4). Confidence: Documented; H4 in Gregory's voice.
  6. **The Spirit (c. 374-375).** Praying with his people, Basil closed with praise to God the Father "with the Son, together with the Holy Spirit", and at other times "through the Son, in the Holy Spirit" (Sp1, paraphrase). Some who were there attacked him for new words that contradicted each other (Sp1). They held, he reports, that the Spirit could not be ranked with the Father and the Son, being lower in nature and rank (Sp4). At a friend's request he wrote On the Holy Spirit (Sp2, Sp3). His answer came from what the church already did (Sp5). In a letter he put it this way: Quote 3 (Sp6). Plain sense: the church baptizes in the name of Father, Son and Spirit, so it must praise them alike. His friend Gregory admits he held back the bare words "the Spirit is God" in writing; Gregory says enemies were waiting for them to drive him out, and that Basil preached and confessed it (Sp7). Confidence: Documented; Sp7 in Gregory's voice.
- **What came of them** (ranked; about a third of the story):
  1. **His rule.** Rufinus of Aquileia put his works, the Rule among them, into Latin (I1). In the sixth century Benedict of Nursia, closing his own Rule, pointed his monks to "the Rule of our holy Father Basil" as an example of good living (I2, paraphrase, not a quote). In 787 a council told the double monasteries still standing to keep the rule of Basil (I3). Optional, with the connection: the tree's own tradition entry says his rules remain foundational for Eastern Orthodox monasticism (I9).
  2. **What he defended: the Spirit's place.** A council at Constantinople in 381, after his death, confessed the Spirit worshipped and glorified with the Father and the Son (I5). State it beside his fight; do not say the council followed him. His brother carried on his answer to Eunomius (sibling clause; I6 for the card).
  3. **His house.** Two generations later Sozomen wrote that it still bore his name, the Basileias, and was the most famous lodging for the poor (I7).

- **Connection to carry into the story:** Basil's pattern of monks living together under a rule, to serve one another and the poor, passed to the West in Latin. Rufinus translated the Rule into Latin in 397 or 398 (I1). In the sixth century Benedict of Nursia, at the close of his own Rule, set 'the Rule of our holy Father Basil' before his monks as an example (I2). In 787 a council told the double monasteries already standing to keep his rule (I3). The writer must make plain that Rufinus put the Rule into Latin, and that Benedict names it as a model to read, not as the rule his monks kept. Do not say which Latin text Benedict read. Carry it in Beat 2's rule and the impact, through what Rufinus and Benedict did.
- **Ending:**
