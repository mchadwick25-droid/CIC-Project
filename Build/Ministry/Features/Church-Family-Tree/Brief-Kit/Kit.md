# The Church Family Tree brief kit

One kit for every text a visitor reads or hears on the Church Family Tree, and for any fix to a live one. Part 1 is the standard every text meets. Part 2 is the outline the researcher fills in. Part 3 is how a text is made and judged. What is particular to each kind of text, its purpose, heart, shapes and length, lives in its own kind kit in `kinds/`: `person.md`, `council.md`, `tradition.md`, `story.md` (documented and map stories), `connection-note.md` and `era.md`. Each step has one role prompt in `roles/`, which points to this kit and the kind kit and adds only what its step does; no step runs from a prompt of its own. Every standing rule lives once.

## Part 1. The standard

Every text lets an interested non-specialist meet one part of the church's history: a person, a council, a tradition, a happening, a link between two of them, or an era. It is heard once (a connection note is read), by adults, many of them in a second language. Its kind kit and its outline set its length, shorter when the record is thin.

**The test.** After one hearing or reading, the listener can explain who or what this was, what happened, what it was about, and why the connection matters. Meet it without making the story simpler. The kind kit says what each line means for it.

### Craft

- One adult documentary narrator who knows the record well tells it, in the register of a BBC or National Geographic documentary: never quotations linked by commentary, never a report about the record, never talking down.
- **Explain it or leave it out.** Each thing the story names (a book, sermon, letter, teaching, charge, quarrel or ruling, or a label such as "heresy") says in plain words what it was, and why it mattered to the people in it. Each act of conflict or punishment (an arrest, exile, condemnation or break) says why it happened, where the old sources give the reason; where they give only the act, tell it as they give it, with no reason added and no word on the missing one, since the record's force is never cut. "His ideas" or "his reforms" never stands alone; ordinary acts, such as a journey or a founding, need no gloss. If there is no room to explain it, or the old sources do not, leave it out: do not fill the gap, and do not announce it unless the gap is itself the story.
- Name a person only when they act in the story or link to another page on the tree; give anyone else by role. Past about six names, ask whether each is needed.
- Link ideas with cause, contrast and time, as an adult narrator does: a longer sentence for a chain of events, a short one for a turn. Aim to keep sentences under about 30 words, and avoid runs of short sentences that each tell one act. These are targets, not cuts: never split or trim a sentence that runs a word or two over.
- It should sound like one person telling it, not a script. No moral or summing-up close. No sentence that only says what is missing. No "not X but Y" turn, no dash for drama, no question asked only to answer it, and no stock word (legacy, profound, testament, tapestry, enduring, remarkable).

**In a narrated story** (every kind but a connection note):

- Open on one moment, image or line from the record, in about three sentences, bringing the name in where the moment allows, never as a held-back reveal. Then say the essence plainly, as its kind kit defines it. Both come within the first minute. Then tell it once, in order, never the same event twice and never the ending and then back. Let time, place and tradition come in a clause at a time.
- Keep the story's own question alive: the risk, quarrel or choice the record holds, carried through to where it resolves. Hold the listener with what happened and what people said, never with the narrator's drama.
- Give the connection room to become clear, through what people did: what they left the church and what they defended. End on the subject itself: a scene, its own words, a death or an end, or the opening image again; on a later writer only when that is the story's point.

### Rigor

- **Tell only what the old sources hold:** the words of the people in the story and the writers of the record, at or near the time, as the outline gives them. Every claim, date, and "so" or "because" stands on a line from one of them. Add no feeling or detail for effect, and honor the outline's Do not say list.
- **A modern work is a tool, never a witness.** It may clarify an old text (its edition, its translation, what a word or custom meant) or lead to the sources of that period. It is never named in the story, and never used for its opinion or speculation. A date, identification or judgment that only a modern work makes, or that the outline rates Dominant Modern Reconstruction or Inferential-Thin, stays out of the story and the card. A date the old sources fix, or one so settled it is Widely Accepted, such as the fire of Rome in 64, is told plainly; a date that rests on one editor's reckoning is told only as roughly as the old sources allow ("in his last years"), or left out. A modern event in the record, such as a lost text found, may be told as an event. An era survey is the one exception: it also draws on the broad historical scholarship, as `kinds/era.md` says.
- **We are a witness, not a judge.** Tell what the old sources agree on in the narrator's own voice. Name a witness only where the listener would otherwise be misled: where the old sources differ (say so, and credit each), for a wonder (it stays in its witness's voice), for a judgment of praise or blame (the facts the same witness reports are told plainly), for an enemy's charge, or where the source wrote long after. When the whole story rests on one witness, say so once, early, inside a sentence about that witness, never as a stock how-we-know sentence. Where the doubt is itself the story, make it a scene. People who speak and act in the story are part of it, not citations. Give an enemy's charge in its own content, never dropped because only an enemy says it. Never carry one side's view forward, and never soften the record by leaving out its force or conflict. Say plainly what is uncertain, without hedging.
- Use only the outline's quotes, word for word, and only where their words do what the narrator's cannot. A quote unclear in English by itself may stand with its plain sense alongside.
- **The four faults** every hand re-reads its own work for: a claim told as fact where the old sources differ, or a wonder, praise or blame told as the narrator's; a modern view told as the record, named or not; a date or detail moved onto the wrong event; and the record's force or conflict softened by leaving it out.

### Accessibility

- Plain modern words: simplify the language, never the thought. Use the precise words a grown listener knows, and work any puzzling word or custom into the sentence as part of what happens.
- The audio tells the story. The card carries the rest: the Original sources list, the fuller qualifications and the debates among the old sources, and a Modern works list for further reading, each work with one line on what it covers, never what it argues.
- Aim for reading grade 8 to 9. Grade 10 and reading ease 60 are hard limits; inside them a score is a warning for the listening review, never a goal. Never simplify to move it.

## Part 2. The outline

The researcher fills this in for one text, from the source sheet. One page, no prose. Every line points to its place in a held source (file and line). A connection note fills only the fields its kind kit names.

- **The heart:** the fields the kind kit names, set first, from the census entry and what the subject is known for, then checked against the Library. Where the sources will not hold a claim, say what they do hold. Its first field is **the essence**, one or two plain sentences told within the first minute.
- **Length:** set last, once the beats, Do not say and Leave out are fixed: as long as the heart and what must stay in need, and no longer. Count the words the story must carry first, its quotes, the plain sense of each word to explain and each witness it credits, then set the time from the kind kit's range. Words follow the voice as heard: about 150 a minute, 130 for a council. Plan only the beats that count can carry: whatever else the record holds goes to Leave out or to the card, named there. End the outline with a **Cut first** list, in order, each item marked for the card or Leave out, that brings the count to the approved length. Never fill to reach a length. To meet one, never cut the record's force, the explanation Part 1 requires (Explain it), or the scene that carries the story's craft: cut an event before its meaning.

- **Setting:** the century or year, the place and the tradition, each with its supporting lines and the plainest spoken word for it. These are facts for the writer, not a sentence to paste. The writer weaves them in a clause at a time, never all in one sentence.
- **Words to explain:** each church or period word a general listener would not know at once, with a plain word to use instead or a short gloss to work into the sentence where it is first heard. Words a grown listener knows, such as bishop, deacon, Scripture, gospel and church, are not glossed. Do not say bars each listed word used without its plain sense the first time.
- **Shape:** one of the kind kit's shapes, where it has them.
- **Sibling:** any story on the tree or in the batch that shares an event or a text with this one, with the job each does, as the slate set it. The researcher reads the sibling's outline or live story first. Write "none" if there is none.
- **Openings:** one or two things from the record, each told in about three sentences, that draw a listener in and lead straight into the essence: a moment, an image, their own words, someone else's words about them, or a question the record raises. It must make plain sense heard once.
- **Story beats:** 5 to 7 events, in plain paraphrase, fewer for a short kind. After the hook and the essence, the beats follow the story once, in time order. No event is in two beats, and the ending is not told early. The hook and the essence may reach ahead; the rest does not go back. Each beat gives the reason for the act, the meaning of any word or custom that would puzzle a newcomer, its source, and its confidence level. For each thing or act it names, it also gives the explanation Part 1 requires (Explain it), in one plain line with its source.
- **What came of it:** ranked heaviest first, where weight is what later witnesses carried on and used, not what is easiest to tell. What was defended is marked. Optional: one line on who keeps this person, text or practice today, only where a held source or the tree's own sourced tradition entry says it, with that source, told with what came of them and never as the close. Otherwise leave it out.
- **Quotes, 2 or 3 at most:** each with its modern rendering, its source, and the job it does in the story. A quote another story on the tree already uses may be used again only where its author or subject touched both people or traditions, and it does a new job here, never the same event told the same way. The slate lists each reuse.
- **Names:** each person the story will name, marked "acts" or "link", with the spelling used across the tree. Anyone else is given by role.
- **Connection to carry into the story:** what specifically passed from this person or event to another person, community, text, practice or decision. Give the supporting passage, and state what the listener needs to understand about that relationship. Naming a later person or event is not enough: say what the writer must explain where a listener would otherwise have to infer it. It should come through source-supported action, never a forced "this matters because" sentence or an appended moral.
- **Ending:** one or two ways to close on the subject, from the record: a scene, their words, their death, or the opening image seen again. Give the supporting lines.
- **Links:** each tie to another entry on the tree, labeled by kind, such as met, taught, wrote to, quoted, wrote the life of, kept the feast of, continued as or broke with.
- **Who tells it:** for each main source, who wrote it and whether in the subject's own time or later; where it matters, what the listener must know about the witness. Mark each source old (the original sources and the writers of the record itself) or modern (an editor, a translator's note or a historian writing on the record). A modern source is marked for what it does: clarifies an old text or finds one, or makes a claim of its own (Part 1, Rigor). Mark the claim each witness is credited beside. If the whole story rests on one witness, say where the story says so. If the doubt is itself the story, name the scene that tells it.
- **Do not say:** the wordings a writer could easily get wrong from this outline, each with the right wording beside it. These include a wonder that must stay in its witness's voice, a qualifier that must not be dropped ("partly", "forty or more"), an outcome or motive the record does not give, and a detail a writer might add for color. When a later check or review finds a writer's error that the outline left room for, it is added here.
- **Leave out:** what the record holds that this story should not carry, and why: doubtful claims, side stories. Never the record's force, coercion or conflict: those are told as plainly as its virtues.

Confidence uses the project's five levels: Documented, Widely Accepted, Dominant Modern Reconstruction, Contested, Inferential-Thin.


## Part 3. How a text is made

Each step is run by a fresh hand with its own role prompt in `roles/`, filled in with the batch header below; where a kind kit gives its own source rule or test, every role follows it: Opus for research, every check and review, writing, revision and blind readings; Sonnet only to count and to apply changes word for word. A batch is launched from `Launch_Template.md`, with its blanks filled in and nothing else, and runs as two halves of about six stories, each half in a fresh session, with one slate, one hearts page and one release for the whole batch. A session near its context limit hands over to a fresh one from the batch README, which carries each story's step, the open items and the rulings. A batch is recorded only once every brief and card in it has finished every step. A kind kit names any step its kind skips, such as the card or the recording. A fix to a live text is a batch of its own: it runs every step on what it changes, from the story's outline or a new one for those beats.

0. **Slate.** When the batch is proposed, each candidate is checked against the entries and stories already in the census, and its home tradition, where it has one, against its direct line as the tradition's card shows it (continued as, gave rise to, broke with). A descent or break the story will tell that the census lacks or contradicts is shown on the slate, and Mark decides each census change; a story never changes the census's links on its own. An overlap is shown with a line on how the two would differ, and Mark decides to keep, swap or link before research starts. Siblings, stories that share an event or a text, such as a council and its central figure or a saint and her biographer, each get their own job in one line. The later one is researched after the sibling's outline is done. A story approved before this kit goes through every step in its era's batch; the writer keeps its approved wording wherever it passes.
1. **Research** (`roles/researcher.md`). A source sheet from held texts, then a one-page outline to Part 2 and the kind kit.
   - **The hearts to Mark.** When the batch's research is done, the heart of each piece comes to Mark on one page: its essence in one or two sentences, then the rest of its heart, each with its confidence, and the length the outline plans beside an independent count of the words its beats, quotes, glosses and credited witnesses must carry. For each he says yes, change it, or ask, and he rules on the length there. A change goes back to the researcher before the outline check.
2. **Outline check** (`roles/checker.md`). Every line tested against the source texts. The researcher applies the findings (its fix pass), and the checker rechecks only what the fix changed. Each exact replacement the recheck gives is applied to `Outline.md` word for word before the writer starts.
3. **Write** (`roles/writer.md`). From the outline, Part 1, the kind kit and its reference stories only.
4. **Review** (`roles/reviewer.md`). First, across the whole batch, `python tools/check_brief_variety.py <briefs>` counts the moves that repeat: a report for Mark and the reviewer, kept with the batch, and nothing is recast because of it. `python tools/check_tree_overlap.py <briefs>` lists any quote already used elsewhere on the tree that the slate did not list, and any name spelled differently from the census; the writer fixes these before review. No recast may cut or delay the heart. Then each piece gets the reviewer role's reads: the trust read with the sources open, a second trust read of every sentence the writer added or changed, and two listeners. The verdict rests on the faults they count.
5. **One revision, one fresh read** (`roles/reviser.md`, then the reviewer role by a hand that has not seen the review or the drafts, which also reads its kind's stories in `reference/`, where the kind has any, in the same way). A brief that still fails comes to Mark with the reason; one that passes the trust read but still carries listener faults gets one craft pass on those faults, checked, adding nothing. If a reference story's counts differ from those in `Build/Ministry/Features/Church-Family-Tree/Brief-Kit/reference/Scores.md`, or the batch carries more faults than the references, the release says so. The fresh read's exact changes, MINOR ones included, are applied word for word before the card.
   - **Mark reads the first two.** The first two briefs of a batch or fix to pass step 5 come to Mark before any other brief in it is written. The rest go on when he says so.
6. **Card** (`roles/card.md`). For a kind with a card, the card writer keeps an existing card word for word and adds only what the new brief brings, or builds a new card, and for a new entry drafts the census entry. An independent card checker (the card role's check pass) confirms every edit or field against its evidence and gives each correction as an exact change.
7. **Second readings** (`roles/second-reader.md`). `python tools/list_second_readings.py <folder>` lists every quoted span in the final brief and the card entry that holds it, and any entry the brief no longer quotes. Each span of six words or more is read blind unless it stands word for word inside a passage already read blind, which its card entry says. A shorter span is checked instead by the card checker against its original and print, and its card entry says "checked at card". A comparer sets each blind reading beside our wording.
8. **Release** (`roles/release.md`). The card checker's and comparer's changes applied word for word (a rendering that changes meaning is changed in the brief as well, with a targeted check), the entries and cards put into the census, the tree rebuilt, every local check run, each ruling Mark made in the batch listed with where it now lives (the kit line it changed, or "this batch only"), and one pull request for the batch.
9. **Recording.** A paid run, under a cap Mark approves, from the final census once the release's census check passes. One story is recorded first for his ear; the rest follow once he has heard it. Every setting in `Build/Ministry/Features/Church-Family-Tree/Brief-Kit/Recording_Settings.md` is passed on the command and printed, and the balance is read before and after. Then `python Build/tools/align_tree_narration.py --only <ids>` remakes the paragraph times, and its `--check` lists none stale. A recording whose text later changes comes off the branch until it is recorded again.

### The batch header

A role prompt is filled in with this header and nothing else: the story's name, slug, folder and card file, and its kind; its job on the slate and its siblings; its approved heart and length from the hearts page; and any ruling Mark made for this batch alone, quoted. Rule text that would hold for the next batch belongs in this kit, not in a header. No hand is sent anything after its prompt: content the outline lacks goes back to the researcher and the outline check, never straight to the writer.

### The bar

Block only for unsupported claims (a sentence that softens its source, adds an assumption or breaks Part 1's modern-work rule is one), misleading implications, missing essential material, a genuine failure to understand on one hearing, a stretch that reads as a children's book, or a tell Part 1 names. An event told twice, or a jump back after the essence, is a failure to understand on one hearing. A thing or act named without the explanation Part 1 requires (Explain it) is missing essential material. A name heard once that neither acts nor links is minor, and blocking only where the listener is lost. "Could be stronger" is not a finding. Readability scores and the free check's lists are warnings, not quotas; the readers' counted faults decide. The aim is academic soundness, not perfection.

### Keeping the kit clean

- Fix a problem where it starts. An error a writer could repeat goes into that outline's Do not say list. A gap in the method goes into the kit.
- A new ruling rewrites the line it touches. A line is added only when no existing line covers it. Part 1 stays one page. A ruling that changes this kit is merged to `main` the day it is made, on its own, before any batch uses it; it never lives only on a working branch.
- A fix whose wording the fixer writes, whoever the fixer is, and any edit that carries out a ruling, gets a targeted independent check that reads the sentences on either side. A fix applied word for word as a checker worded it does not.
- Make targeted corrections and preserve approved text. Do not reopen finished briefs for optional polishing. A change to this kit governs the next batch on; it reopens a finished story only when Mark says so, as a named change order.
