# The Church Family Tree brief kit

One kit for every text a visitor reads or hears on the Church Family Tree: person, council and tradition briefs, documented stories, connection notes and card fields, and any fix to a live one. No step runs from a prompt of its own. Part 1 is the standard a story must meet. Part 2 gives the shapes a life can take. Part 3 is the outline the researcher fills in. Part 4 is how a brief is made and judged. Each step has one role prompt in `roles/`, which points to this kit and adds only what its step does. Every standing rule lives once, in this kit or its roles.

## Part 1. The standard

A story that lets an interested non-specialist meet one person, council, tradition or event from the church's history. It is heard once, by adults, many of them listening in a second language. It aims at about three minutes and may run to about four and a half where the content calls for it; a council may run longer where its business truly needs it, and any story runs shorter when the record is thin (Part 3, Length).

**The test.** After one hearing, the listener can explain who or what this was, what happened, what it was about, and why the connection matters. Meet it without making the story simpler.

### Craft

- One adult documentary narrator who knows the record well tells it, in the register of a BBC or National Geographic documentary: never quotations linked by commentary, never a report about the record, never talking down.
- Open on one moment, image or line from the record, in about three sentences, bringing the name in where the moment allows, never as a held-back reveal. Then say the essence plainly: what this person did, what it changed, and why they are on the tree. Both come within the first minute. Then tell the life once, in order, never the same event twice and never the ending and then back. Let time, place and tradition come in a clause at a time.
- Keep the story's own question alive: the risk, quarrel or choice the record holds, carried through to where it resolves. Hold the listener with what happened and what people said, never with the narrator's drama.
- **Explain it or leave it out.** Everything the story says was done, said, written, taught, charged or decided (a book, sermon, letter, teaching, charge, arrest, exile, quarrel or ruling, or a label such as "heresy") says in plain words what it was, and why it happened or mattered to the people in it. "His ideas" or "his reforms" never stands alone. If there is no room to explain it, or the old sources do not, leave it out: do not fill the gap, and do not announce it.
- Name a person only when they act in the story or link to another page on the tree; give anyone else by role. Past about six names, ask whether each is needed.
- Link ideas with cause, contrast and time, as an adult narrator does: a longer sentence for a chain of events, a short one for a turn. No sentence past about 30 words, and no run of short sentences that each tell one act.
- Give the connection room to become clear, through what people did: what they left the church and what they defended. One clause may say who keeps this today, where the outline holds it, never as the close. End on the person: a scene, their words, their death, or the opening image again; on a later writer only when that is the story's point.
- It should sound like one person telling it, not a script. No moral or summing-up close. No sentence that only says what is missing. No "not X but Y" turn, no dash for drama, no question asked only to answer it, and no stock word (legacy, profound, testament, tapestry, enduring, remarkable).

### Rigor

- **Tell only what the old sources hold:** the person's own words and the writers of the record, at or near the time, as the outline gives them. Every claim, date, and "so" or "because" stands on a line from one of them. Add no feeling or detail for effect, and honor the outline's Do not say list.
- **A modern work is a tool, never a witness.** It may clarify an old text (its edition, its translation, what a word or custom meant) or lead to the sources of that period. It is never named in the story, and never used for its opinion or speculation. A date, identification or judgment that only a modern work makes, or that the outline rates Dominant Modern Reconstruction or Inferential-Thin, stays out of the story. A modern event in the record, such as a lost text found, may be told as an event.
- **We are a witness, not a judge.** Tell what the old sources agree on in the narrator's own voice. Name a witness only where the listener would otherwise be misled: where the old sources differ (say so, and credit each), for a wonder (it stays in its witness's voice), for praise or blame one side gives, for an enemy's charge, or where the source wrote long after. When the whole life rests on one witness, say so once, early, inside a sentence about that witness, never as a stock how-we-know sentence. Where the doubt is itself the story, make it a scene. People who speak and act in the story are part of it, not citations. Give an enemy's charge in its own content, never dropped because only an enemy says it. Never carry one side's view forward, and never soften the record by leaving out its force or conflict. Say plainly what is uncertain, without hedging.
- Use only the outline's quotes, word for word, and only where their words do what the narrator's cannot. A quote unclear in English by itself may stand with its plain sense alongside.
- **The four faults** every hand re-reads its own work for: a claim told as fact where the old sources differ, or a wonder, praise or blame told as the narrator's; a modern view told as the record, named or not; a date or detail moved onto the wrong event; and the record's force or conflict softened by leaving it out.

### Accessibility

- Plain modern words: simplify the language, never the thought. Use the precise words a grown listener knows, and work any puzzling word or custom into the sentence as part of what happens.
- The audio tells the story. The card carries the rest: the Original sources and Modern works lists, any modern view (named), and the fuller qualifications and debates.
- Aim for reading grade 8 to 9. Grade 10 and reading ease 60 are hard limits; below them a score is a warning for the listening review, never a goal. Never simplify to move it.

## Part 2. The shapes

Each shape gives how that kind of life usually moves, where its impact sits, and the trap to avoid. The outline names one. The record decides the order, not the shape. A shape is added only when a person fits none of these.

**Martyr.** A life that comes to a stand, and what the stand cost. The impact is in how the church remembered it and what it did with that memory. Trap: the death swallows the story. Give the life and what they left as much room as the end.

**Teacher or thinker.** The question they were trying to answer, what they taught, and who learned it from them. The impact is in their students and later readers. Trap: a list of books. Show one idea through a moment or their own words.

**Monk or ascetic.** The turn away from ordinary life, the life they chose instead, and the others drawn after them. The impact is in the pattern others copied. Trap: a pile of wonder stories. Choose one or two, kept in the witness's own voice.

**Founder or leader.** An abbess, bishop, queen or community head: what they built, how it ran, and who it served. The impact is in what outlasted them. Trap: titles and offices in place of acts.

**Missionary or translator.** The journey, the people they met, and the bridge they built: a language, letters, a church. The impact is in what the people who received it did with it. Trap: telling it only from the sender's side. Say whose record it is.

**Outside witness.** A writer outside the church whose words record it: who they were, why they wrote, exactly what they saw or heard of the Christians, and what those words can and cannot tell us. The impact is in how later readers used the passage: the Christians who answered it, copied it or argued over it. Trap: making the witness a friend or a foe of the church, or stretching a few lines past what they say. Say plainly how short the passage is, and what the old sources leave open; the card carries the modern views on it.

**Council.** It should include what called it, the major issue and the story of it, what else it did, its decisions, and its impact, in whatever order tells it best, but lead with the major issue and keep it as the spine. Include other essential business from the outline so that one dispute does not misleadingly stand for the whole council. The impact is in whether its decisions held, and who they divided. Trap: one dispute swallowing the council, or a roll of names. Follow two or three voices through it.

## Part 3. The outline

The researcher fills this in for one person, from the source sheet. One page, no prose. Every line points to its place in a held source text (file and line). The writer gets this outline, Parts 1 and 2 of this kit, and the three reference stories in `reference/`, read for voice, never for their order or moves.

- **The essence:** one or two plain sentences: what they did, what it changed, and why they are on the tree. It is told within the first minute of the story.
- **Also told:** the rest of the heart, told as substance: what they did and made, what it held, and what it changed. For a writer, what the book says. For a founder, what was founded and how it lived. For a council, what it decided and what that did to the churches. Each part is told with its weight wherever the story is strongest, not on a clock.
  Set both first, from the census entry and what the person is known for, then check every claim in them against the Library. Where the sources will not hold a claim, say what they do hold.
- **Length:** set last, once the beats, Do not say and Leave out are fixed: as long as the heart and what must stay in need, and no longer. Count the words the story must carry first, its quotes, the plain sense of each word to explain and each witness it credits, then set the time. Aim at about 3 minutes, allow up to about 4 minutes 30 where the sources and the content call for it, and go as short as 1 minute when the record is thin. Words follow the voice as heard: about 150 a minute for a person (about 450 words aimed, 675 at most), about 130 for a council (about 390 aimed, 585 at 4 minutes 30). A council may run past 4 minutes 30, and past 585 words, only where its business truly needs it; the outline says why, and Mark rules on that length with the hearts. Plan only the beats that count can carry: whatever else the record holds goes to Leave out or to the card, named there. End the outline with a **Cut first** list, in order, each item marked for the card or Leave out, that brings the count to the approved length. Never fill to reach a length. To meet one, never cut the record's force, what a named thing was about or why it happened (Part 1, Explain it), or the scene that carries the story's craft: cut an event before its meaning.
- **Setting:** the century or year, the place and the tradition, each with its supporting lines and the plainest spoken word for it. These are facts for the writer, not a sentence to paste. The writer weaves them in a clause at a time, never all in one sentence.
- **Words to explain:** each church or period word a general listener would not know at once, with a plain word to use instead or a short gloss to work into the sentence where it is first heard. Words a grown listener knows, such as bishop, deacon, Scripture, gospel and church, are not glossed. Do not say bars each listed word used without its plain sense the first time.
- **Shape:** one of the shapes in Part 2.
- **Sibling:** any story on the tree or in the batch that shares an event or a text with this one, with the job each does, as the slate set it. The researcher reads the sibling's outline or live story first. Write "none" if there is none.
- **Openings:** one or two things from the record, each told in about three sentences, that draw a listener in and lead straight into the essence: a moment, an image, their own words, someone else's words about them, or a question the record raises. It must make plain sense heard once.
- **Story beats:** 5 to 7 events, in plain paraphrase. After the hook and the essence, the beats follow the life once, in time order. No event is in two beats, and the ending is not told early. The hook and the essence may reach ahead; the rest does not go back. Each beat gives the reason for the act, the meaning of any word or custom that would puzzle a newcomer, its source, and its confidence level. For each thing it names (Part 1, Explain it), it also gives what that was about and why it happened, in one plain line with its source.
- **What came of them:** ranked heaviest first, where weight is what later witnesses carried on and used, not what is easiest to tell. What they defended is marked. This part carries about a third of the story. Optional: one line on who keeps this person, text or practice today, only where a held source or the tree's own sourced tradition entry says it, with that source. Otherwise leave it out.
- **Quotes, 2 or 3 at most:** each with its modern rendering, its source, and the job it does in the story. A quote another story on the tree already uses may be used again only where its author or subject touched both people or traditions, and it does a new job here, never the same event told the same way. The slate lists each reuse.
- **Names:** each person the story will name, marked "acts" or "link", with the spelling used across the tree. Anyone else is given by role.
- **Connection to carry into the story:** what specifically passed from this person or event to another person, community, text, practice or decision. Give the supporting passage, and state what the listener needs to understand about that relationship. Naming a later person or event is not enough: say what the writer must explain where a listener would otherwise have to infer it. It should come through source-supported action, never a forced "this matters because" sentence or an appended moral.
- **Ending:** one or two ways to close on the person, from the record: a scene, their words, their death, or the opening image seen again. Give the supporting lines.
- **Links:** each tie to another person on the tree, labeled by kind: met, heard him teach, wrote to, kept his letters, quoted his book, wrote his life, kept his feast.
- **Who tells it:** for each main source, who wrote it and whether in the person's own time or later; where it matters, what the listener must know about the witness. Mark each source old (the original sources and the writers of the record itself) or modern (an editor, a translator's note or a historian writing on the record). A modern source is marked for what it does: clarifies an old text or finds one, or makes a claim of its own (Part 1, Rigor). Mark the claim each witness is credited beside. If the whole life rests on one witness, say where the story says so. If the doubt is itself the story, name the scene that tells it.
- **Do not say:** the wordings a writer could easily get wrong from this outline, each with the right wording beside it. These include a wonder that must stay in its witness's voice, a qualifier that must not be dropped ("partly", "forty or more"), an outcome or motive the record does not give, and a detail a writer might add for color. When a later check or review finds a writer's error that the outline left room for, it is added here.
- **Leave out:** what the record holds that this story should not carry, and why: doubtful claims, side stories. Never the record's force, coercion or conflict: those are told as plainly as its virtues.

Confidence uses the project's five levels: Documented, Widely Accepted, Dominant Modern Reconstruction, Contested, Inferential-Thin.

## Part 4. How a brief is made

Each step is run by a fresh hand with its own role prompt in `roles/`, filled in with the batch header below: Opus for research, every check and review, writing, revision and blind readings; Sonnet only to count, build cards and apply changes word for word. A batch is launched from `Launch_Template.md`, with its blanks filled in and nothing else, and runs as two halves of about six stories, each half in a fresh session, with one slate, one hearts page and one release for the whole batch. A session near its context limit hands over to a fresh one from the batch README, which carries each story's step, the open items and the rulings. A batch is recorded only once every brief and card in it has finished every step. A fix to a live text is a batch of its own: it runs steps 1 to 5 on the beats it changes, with these roles, from the story's outline or a new one for those beats.

0. **Slate.** When the batch is proposed, each candidate is checked against the people and stories already in the census, and its home tradition against its direct line as the tradition's card shows it (continued as, gave rise to, broke with). A descent or break the story will tell that the census lacks or contradicts is shown on the slate, and Mark decides each census change; a story never changes the census's links on its own. An overlap is shown with a line on how the two would differ, and Mark decides to keep, swap or link before research starts. Siblings, stories that share an event or a text, such as a council and its central figure or a saint and her biographer, each get their own job in one line. The later one is researched after the sibling's outline is done. A story approved before this kit is reviewed in its era's batch, keeping its approved wording wherever it passes. The researcher first makes its source sheet, with the Library sweep, and its outline, giving what each thing it names was about and why it happened (Part 1, Explain it), and both pass the outline check before review.
1. **Research** (`roles/researcher.md`). A source sheet from held texts, then a one-page outline to Part 3.
   - **The hearts to Mark.** When the batch's research is done, the heart of each life comes to Mark on one page: its essence in one or two sentences, then what is also told, each with its confidence, and the length the outline plans beside an independent count of the words its beats, quotes, glosses and credited witnesses must carry. For each he says yes, change it, or ask, and he rules on the length there. A change goes back to the researcher before the outline check.
2. **Outline check** (`roles/checker.md`). Every line tested against the source texts. The researcher applies the findings (its fix pass), and the checker rechecks only what the fix changed. Each exact replacement the recheck gives is applied to `Outline.md` word for word before the writer starts.
3. **Write** (`roles/writer.md`). From the outline, Parts 1 and 2 and the reference stories only.
4. **Review** (`roles/reviewer.md`). First, across the whole batch, `python tools/check_brief_variety.py` counts the moves that repeat, and `python tools/check_tree_overlap.py` lists any quote already used elsewhere on the tree and any name spelled differently from the census. For each draft, `python tools/check_brief_craft.py` lists the sentences Part 1's rules most often catch; the reviewer clears every line. The counts are a report for Mark and the reviewer, kept with the batch, and nothing is recast because of them. A shared quote the slate did not list, or a variant spelling, is fixed before review. No recast may cut or delay the heart. Then, for each piece, the acceptance test from one hearing, and the separate source check. A clear story can still hold a factual error.
5. **One revision, one fresh read** (`roles/reviser.md`, then the reviewer role by a hand that has not seen the review or the drafts, which also scores the three stories in `reference/` in the same way). A brief that still fails comes to Mark with the reason; one at 3 or less on craft gets one craft pass on the read's points, checked, adding nothing. If a reference story scores a point lower or higher than its fixed score in `Build/Ministry/Features/Church-Family-Tree/Brief-Kit/reference/Scores.md`, or the batch averages below the references, the release says so.
   - **Mark reads the first two.** The first two briefs of a batch or fix to pass step 5 come to Mark before any other brief in it is written. The rest go on when he says so.
6. **Card** (`roles/card.md`). The card writer keeps an existing card word for word and adds only what the new brief brings, or builds a new card, and for a new person drafts the census entry. An independent card checker (the card role's check pass) confirms every edit or field against its evidence and gives each correction as an exact change.
7. **Second readings** (`roles/second-reader.md`). `python tools/list_second_readings.py <folder>` lists every quoted span in the final brief and the card entry that holds it, and any entry the brief no longer quotes. Each span of six words or more is read blind unless it stands word for word inside a passage already read blind, which its card entry says. A shorter span is checked instead by the card checker against its original and print, and its card entry says "checked at card". A comparer sets each blind reading beside our wording.
8. **Release** (`roles/release.md`). The card checker's and comparer's changes applied word for word, the entries and cards put into the census, the tree rebuilt, every local check run, each ruling Mark made in the batch listed with where it now lives (the kit line it changed, or "this batch only"), and one pull request for the batch.
9. **Recording.** A paid run, under a cap Mark approves, from the final census once the release's census check passes. One story is recorded first for his ear; the rest follow once he has heard it. Every setting in `Build/Ministry/Features/Church-Family-Tree/Brief-Kit/Recording_Settings.md` is passed on the command and printed, and the balance is read before and after. Then `python Build/tools/align_tree_narration.py --only <ids>` remakes the paragraph times, and its `--check` lists none stale. A recording whose text later changes comes off the branch until it is recorded again.

### The batch header

A role prompt is filled in with this header and nothing else: the story's name, slug, folder and card file; its job on the slate and its siblings; its approved heart and length from the hearts page; and any ruling Mark made for this batch alone, quoted. Rule text that would hold for the next batch belongs in this kit, not in a header. No hand is sent anything after its prompt: content the outline lacks goes back to the researcher and the outline check, never straight to the writer.

### The bar

Block only for unsupported claims (a sentence resting on a modern opinion, guess or reconstruction is one), misleading implications, missing essential material, a genuine failure to understand on one hearing, or a stretch that reads as a children's book. An event told twice, or a jump back after the essence, is a failure to understand on one hearing. A thing named without what it was about, or an act without why it happened (Part 1, Explain it), is missing essential material. A name heard once that neither acts nor links is minor, and blocking only where the listener is lost. "Could be stronger" is not a finding. Scores and counts are warnings, not quotas. The aim is academic soundness, not perfection.

### Keeping the kit clean

- Fix a problem where it starts. An error a writer could repeat goes into that outline's Do not say list. A gap in the method goes into the kit.
- A new ruling rewrites the line it touches. A line is added only when no existing line covers it. Part 1 stays one page. A ruling that changes this kit is merged to `main` the day it is made, on its own, before any batch uses it; it never lives only on a working branch.
- A fix whose wording the fixer writes, whoever the fixer is, and any edit that carries out a ruling, gets a targeted independent check that reads the sentences on either side. A fix applied word for word as a checker worded it does not.
- Make targeted corrections and preserve approved text. Do not reopen finished briefs for optional polishing. A change to this kit governs the next batch on; it reopens a finished story only when Mark says so, as a named change order.
