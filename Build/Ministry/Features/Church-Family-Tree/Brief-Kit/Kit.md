# The Church Family Tree brief kit

One kit for every Church Family Tree story, person or council. Part 1 is the standard a story must meet. Part 2 gives the shapes a life can take. Part 3 is the outline the researcher fills in. Part 4 is how a brief is made and judged. Each step has one role prompt in `roles/`, which points to this kit and adds only what its step does. Every standing rule lives once, in this kit or its roles.

## Part 1. The standard

A story that lets an interested non-specialist meet one person, council or event from the church's history. It is heard once, by adults, many of them listening in a second language. The outline sets its length, for a person or a council alike: aim at about three minutes, and allow up to about four and a half where the sources and the content call for it, as for a council that made ten decisions rather than one. Shorter when the record is thin.

**The test.** After one hearing, the listener can explain who or what this was, what happened, what it was about, and why the connection matters. Meet it without making the story simpler.

### Craft

- A well-sourced story, told by an adult documentary narrator who knows the record well, in the register of a BBC or National Geographic documentary. Never quotations linked by commentary, never a report about the record, and never talking down.
- Open on one moment, image or line from the record, in no more than about three sentences, and let it lead straight into the essence. Bring the name in where the moment allows; a held-back reveal ("The writer was ...") is not a stock hinge. Then say the essence plainly: what this person did, what it changed, and why they are on the tree. Both come within the first minute. After the essence, tell the life once, in order: never the same event twice, and never the ending and then back. The rest of their life and work deepens it, told with its weight where the story is strongest. Let the time, the place and the tradition come in a clause at a time, as the story needs them, never stacked in one sentence.
- Explain it or leave it out. Every charge, teaching, book, quarrel or decision the story names says in plain words what it was, and why it mattered to the people in it: what an act meant in its own time, what it risked and what it changed. If there is no room to explain it, the story does not name it.
- Every name earns its place. Name a person only when they act in the story or are a link to another page on the tree; give anyone else by role, such as "a historian a century later". Past about six names, ask whether each one is needed. That is a warning, not a cap.
- Link ideas the way an adult narrator does, with cause, contrast and time. Let a longer sentence carry a chain of events and a short one mark a turn. No sentence past about 30 words, and no run of short sentences that each tell one act.
- Give the outline's connection enough room to become understandable, carried through what people did: what they left the church and what they defended. Tell it where it is strongest. Where the outline holds it, one clause may say who keeps this person, text or practice today; it goes with what came of them, never as the closing line and never as a moral. Once the connection has landed, end on the person: a scene, their own words, their death, or the opening's image seen again. End on a later writer or institution only when that is the story's point. No moral, no summing up.

### Rigor

- Tell only what the outline holds, transitions included: a "so" or "because" that implies motive or cause must be in the sources. Add no feeling or detail for effect. Honor the outline's Do not say list.
- Use only the outline's quotes, word for word, and only where their particular words do something the narrator's cannot. A quote whose wording is unclear in English by itself, such as an image or older wording, may stand when the narrator gives its plain sense alongside. The narrator carries the story.
- Nothing in the story rests on a modern opinion, guess or reconstruction, credited or not. A modern editor's or historian's judgment, and any claim the outline rates Dominant Modern Reconstruction or Inferential-Thin, goes to the card as a modern view, never into the story. Where the old sources are silent, the story says so or leaves the point out. A modern event in the record, such as a lost text found, may be told as an event, without anyone's judgment of it.
- We are a witness, not a judge. Credit a witness where their claim is told, and name each source once. Name a witness when the listener needs to know who is speaking: praise or blame, a wonder, a much later source, a hostile view, or a link on the tree. When the whole life rests on one witness, say so once, inside a sentence about that witness, where it matters. Where the doubt is itself the story, make it a scene. No stock how-we-know sentence. A wonder stays in its witness's voice. A hostile view is told openly and credited to its source. An enemy's charge is given in its own content ("his accusers charged that ..."), never dropped because only an enemy says it. The narrator never carries any side's view forward, and never softens the record by leaving out its force or conflict.
- **The four faults** the checks find most, which every hand re-reads its own work for before handing on: one witness's claim told as fact; an editor's view given to the source; a date or detail moved onto the wrong event; and the record's force, coercion or conflict softened by leaving it out.
- Say plainly what is uncertain. Do not present it as settled, and do not bury it in hedging. Where two old sources differ, say so and credit each, rather than choosing one; the card carries what the story has no room for.

### Accessibility

- Plain modern words: simplify the language, never the thought. Use the precise words a grown listener knows without stopping to define them, and work any puzzling word or custom into the sentence as part of what happens.
- The audio tells the story; the card carries the references, fuller qualifications and debates.
- Aim for reading grade 8 to 9. Grade 10 and reading ease 60 are hard limits; below them, a score is a warning for the listening review, never a goal. Never simplify to move it.

## Part 2. The shapes

Each shape gives how that kind of life usually moves, where its impact sits, and the trap to avoid. The outline names one. The record decides the order, not the shape. A shape is added only when a person fits none of these.

**Martyr.** A life that comes to a stand, and what the stand cost. The impact is in how the church remembered it and what it did with that memory. Trap: the death swallows the story. Give the life and what they left as much room as the end.

**Teacher or thinker.** The question they were trying to answer, what they taught, and who learned it from them. The impact is in their students and later readers. Trap: a list of books. Show one idea through a moment or their own words.

**Monk or ascetic.** The turn away from ordinary life, the life they chose instead, and the others drawn after them. The impact is in the pattern others copied. Trap: a pile of wonder stories. Choose one or two, kept in the witness's own voice.

**Founder or leader.** An abbess, bishop, queen or community head: what they built, how it ran, and who it served. The impact is in what outlasted them. Trap: titles and offices in place of acts.

**Missionary or translator.** The journey, the people they met, and the bridge they built: a language, letters, a church. The impact is in what the people who received it did with it. Trap: telling it only from the sender's side. Say whose record it is.

**Outside witness.** A writer outside the church whose words record it: who they were, why they wrote, exactly what they saw or heard of the Christians, and what those words can and cannot tell us. The impact is in how later readers used the passage: the Christians who answered it, copied it or argued over it. Trap: making the witness a friend or a foe of the church, or stretching a few lines past what they say. Say plainly how short the passage is, and what the old sources leave open; the card carries the modern views on it.

**Council.** It should include what called it, the major issue and the story of it, what else it did, its decisions, and its impact, in whatever order tells it best, but lead with the major issue and keep it as the spine. Include other essential business from the outline so that one dispute does not misleadingly stand for the whole council. The impact is in whether its decisions held, and who they divided. Trap: one dispute swallowing the council, or a roll of names. Follow two or three voices through it.

## Part 3. The outline

The researcher fills this in for one person, from the source sheet. One page, no prose. Every line points to its place in a held source text (file and line). The writer gets this outline and Parts 1 and 2 of this kit, and nothing else.

- **The essence:** one or two plain sentences: what they did, what it changed, and why they are on the tree. It is told within the first minute of the story.
- **Also told:** the rest of the heart, told as substance: what they did and made, what it held, and what it changed. For a writer, what the book says. For a founder, what was founded and how it lived. For a council, what it decided and what that did to the churches. Each part is told with its weight wherever the story is strongest, not on a clock.
  Set both first, from the census entry and what the person is known for, then check every claim in them against the Library. Where the sources will not hold a claim, say what they do hold.
- **Length:** set last, once the beats, Do not say and Leave out are fixed: as long as the heart and what must stay in need, and no longer. Count the words the story must carry first, its quotes, the plain sense of each word to explain and each witness it credits, then set the time. Aim at about 3 minutes, allow up to about 4 minutes 30 where the sources and the content call for it, and go as short as 1 minute when the record is thin. Words follow the voice as heard: about 150 a minute for a person (about 450 words aimed, 675 at most), about 130 for a council (about 390 aimed, 585 at most). Plan only the beats that count can carry: whatever else the record holds goes to Leave out or to the card, named there. End the outline with a **Cut first** list, in order, each item marked for the card or Leave out, that brings the count to the approved length. Never fill to reach a length. To meet one, never cut the record's force, what a named charge, teaching, book, quarrel or decision was about, or the scene that carries the story's craft: cut an event before its meaning.
- **Setting:** the century or year, the place and the tradition, each with its supporting lines and the plainest spoken word for it. These are facts for the writer, not a sentence to paste. The writer weaves them in a clause at a time, never all in one sentence.
- **Words to explain:** each church or period word a general listener would not know at once, with a plain word to use instead or a short gloss to work into the sentence where it is first heard. Words a grown listener knows, such as bishop, deacon, Scripture, gospel and church, are not glossed. Do not say bars each listed word used without its plain sense the first time.
- **Shape:** one of the shapes in Part 2.
- **Sibling:** any story on the tree or in the batch that shares an event or a text with this one, with the job each does, as the slate set it. The researcher reads the sibling's outline or live story first. Write "none" if there is none.
- **Openings:** one or two things from the record, each told in about three sentences, that draw a listener in and lead straight into the essence: a moment, an image, their own words, someone else's words about them, or a question the record raises. It must make plain sense heard once.
- **Story beats:** 5 to 7 events, in plain paraphrase. After the hook and the essence, the beats follow the life once, in time order. No event is in two beats, and the ending is not told early. The hook and the essence may reach ahead; the rest does not go back. Each beat gives the reason for the act, the meaning of any word or custom that would puzzle a newcomer, its source, and its confidence level. For each charge, teaching, book, quarrel or decision it names, it also gives what that was about, in one plain line with its source.
- **What came of them:** ranked heaviest first, where weight is what later witnesses carried on and used, not what is easiest to tell. What they defended is marked. This part carries about a third of the story. Optional: one line on who keeps this person, text or practice today, only where a held source or the tree's own sourced tradition entry says it, with that source. Otherwise leave it out.
- **Quotes, 2 or 3 at most:** each with its modern rendering, its source, and the job it does in the story. A quote another story on the tree already uses may be used again only where its author or subject touched both people or traditions, and it does a new job here, never the same event told the same way. The slate lists each reuse.
- **Names:** each person the story will name, marked "acts" or "link", with the spelling used across the tree. Anyone else is given by role.
- **Connection to carry into the story:** what specifically passed from this person or event to another person, community, text, practice or decision. Give the supporting passage, and state what the listener needs to understand about that relationship. Naming a later person or event is not enough: say what the writer must explain where a listener would otherwise have to infer it. It should come through source-supported action, never a forced "this matters because" sentence or an appended moral.
- **Ending:** one or two ways to close on the person, from the record: a scene, their words, their death, or the opening image seen again. Give the supporting lines.
- **Links:** each tie to another person on the tree, labeled by kind: met, heard him teach, wrote to, kept his letters, quoted his book, wrote his life, kept his feast.
- **Who tells it:** for each main source, who wrote it and whether in the person's own time or later; where it matters, what the listener must know about the witness. Mark each source old (the original sources and the writers of the record itself) or modern (an editor, a translator's note or a historian writing on the record). Mark the claim each witness is credited beside. If the whole life rests on one witness, say where the story says so. If the doubt is itself the story, name the scene that tells it.
- **Do not say:** the wordings a writer could easily get wrong from this outline, each with the right wording beside it. These include a wonder that must stay in its witness's voice, a qualifier that must not be dropped ("partly", "forty or more"), an outcome or motive the record does not give, and a detail a writer might add for color. When a later check or review finds a writer's error that the outline left room for, it is added here.
- **Leave out:** what the record holds that this story should not carry, and why: doubtful claims, side stories. Never the record's force, coercion or conflict: those are told as plainly as its virtues.

Confidence uses the project's five levels: Documented, Widely Accepted, Dominant Modern Reconstruction, Contested, Inferential-Thin.

## Part 4. How a brief is made

Each step is run by a fresh hand with its own role prompt in `roles/`, filled in with the batch header below: Opus for research, every check and review, writing, revision and blind readings; Sonnet only to count, build cards and apply changes word for word. A batch is launched from `Launch_Template.md`, with its blanks filled in and nothing else, and runs as two halves of about six stories, each half in a fresh session, with one slate, one hearts page and one release for the whole batch. A session near its context limit hands over to a fresh one from the batch README, which carries each story's step, the open items and the rulings. A batch is recorded only once every brief and card in it has finished every step.

0. **Slate.** When the batch is proposed, each candidate is checked against the people and stories already in the census, and its home tradition against its direct line as the tradition's card shows it (continued as, gave rise to, broke with). A descent or break the story will tell that the census lacks or contradicts is shown on the slate, and Mark decides each census change; a story never changes the census's links on its own. An overlap is shown with a line on how the two would differ, and Mark decides to keep, swap or link before research starts. Siblings, stories that share an event or a text, such as a council and its central figure or a saint and her biographer, each get their own job in one line. The later one is researched after the sibling's outline is done. A story approved before this kit is reviewed in its era's batch, keeping its approved wording wherever it passes.
1. **Research** (`roles/researcher.md`). A source sheet from held texts, then a one-page outline to Part 3.
   - **The hearts to Mark.** When the batch's research is done, the heart of each life comes to Mark on one page: its essence in one or two sentences, then what is also told, each with its confidence, and the length the outline plans beside an independent count of the words its beats, quotes, glosses and credited witnesses must carry. For each he says yes, change it, or ask, and he rules on the length there. A change goes back to the researcher before the outline check.
2. **Outline check** (`roles/checker.md`). Every line tested against the source texts. The researcher applies the findings (its fix pass), and the checker rechecks only what the fix changed.
3. **Write** (`roles/writer.md`). From the outline and Parts 1 and 2 only.
4. **Review** (`roles/reviewer.md`). First, across the whole batch, `python tools/check_brief_variety.py` counts the moves that repeat, and `python tools/check_tree_overlap.py` lists any quote already used elsewhere on the tree and any name spelled differently from the census. The counts are a report for Mark and the reviewer, kept with the batch, and nothing is recast because of them. A shared quote the slate did not list, or a variant spelling, is fixed before review. No recast may cut or delay the heart. Then, for each piece, the acceptance test from one hearing, and the separate source check. A clear story can still hold a factual error.
5. **One revision, one fresh read** (`roles/reviser.md`, then the reviewer role by a hand that has not seen the review or the drafts, which also scores the two stories in `reference/` in the same way). A brief that still fails comes to Mark with the reason; one at 3 or less on craft gets one craft pass on the read's points, checked, adding nothing. If a reference story scores a point lower or higher than its fixed score in `Build/Ministry/Features/Church-Family-Tree/Brief-Kit/reference/Scores.md`, or the batch averages below the references, the release says so.
6. **Card** (`roles/card.md`). The card writer keeps an existing card word for word and adds only what the new brief brings, or builds a new card, and for a new person drafts the census entry. An independent card checker (the card role's check pass) confirms every edit or field against its evidence and gives each correction as an exact change.
7. **Second readings** (`roles/second-reader.md`). `python tools/list_second_readings.py <folder>` lists every quoted span in the final brief and the card entry that holds it, and any entry the brief no longer quotes. Each span of six words or more is read blind unless it stands word for word inside a passage already read blind, which its card entry says. A shorter span is checked instead by the card checker against its original and print, and its card entry says "checked at card". A comparer sets each blind reading beside our wording.
8. **Release** (`roles/release.md`). The card checker's and comparer's changes applied word for word, the entries and cards put into the census, the tree rebuilt, every local check run, each ruling Mark made in the batch listed with where it now lives (the kit line it changed, or "this batch only"), and one pull request for the batch.
9. **Recording.** A paid run, under a cap Mark approves, from the final census once the release's census check passes. One story is recorded first for his ear; the rest follow once he has heard it. Every setting in `Build/Ministry/Features/Church-Family-Tree/Brief-Kit/Recording_Settings.md` is passed on the command and printed, and the balance is read before and after.

### The batch header

A role prompt is filled in with this header and nothing else: the story's name, slug, folder and card file; its job on the slate and its siblings; its approved heart and length from the hearts page; and any ruling Mark made for this batch alone, quoted. Rule text that would hold for the next batch belongs in this kit, not in a header.

### The bar

Block only for unsupported claims (a sentence resting on a modern opinion, guess or reconstruction is one), misleading implications, missing essential material, a genuine failure to understand on one hearing, or a stretch that reads as a children's book. An event told twice, or a jump back after the essence, is a failure to understand on one hearing. A charge, teaching, book, quarrel or decision named without what it was about is missing essential material. A name heard once that neither acts nor links is minor, and blocking only where the listener is lost. "Could be stronger" is not a finding. Scores and counts are warnings, not quotas. The aim is academic soundness, not perfection.

### Keeping the kit clean

- Fix a problem where it starts. An error a writer could repeat goes into that outline's Do not say list. A gap in the method goes into the kit.
- A new ruling rewrites the line it touches. A line is added only when no existing line covers it. Part 1 stays one page.
- A fix whose wording the fixer writes, whoever the fixer is, and any edit that carries out a ruling, gets a targeted independent check that reads the sentences on either side. A fix applied word for word as a checker worded it does not.
- Make targeted corrections and preserve approved text. Do not reopen finished briefs for optional polishing. A change to this kit governs the next batch on; it never reopens a finished story.
