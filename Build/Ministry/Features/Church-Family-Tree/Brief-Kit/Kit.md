# The Church Family Tree brief kit

One kit for every Church Family Tree story, person or council. Part 1 is the standard a story must meet. Part 2 gives the shapes a life can take. Part 3 is the outline the researcher fills in. Part 4 is how a brief is made and judged. The role prompts in `roles/` quote this kit and add only what their step does.

## Part 1. The standard

A story that lets an interested non-specialist meet one person, council or event from the church's history. It is heard once, by adults, many of them listening in a second language. The outline sets its length: as long as the story needs and no longer, about three minutes for a person and four for a council, shorter when the record is thin. Those times are a pressure, not a cap: a major figure whose turning points need more may run longer. Craft comes before time.

**The test.** After one hearing, the listener can explain who or what this was, what happened, and why the connection matters. Meet it without making the story simpler.

### Craft

- A well-sourced story, told by an adult documentary narrator who knows the record well, in the register of a BBC or National Geographic documentary. Never quotations linked by commentary, never a report about the record, and never talking down.
- Open on one moment, image or line from the record, in no more than about three sentences, and let it lead straight into the essence. Bring the name in where the moment allows; a held-back reveal ("The writer was ...") is not a stock hinge. Then say the essence plainly: what this person did, what it changed, and why they are on the tree. Both come within the first minute. After the essence, tell the life once, in order: never the same event twice, and never the ending and then back. The rest of their life and work deepens it, told with its weight where the story is strongest. Let the time, the place and the tradition come in a clause at a time, as the story needs them, never stacked in one sentence.
- Tell what was at stake, not only what happened: what an act meant in its own time, what it risked and what it changed.
- Every name earns its place. Name a person only when they act in the story or are a link to another page on the tree; give anyone else by role, such as "a historian a century later". Past about six names, ask whether each one is needed. That is a warning, not a cap.
- Link ideas the way an adult narrator does, with cause, contrast and time. Let a longer sentence carry a chain of events and a short one mark a turn. No sentence past about 30 words, and no run of short sentences that each tell one act.
- Give the outline's connection enough room to become understandable, carried through what people did: what they left the church and what they defended. Tell it where it is strongest. Where the outline holds it, one clause may say who keeps this person, text or practice today; it goes with what came of them, never as the closing line and never as a moral. Once the connection has landed, end on the person: a scene, their own words, their death, or the opening's image seen again. End on a later writer or institution only when that is the story's point. No moral, no summing up.

### Rigor

- Tell only what the outline holds, transitions included: a "so" or "because" that implies motive or cause must be in the sources. Add no feeling or detail for effect. Honor the outline's Do not say list.
- Use only the outline's quotes, word for word, and only where their particular words do something the narrator's cannot. A quote whose wording is unclear in English by itself, such as an image or older wording, may stand when the narrator gives its plain sense alongside. The narrator carries the story.
- We are a witness, not a judge. Credit a witness where their claim is told, and name each source once. Name a witness when the listener needs to know who is speaking: praise or blame, a wonder, a much later source, a hostile view, or a link on the tree. When the whole life rests on one witness, say so once, inside a sentence about that witness, where it matters. Where the doubt is itself the story, make it a scene. No stock how-we-know sentence. A wonder stays in its witness's voice. A hostile view is told openly and credited to its source; the narrator never carries any side's view forward, and never softens the record by leaving out its force or conflict.
- Say plainly what is uncertain. Do not present it as settled, and do not bury it in hedging.

### Accessibility

- Plain modern words: simplify the language, never the thought. Use the precise words a grown listener knows without stopping to define them, and work any puzzling word or custom into the sentence as part of what happens.
- The audio tells the story; the card carries the references, fuller qualifications and debates.
- Aim for reading grade 8 to 9. Grade 10 and reading ease 60 are hard limits; below them, a score is a warning for the listening review, never a goal. Never simplify to move it.

## Part 2. The shapes

Each shape gives how that kind of life usually moves, where its impact sits, and the trap to avoid. The outline names one. The record decides the order, not the shape. A shape is added only when a person fits none of these.

**Martyr.** A life that comes to a stand, and what the stand cost. The impact is in how the church remembered it and what it did with that memory. Trap: the death swallows the story. Give the life and what they left as much room as the end.

**Teacher or thinker.** The question they were trying to answer, what they taught, and who learned it from them. The impact is in their students and later readers. Trap: a list of books. Show one idea through a moment or their own words.

**Monk or ascetic.** The turn away from ordinary life, the life they chose instead, and the others drawn after them. The impact is in the pattern others copied. Trap: a pile of wonder stories. Choose one or two, kept in the witness's own voice.

**Founder or leader.** An abbess, bishop, queen or community head: what they built, how it ran, and who it served. The impact is in what outlasted them. Trap: titles and offices in place of acts.

**Missionary or translator.** The journey, the people they met, and the bridge they built: a language, letters, a church. The impact is in what the people who received it did with it. Trap: telling it only from the sender's side. Say whose record it is.

**Outside witness.** A writer outside the church whose words record it: who they were, why they wrote, exactly what they saw or heard of the Christians, and what those words can and cannot tell us. The impact is in how later readers used the passage: the Christians who answered it, and the historians who still weigh it. Trap: making the witness a friend or a foe of the church, or stretching a few lines past what they say. Say plainly how short the passage is, and what about it is disputed.

**Council.** It should include what called it, the major issue and the story of it, what else it did, its decisions, and its impact, in whatever order tells it best, but lead with the major issue and keep it as the spine. Include other essential business from the outline so that one dispute does not misleadingly stand for the whole council. The impact is in whether its decisions held, and who they divided. Trap: one dispute swallowing the council, or a roll of names. Follow two or three voices through it.

## Part 3. The outline

The researcher fills this in for one person, from the source sheet. One page, no prose. Every line points to its place in a held source text (file and line). The writer gets this outline and Parts 1 and 2 of this kit, and nothing else.

- **The essence:** one or two plain sentences: what they did, what it changed, and why they are on the tree. It is told within the first minute of the story.
- **Also told:** the rest of the heart, told as substance: what they did and made, what it held, and what it changed. For a writer, what the book says. For a founder, what was founded and how it lived. For a council, what it decided and what that did to the churches. Each part is told with its weight wherever the story is strongest, not on a clock.
  Set both first, from the census entry and what the person is known for, then check every claim in them against the Library. Where the sources will not hold a claim, say what they do hold.
- **Length:** set last, once the beats, Do not say and Leave out are fixed: as long as the heart and what must stay in need, and no longer. About 3 minutes for a person and about 4 minutes 15 for a council, as a pressure, not a cap: longer when a major figure's turning points need it, and as short as 1 minute when the record is thin. Never fill to reach a length, and never cut the record's force, or the scene that carries the story's craft, to meet one. Words follow the voice as heard: about 150 a minute for a person (Neo), about 130 for a council (Daniel).
- **Setting:** the century or year, the place and the tradition, each with its supporting lines and the plainest spoken word for it. These are facts for the writer, not a sentence to paste. The writer weaves them in a clause at a time, never all in one sentence.
- **Words to explain:** each church or period word a general listener would not know at once, with a plain word to use instead or a short gloss to work into the sentence where it is first heard. Words a grown listener knows, such as bishop, deacon, Scripture, gospel and church, are not glossed. Do not say bars each listed word used without its plain sense the first time.
- **Shape:** one of the shapes in Part 2.
- **Sibling:** any story on the tree or in the batch that shares an event or a text with this one, with the job each does, as the slate set it. The researcher reads the sibling's outline or live story first. Write "none" if there is none.
- **Openings:** one or two things from the record, each told in about three sentences, that draw a listener in and lead straight into the essence: a moment, an image, their own words, someone else's words about them, or a question the record raises. It must make plain sense heard once.
- **Story beats:** 5 to 7 events, in plain paraphrase. After the hook and the essence, the beats follow the life once, in time order. No event is in two beats, and the ending is not told early. The hook and the essence may reach ahead; the rest does not go back. Each beat gives the reason for the act, the meaning of any word or custom that would puzzle a newcomer, its source, and its confidence level.
- **What came of them:** ranked heaviest first, where weight is what later witnesses carried on and used, not what is easiest to tell. What they defended is marked. This part carries about a third of the story. Optional: one line on who keeps this person, text or practice today, only where a held source or the tree's own sourced tradition entry says it, with that source. Otherwise leave it out.
- **Quotes, 2 or 3 at most:** each with its modern rendering, its source, and the job it does in the story. None already used in another story on the tree, unless Mark says so.
- **Names:** each person the story will name, marked "acts" or "link", with the spelling used across the tree. Anyone else is given by role.
- **Connection to carry into the story:** what specifically passed from this person or event to another person, community, text, practice or decision. Give the supporting passage, and state what the listener needs to understand about that relationship. Naming a later person or event is not enough: say what the writer must explain where a listener would otherwise have to infer it. It should come through source-supported action, never a forced "this matters because" sentence or an appended moral.
- **Ending:** one or two ways to close on the person, from the record: a scene, their words, their death, or the opening image seen again. Give the supporting lines.
- **Links:** each tie to another person on the tree, labeled by kind: met, heard him teach, wrote to, kept his letters, quoted his book, wrote his life, kept his feast.
- **Who tells it:** for each main source, who wrote it and whether in the person's own time or later; where it matters, what the listener must know about the witness. Mark the claim each witness is credited beside. If the whole life rests on one witness, say where the story says so. If the doubt is itself the story, name the scene that tells it.
- **Do not say:** the wordings a writer could easily get wrong from this outline, each with the right wording beside it. These include a wonder that must stay in its witness's voice, a qualifier that must not be dropped ("partly", "forty or more"), an outcome or motive the record does not give, and a detail a writer might add for color. When a later check or review finds a writer's error that the outline left room for, it is added here.
- **Leave out:** what the record holds that this story should not carry, and why: doubtful claims, side stories. Never the record's force, coercion or conflict: those are told as plainly as its virtues.

Confidence uses the project's five levels: Documented, Widely Accepted, Dominant Modern Reconstruction, Contested, Inferential-Thin.

## Part 4. How a brief is made

Each step is run by a fresh hand with its own prompt in `roles/`. A batch is worked one at a time, and is recorded only once every brief and card in it has finished every step, revisions included.

0. **Slate.** When the batch is proposed, each candidate is checked against the people and stories already in the census. An overlap is shown with a line on how the two would differ, and Mark decides to keep, swap or link before research starts. Siblings, stories that share an event or a text, such as a council and its central figure or a saint and her biographer, each get their own job in one line. The later one is researched after the sibling's outline is done.
1. **Research** (`roles/researcher.md`). A source sheet from held texts, then a one-page outline to Part 3.
   - **The hearts to Mark.** When the batch's research is done, the heart of each life comes to Mark on one page: its essence in one or two sentences, then what is also told, each with its confidence. For each he says yes, change it, or ask. A change goes back to the researcher before the outline check.
2. **Outline check** (`roles/checker.md`). Every line tested against the source texts. Blocking faults are fixed and rechecked independently.
3. **Write** (`roles/writer.md`). From the outline and Parts 1 and 2 only.
4. **Review** (`roles/reviewer.md`). First, across the whole batch, `python tools/check_brief_variety.py` counts the moves that repeat, and `python tools/check_tree_overlap.py` lists any quote already used elsewhere on the tree and any name spelled differently from the census. The counts are a report for Mark and the reviewer, kept with the batch, and nothing is recast because of them. A shared quote or a variant spelling is fixed before review. No recast may cut or delay the heart. Then, for each piece, the acceptance test from one hearing, and the separate source check. A clear story can still hold a factual error.
5. **One revision, one recheck.** A brief that still fails comes to Mark with the reason.
6. **Card** (`roles/card.md`). Keep the existing card word for word. Add only what the new brief brings, and correct only what is clearly wrong, with every edit listed with its evidence. The checker confirms each edit is justified, that nothing else moved, and rules on each claim flagged as not found.
7. **Second readings.** `python tools/list_second_readings.py <folder>` lists every quoted span in the final brief and the card entry that holds it, and any entry the brief no longer quotes. Each span is read blind unless it stands word for word inside a passage already read blind, which its card entry says.

### The bar

Block only for unsupported claims, misleading implications, missing essential material, a genuine failure to understand on one hearing, or a stretch that reads as a children's book. An event told twice, or a jump back after the essence, is a failure to understand on one hearing. A name heard once that neither acts nor links is minor, and blocking only where the listener is lost. "Could be stronger" is not a finding. Scores and counts are warnings, not quotas. The aim is academic soundness, not perfection.

### Keeping the kit clean

- Fix a problem where it starts. An error a writer could repeat goes into that outline's Do not say list. A gap in the method goes into the kit.
- A new ruling rewrites the line it touches. A line is added only when no existing line covers it. Part 1 stays one page.
- A fix whose wording the fixer writes, whoever the fixer is, gets a targeted independent check. A fix applied word for word as a checker worded it does not.
- Make targeted corrections and preserve approved text. Do not reopen finished briefs for optional polishing. A change to this kit governs the next batch on; it never reopens a finished story.
