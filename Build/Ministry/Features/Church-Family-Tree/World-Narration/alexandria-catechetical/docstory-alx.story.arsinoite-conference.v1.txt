The village churches near Arsinoe loved a bishop named Nepos, who had written a book against the allegorists. The promises in Scripture, he said, meant a thousand-year kingdom of plenty on this earth, plainly read. Nepos had died, and his book now ruled whole congregations. That book is lost, and it is known only from the bishop who answered it.

Bishop Dionysius went out to the villages. He told the story himself in his book On the Promises, which Eusebius later quoted. He called together the elders and the teachers, and any of the people who wished to come. For three days, from morning to evening, he sat with them and examined the book, argument by argument.

Afterward he wrote that he loved Nepos for his faith, his hard work in the Scriptures and his psalms. Even so, he wrote, "the truth should be loved and honored most of all." He rejoiced in the constancy and sincerity of the believers, and in their willingness to reconsider as they weighed the arguments together.

At the end, Coracion, the leader of the other side, was persuaded, and he said so in front of everyone. Some of the other believers, the account says, were glad at the spirit of agreement all had shown.
