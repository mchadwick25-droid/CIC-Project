# Sheet: alexandria-catechetical / docstory-alx.story.origen-daring-deed

## Original text (as narrated now)

Origen was still young, and teaching the faith in Alexandria. He taught men and women together, day and night.

Eusebius alone tells what Origen did then, and no account from Origen himself describes it. Eusebius says Origen wanted no one to accuse him of wrong, so he took a hard verse from Matthew in the most literal sense. It speaks of those who "have made themselves eunuchs for the kingdom of heaven's sake." By this account he acted on it himself, and tried to keep it secret, but he could not. Eusebius calls the act immature, and in the same breath a proof of unusual faith.

When his bishop, Demetrius, learned of it, he admired the young man's daring and faith, and urged him to keep teaching. Later, once Origen had grown famous beyond Alexandria, the same Demetrius turned that youthful act against him. He wrote to bishops everywhere calling it foolish. He also attacked Origen's ordination by the bishops of Caesarea and Jerusalem.

Some have doubted that it happened at all, and thought the report grew from Origen's own figures of speech. Others see no reason to doubt it.

Origen himself later judged the verse differently. He wrote against reading it so literally.

- divergence_note: The structural sequence (a drastic youthful act; Demetrius's initial admiration; Demetrius's later use of the same act against Origen) is Widely Accepted as what Eusebius reports. Whether the act itself happened as told - or at all - is Contested: the vendored edition's own translator endnote states plainly that 'some have even gone so far as to believe that he never committed the act, but that the report of it arose from a misunderstanding of certain figurative expressions used by him,' naming three nineteenth-century scholars (Boehringer, Schnitzer, Baur) who held that reading, before concluding in its own voice that there is no reason to doubt the report. This record keeps that live disagreement visible rather than adopting either side.
- absent_detail: Eusebius alone reports this; no first-person account from Origen himself describes the act, only his later Commentary on Matthew arguing against a literal reading of the same verse. Whether the act happened exactly as told, happened at all, or grew out of a misunderstanding of Origen's own words is a real, named disagreement in the source's own apparatus (see confidence.divergence_note), not one this record settles. What exactly changed Demetrius's mind, and when, the record does not say beyond the passing of years and Origen's growing fame.
