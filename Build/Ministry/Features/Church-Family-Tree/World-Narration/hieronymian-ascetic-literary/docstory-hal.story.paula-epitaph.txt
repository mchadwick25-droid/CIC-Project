Paula's own voice was not kept. She survives in the memorial Jerome wrote, and it is told here as the ideal that memorial teaches.

Paula came from the oldest blood in Rome, and she counted the Gracchi in her family line. She was a wife and mother in the grandest style the city knew. Then her husband died, and she changed. She wept so hard, Jerome wrote, that she nearly died of grief, and then she turned that whole ruined love toward God.

She dressed plainly and slept on the ground. She gave until she was borrowing to give more, the memorial says. She left Rome for a cave-town in Palestine, and her children stood on the shore. There, Jerome writes, her fortune built houses for monks, for virgins and for strangers on the road. She led her women in psalms and in scripture, and she learned Hebrew well enough to chant the psalms in it. She nursed the sick with her own hands.

She left her daughter a mass of debt, her memorial says, and faith as her only wealth. When she died, bishops carried her body, and the memorial says the whole population of Palestine's cities came. Paula was laid beneath the church at the place of the Lord's birth.
