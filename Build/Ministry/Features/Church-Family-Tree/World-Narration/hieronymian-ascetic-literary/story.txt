This is the story of one circle, from 382 to 420 CE, in Rome and in Bethlehem. In 382 a scholar named Jerome reached Rome as secretary and scriptural adviser to Pope Damasus. There he met a household that already existed. The widow Marcella had made her house on the Aventine hill a place of fasting and scripture study, and historians think other women had joined her before Jerome came. He brought what the household lacked, a scholar's training in texts.

The women of the circle gave up wealth, marriage prospects and rank on purpose, as devotion to Christ. For them, this giving up was itself how a Christian life took shape. Jerome says Marcella turned down a rich suitor and chose a plain life.

In 384 Blaesilla, the daughter of the widow Paula, died in Rome at about twenty. She had been widowed young and had taken up a severe ascetic discipline only months before. Jerome reports that the funeral crowd said fasting had killed her, and that they asked why the detestable monks were not stoned or thrown into the Tiber. That December Pope Damasus died, and the protection he had given Jerome ended with him. In August 385 Jerome sailed east. From the ship he wrote that all Rome had praised him before he knew Paula's house, and that now he was called infamous.

Paula followed him east and settled in Bethlehem. She founded a community of women there and led it until she died in 404. Jerome writes that her fortune paid for houses for monks and for virgins, and for a hostel for travelers. Jerome himself was only a presbyter, under the bishop of Jerusalem. After Paula's death her daughter Eustochium led the women.

Jerome held that the Hebrew text of scripture stood closest to what God said, closer than the Greek version the church had long used. At Bethlehem he spent years turning the Old Testament into Latin from the Hebrew, book by book. Nearly every book came with a short, fighting preface that defended his choices against critics. By Jerome's account, Paula's fortune also paid for his Hebrew teachers.

Around 403, in the North African town of Oea, a bishop read the prophet Jonah aloud from the new translation. The familiar version said a gourd had shaded the prophet, but the new one said ivy, and the congregation erupted. Augustine, bishop of Hippo, told Jerome what happened. According to Augustine, the bishop of Oea was close to losing his people, so he asked the town's Jewish residents about the Hebrew. They backed the old word, and the bishop corrected the reading. Augustine feared that setting aside the old Greek text would put the church's unity at risk, and he wrote that the story made him think Jerome might sometimes be mistaken. Jerome answered point by point. Neither man conceded, and their courteous letters kept crossing for years.

Marcella stayed in Rome. Jerome wrote after her death that, once he left, people in Rome brought their disputes about scripture to her to settle. Many men put hard questions to her, he adds, and priests were sometimes among them. She gave her answers as if they came from him or someone else. In 410 Alaric's Goths took Rome. As Jerome heard it, soldiers came to Marcella's house demanding gold. They did not believe her when she pointed to her plain dress, and they beat her. She died a few days later.

In 416 a quarrel over the teaching of Pelagius reached the Bethlehem monasteries as an attack. Pope Innocent wrote that Eustochium and her young niece, also named Paula, had reported murders and fires to him. He added that they would not name the man behind it. Jerome wrote that the Bethlehem house had lost its earthly wealth but was still filled with spiritual riches.

Jerome died in 420, by the accepted count. Eustochium died in 418, 419 or 420. Years later Augustine called Jerome a most learned man who had translated the scriptures from the Hebrew, and he granted that even the Jews called the work faithful. Still, he said, the churches of Christ judged that no one should be preferred to the authority of the Septuagint translators.
