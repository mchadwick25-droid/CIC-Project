# Sheet: lutheran-wittenberg-and-its-congregations / story

## Original text (as narrated now)

From 1517 to 1580, a reform grew out of Wittenberg. Martin Luther taught there at the university.

At the heart of this tradition sits a frightened conscience. The commandments teach a person to despair of his own powers, and then God's promise comforts him. A sinner is freely justified for Christ's sake, through faith. The sacraments carry that promise. Baptism means the old self is drowned by daily sorrow and repentance, and absolution is given as though from God himself. In the Supper, Christ's body and blood are truly present, in and under the bread and wine, and this church gives no explanation of how.

On 31 October 1517 Luther, a friar, wrote to Albrecht, the Archbishop of Mainz. Preachers were offering indulgences in the archbishop's name, to help build St. Peter's in Rome. Luther was troubled by what their hearers now believed. He asked the archbishop to withdraw the instruction the preachers worked from. He sent ninety-five statements for debate with it, the Ninety-Five Theses.

In 1518 Luther was summoned to Augsburg. By his own account, told years later at his table, he knelt before Cardinal Cajetan. He would not say the one word an Italian urged on him, Revoco, which means I recant.

In 1521 Luther stood before the Emperor and the Electors at the Diet of Worms. Years afterward he told the story to his students at his own table. By his account, he would not take back his books of teaching. If he had been too violent in his books against opponents, he was willing to be corrected. The next day he would accept instruction in everything except what touches Holy Scripture.

The reform ran ahead of its founder. In March 1522 Luther left the Wartburg, where he had been hidden since Worms, against the Elector's instruction to stay. Wittenberg was in disorder over how fast to sweep away the old forms of worship. Luther preached eight days running and told his congregation he saw no signs of love among them.

On 1 July 1523 two young monks, John and Henry, were burned at Brussels after they refused to give up their teaching. Within months Luther wrote a ballad about them.

The reform also taught people in their own language. German hymns were sung inside the Mass. Wittenberg's first hymn-book was printed in 1524 and held eight hymns, four of them Luther's. Johann Walter later remembered that Luther kept him at Wittenberg for three weeks. Walter wrote out the notes for some of the Gospels and Epistles, until the first German Mass was sung.

The reform's chief tool for forming people was a household catechism, written in 1529. Its three parts were the Ten Commandments, the Creed and the Lord's Prayer. A father was to question his children and servants at least once a week. The young were to recite the parts daily and get neither food nor drink until they could. After each part came the question, what does this mean? At his table, Luther told his students that his wife, Katharina von Bora, had asked him a question. Why did the old church pray so hard, and their own household so coldly?

In 1530 the Emperor called a Diet to Augsburg, to deal with the Turk and to hear the quarrels over religion. Melanchthon wrote the Augsburg Confession for it. An elector, several princes and the senates of two cities signed it. Luther's name is not among them. The Confession rejected those who taught otherwise about the Supper.

In 1525 Luther wrote against the peasants' rising, and in 1543 he wrote a treatise against the Jews. The library holds the 1525 writings. It does not hold the 1543 treatise.

Luther died in the mid-1540s. In 1580 a fuller book of confessions was gathered, the Book of Concord. It drew together the catechisms and the confession given decades before.
