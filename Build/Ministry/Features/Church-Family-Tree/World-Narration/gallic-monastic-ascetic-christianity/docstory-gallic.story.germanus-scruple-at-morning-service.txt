Cassian wrote this down years later, in Gaul. He and his friend Germanus had spent an evening in Egypt, at the cell of an old man named Chaeremon near Panephysis, hearing him teach on chastity. One sentence troubled Germanus. The old man had said that even striving with all one's might cannot make a person master of the good, and that it must be received as a gift.

They slept a little and came back for morning prayer, still whispering about it. Chaeremon noticed, cut the service short and asked what was wrong.

Germanus said that chastity is won by long, hard toil, and that the credit should go mainly to the one who does the work, as a farmer earns his harvest. Chaeremon answered that no farmer can claim his harvest by his labor alone, because he needs rain he cannot control. Yet God gives no rich crop to a farmer who never plows. In the same way, the wish to do good and the chance to do it both come first from God. The human part, he said, is to follow day by day the grace that draws a person on.

Later in the same Conference, Chaeremon says that grace works together with the human will, and sometimes waits for some effort from it. Elsewhere he says that wanting the good, doing it and holding on to it are all God's gifts. Yet none of them takes away free will, and how both can be true, he says, the human mind cannot fully grasp.
