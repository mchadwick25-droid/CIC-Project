Barbarians were pushing into Gaul, and Julian Caesar had gathered his army to meet them. He was handing out a donative, a gift of money, and calling his soldiers forward one by one. When Martin's turn came, he would not take the money. He had served Caesar long enough, he said, and now he asked to serve God, since, as Christ's soldier, he could not lawfully fight.

Julian, whom Sulpitius calls the tyrant, said Martin was running from the coming battle out of fear. The charge only made Martin firmer. If this was cowardice, he said, he would stand before the enemy line the next day with no weapon, and the sign of the cross would be his only protection. He was sent back to prison to wait for morning.

The next day the enemy sent envoys to ask for peace. Sulpitius says God was sparing Martin the sight of other men dying. The enemy gave up themselves and everything they had, and no battle was fought.
