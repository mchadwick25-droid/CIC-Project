# Sheet: gallic-monastic-ascetic-christianity / docstory-gallic.story.death-of-martin-at-condate

## Original text (as narrated now)

Martin had long known when he would die, Sulpitius writes, and he had told the brothers so. When the clergy at Condate fell to quarreling, he went to make peace among them. One more church at peace, he judged, would be a fitting end to his life.

Peace was made, but as he was about to return to the monastery, his strength failed. The brothers wept and begged him not to leave them, and Martin wept too. He prayed that he would not refuse more work if he was still needed, and he left the choice to God.

For some days he lay in fever on sackcloth and ashes, praying through whole nights. When his disciples asked to put some ordinary straw under him, he refused. A Christian should die among ashes, he said, and he would be sinning if he left them a different example. Those who were there told us that he saw the devil standing near and rebuked him without fear. Then he died, and they said his face looked like an angel's.

The whole city his body was brought to poured out to meet it, with people from the villages and towns around. Almost two thousand monks, it is said, came to his funeral. Old men walked with young soldiers of Christ, and a choir of virgins kept their grief hidden.

### gallic.story.death-of-martin-at-condate (records/gallic/story/gallic.story.death-of-martin-at-condate.md)
- formation_confidence: Widely Accepted
- divergence_note: Widely Accepted at the narrative level: a named author's letter to a named addressee, written within months of the death (397), on the testimony of "those who were there present" - Sulpitius says so himself and disclaims presence. Contested for the deathbed's supernatural elements (the devil at the bedside; the face "as if ... of an angel"; the snow-white limbs; the water-fowl), carried as what the witnesses "have testified to us." Martin's long soldier's speech is Sulpitius's own rhetorical expansion of one recorded sentence, and is marked as his in the narrative. The funeral's numbers are given as report ("are said to have assembled"). The letter never names the city the body was brought to. Registry row 2 (the Letters) is rated B on a "full text not read" session note and the source record stands at named-not-rechecked; Letter III was read in full at Doc_09 construction (npnf211 div ii.iii.iii) - a fresh verification the source record's own state does not yet reflect, and a scope judgment disclosed at Doc_09 §5 (a letter narrating the death of the tradition's own founder falls within the row's "Martin-circle correspondence context"). Roberts's notes on "Condate" (his Cosne), on Halm's readings, and on the "affectation" of Sulpitius's reluctance to publish are editorial and not adopted.
- absent_detail: The letter never names the city the body was brought to; Sulpitius was not present at the death; the numbers at the funeral are report. The "choir of virgins" is the only trace of the women who chose this life at Tours, and not one of them speaks. Everything after this - the basilica, the cult's growth, the chroniclers of Martin's own city - lies outside our window and evidence.
- tellable_as: Foreknowing his death, Martin goes to make peace among quarreling clerics. He sickens, and refuses straw for his bed of sackcloth and ashes rather than set his disciples a softer example. At his funeral, it is said, nearly two thousand monks gather, old men and young soldiers of Christ, with a choir of virgins.
