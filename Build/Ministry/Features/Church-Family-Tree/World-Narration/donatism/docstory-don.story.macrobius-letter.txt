Maximianus, a soldier of Christ in the letter's words, was chosen to face the Roman proconsul first. The Donatist bishop Macrobius told the story in a letter to the people of Carthage. In it, the night before, at a shared meal, the wine in Maximianus's cup seemed to take the shape of a crown. It shone with the colors of blood and light, and Macrobius took it as an omen of what the next day would ask.

Maximianus was beaten with leaded whips and then with rods, and Isaac was flogged beside him. Isaac called his floggers traditores, men who had handed over the scriptures, and told them to come and heal their madness of unity. Both men were sentenced to exile and shut in prison, and there Isaac died.

That night, Macrobius writes, Maximianus dreamed he fought the emperor's officials and then the emperor himself, and struck out his eye. Then a bright youth crowned him. The proconsul wanted neither man honored as a relic, so he had both bodies weighted with jars of sand and thrown into the sea. By the letter's account, Maximianus was still alive.

In Macrobius's account, the sea would not keep them. For six days, he says, the bodies were driven back toward the shore, until they came up whole to the people waiting. Macrobius closes by telling his readers that the two martyrs wait for them with arms stretched out from heaven, and he urges the living to hurry after them.
