# Sheet: donatism / docstory-don.story.gesta-apud-zenophilum

## Original text (as narrated now)

In 320 a Roman official named Zenophilus asked what had become of the church's money. The charge came from Nundinarius, a deacon whom his own bishop had demoted. Optatus, our opponent, kept the court record among his documents, because its findings tell against us.

The charge named Silvanus, bishop of Cirta, as a betrayer and a thief of the goods of the poor. It said the noble woman Lucilla had given four hundred pieces of silver. For that money, it said, the clergy had plotted to make Majorinus bishop, and the split came out of it.

Then letters were read aloud. Purpurius, a bishop, urged Silvanus to settle with the deacon quietly, before a flame broke out that could not be put out without spiritual bloodshed. In the same letter he said he knew the charges were true. A bishop named Fortis wrote that they should not go into a public court and be condemned by pagans. Several of the letters end the same way. Let no one know about it.

Then a witness named Victor was questioned. Had Silvanus handed anything over? He had, Victor said, but not in his presence. Victor said he had fought against making Silvanus bishop. Pressed to confess what he knew, he said it plainly. "He was a betrayer."

## Grounding records

### don.story.gesta-apud-zenophilum (records/don/story/don.story.gesta-apud-zenophilum.md)
- formation_confidence: Documented
- divergence_note: Documented, and unusually so for this corpus: a court transcript with named deponents, recalled on its central fact by a second source written on the opposing side decades later, which drew on the Acts of the same inquiry and so is not independent of it. The one thing that must not be forgotten is where the transcript survives - inside Optatus's own appendix of documents. The proceeding's record is not his composition, but the selection of it for preservation is his act, and it is preserved because its findings tell against us.
- absent_detail: Silvanus himself is questioned nowhere in what survives here; the proceeding is about him rather than with him, and no answer of his to any of it is on the record. Nor is there any account from our own side of what the money was for, what the seniors thought they were doing with it, or what Lucilla believed she was paying for.
- tellable_as: In 320 a Roman official hears a case against Silvanus, bishop of Cirta. A witness agrees that he was a betrayer, and letters from our own bishops beg that no one find out. The charge also names four hundred pieces of silver from Lucilla as the price of our rival bishop.

### don.figure.silvanus-of-cirta (records/don/figure/don.figure.silvanus-of-cirta.md)
- formation_confidence: Documented
- divergence_note: Documented for the finding against him: the deacon's written accusation, the letters of two fellow bishops conceding it, and a consular official's conclusion from the acts read aloud that Silvanus was a betrayer. Held below any stronger reading for two reasons. He is nowhere questioned in what survives - the proceeding is about him, not with him - so no answer of his is on the record; and the transcript survives inside a hostile compiler's own appendix, chosen for preservation because of what it establishes.

### don.figure.lucilla (records/don/figure/don.figure.lucilla.md)
- formation_confidence: Contested
- divergence_note: Contested, and the banding is strict. What the record supports is a named woman of means at Carthage with the standing to make a rival consecration happen: a fortune, a household, a devotional practice she was rebuked for, and money that a court six years later traced to the founding of a rival altar. Any claim about her own motivations, private character, or theological commitments beyond what Optatus's hostile narrative attributes to her is Inferential-Thin and is not made anywhere in these records. She left no text. Everything reaches us inside the narrative that blames her for the schism.

### don.figure.purpurius (records/don/figure/don.figure.purpurius.md)
- formation_confidence: Documented
- divergence_note: Documented, and unusually well for a figure on our side of the line, because he appears in two independent kinds of evidence: a narrated council scene and his own letters, read into a court record in 320 and preserved verbatim in that record. The letters are the strongest thing here and the hardest - they are his own words, not an opponent's paraphrase, and in them he writes that the accusation against a fellow bishop is true and urges that no one be allowed to find out.

### don.figure.majorinus (records/don/figure/don.figure.majorinus.md)
- formation_confidence: Documented
- divergence_note: Documented for the one act that matters - his consecration as bishop of Carthage against Caecilian, which begins the parallel line of ordination this communion held for more than a century. Almost nothing else about him is recorded. He wrote nothing that survives, held the see only briefly, and reaches us mainly as the name in a sentence about someone else's money.

### don.figure.optatus-of-milevis (records/don/figure/don.figure.optatus-of-milevis.md)
- formation_confidence: Documented
- divergence_note: Documented as author, office and adversarial position. Everything he transmits about this communion is transmitted in the course of arguing against it, roughly five decades after the founding events he narrates; his narrative richness and documentary specificity must never be converted into independent corroboration. The dossier appended to his work contains genuine court and conciliar acts, but the selection of them is his own act, and every one of them tells against us.

### don.figure.augustine (records/don/figure/don.figure.augustine.md)
- formation_confidence: Documented
- divergence_note: Documented as author, office and position, and by far the largest single body of evidence for this world - which is itself the risk rather than a reassurance. His anti-Donatist corpus spans three decades of his episcopate and weighs disproportionately toward the years after his own side's institutional victory. He preserves more of our own words than anyone else, in the form of clauses selected for answerability. No world-level claim should rest on him alone without that being said.
