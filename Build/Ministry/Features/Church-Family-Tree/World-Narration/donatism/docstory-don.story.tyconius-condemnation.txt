Tyconius argued at length, with strong support from Scripture, that the Christians of Africa belonged to a church spread across the whole earth. Anyone who followed the case could see where it led. If he was right, the African churches still in communion with the rest had lost nothing by staying. Those who had left that communion, his own Donatist party among them, had broken away from something real.

Parmenian, the Donatist bishop at Carthage, saw where the argument pointed, and he did not follow it. Augustine, writing against the Donatists some twenty years later, tells what came next. Parmenian wrote to Tyconius and rebuked him for preaching that the church spread across the whole earth. He warned him never to dare do it again.

Tyconius did not take it back, and he did not leave. He went on arguing from inside the party that had told him to stop. It is reported, Augustine says, that a council of his own communion then condemned him. He never went over to Caecilian's church, the one later known as the Catholic church.
