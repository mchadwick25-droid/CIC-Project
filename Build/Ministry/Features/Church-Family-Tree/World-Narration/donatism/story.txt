From around 311, two rival churches stood side by side in Roman North Africa, centered on Carthage and Numidia. Both churches confessed one faith in the Trinity and in Christ, but they split over whether a bishop's conduct under persecution could void what his hand later gave. One side said it could. That side saw itself as the pure, persecuted church of the martyrs, the one true church. History calls its members Donatists, after an early bishop. Optatus, an opponent, says its bishops called themselves 'the party of Donatus' when questioned in court. Whether they used that name among themselves is not known.

The quarrel began in the persecution of 303 to 305, when officials demanded that clergy hand over the scriptures and the church's vessels to be destroyed. Some did. Others refused and suffered for it. The Donatists held that what a minister did in that moment decided whether his hand could be trusted afterward. In 311 or 312, Caecilian was elected bishop of Carthage. Felix of Aptungi, who ordained him, was accused of handing over the scriptures. If that was true, the Donatists held, the new bishop was no bishop at all. A rival party made Majorinus bishop in his place. The emperor later had the charge against Felix tried. Under threat of torture, a witness admitted forging part of the evidence, and Felix was cleared.

For a century, anyone who crossed over from the rival church was washed again, though the Donatists did not count it as a second baptism. In their view, a hand from a broken line had given nothing the first time. Imperial edicts named the practice as an offense.

Persecution came again in 347 and 348. This time it came at the rival church's urging, through the emperor's commissioners. The Donatists' own account says that Bishop Marculus was held four days at a cliff called Novapetra. There, it says, he fasted and was shown a vision of a cup, a crown and a palm. Then he was thrown down. Two laymen of Carthage, Isaac and Maximianus, were flogged and sunk in the sea. Every year, at the grave, the Donatists read such accounts aloud.

By the later fourth century the Donatist church was a full parallel church, with its own bishops, basilicas and councils in the same towns as its rival. In Numidia it far outnumbered the other church, and there it was the ordinary church of the village.

Optatus also reports that their bishop Donatus once asked what the emperor had to do with the church. The hearing at Rome in 313 and the Council of Arles in 314 ruled against the Donatists, and they rejected both rulings. In their view, a council called and enforced by a state that already favored their rival was not the church judging itself. Still, three times they turned to the state when that served their case. They petitioned Constantine in 313 and Julian in 361, and in the 390s they invoked imperial law against their own breakaway Maximianists.

At the Conference of Carthage in 411, about two hundred and eighty Donatist bishops faced a slightly larger number of rivals. The imperial judge offered them chairs, and they refused. Christ had stood before his governor, said Petilian, one of their speakers, and so would they. The judge granted that the name Catholic belonged to whichever side held the truth. But then he said he was bound by an imperial rescript that had already given the name to the other side. He ruled against the Donatists, and penal laws followed.

In 439 a Vandal army took Carthage. That removed the Roman power the Donatists had defined themselves against, but the contest went on under the Vandals, and then under the emperors of the East. In the 590s Pope Gregory the Great wrote of rebaptism still going on in Numidia and pressed for an inquiry.

Almost everything known about the Donatists was written by two men arguing against them, and most of their own words survive only as quotations in those men's books. Four short texts speak for them directly, and so do their bishops at the hearing of 411. So do two words cut into pillars near Bagai, Deo laudes, praise to God.
