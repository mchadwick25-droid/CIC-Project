The judge sat, and the Donatist bishops stood. Flavius Marcellinus offered them chairs, and said it weighed on him to sit while so many venerable men stood. Petilian thanked him and refused. The Donatists did not blush and did not fear, he said. Christ himself stood before the governor, so how could they refuse to stand?

This was Carthage in the summer of 411. The emperor had ordered the whole quarrel settled at law, and imperial notaries took down what was said.

Emeritus of Caesarea carried the Donatist argument. He would not let the court touch the case itself until it had settled the day, the mandates and who was bringing the charge. When the other side claimed the name Catholic, Petilian answered that it meant nothing until the case was decided. Marcellinus agreed that the name belonged to whichever side the truth was found with. But then he said he was bound by the emperor's written order, which had already given them that name.

On the third day Augustine argued from the wheat and the weeds that the church holds good and bad together. Emeritus answered him verse for verse from the same books. He signed the notary's record of most of what he said, and at times he added that he kept his right to appeal.

In the end Marcellinus ruled against the Donatists, though the text of the acts used here breaks off before his sentence.
