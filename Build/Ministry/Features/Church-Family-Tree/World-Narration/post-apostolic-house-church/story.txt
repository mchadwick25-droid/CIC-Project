From about 70 to 200 CE, Christians gathered in households. They lived in Antioch and Syria, in the cities of western Asia Minor, and in Rome. Letters and travelers kept them joined as one, even as the last of the apostles' generation was passing and no central body ruled these churches.

These churches held that legitimate teaching and practice must trace back to what the apostles had testified. Rome's letter to Corinth sets out a chain: God sent Christ, Christ sent the apostles, and the apostles appointed successors. Ignatius, a bishop of Antioch, writes to several churches. He tells them to be subject to the bishop, as the apostles were subject to Christ.

The surviving letters give two different answers to who should lead once the apostles were gone. Ignatius's letters put one bishop at the center, with elders and deacons beside him, and one letter says the elders fit the bishop as strings fit a harp. In Rome, the surviving letters show a council of elders leading together. Ignatius addresses Polycarp, a church leader at Smyrna, as a bishop, yet Polycarp's own letter names him only beside the elders with him.

Letters held these scattered households together. The church at Rome wrote at length to Corinth, which had removed some of its elders from office. Rome argued that men who had served blamelessly should not have been removed. Seven surviving letters under Ignatius's name say he wrote them on a guarded journey toward Rome. Later, Polycarp sent the Philippians the letters of Ignatius that his church held, because they had asked for them.

Giving thanks over bread and cup stood at the center of these gatherings, and its order changed from one household to the next. One community's manual, the Didache, gives thanks over the cup before the bread, and it tells no story of a supper or a betrayal. Ignatius says that only a thanksgiving the bishop leads, or entrusts to another, counts as proper. Justin, a philosopher in Rome, describes a fuller Sunday gathering there.

The danger from the state was always there, but the actual incidents came and went. Tacitus, a Roman historian, reports that Nero blamed the Great Fire of Rome in 64 on a group the crowd called Christians. A great number, he says, were convicted and put to death. No source names one of them. About fifty years later, Pliny, the governor of Bithynia and Pontus, wrote to the emperor Trajan for guidance. He found, in his words, "nothing but a depraved superstition that went too far." Trajan replied that Christians were not to be sought out, though those accused and convicted were to be punished.

Some communities read a death for the name as the completion of a life. Ignatius writes that he wants to be ground by the teeth of wild beasts, so that he may be found the pure bread of Christ. The church at Smyrna told how Polycarp died. Its letter says the community kept his bones in a fitting place, where they hoped to gather each year, as they were able, to keep the day of his death. The same letter names Quintus, who came forward for trial and lost his nerve at the sight of the beasts. It says the gospel does not teach people to give themselves up. Only these two inside accounts survive, Ignatius's letters and Smyrna's letter, and both come from Antioch and Asia Minor.

By about 200, one bishop was becoming the dominant pattern, even in Rome. One modern scholar, Peter Lampe, thinks Rome may not have had a single bishop before Victor, its bishop from 189 to 199. The texts of these churches do not say how or when Rome changed. From about 180, writers in other regions argued from an apostolic line they treated as already secure.
