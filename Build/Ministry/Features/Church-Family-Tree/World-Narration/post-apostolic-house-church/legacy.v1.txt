The question of who held authority in the churches did not stay open forever. By around 200 CE, one bishop had become the common pattern almost everywhere, including in Rome, which had once been led by a council of elders with nothing felt to be missing. No text says how or when that change was actually felt. Only the quiet disappearance of the older, argued option is visible.

The texts of these churches did not survive evenly. Ignatius's letters mattered so much to the later, settled office he argued for that it took a two-century scholarly fight just to prove they were genuine. First Clement was copied into a great Bible manuscript. The Shepherd of Hermas was once read as scripture by three major later teachers, but it was later moved to the shelf newcomers were given to read. That was a real demotion, though not the outright ban later memory sometimes assumes. The Didache, a teaching manual of these churches, disappeared from use for roughly eighteen centuries. What survived best was not always what mattered most at the time. It was what a later settlement found useful to keep.
