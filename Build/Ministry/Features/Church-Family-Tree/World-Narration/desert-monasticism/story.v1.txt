Between about 320 and 430, Christians in Egypt left their villages for the edge of the desert. The last empire-wide persecution had ended between 311 and 313, and until then, dying for the faith had been a way to offer everything. The monks' own tradition describes their struggle in the language of that total confrontation. In that language, the fight was now against demons, and the tempting thoughts they stirred in a person's mind. These Christians saw the leaving itself as the whole work of a Christian life.

Athanasius, bishop of Alexandria, wrote the Life of Antony between about 356 and 362, and the movement's founding portrait rests mostly on that one book. Scholars question how far its details are history. Athanasius says Antony was about eighteen or twenty when his parents died. That was about 270. Soon after, Antony heard the Gospel read in church as if it had been read for him, gave the family land to the villagers, and then gave away the rest.

Antony then left in stages. He lived first near his village, then in an abandoned fort across the river, and last on a mountain in the inner desert, three days' journey out. Athanasius gives a reason only for the last move: crowds kept finding Antony, and he wanted quiet. By this account he stayed on that mountain for most of the rest of his life.

Not every monk lived alone. About 320, Pachomius gathered monks into a community at Tabennesi. Palladius, a monk who lived in Egypt, wrote long after Pachomius died, and he says an angel gave Pachomius a tablet with a rule on it. Tradition says the community had nine houses for men and two for women when he died in 346, and it puts the members, roughly, in the low thousands.

Monks also lived in settlements such as Nitria, Kellia and Scetis, where they kept small cells and gathered once a week for worship. Authority worked in two ways. Hermits and the monks of the settlements looked to an elder called abba or amma, a person whose wisdom had been proved. Elders held no office. In Pachomius's houses, by contrast, a written rule and appointed offices governed. Even so, people remembered Pachomius himself as a father-elder. Both kinds of authority stood side by side through these years.

The desert had no fixed course of study, and discernment did that work. Called diakrisis, it was the skill of judging how much discipline a particular person could bear. In the collected sayings of the elders, one pattern recurs: an eager newcomer asks an elder for an extreme practice, and the elder steers him toward something more moderate.

Evagrius was a formally educated man. Basil of Caesarea ordained him a reader, and Gregory of Nazianzus made him a deacon. About 385 he settled at Kellia, where he lived for fourteen years. There he wrote a system for the struggle. It sorted the tempting thoughts, the logismoi, into eight kinds, and it set out a path from practice to freedom from passion, and then to contemplation. The system belonged to him and his learned circle.

Evagrius died at Kellia in January 399. That year Theophilus, the bishop of Alexandria, sent an Easter letter saying that God has no human body. John Cassian, a monk who had lived at Scete, wrote about it decades later. He reports that almost all the monks of Egypt took the letter badly: they felt it went against Scripture, which they took to give God a human form. Theophilus then reversed course, for reasons scholars dispute, and campaigned against the followers of the teacher Origen.

In 400, a council at Alexandria condemned Origen and the monks who followed him. That year four monk leaders called the Tall Brothers, and others among them, had to leave Egypt, and the circle around Evagrius was broken up.
