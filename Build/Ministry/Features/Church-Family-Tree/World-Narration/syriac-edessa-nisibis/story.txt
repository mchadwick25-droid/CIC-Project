From about 200 to 410 CE, Syriac-speaking Christians lived on the frontier between the Roman and Persian empires. Edessa lay under Roman rule, though its people spoke Aramaic, and Nisibis was a Roman fortress city from 298 until Rome gave it up to Persia in 363. Aphrahat wrote his letters from inside Persia. The city chronicle of Edessa holds the earliest trace of a church there. Written about 340 years later, it says a flood in about 201 destroyed a church building, though scholars dispute whether that line is original.

These Christians taught through song and symbol. Ephrem, a deacon, wrote teaching hymns called madrashe, and each had a refrain, so a congregation learned the faith by singing it. He read Scripture and the world through the raza, a symbol bound to the truth it shows. In this reading, Eden answers the cross, and the Jordan answers the font of baptism. In his Pearl hymns, he reads a whole theology out of one pearl, and the first hymn begins, "One day, my brothers, I picked up a pearl."

These churches heard the Gospel as one continuous story. Around 172, a man named Tatian wove Matthew, Mark, Luke and John into a single harmony, which Aphrahat quotes and Ephrem explained in a commentary.

At the core of the vowed life of these churches stood the covenant, called the qyama. Its members were men and women who promised to stay unmarried for life and to serve the congregation, and they lived in town among their own families. The earliest dated witness is Aphrahat's sixth Demonstration, from 337, which speaks to an order already in place. Members were also called ihidaye, a word that means single ones and is also the Syriac title for Christ, the Only-Begotten. Later accounts say that women of the covenant sang Ephrem's hymns in choirs.

Who held authority was never settled in these years. Bishops led their churches, but the vowed men and women of the covenant had a standing of their own, and a trusted teacher also carried weight. Aphrahat drafted a letter for a meeting of bishops and clergy, though whether he was a bishop is unknown. In Persia, Papa, bishop of the royal city of Seleucia-Ctesiphon, claimed to lead all the bishops. According to later chronicles, other bishops fiercely contested his claim around 315.

Ephrem also defined the faith by answering rivals who preached in the same cities and the same language. Bardaisan was a thinker at the court of the king of Edessa, and his following lasted for two centuries after his death. Marcion rejected the Maker of the world, and his followers lived in the same region. Mani started a new religion in the third century. Ephrem answered all three by name, in prose and in hymns, and he treated them as one kind of deception, though their systems were different.

On the Persian side, faith meant endurance under a hostile crown. In the 340s, King Shapur II turned on his Christian subjects. Sozomen, a church historian of the 400s, says Shapur first taxed them heavily and then had their priests killed. Simeon bar Sabbae, bishop of Seleucia-Ctesiphon, was accused of siding with Rome, and he refused to worship the sun and was executed with his clergy. Tradition dates his death to 341, though some scholars argue for about 344. Bishop after bishop was killed, and after Barba'shmin, a later bishop, was killed, the church's chief seat in Persia stayed empty for about twenty years. Roman Edessa was largely free of this persecution.

Around 400, Marutha, a bishop in Roman Mesopotamia, went to the Persian court as the Roman emperor's envoy. His access to King Yazdegerd I helped win royal permission for the synod of 410. That year the Persian state recognized the church, and the synod set it in order as one body, with provinces under Isaac, bishop of the royal city.
