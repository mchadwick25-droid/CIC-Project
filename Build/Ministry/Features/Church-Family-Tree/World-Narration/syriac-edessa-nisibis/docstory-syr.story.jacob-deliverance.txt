Ephrem's hymns sing that Nisibis was saved when Shapur, king of Persia, laid siege to it, but they say nothing of gnats. The story of Jacob's prayer comes from Theodoret of Cyrus, a Greek church historian, writing about a century later. Even the siege it belongs to is unsettled: it may be that of 338, 346 or 350.

Shapur's army lay against the walls, and the river had been turned against the city. In Theodoret's telling, Ephrem begged the aged bishop Jacob to go up on the wall and curse the enemy. The old man agreed and climbed into a tower, where he looked out over the numberless camp.

Then, Theodoret writes, "he called down no other curse on them than that mosquitoes and gnats should be sent against them." In his account, clouds of them came after the prayer and drove the elephants and the horses wild, until the Persians left their camp and fled.
