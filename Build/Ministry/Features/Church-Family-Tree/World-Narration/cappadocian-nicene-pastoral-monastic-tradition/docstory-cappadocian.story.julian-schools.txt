Julian spent part of his boyhood at Macellum, an estate in Cappadocia. His family had been purged, and he was kept there under Christian tutors he did not choose. As a grown man he rejected that schooling entirely and turned back to the old gods and to the classical culture.

Julian ruled as emperor from 361 to 363. He issued a school law, and separately a second, narrower ruling aimed at Christian teachers. Those who taught Homer and the old poets, he ruled, must believe in the gods those texts honored, or stop teaching them. His reasoning survives in his own words. A Christian teacher who explained stories he said he did not believe was dishonest, Julian argued, at the very root of his craft.

The measure struck at the Greek learning, the paideia, which the leaders of these churches had spent their lives mastering and had defended as compatible with their faith. Julian is also remembered for taking measures against Caesarea itself, in the region of his own schooling, because of its Christian loyalties.

Julian died on campaign in Persia in 363, but the fury of Gregory of Nazianzus outlived him. Gregory wrote a pair of speeches against the dead emperor, ferocious even for attacks of that kind. They refused Julian any of the honor a dead emperor might expect.
