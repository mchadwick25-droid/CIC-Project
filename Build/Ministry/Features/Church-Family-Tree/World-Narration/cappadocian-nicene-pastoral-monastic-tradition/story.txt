From about 325 to 394, churches in Cappadocia and Pontus held to the faith of the Council of Nicaea. Nearly everything that survives from them was written by three men. Two were brothers, Basil of Caesarea and Gregory of Nyssa. The third, Gregory of Nazianzus, had been Basil's friend since their student days.

These churches held that the Son is fully what the Father is, and that the Holy Spirit is no lesser presence trailing behind them. Two Greek words carried the idea. Ousia meant what God is, one being, and hypostasis meant who God is. Father, Son and Holy Spirit were each a real someone, none a costume and none a creature. Their word for reverence, eusebeia, covered exact words about God, and it also covered right living, with money and power included.

About 368, drought struck Caesarea and its countryside. Landowners with full storehouses held back their grain and waited for higher prices. Basil, then a priest, preached against them. He charged that to hoard a neighbor's bread while the neighbor starves is theft, whatever the law calls it. Granaries opened, and relief was organized for as long as the crisis lasted.

Basil became bishop of Caesarea in 370. Later he built a poorhouse outside the city walls, with a guest-house, an infirmary and a house for people with leprosy, where ascetics served as nurses. Gregory of Nazianzus called it the new city.

For most of this period the court backed a rival creed, which held the Son to be merely like the Father. In 371 or 372 the emperor Valens sent his prefect Modestus to bring Basil into line. According to Gregory, Basil's friend, Modestus threatened him with confiscation, exile, torture and death. Basil is remembered answering that a man who owns nothing has nothing to confiscate.

One flashpoint in the fight over the Holy Spirit was a prayer. About 375, Basil prayed with his people and gave glory to the Father in two forms: with the Son, together with the Holy Spirit, and through the Son, in the Holy Spirit. Basil reports that some who heard him called his words new and contradictory. To glorify the Spirit together with the Father and the Son placed the Spirit on the Creator's side of the line.

Amphilochius of Iconium, a younger bishop and friend, asked Basil to answer in writing, and the answer became a book, On the Holy Spirit. Basil argued that the words of worship carry what the church believes. Yet in his own writings he never plainly called the Spirit God. His allies argued about this at the time, with some wanting the plain word and others defending his restraint.

Macrina was the family's eldest child, and her circle called her the Teacher. She led a community of ascetic women on the family estate at Annisa. In 379 her brother Gregory of Nyssa found her dying. He later shaped their last talk into a dialogue on the soul and the resurrection, and scholars disagree on how much of it is hers.

Gregory of Nazianzus carried the argument to Constantinople. In 379 he came to lead the small Nicene church there, but a rival church, long favored by the court, still held the city's great churches. So his people met in a private house he named Anastasia, which means resurrection, and there he preached the five Theological Orations.

The tide turned under a new emperor, Theodosius, who favored the Nicene faith. In 381 the Council of Constantinople met. Gregory of Nazianzus was made bishop of the city and given the council's presiding chair, but he did not hold it long. He resigned before the work was done. By his own telling, his election was disputed and he was worn out.

The council ratified the faith of Nicaea. Its creed says the Holy Spirit is worshipped and glorified together with the Father and the Son. The bishops wrote the faith the way these churches had already been singing it. Eastern Orthodox churches still confess this creed, and Roman Catholics honor Basil and Gregory of Nazianzus as teachers of the whole church.
