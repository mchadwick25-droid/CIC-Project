People who respected both churches, Zurich's and Geneva's, had begun to worry. By 1549 they feared that Calvin's teaching on the Supper differed from what Zurich taught. Calvin wrote that it seemed to differ "in some respect, I scarcely know what." Zurich's pastors named what many thought divided them: Christ's bodily presence, the meaning of the words of the Supper, the eating of his body, and what the Supper does.

Calvin thought a friendly conference was the best way to clear up the worry. He went to Zurich himself, with his colleague William Farel.

The two men met with Zurich's pastors, and they set their agreement down in twenty-six short articles. This was the Consensus Tigurinus.

The articles place Christ's body in heaven and reject any idea of him enclosed in the bread. They take "This is my body" figuratively. Christ feeds the soul through faith, by the Holy Spirit.

Calvin's letter afterward calls it a faithful record of the conference. It adds that every colleague serving Christ under Geneva's jurisdiction had signed it to show their approval.
