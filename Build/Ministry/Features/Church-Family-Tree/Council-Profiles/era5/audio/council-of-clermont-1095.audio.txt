[calmly] At Clermont in November 1095, Pope Urban II sat in a raised pulpit and spoke of Christians suffering in Jerusalem, Antioch and other cities of the East. Some hearers wept, some trembled and some argued. Then Adhémar, bishop of Le Puy, came forward with a glad face, knelt, and asked the pope's leave and blessing to go. At once, the nobles sewed the cross onto their clothes.

Baldric, abbot of Bourgueil, tells it so, and says he watched it happen. His account is the fullest from inside the council, but it is no transcript. Baldric wrote the pope's speech in his own Latin, and he wanted the journey made. The acts are lost to us. Two of its canons survive, printed by Carl Mirbt, a German editor.

Urban had crossed the Alps, William of Tyre says, to escape the anger of the emperor Henry IV. After a council at Piacenza, he called another in Auvergne. Baldric speaks of many bishops and abbots of Gaul, and of powerful laymen without number. No count of them survives.

William wrote in the crusader kingdom nearly ninety years later, and he gives another speech. In his telling, Peter the Hermit had brought the pope a letter from the patriarch of Jerusalem, and Peter was present. Baldric does not name Peter at the council.

Under the second canon, whoever went to free the church of God at Jerusalem, for devotion alone and not for honor or money, would have the journey counted in place of all penance. Because Gaul was short of food, another canon set a truce. For three years it was to hold without a break for peasants and merchants, and for knights from Thursday to Sunday each week. Pilgrims to the holy places came first of all.

Envoys came from Raymond, count of Toulouse, to say he had already taken the cross. After the council, Baldric writes, the bishops preached the journey and lay people shouted it, and every day more were bound for Jerusalem.

As the armies came east, Anna Komnene, the emperor's daughter, wrote that her father feared their greed and their broken promises. She traced the whole movement to Peter.

[warmly] Behind the soldiers, she wrote, came a crowd more than the sand and the stars, with crosses on their shoulders, and women and children who had left their homes. They looked like rivers flowing together from everywhere.
