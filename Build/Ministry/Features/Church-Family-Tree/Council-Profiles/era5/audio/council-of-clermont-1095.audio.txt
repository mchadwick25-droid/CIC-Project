[calmly] At Clermont in November 1095, Pope Urban II sat in a pulpit. He spoke of Christians suffering in Jerusalem and the East, and called the knights to fight for Jerusalem. Some hearers wept, some trembled and some argued. Then Adhémar, bishop of Le Puy, came forward with a glad face, knelt, and asked the pope's leave and blessing to go. The nobles sewed the cross onto their clothes.

Baldric, abbot of Bourgueil, tells it so, and says he watched. Baldric gives the pope's words, or words like them, in his own Latin, and he wanted the journey made. The acts are not among our sources. Some of the council's rulings, its canons, survive in later copies, among them the record of a council at Beauvais in 1114.

Urban had crossed the Alps, William of Tyre says, to escape the emperor Henry IV's anger. After a council at Piacenza, he called another in Auvergne. Baldric speaks of many bishops and abbots of Gaul, and of powerful laymen without number. Lambert, a canon of Saint-Omer, counts 310 bishops.

William wrote in the crusader kingdom nearly ninety years later and gives another speech. In his telling, Peter the Hermit had brought the pope a letter from the patriarch of Jerusalem, and Peter was present. Baldric does not name Peter at the council.

Under one canon, whoever went to free the church of God at Jerusalem would have the journey counted in place of all penance. He had to go for devotion alone, and not to gain honor or money. Another forbade kings and princes to invest anyone with a church office. Because many regions of Gaul were short of food, a third set a truce. Peasants and merchants were to have it without a break for three years.

Envoys came from Raymond, count of Toulouse, to say he had already taken the cross. After the council, Baldric writes, the bishops preached the journey and lay people shouted it. Every day more were bound for Jerusalem.

Anna Komnene, daughter of the emperor Alexios, wrote that as the armies came east, her father feared what she called their greed and broken promises. She traced the whole movement to Peter.

[warmly] With the soldiers, she wrote, came an unarmed crowd more than the sand and the stars, with crosses on their shoulders. Women and children had left their homes. They looked like rivers flowing together from everywhere.
