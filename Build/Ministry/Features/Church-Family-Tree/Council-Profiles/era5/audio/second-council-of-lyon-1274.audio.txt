[calmly] Before the council at Lyon had ended, the Greek envoys arrived. A great crowd of prelates went out half a league to meet them, and Pope Gregory X received them with a kiss and tears of joy. Then, as the pope celebrated Mass, the Greeks sang the Creed three times, with the article on how the Holy Spirit proceeds. Their interpreter, a Franciscan friar from Constantinople, had taught them.

Nicholas Glassberger, a Franciscan chronicler writing in 1508, tells it so. Of the council's acts, only three texts are among our sources, in modern editors' extracts. The Greek side reaches us from later writers, from about 1300 on.

Gregory opened it on the feast of Philip and James in 1274, Glassberger says. There he told of a dream in which Christ promised that through him the church would win back the Holy Land and the Greeks.

Manuel Kalekas, a Greek who sided with the Latins and died in 1410, gives about 400 bishops. Thomas Aquinas set out carrying his book against the Greeks' errors, but fell ill on the road and died.

The council taught that the Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son as from one source, not two. Michael VIII, emperor at Constantinople, sent a profession of faith. Clemens Bannwart, Denzinger's editor, notes that Pope Clement IV had first put it to him in 1267.

A new rule locked the cardinals in one room to elect a pope. After eight days without a choice, they were to get only bread, wine and water.

Bonaventure, the Franciscan cardinal, died while the council still sat. Greeks and Latins followed his body to burial with bitter tears.

Gennadios Scholarios, a Greek opponent of the union, says Michael feared Latin revenge for Constantinople and wanted his rule safe for himself and his sons. He told the church the union was only a concession, but his envoys brought back open Latinism. Patriarch Joseph of Constantinople refused it and lost his throne. The chronicle printed under Sphrantzes's name says Michael flogged, ill-treated and exiled many who resisted. After his death, Gennadios writes, a great synod overturned the act of Lyon.

[warmly] In 1870 the First Vatican Council looked back to Lyon. With that council's approval, it said, "the Greeks professed that the holy Roman Church enjoys supreme and full primacy and pre-eminence over the whole Catholic Church."
