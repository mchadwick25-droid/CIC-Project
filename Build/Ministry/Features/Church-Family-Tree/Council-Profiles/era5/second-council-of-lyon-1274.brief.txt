Before the council at Lyon had ended, the Greek envoys arrived. A great crowd of prelates went out half a league to meet them, and Pope Gregory X received them with a kiss and tears of joy. Then, as the pope sang Mass, the Greeks sang the Creed three times, with the article on how the Holy Spirit proceeds. Their interpreter, a Franciscan friar from Constantinople, had taught them.

Nicholas Glassberger, a Franciscan chronicler writing in 1508, tells it so. Of the council's acts, only three texts survive, in modern editors' extracts. No Greek account from the time is among our sources. The Greek side reaches us from writers of the 1400s and later.

Gregory opened it on 1 May 1274, Glassberger says. In a dream, he told the bishops, Christ had promised that through him the church would win back the Holy Land and the Greeks. Manuel Kalekas, a Greek who sided with the Latins more than a century later, gives about 400 bishops. Thomas Aquinas set out carrying his book against the Greeks' errors, but fell ill on the road and died.

The council taught that the Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son as from one source, not two. The emperor Michael VIII sent a profession of faith. The Roman church, it said, holds supreme and full primacy over the whole Catholic church. Denzinger's editor notes that Pope Clement IV had first put that text to Michael in 1267.

A new rule locked the cardinals in one room to elect a pope. After eight days without a choice, they were to get only bread, wine and water.

Bonaventure, the Franciscan cardinal, died while the council still sat. Greeks and Latins followed his body to burial with bitter tears.

Gennadios Scholarios, a Greek opponent of the union, says Michael feared Latin revenge for Constantinople and wanted his rule safe for himself and his sons. He told the church the union was only a concession, but his envoys brought back open Latinism. Patriarch Joseph refused it and lost his throne. A later Greek chronicle says Michael flogged, maimed and exiled many who resisted. After his death, Gennadios writes, a great synod overturned the act of Lyon.

In 1870 the First Vatican Council recalled that, with the approval of the Second Council of Lyons, "the Greeks professed that the holy Roman Church enjoys supreme and full primacy."
