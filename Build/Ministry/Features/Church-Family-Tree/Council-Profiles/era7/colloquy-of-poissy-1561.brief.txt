About noon on 9 September 1561, the French court gathered in the great refectory of the nuns at Poissy. The king sat with his mother, Queen Catherine, and the King and Queen of Navarre. Below them sat six cardinals and thirty-six bishops and archbishops. The Reformed ministers stood bare-headed at a barrier, and Theodore Beza spoke for them.

Our main source is the Ecclesiastical History of the Reformed Churches, printed in 1580 with no author named. It was long credited to Beza. Its 1889 editor, Rodolphe Reuss, thought Beza directed the work and gave his memories of Poissy, but did not compile it. It tells the Reformed side. The Catholic side speaks through the prelates' own decisions, through Jesuits who came with the pope's legate, and through the historians Sarpi and Pallavicino.

The Estates had asked for a free national council. The crown gave the ministers a safe-conduct, the History says, to see whether they could be brought back to the Roman Church.

Beza prayed that all might sing to the Lord with one voice. Christ's body, he said, is truly offered in the Supper. Yet it is as far from the bread as the highest heaven from the earth. The prelates murmured, "Blasphemavit," he has blasphemed. Cardinal de Tournon asked that Beza be silenced. The king did not stir, the History says, and Beza finished. Sarpi says he was told to conclude.

On 16 September the Cardinal of Lorraine answered. The king, he said, was the Church's son, not its lord. On 26 September the Jesuit Diego Laínez spoke to the queen in Italian. It was not for her, he told her, nor for any prince, to handle matters of faith.

Five men from each side then drafted a formula on the Supper. On 9 October the prelates rejected it as captious. Unless the ministers signed the Catholic confession, they would not hear them again. They asked that the ministers be kept out of a kingdom of one God, one king, one faith and one law.

Catherine wrote that the meetings bred disputes upon disputes, far more than union. Pallavicino says neither side gave way, and each claimed a kind of victory.

In January 1562 a royal edict let the Reformed meet by day outside the towns until a general council decided. It still hoped to bring them back into one fold.
