In March 1541 Cardinal Gaspar Contarini, the pope's legate, came to the emperor's Diet at Regensburg, which English writers called Ratisbon. He gave so many blessings, John Calvin wrote, "that for two days afterwards his arm, I think, must have felt the fatigue of it."

Calvin, then at Strasbourg, had been chosen as a deputy to the Diet. Contarini, he wrote, wished to bring the Protestants "under the yoke of subjection without bloodshed." The emperor Charles V, Calvin thought, wanted peace, for he feared the Turks and the king of France.

None of the colloquy's own papers is among our sources: not the book they worked from, nor the articles they agreed. Calvin's letters stand in for them. Two rival historians of the Council of Trent tell it too. Paolo Sarpi was hostile to Rome; the Jesuit Sforza Pallavicino wrote to answer him.

On 20 April the emperor named six men to talk. For the Catholics he chose Julius Pflug, John Gropper and John Eck. For the Protestants he chose Philip Melanchthon, Martin Bucer and John Pistorius. He gave them his hand, Calvin says, and told them to aim at "the glory of God, the public peace."

They worked from a book that the emperor's minister Granvelle gave the legate. Contarini and the nuncio judged Gropper its author, Pallavicino says.

They agreed on original sin and free will. On justification, after keen debate, a formula "was accepted on both sides." Calvin wrote to his friend Farel, "You will be astonished, I am sure, that our opponents have yielded so much." In Rome, Pallavicino says, the agreed words did not satisfy, because they were ambiguous.

Then came the Lord's Supper. "There stood the impassable rock which barred the way to farther progress," Calvin wrote. The Protestants rejected transubstantiation. Pallavicino says the Lutherans began the quarrel by refusing the word, as one the ancient Fathers did not use.

On 28 July, by Sarpi's account, the emperor closed the Diet with his decree, the recess. All was referred to a general council, or a national one, Calvin wrote, and "the whole plan of pacification passed off in smoke." Pallavicino agrees that neither side received the articles.

A year later Pope Paul III called a council to Trent. In his bull he recalled proposing a council "especially and finally in the diet of Ratisbon," where Contarini served "with very great learning and integrity."
