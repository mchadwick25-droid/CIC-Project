In March 1541 Cardinal Gaspar Contarini, the pope's legate, came to the emperor's Diet at Regensburg, which English writers called Ratisbon. He made so many crosses, John Calvin wrote, "that for two days afterwards his arm, I think, must have felt the fatigue of it."

Calvin, a Protestant at Strasbourg, had come as a deputy. Contarini, he wrote, wished to bring the Protestants "under the yoke of subjection without bloodshed." The emperor Charles V, Calvin thought, wanted peace, for he feared the Turks and trouble with France.

None of the colloquy's papers is among our sources: not the book they worked from, nor the agreed articles. Calvin's letters stand in for them. Two rival historians of the Council of Trent tell it too. The Venetian friar Paolo Sarpi was hostile to the papal court. The Jesuit Sforza Pallavicino answered him.

On 20 April the emperor named six men to talk. For the Catholics he chose Julius Pflug, John Gropper and John Eck. For the Protestants he chose Philip Melanchthon, Martin Bucer and John Pistorius.

They worked from a book the emperor's minister Granvelle gave the legate. The two papal envoys saw that Gropper wrote it, Pallavicino says.

They agreed on original sin and free will, Calvin reports. On justification, after hard debate, a formula "was accepted on both sides." He wrote to his friend Farel, "You will be astonished, I am sure, that our opponents have yielded so much." The Jesuit Peter Faber was at the Diet. On 12 May he wrote that the faith's business went on in great doubt. In Rome, Pallavicino says, the agreed words did not satisfy, because their meaning was unclear.

Then came the Lord's Supper. "There stood the impassable rock which barred the way to farther progress," Calvin wrote. The Protestants rejected transubstantiation. Pallavicino says the Lutherans began the quarrel by refusing the word, as one the ancient Fathers did not use.

On 28 July, by Sarpi's account, the emperor closed the Diet with his decree, the recess. All was referred to a general council, or a national one, Calvin wrote, and "the whole plan of pacification passed off in smoke." Pallavicino says neither side received the articles.

The next year Pope Paul III called a council to Trent. In his bull he recalled proposing a council "especially and finally in the diet of Ratisbon," where Contarini served "with very great learning and integrity."
