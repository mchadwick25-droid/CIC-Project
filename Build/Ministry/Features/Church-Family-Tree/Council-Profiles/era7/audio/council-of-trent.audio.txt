[calmly] On 4 December 1563, the last day of the Council of Trent, the Cardinal of Lorraine led the fathers in acclamations. The acts record their answers: "Ever let us confess, ever keep." Then came "Anathema to all heretics," and the reply, "Anathema, anathema." Many wept for joy, wrote Servanzio, as Waterworth quotes his diary.

The council's own acts are held, in English, in the edition of the Catholic editor J. Waterworth. Two rival histories tell the rest. Paolo Sarpi, a Venetian friar, wrote against the Roman court. The Jesuit Sforza Pallavicino wrote to refute him.

Pope Paul III called the council in 1542. In his bull he chose Trent because the Germans "wished for the city of Trent." It opened on 13 December 1545, with twenty-five prelates by Sarpi's count, and met in three periods.

In a letter its editor dates to 1545, John Calvin called the pope Antichrist. His side, he wrote, "will never permit him to be the judge." In 1552 envoys from Saxony came. Before anything else, Sarpi says, they asked the council to affirm that it stood above the pope. In 1562 the English bishop John Jewel called it "a conspiracy and not a council."

The council ruled that Scripture and the unwritten traditions, "transmitted as it were from hand to hand," be received with equal reverence. It condemned the teaching that faith alone justifies, with nothing else required. Bishops must live in their dioceses. Cathedral churches were to train youths in "a perpetual seminary of ministers of God."

In 1547 the acts call a disease at Trent "notoriously certain," and the council moved to Bologna. Sarpi says the legate del Monte put men up to ask the doctors if it was contagious. Pallavicino cites the sworn word of the council's physician. In 1562 they split over whether bishops must reside by divine right. Each historian counts the votes his own way. The decree of 1563 grounds pastors' duties in divine precept, but does not call residence itself divine right.

At the close, the acts count 255 signers, 168 of them bishops. Pope Pius IV confirmed the decrees in January 1564. Abdisho IV, a patriarch from Assyria, had sworn in Rome to teach what the council would settle.

[warmly] Sarpi judged that it left the discords "irreconcileable." Pallavicino writes that the fathers saw a work finished after eighteen years, thorny, hard and rich in fruit.
