[calmly] About noon on 9 September 1561, the French court gathered in the refectory of the nuns at Poissy. The king sat with his mother, Queen Catherine. Three cardinals sat on each side, and below them thirty-six bishops and archbishops. The Reformed ministers stood bare-headed at a barrier, and Theodore Beza spoke for them.

That count comes from our main source, the Ecclesiastical History of the Reformed Churches, printed in 1580 with no author named. It was long credited to Beza. Its 1889 editor, Rodolphe Reuss, thought Beza directed it and gave his memories of Poissy, but did not compile it. It tells the Reformed side, and prints the prelates' own decisions. The Catholic side speaks through Jesuits who came with the pope's legate. So do two historians of Trent, the Jesuit Sforza Pallavicino and the Venetian friar Paolo Sarpi, hostile to the papal court.

In July an assembly resolved to give the ministers a safe-conduct and a hearing. The aim, the History says, was to see if they could be reunited with the Roman Church.

Beza said believers truly shared in Christ's body and blood in the Supper, by faith. But in distance of place, his body was as far from the bread and wine as the highest heaven from the earth. Some prelates murmured, "Blasphemavit," he has blasphemed. Cardinal de Tournon asked that Beza be silenced. The king did not stir, the History says, and Beza finished. Sarpi says he was told to conclude.

On 16 September the Cardinal of Lorraine answered. The king, he said, was the Church's son, not its lord. On 26 September the Jesuit Diego Laínez spoke to the queen. It was not for her, he told her, nor for temporal princes, to handle matters of faith.

At the queen's order, five from each side reached a formula on the Supper. On 9 October the prelates rejected it as captious. They wanted ministers who would not sign the Catholic confession barred from a kingdom of one God, one king, one faith and one law.

Catherine wrote that two meetings bred disputes upon disputes, far more than union. Pallavicino says neither side gave way, and each claimed a kind of victory.

[warmly] In January 1562 a royal edict let the Reformed meet by day outside the towns, until the general council decided or the king ordered otherwise. It still hoped to bring them back into one fold.
