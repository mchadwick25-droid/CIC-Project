[calmly] In the Angrogna valley of Piedmont, the Waldensian pastors, called barbes, met with the heads of their families. All the people were present too, a manuscript of the meeting says. On the first day they debated whether a Christian might ever swear an oath.

That manuscript reaches us only in quotations by two later historians, Alexis Muston and Édouard Montet. The articles survive in printings by the valleys' own historians, Jean-Paul Perrin, Pierre Gilles and Jean Léger, and by the English envoy Samuel Morland. All wrote on the Reformed side. Most date the meeting 12 September 1532. Perrin, the earliest, gives 1535.

In 1530 the Waldensians had sent the barbe George Morel to the reformers at Basel and Strasbourg. Their teachers, Morel wrote, had lived more than four hundred years among cruel thorns. Oecolampadius replied that fear of persecution made them hide their faith.

Gilles says pastors came from the valleys and nearby lands. A guard later told a ducal inquiry he had kept watch for ministers at the hamlet of Chanforans. Among them was "M. Farel, qui avoit la barbe rouge": Master Farel, with his red beard. Gilles places that testimony in an inquiry of 1535. Muston ties it to this meeting.

Perrin writes that letters from Oecolampadius and Martin Bucer were read out. Then articles were drawn up, signed and sworn by all present. Seventeen are printed. A Christian may swear by God's name. Those chosen for salvation cannot fail to be saved. There are two sacraments, baptism and the Eucharist. Gilles adds that they agreed to drop all pretense and take no part in Roman ceremonies.

Two barbes, Daniel de Valence and Jean de Molines, would not go along. Gilles, who opposed them, says they thought the new rules not wholly necessary and a slight to the leaders before them. They went to the Brethren in Bohemia. The Brethren's chronicle, as its editor reads it, says the two stayed six months and left on good terms.

In 1535 Pierre Robert Olivetan's French Bible was printed at Neuchâtel, at the valleys' cost.

What the meeting changed is still argued. Léger wrote in 1669 that he could not find the least thing they had changed. Montet called Protestant teaching a novelty for the Waldensians.

[warmly] Léger also quoted Theodore Beza: through the Waldensians of the valleys, France has the Bible in its own language.
