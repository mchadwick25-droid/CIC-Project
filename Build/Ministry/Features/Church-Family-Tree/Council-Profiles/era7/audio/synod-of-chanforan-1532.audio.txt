[calmly] In the Angrogna valley of Piedmont, the Waldensian pastors, called barbes, met with the heads of families. The people were present too, a manuscript of the meeting says. On the first day they debated whether a Christian might ever swear an oath.

That manuscript reaches us only in quotations by the historians Alexis Muston and Édouard Montet. The articles survive in printings by the Dauphiné pastor Jean-Paul Perrin, the valleys' historians Pierre Gilles and Jean Léger, and the English envoy Samuel Morland. All wrote on the Reformed side. Most date the meeting 12 September 1532. Perrin, the earliest, gives 1535.

In 1530 the Waldensians had sent the barbe George Morel to the reformers at Basel and Strasbourg. Their teachers, Morel wrote, had lived more than four hundred years among cruel thorns. Oecolampadius replied that fear of persecution made them hide their faith.

Gilles says pastors came from the valleys and nearby lands. In 1535, he writes, a ducal raid caught a guard keeping watch for ministers at the hamlet of Chanforans. Among them was "M. Farel, qui avoit la barbe rouge": Master Farel, with his red beard. Muston puts Farel at this meeting.

Perrin writes that letters from Oecolampadius and Martin Bucer were read out. Then articles were drawn up, signed and sworn by all present. Seventeen are printed. A Christian may swear by God's name. Those chosen for salvation cannot fail to be saved. There are two sacraments, baptism and the Eucharist. Gilles adds that they agreed to drop all pretense and take no part in Roman ceremonies.

Gilles, on the majority's side, says not all agreed. Some barbes objected, thinking the new rules not wholly necessary and a slight to the leaders before them. Two of their leaders, Daniel de Valence and Jean de Molines, left without leave for the Brethren in Bohemia. The Brethren's chronicle by Jan Blahoslav tells of two envoys who said the Waldensians had sent them. They stayed six months, praised all they saw and went home. Its editor, Jaroslav Goll, names them Valence and Molines.

In 1535 Pierre Robert Olivetan's French Bible was printed at Neuchâtel, at the valleys' cost.

Léger wrote in 1669 that the valleys changed nothing but their view on bearing arms. Montet called Protestant teaching a novelty for the Waldensians.

[warmly] Léger also quoted Theodore Beza: through the Waldensians of the valleys, France has the Bible in its own language.
