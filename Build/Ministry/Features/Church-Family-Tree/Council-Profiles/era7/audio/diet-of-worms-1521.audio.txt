[calmly] On the evening of 18 April 1521, Martin Luther waited two hours in the emperor's court at Worms, on the Rhine. The crowd was so thick, John Foxe writes, that he was "almost smothered for the press." Then he was asked again to take back his books.

The Diet's own record is not among our sources. Carl Mirbt's collection prints part of its Edict, in German. Foxe, a Protestant, gives Luther's speech in English, drawn from Melanchthon and others. The Catholic side reaches us through Sforza Pallavicino, a Jesuit whose 1664 history draws on the papal envoy Aleander's letters.

In 1520 Pope Leo X condemned Luther's teaching, and Luther burned the bull. Aleander argued, Pallavicino says, that a heretic the pope had sentenced should not be heard at all. The emperor Charles V and the princes summoned him anyway, under safe-conduct.

At the first hearing, on 17 April, Johann Eck, a Trier church official, pointed to a table of his books. Would he revoke them? Luther asked for time. Charles remarked, Pallavicino says, that this man would never make him a heretic.

The next evening he answered. "My conscience is so bound and captived in these Scriptures and the word of God," he said in Foxe's version. "Hereupon I stand and rest: I have not what else to say. God have mercy upon me!" Of our sources, only Herrnschmid's Life of Luther, printed by John Wesley in 1778, gives the German "Hier stehe ich."

Next morning, Pallavicino says, Charles read out a paper in his own hand. He would hear Luther no more, and would proceed against him as a notorious heretic.

On the road home, horsemen seized Luther and carried him to the Wartburg, a castle of Frederick, elector of Saxony. Pallavicino says it was staged, so that Frederick could swear he did not know where Luther was.

The Edict, dated 8 May, was signed on 26 May, by Mirbt's account. It declared Luther an open heretic whom no one was to house or feed. Buchheim, an 1885 historian on Luther's side, says it went out without the Diet's formal consent. Pallavicino says the electors approved it. Luther wrote in 1530 that it was so harsh it "had to be changed at the diet at Nuremberg."

[warmly] In the Wartburg, Buchheim writes, Luther worked at "the great work of his life, the translation of the Bible from the original text."
