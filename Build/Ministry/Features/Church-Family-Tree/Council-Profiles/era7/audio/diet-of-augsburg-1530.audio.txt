[calmly] At three on a Saturday afternoon, 25 June 1530, two Saxon chancellors stepped into the middle of a hall in the bishop's palace at Augsburg. One held a German text, the other a Latin one. Before the emperor Charles V, Christian Beyer read the German aloud for two hours. It is said, Oscar Bensow writes, that people in the courtyard caught every word.

Bensow, a Swedish Lutheran editor, tells the day most fully. Part of the Diet's own record is held: the signed confession, two clauses of its final decree, and the other confessions. The Catholic reply is not among our sources. It reaches us through Philip Melanchthon, who answered it, and through Catholic historians.

Charles called the Diet to deal with the Turks and with "dissensions in the matter of our holy religion," in the confession's words. The Catholic historian Joseph Waterworth says Charles meant to bring the Lutherans back to the unity of the church.

Martin Luther, under the imperial ban, stayed behind in the castle of Coburg. "It is not fitting, dear sirs, that I should appear in person at this diet," he wrote to the clergy there.

Melanchthon drafted the confession. Seven princes signed it, led by John, elector of Saxony, with the cities of Nuremberg and Reutlingen. Four southern cities, Strasbourg among them, handed in their own confession. Ulrich Zwingli sent his from Zurich.

Catholic theologians wrote a reply. Waterworth names Johann Faber, Johann Eck and Johann Cochlaeus among them. It was read on 3 August in the same hall, and Charles accepted it as his own. Told to assent, the princes asked for a copy, Melanchthon writes, and were refused except on terms they could not accept. "How could they assent to a writing into which they had not looked?"

Then came talks, seven a side. Paolo Sarpi says they agreed only on a few small points. The Jesuit Sforza Pallavicino, answering him, says they agreed on the most important articles. In September the emperor refused Melanchthon's defense of the confession, the Apology.

The final decree of 19 November gave the Lutherans until 15 April to return, by Waterworth's account. They refused. In December their princes met at Smalcald to plan a common defense.

[warmly] In 1593 a church council at Uppsala bound Sweden to the unaltered confession handed to Charles at Augsburg. The two copies the emperor received, Bensow writes, have never been found.
