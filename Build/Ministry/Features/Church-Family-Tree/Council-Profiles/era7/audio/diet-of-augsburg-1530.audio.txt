[calmly] At three on a Saturday afternoon, 25 June 1530, two Saxon chancellors stepped into the middle of a hall in the bishop's palace at Augsburg. One held a German text, the other a Latin one. Before the emperor Charles V, the chancellor Beyer read the German for two hours. It is said, writes the Swedish Lutheran editor Oscar Bensow, that people in the courtyard caught every word.

Part of the Diet's own record is held: the confession in printed texts, part of the final decree, and two other confessions. The Catholic reply is not among our sources. It reaches us through Philip Melanchthon, who answered it, and through Catholic historians.

Charles called the Diet to deal with the Turks and with "dissensions in the matter of our holy religion," in the confession's words. The Catholic historian Waterworth says Charles meant to bring the Lutherans back to the unity of the church.

Martin Luther, under the imperial ban, stayed behind at Coburg castle. "It is not fitting, dear sirs, that I should appear in person at this diet," he wrote to the clergy there.

Melanchthon drafted the confession. Seven princes signed it, led by John, elector of Saxony, with the cities of Nuremberg and Reutlingen. Four southern cities, Strasbourg among them, handed in their own confession. Ulrich Zwingli sent his from Zurich.

Catholic theologians wrote a reply. Waterworth names John Faber, Eck and Cochlaeus among them. It was read on 3 August, and Charles accepted it as his own. The princes asked for a copy, Melanchthon writes, but could not accept the terms.

Talks followed, seven a side. The Venetian friar Paolo Sarpi says they agreed only on a few small points. The Jesuit Sforza Pallavicino, answering him, says they agreed on the most important articles. Told to assent, Melanchthon writes, the princes refused. "How could they assent to a writing into which they had not looked?" In September the emperor refused Melanchthon's defense of the confession, the Apology.

The Lutherans were given until 15 April to return. Waterworth puts the term in a September draft. Buchheim, writing on Luther's side in 1885, puts it in the final decree of 19 November. They refused. In December their princes met at Smalcald for common defense.

[warmly] In 1593 a council at Uppsala bound Sweden to the unaltered confession handed to Charles. The two copies the emperor received, Bensow writes, have never been found.
