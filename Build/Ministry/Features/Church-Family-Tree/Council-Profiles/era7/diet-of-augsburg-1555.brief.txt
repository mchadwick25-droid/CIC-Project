On 24 March 1555, the papal legate Giovanni Morone reached Augsburg. Ferdinand, King of the Romans, came out almost a league to meet him, writes the Jesuit Juan Alfonso de Polanco. Five days later word came that Pope Julius III was dead. On the last day of March, Morone left for Rome.

Julius had sent him unwillingly. Religion was to be handled in an assembly of Catholics and heretics, and he thought a legate no fit witness to such a monstrous thing.

So says Sforza Pallavicino, a Jesuit writing in 1664. Part of the Diet's own record is held: articles of its final decree, the recess, in Carl Mirbt's collection. The rest comes from Jesuits in the city, from Pallavicino, and from Paolo Sarpi, the Venetian friar he wrote to answer. No Lutheran who was there speaks in our sources.

This was not the Diet of 1530, which heard the Augsburg Confession. The emperor Charles V called it, Sarpi says, chiefly to settle the quarrels over religion. His brother Ferdinand opened it on 5 February, when very few had come.

The recess was made on 25 September. No estate of the empire was to be forced from the Augsburg Confession against its conscience. The same held for what the recess calls the old religion. All who held to neither were shut out of the peace. A bishop who left the old religion was to give up his see. Subjects of either faith could move away with their wives and children. If no agreement came, the peace was to last for ever.

Sarpi says the bargain was over cities and bishops: "The Catholicks were content that the Cities should do as they pleased." When subjects of Ferdinand and of Bavaria asked for the same freedom, Pallavicino says, they were told to follow their lords' religion or leave.

On 18 December the new pope, Paul IV, called the recess ruinous, reached against his will and that of all Catholics. Ferdinand answered sharply, Pallavicino says, that plain necessity cleared him of blame.

A draft order of Duke Christoph of Württemberg, from 1556, had Anabaptists and Schwenckfelders punished, since the peace did not include them. The duke added the Zwinglians in his own hand, its editor notes.

In 1648 the Peace of Westphalia ordered the peace of 1555 kept holy and inviolable. It gave its rights also to those called the Reformed.
