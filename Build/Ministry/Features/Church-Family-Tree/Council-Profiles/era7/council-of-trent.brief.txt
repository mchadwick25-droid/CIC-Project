On 4 December 1563, the last day of the Council of Trent, the Cardinal of Lorraine led the fathers in acclamations. The acts record their answers: "Ever let us confess, ever keep." Then came "Anathema to all heretics," and the reply, "Anathema, anathema." Many wept for joy, wrote Servanzio, as Waterworth quotes his diary.

The council's own acts are held, in English, in the edition of the Catholic editor J. Waterworth. Two rival histories tell the rest. Paolo Sarpi, a Venetian friar, wrote against the Roman court. The Jesuit Sforza Pallavicino wrote to refute him.

Pope Paul III called the council in 1542. In his bull he chose Trent because the Germans "wished for the city of Trent." It opened on 13 December 1545, with about twenty-five prelates by Sarpi's count, and met in three periods.

In 1545 John Calvin wrote of the pope, whom his side called Antichrist: "they will never permit him to be the judge." In 1552 envoys from Saxony came, and asked first that the council declare itself above the pope, Sarpi says. In 1562 the English bishop John Jewel called it "a conspiracy and not a council."

The decrees set the Catholic answer. Scripture and the unwritten traditions, "transmitted as it were from hand to hand," were to be received with equal reverence. It condemned the teaching that faith alone justifies, with nothing else required. Bishops must live in their dioceses. Cathedral churches were to train youths in "a perpetual seminary of ministers of God."

In 1547 the acts call a disease at Trent "notoriously certain," and the council moved to Bologna. Sarpi says the legate del Monte secretly set men to ask the doctors if it was contagious. Pallavicino cites the sworn word of the council's physician. In 1562 they split over whether bishops must reside by divine right. Each historian counts the votes his own way. The final decree speaks of a divine precept and stops there.

At the close, the acts count 255 signers, 168 of them bishops. Pope Pius IV confirmed the decrees in January 1564. Far to the east, Abdisho IV, a patriarch from Assyria, had sworn in Rome to teach what the council would settle.

Sarpi judged that it left the discords "irreconcileable." Pallavicino writes that the fathers saw a work finished after eighteen years, hard won and rich in fruit.
