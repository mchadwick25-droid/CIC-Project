In March 1438 Joseph II, patriarch of Constantinople, sat in his boat at Ferrara. Bishops came from Pope Eugenius IV to tell him he must kiss the pope's foot. "Did the other apostles fall on their knees to kiss Saint Peter's feet?" Joseph asked. He would embrace the pope as a brother, or never leave the boat. The pope gave way, receiving him in his room before the cardinals alone.

Sylvester Syropoulos, a Greek delegate who signed the union but wrote a memoir hostile to it, tells the story. The Greek acts, a delegate's record, leave out the quarrel. The acts of the Basel years are not among our sources, save where later writers quote them.

The council had opened at Basel in 1431, and in 1433 it heard the Bohemian Hussites. Its fathers held, as Constance had, that a council's power came directly from Christ. In 1437 Eugenius moved it to Ferrara to meet the Greeks, and in 1439 to Florence. Those who stayed at Basel deposed him and chose a pope of their own.

The emperor John VIII came with the patriarch and bishops from about twenty sees, by Syropoulos's list. They argued over purgatory, the words the Latins had added to the Creed, and how the Holy Spirit proceeds. Joseph died in Florence in June 1439. The acts print a note found after his death: "Everything the church of elder Rome holds and teaches, I too hold."

On 6 July the decree was read in Latin and Greek. "Let the heavens rejoice and the earth be glad," it began. "The wall that divided the western and the eastern church has been taken away." It allowed leavened or unleavened bread and gave the pope primacy over the whole world. "With tears and sighs most of us signed," Syropoulos writes. Mark, metropolitan of Ephesus, refused, and on hearing it Eugenius said, "Then we have achieved nothing."

Later, envoys of the Armenians, the Copts, the Syrians, and the Chaldeans and Maronites of Cyprus accepted union.

Doukas, a Greek historian, says the bishops coming home to Constantinople were asked, "Did we win?" They answered, "We have sold our faith." Mark wrote that between truth and falsehood there is no middle ground.

A later Greek history of the patriarchs calls Florence the eighth synod. It came to no good end, it says, because the bishop of Ephesus refused to sign.
