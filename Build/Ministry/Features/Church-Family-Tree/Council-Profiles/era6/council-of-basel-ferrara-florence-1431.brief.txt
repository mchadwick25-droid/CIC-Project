In March 1438 Joseph II, patriarch of Constantinople, sat in his boat at Ferrara. Bishops from Pope Eugenius IV told him he must kiss the pope's foot. "Did the other apostles fall on their knees to kiss Saint Peter's feet?" Joseph asked. He would embrace the pope as a brother, or never leave the boat. The pope gave way, receiving him in his room before the cardinals alone.

Sylvester Syropoulos, a Greek delegate who signed but wrote a memoir hostile to the union, tells the story. The Greek acts, a delegate's record, leave out the quarrel. For Basel we hold some of its letters, but its session records reach us only in quotation.

The council had opened at Basel in 1431, and in 1433 heard the Bohemian Hussites. Its fathers held, as Constance had, that a council's power came directly from Christ. In 1437 Eugenius moved it to Ferrara to meet the Greeks, and in 1439 to Florence. Those who stayed at Basel denied he could move it, deposed him and chose their own pope.

The emperor John VIII came with the patriarch and bishops from twenty sees, by Syropoulos's list. They argued over purgatory, the words the Latins added to the Creed, and how the Holy Spirit proceeds. Joseph died in Florence in June 1439. The acts print a note, found after his death, accepting all that the church of elder Rome holds and teaches.

The Greeks signed on 5 July. "With tears and sighs most of us signed," Syropoulos writes. Mark of Ephesus refused. Hearing it, Eugenius said, "Then we have achieved nothing." Next day the decree was read in Latin and Greek. "Let the heavens rejoice and the earth be glad," it began. "The wall that divided the western and the eastern church has been taken away." It allowed leavened or unleavened bread and gave the pope primacy over the whole world.

Later, envoys of the Armenians, the Copts, the Syrians, and the Chaldeans and Maronites of Cyprus accepted union.

Doukas, a Greek historian on the union's side, says the returning bishops were asked, "Did we win?" They answered, "We have sold our faith." Mark wrote that between truth and falsehood there is no middle ground.

A later Greek chronicle says the synod called the eighth came to no good end. Mark of Ephesus had refused to sign, it says, and at home many renewed the quarrel.
