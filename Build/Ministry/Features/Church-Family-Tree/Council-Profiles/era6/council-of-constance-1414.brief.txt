Pope John XXIII was bound for Constance when his wagon overturned in the snow on the Arlberg. Asked if anything ailed him, he answered, "I lie here in the devil's name." Seeing the land below, he said, "Thus are foxes caught."

Ulrich Richental, a citizen of Constance who chronicled the council, tells the story. The council's own acts survive, and so does the diary of Cardinal Guillaume Fillastre, who sat in it. Jan Hus, the Prague preacher it condemned, speaks in his own letters and through his friend Peter of Mladoňovice.

For thirty years, Fillastre writes, rival popes had split the Western church. A council at Pisa in 1409 deposed two of them and chose a third, but the two would not yield, leaving three. Sigismund, king of the Romans, pressed John to call a new council, which opened in November 1414.

Richental counts 145 bishops and envoys of the emperor at Constantinople.

John fled in March 1415, and on 6 April the council declared that its power came directly from Christ. Even a pope had to obey it on faith, the schism and reform. It deposed John in May, Gregory XII resigned in July, and Benedict XIII was deposed in 1417. It also condemned the teaching of John Wyclif, long dead, and ordered his bones dug up.

Hus said he came under the king's safe-conduct to show his innocence, but within four weeks he was arrested. He would not recant, he wrote, because he would have to give up many truths. On 6 July 1415 the council condemned him. Peter writes that the bishops stripped him of his priesthood and set on his head a paper crown painted with devils. "We commit your soul to the devil," they said, and Hus answered, "And I commit it to the most merciful Lord Jesus Christ." The secular court burned him that day.

In November 1417 the electors chose Oddone Colonna as Martin V, and Fillastre gave thanks that the church stood under one head.

The lords of Bohemia sent the council a protest under 452 seals, vowing to defend the preachers of God's law "to the shedding of blood." In 1420 Prague wrote that Sigismund had raised "the impious cross of Antichrist" against Bohemia.

In 1520 Martin Luther wrote that he had "not yet found any errors" in Hus's writings. Those who burned him, he said, had broken a Christian safe-conduct.
