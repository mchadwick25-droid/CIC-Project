Pope John XXIII's wagon overturned in the Arlberg snow on his way to Constance. Asked if anything ailed him, he answered, "I lie here in the devil's name." Seeing the land below, he said, "Thus are foxes caught."

Ulrich Richental, a Constance citizen and chronicler, tells the story. The council's acts survive, as does the diary of Cardinal Guillaume Fillastre, who sat in it. Jan Hus, the Prague preacher it condemned, speaks in his letters and through his friend Peter of Mladoňovice.

For thirty years, Fillastre writes, rival popes had split the Western church. A council at Pisa in 1409 deposed two and chose a third. Neither yielded, leaving three. Sigismund, king of the Romans, pressed John to call a council, which opened in November 1414.

Richental counts 145 bishops and names envoys from Constantinople.

John fled in March 1415. On 6 April the council declared that its power came directly from Christ. Even a pope had to obey it on faith, the schism and reform. It deposed John in May, Gregory XII resigned in July, and Benedict XIII was deposed in 1417. It also condemned John Wyclif's teaching and ordered his bones dug up.

Hus said he came under the king's safe-conduct to show his innocence, but within four weeks was arrested. He would not recant, he wrote, because he would have to give up many truths. On 6 July 1415 the council condemned him. Peter writes that the bishops stripped him of his priesthood and set on his head a paper crown painted with devils. "We commit your soul to the devil," they said, and Hus answered, "And I commit it to the most gracious Lord Jesus Christ." The secular court burned him that day. In September the council ruled that no safe-conduct shields a heretic from a church court.

In November 1417 the electors chose Oddone Colonna as Martin V. Fillastre praised God that the church stood under one head.

The lords of Bohemia and Moravia sent the council eight letters under 452 seals, by their editor František Palacký's count. They would defend the preachers of God's law "to the shedding of blood." In 1420 Prague wrote that Sigismund had his legate raise "the impious cross of Antichrist" against Bohemia.

In 1520 Martin Luther wrote that he had "not yet found any errors" in Hus's writings. Those who burned him, he said, had broken a Christian safe-conduct.
