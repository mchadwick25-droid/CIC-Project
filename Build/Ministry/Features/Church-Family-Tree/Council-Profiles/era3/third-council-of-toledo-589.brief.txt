A three-day fast had been proclaimed for the bishops of Spain and Gaul at Toledo. On 8 May, the council's acts say, they took their seats and prayed. Then King Reccared of the Visigoths appeared in their midst and prayed with them. Spain had long laboured in the error of the Arians, he told them.

The acts date the council to his fourth year, which Henry Percival, the English editor of the councils, gives as 589. They survive in Latin, with the signatures, and are told here in modern words. Every witness here writes from the Catholic side.

The Gothic kings and their bishops held the Homoian faith, which their opponents called Arian. An envoy of Reccared's father, King Leovigild, once put it to Gregory of Tours, the Frankish bishop. It "was a wrong opinion of the ancient bishops which asserted the Son equal to the Father," he said.

John of Biclaro, a Catholic chronicler exiled by Leovigild, tells of a synod of Leovigild's bishops. It ruled that Catholics joining them need not be baptized again.

The king had called them, the acts say. The Latin heading counts sixty-two bishops, as does one text of John's chronicle. Another reads seventy-two. Masona of Mérida signed first after the king. John says the council's business lay with Leander of Seville and Eutropius, an abbot.

A notary read out the king's book of faith. It says the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son, and Queen Baddo signed it. Then the Gothic bishops, clergy and nobles condemned the faith they had held, in twenty-three anathemas. They condemned the booklet of Leovigild's twelfth year, which had drawn Romans to their side. Eight former Arian bishops signed. A canon ordered the people to recite the creed aloud before the Lord's Prayer.

John compared Reccared to the emperor Constantine at the Council of Nicaea. There, he wrote, the heresy was condemned with its roots left in. At Toledo it was cut out by the roots. After the council, a duke named Argimund tried to seize the throne. He was shorn, lost his right hand and was paraded through Toledo on a donkey.

Pope Gregory the Great wrote to Leander that he could not "at all fully express in words" his joy. Isidore of Seville, Leander's brother, later wrote that through Leander the Goths turned from the Arian madness to the Catholic faith.
