[calmly] Abraham, the catholicos or head of the Armenian church, did all he could to win the Iberians to his teaching. When he failed, he drew the spiritual sword and cursed them, with the assent of all the Armenians. So Stephen of Taron, an Armenian historian writing around the year 1000, tells it. Stephen names no council and gives no year.

Malachia Ormanian, a former Armenian patriarch of Constantinople, calls these Iberians the Georgians. Their own church head, Kyrion, had been made catholicos by Movses, who led the Armenian church before Abraham. Shortly before Movses died, Kyrion came to doubt what Stephen calls the orthodox faith. He accepted the Council of Chalcedon. Ormanian says he did it to win the emperor's favor.

No record of the council survives among the books this account draws on, and no letter between the two church heads. Of the writers drawn on here, only modern ones tell of a synod at Dvin. The Georgian chronicle, in Marie-Félicité Brosset's French, does not name Kyrion.

Kyrion's own words reach us only through James Issaverdens, a priest of the Armenian Catholic monastery of San Lazzaro in Venice, writing in 1875. He praises Kyrion's learning and piety. He says Abraham sent Kyrion three letters. He gives Kyrion's reply: "I am astonished that you are at variance with all other churches of Christ." Issaverdens names no source for it.

Issaverdens says Abraham called a synod of bishops at Dvin, the chief city of Persian Armenia. They cursed Kyrion "and all those who accepted it," meaning Chalcedon. No writer names the bishops or counts them.

The year is disputed. Ormanian has Abraham elected on 30 April 607 and the council at Dvin in 609. Brosset, in a note of 1876, puts it in 596. Stephen and Issaverdens both set Abraham's quarrel in the reign of the emperor Maurice.

Sebeos, an Armenian bishop of the 600s, tells of a debate before the Persian king. In it the catholicos of Georgia and Albania took Chalcedon's side. Issaverdens adds that Kyrion complained to Maurice, who called bishops of both sides to Constantinople. No other writer tells of that meeting.

[warmly] Ormanian says the Georgian church, with Kyrion at its head, "became part of the Greek Church."
