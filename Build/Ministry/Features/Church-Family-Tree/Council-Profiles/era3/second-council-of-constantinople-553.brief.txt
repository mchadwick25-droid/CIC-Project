Before the council of 553, the question was put at Constantinople whether it was right to curse the dead. Eutychius, envoy of the bishop of Amasea, said it needed no debate. He pointed to King Josiah, who "broke up the sepulchres of those who had long been dead." The emperor Justinian soon made him the city's bishop.

Evagrius of Epiphania, a historian on Chalcedon's side, tells this decades later. Justinian wanted the Three Chapters condemned. One was Theodore of Mopsuestia, with his writings. Another was what Theodoret of Cyrrhus wrote against Cyril of Alexandria. The third was a letter said to be by Ibas of Edessa.

The emperor's letter to the council says the followers of Nestorius used Theodore to bring in their errors. Facundus of Hermiane, an African bishop, wrote in the Chapters' defence. Chalcedon's enemies were behind the case, he said, and Chalcedon had judged the letter of Ibas catholic.

The Library holds only extracts of the acts, in English. Their editor, Henry Percival, notes that some of the most damaging lines about Pope Vigilius are in one Paris manuscript alone.

The council opened on 5 May 553, by the dating of Charles Joseph Hefele, the historian of the councils. Hefele says Eutychius presided. He counts three patriarchs, three envoys for Jerusalem and 145 other bishops. Vigilius was in the city but would not come. "In vain we sent several commands to him," the emperor's letter says.

On 14 May Vigilius sent Justinian his own ruling, signed with sixteen bishops. He did not dare condemn Theodore, he wrote, nor would he let anyone else. In a session given in full only in the Paris copy, the emperor ordered that the pope's name no longer be read in church. The sentence reads, "We therefore anathematize the Three Chapters."

In a December letter to Eutychius, Vigilius gave way. Percival says most scholars accept it as his. He died in Sicily, going home. Victor of Tunnuna, an African bishop exiled for the Chapters, tells of others sent to Egypt for opposing the decrees.

In Persia, a synod of the Church of the East defended Theodore as the Interpreter of Scripture. Jean-Baptiste Chabot, its editor, dates it to 585.

Gregory the Great of Rome later wrote of the synod "called by many the fifth." Nothing was done in it, he said, "with respect to the faith, but only with respect to persons."
