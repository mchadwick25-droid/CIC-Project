[calmly] In 251 the persecution under the emperor Decius was over. At Rome and at Carthage, in Roman North Africa, Christians who had given way in it now asked to come back. Some had sacrificed to the gods, and others had paid for certificates to avoid it. Two councils took up their case, one of the Latin church of North Africa and one of the church of Rome, and the strict churches of the Roman presbyter Novatian grew out of the quarrel.

Cyprian, bishop of Carthage, had refused to settle the matter alone while the danger lasted, so when it ended he met with a large number of African bishops. The book of their decisions is lost, so what they decided reaches us from Cyprian, who called the meeting and defended it afterward. They chose a middle way. The fallen were not to be left without hope, in case despair drove them back to pagan life, but their penance would be long and each case examined. Those with certificates could then be received, and those who had sacrificed would be given peace when dying.

The Africans wrote to Cornelius, bishop of Rome. His church had split, and a stricter party had made the presbyter Novatian bishop against him. Eusebius of Caesarea, working from letters of Cornelius that had reached him, says sixty bishops met there. They agreed that the fallen should be healed through repentance, and they declared Novatian and his followers strangers to the church.

Novatian's side reaches us mostly through such opponents. Socrates of Constantinople, nearly two centuries later, reports what Novatian wrote to the churches. Those who had sacrificed must not be admitted to the sacraments, but urged to repent, with their pardon left to God. In Phrygia and Paphlagonia, Socrates says, the strict churches still kept that rule.

The quarrel divided the East as well. Dionysius of Alexandria wrote to Novatian, "It would have been right to suffer anything at all rather than split the Church of God." Later he reported the eastern churches at one again, and Eusebius says they had rejected Novatian's new teaching.

Carthage's long penance did not last. When a new persecution seemed near, the African bishops wrote to Cornelius that they now gave peace to all who had kept on repenting. The way back itself held, for in 325 the council of Nicaea set terms for Novatian's clergy. [warmly] They could keep their rank only if they promised in writing to share communion with those who had lapsed in persecution and been given a term of penance.
