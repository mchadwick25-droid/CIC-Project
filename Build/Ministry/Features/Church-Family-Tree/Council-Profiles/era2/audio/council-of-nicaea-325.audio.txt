[calmly] The bishops sat in rows on each side of the largest hall in the palace at Nicaea. The emperor Constantine walked in, robed in purple and gold. A low golden chair was set for him. Eusebius of Caesarea says Constantine did not sit until the bishops "had beckoned to him."

In Alexandria, Bishop Alexander had put his presbyter Arius out of the church. The quarrel spread, and in 325 Constantine called bishops "from all quarters" to settle it.

The council's minutes are lost. Its creed, its rules and its letter to Egypt survive, with accounts by three men who were there. Eusebius admired the emperor and was slow to sign. Eustathius of Antioch and Athanasius of Alexandria, then Alexander's deacon, were Arius's foes. Arius's own letters survive only in books by his opponents. The council's letter says he taught "that there was a time when he was not."

The witnesses count the bishops differently. Eusebius says more than 250, Eustathius about 270, and Athanasius "three hundred more or less." A later generation, Archibald Robertson notes, settled on 318. Most came from the Greek East, and Eusebius says a Persian bishop came too. Ossius came from Spain and Caecilian from Carthage. Two presbyters stood in for the aged bishop of Rome.

In Athanasius's telling, the bishops offered words from Scripture about the Son. Arius's friends accepted each one, but were caught "whispering to each other and winking with their eyes." Eustathius says a statement from Arius's party was torn up "in the sight of all." Eusebius of Caesarea read out his church's creed. The emperor approved it, he says, but asked for one word to be added. The Son was to be called homoousios, of one being with the Father.

The council's letter says two bishops, Secundus of Ptolemais and Theonas of Marmarica, shared Arius's sentence. Constantine sent Arius away. Socrates of Constantinople says Eusebius of Nicomedia and Theognis of Nicaea were exiled too. The council also set one day for Easter everywhere and limited the power of Melitius of Lycopolis.

[warmly] Within a few years Eusebius and Theognis were back in their churches. A Syriac text says Marutha of Maipherqat translated the council's rules for Isaac, head of the church in Persia. A Persian synod accepted them in 410. In Carthage, nearly a century after Nicaea, bishops still consulted the copy of its rules that Caecilian had brought home.
