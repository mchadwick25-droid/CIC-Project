On the first of June in 411, the Donatist bishops came into a hall of the Gargilian baths at Carthage, with the Catholics' chosen speakers. Guards and clerks stood by. Notaries of both churches worked beside the court's scribes and wrote down every word. The judge was Flavius Marcellinus, an officer sent by the emperor Honorius to settle their quarrel.

For a century Africa had held two rival churches. The Donatists said the church had been lost by bishops who handed over the Scriptures in persecution, and by all who shared communion with them. Augustine of Hippo reports their claim that the true church was "the one which suffers persecution, not the one inflicting it." The Catholics answered that the church spread through the whole world and held good and bad together until the end.

The full record survives in Latin, each speech signed by its speaker. It breaks off on the third day. For the end we rely on a summary by Augustine, one of the Catholic speakers. Possidius of Calama, another, later told it in his Life of Augustine. Both write for the winning side.

The clerks counted 266 Catholic signatures, and twenty more Catholic bishops came forward who had not signed. The Donatist list held 279 names, including absent bishops and one who had died. Each side chose seven to speak. Among the Donatist seven were Petilian of Constantina and Emeritus of Caesarea.

Marcellinus offered the bishops seats. Petilian refused. Christ, he said, had stood before the governor, so they would gladly stand. The Donatists first argued about the deadline, the mandates and who was the accuser. Only on the third day did the debate reach the old records about Caecilian, the bishop whose ordination had split Africa.

Marcellinus sent both sides out, wrote his sentence, then called them back and read it. Augustine says it found that every document had proved the Donatists wrong. His edict banned their meetings. The churches he had lent them were to go to the Catholics.

The Donatists said the judge had been bribed and had ruled at night. They appealed, and Possidius says the emperor condemned them as heretics.

Later, at Caesarea, Augustine challenged Emeritus to say whatever he had not been allowed to say. Emeritus answered only that the records "of what was done by the bishops at Carthage contain the proof of whether we were victors or vanquished."
