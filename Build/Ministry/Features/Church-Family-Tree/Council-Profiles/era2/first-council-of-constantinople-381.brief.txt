At Constantinople in 381, Gregory of Nazianzus stood before the council's bishops and gave up his see. "Farewell, my throne, envied and perilous height," he told them.

Socrates of Constantinople says the Arians had held the city's churches for forty years. In November 380 the emperor Theodosius drove out their bishop. He then called a council to establish the Nicene Creed and appoint a bishop for the capital.

Some who called the Son fully God would not say the same of the Holy Spirit. Their view reaches us only through opponents, chiefly Gregory and the bishops' letter of the next year. Gregory put their question in an oration: "From whence are you bringing in upon us this strange God, of Whom Scripture is silent?"

The council's minutes are lost. Its creed, its canons and a short letter to Theodosius survive. The creed comes down to us in the acts of the Council of Chalcedon, seventy years later, as Henry Percival, the council's English editor, notes. The histories of Socrates, Sozomen and Theodoret of Cyrrhus all take the Nicene side.

Socrates counts a hundred and fifty bishops. Theodoret says the emperor summoned only those of his half of the empire. Meletius of Antioch led them at first, by Hefele's reading, and died while they sat. Thirty-six bishops of the Macedonian party, led by Eleusius of Cyzicus, also came. They would not call the Son "of one substance" with the Father, and they left.

Some bishops, chiefly from Egypt, objected to Gregory as the city's bishop. That was when he gave his farewell and withdrew. Theodosius then chose Nectarius, an official who had not yet been baptized, Sozomen says.

The council ruled that the faith of Nicaea "shall not be set aside, but shall remain firm." It condemned heresies by name, among them the Pneumatomachi, whose name means fighters against the Spirit, and the Apollinarians. The creed calls the Spirit "the Lord and Giver-of-Life," worshipped and glorified with the Father and the Son. Charles Joseph Hefele, the historian of the councils, thinks the bishops reworked a creed already in use. F. J. A. Hort argued they issued none. A canon ranked the bishop of Constantinople next after Rome, "because Constantinople is New Rome."

Rome accepted the creed but long refused the canons, Hefele says. Later, Gregory the Great of Rome counted this council among four that he compared to the four Gospels.
