[calmly] At the court of King Oswy of Northumbria, it was said, Easter was once kept twice in one year. Bede of Jarrow says the king had kept the feast while Queen Eanfleda, who followed the custom of Kent, was still keeping Palm Sunday. Oswy had been taught and baptized by the Irish, whom Bede calls the Scots, and thought nothing better than what they taught.

No acts of the meeting survive. Bede tells it from the Roman side in his History, finished in 731. Stephen, a priest close to Wilfrid of Ripon, gives a shorter account from the same side in his Life of Wilfrid. The Irish side reaches us only through these two.

The quarrel was over the date of Easter and the tonsure, the way a cleric's head was shaved. In 664, Bede says, a synod met at Whitby, in the house where the abbess Hild presided.

Both kings came, Oswy and his son Alfrid. Colman, bishop of Lindisfarne, brought his Irish clergy, and Agilbert, bishop of the West Saxons, came with the priest Wilfrid. Bede says Hild and her followers were for the Irish, as was Bishop Cedd, who interpreted for both sides. Stephen names Hild only as present.

Oswy said that those who served one God should keep one rule of life. Colman kept the Easter of the elders who had sent him, he said, the Easter of John the Evangelist. In Bede's telling, Wilfrid spoke for the Easter kept at Rome. He called the Picts and the Britons the Irish party's "accomplices in obstinacy."

The king asked Colman whether the keys of heaven were given to Peter. "It is true, O king!" Colman said. Asked if he could show such power given to Columba, the founder of Iona, he answered, "None." Peter, Oswy said, "is the door-keeper, whom I will not contradict." In Stephen's account the king, smiling, put the question of Columba and Peter to the whole synod, which answered with one voice.

Colman left with those who would not change. He took part of the bones of Aidan, Lindisfarne's first Irish bishop, to Iona and then an island off the west of Ireland. Iona itself took the Roman Easter some eighty years after sending Aidan, Bede says.

[warmly] The whole care of the Irish teachers, Bede wrote, "was to serve God, not the world — to feed the soul, and not the belly."
