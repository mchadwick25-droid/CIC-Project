[calmly] In November 869, at a council in Constantinople, the emperor Basil I ordered a fire. Into it went the signed pledges the patriarch Photius had once taken, the acts say, from great and small, down to leather-workers, fishmongers and needle-makers. With them burned his books against Pope Nicholas I.

That council was one of four held at Constantinople over Photius in twenty years. The councils of 861 and 867 have no surviving acts, and those of 867 were burned by order. They reach us through Photius's enemies, Nicetas, who wrote the Life of his rival Ignatius, and Anastasius the Librarian, Rome's translator. The acts of 869 and 879 survive in Latin and Greek.

Ignatius had been removed as patriarch, and Photius, a layman, was made patriarch in six days, Nicetas says. Pope Nicholas sent two envoys to look into it. At the council of 861, Nicetas says, seventy-two false witnesses spoke against Ignatius. He was stripped of his robe, and the pope's envoys joined the cry that he was unworthy. In Rome, Nicholas condemned them.

The quarrel spread to Bulgaria, whose newly baptized ruler had asked Rome for clergy. Photius wrote to the bishops of the East that Latins had burst into Bulgaria like a wild boar into the Lord's vineyard. They even taught, he wrote, that the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Son as well as the Father. In 867 his council condemned Nicholas. Anastasius says Photius forged about a thousand bishops' signatures, with only twenty-one real.

Basil, on becoming emperor, put Photius out. Pope Hadrian II asked for a council with his envoys presiding. In 869 it declared that Photius had never been a bishop, and restored Ignatius. Nicetas says 102 bishops took part. Afterward the envoys of the Eastern patriarchs gave Bulgaria to Constantinople, over Rome's protest.

When Ignatius died, Photius became patriarch again. Pope John VIII sent envoys to ask that the council of Hadrian's time be held null. Photius presided in 879. The council kept the creed with nothing added and nothing taken away.

[warmly] The Latins, says the Pedalion, a Greek book of church law, named the council against Photius the eighth. At the Council of Florence in 1438, Mark of Ephesus said the council of 879 had undone it. In the great church of Constantinople, he said, this was still read: anathema to all written or said against the holy patriarchs Photius and Ignatius.
