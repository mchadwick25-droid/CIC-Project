[calmly] The emperor Justinian II signed first. In the council's list of signatures, the next line is marked in Latin as the place of the most holy pope of Rome. It was left blank. Then came Paul of Constantinople and the bishops of Alexandria, Jerusalem and Antioch.

The bishops met in a domed hall of the palace at Constantinople, called the Trullus. Charles Joseph Hefele, the historian of the councils, dates the meeting between September 691 and September 692. Its record survives in Greek and Latin: the bishops' address to the emperor, 102 canons and the signatures. No minutes of its debates are known.

The councils of 553 and 681 had made no canons, the bishops told the emperor. Gathered at his command, they had now written them.

Henry Percival, the English editor of the councils, counts 211 signers, bishops or their stand-ins. Later Greek writers give 227 and 240. Basil of Gortyna in Crete signed as holding the place of the church of Rome. The Book of the Popes says the pope's envoys were there and were tricked into signing. Percival says the West was not represented at all.

Rome had its deacons and priests promise to stop living with their wives, and the council ruled that such marriages stand. Canon 55 forbade Rome's Saturday fast in Lent. In Armenia, canon 32 says, "they offer wine only on the Holy Table," with no water mixed in. Any bishop or priest who did so was to be deposed. Canon 36 gave Constantinople "equal privileges with the see of Old Rome," second after it.

The canons went to Rome in six volumes for Pope Sergius I to sign. His biographer in the Book of the Popes says he would not let them be opened or read, and rejected them as invalid. The emperor sent his guard officer Zacharias to bring the pope to Constantinople. Soldiers from Ravenna came to stop him, and Zacharias hid under the pope's bed.

No Armenian answer to the council is known. Stephen of Taron, an Armenian historian of about the year 1000, tells of a later synod at Manazkert. It kept the sacrament without water. Malachia Ormanian, a former Armenian patriarch of Constantinople, dates it to 726.

[warmly] At the Second Council of Nicaea in 787, Tarasius of Constantinople said the canons came from the fathers of the Sixth Council. "And let no one doubt concerning them."
