[calmly] On 26 April 681, in the court of the Zeuxippus baths in Constantinople, a dead man was laid on a silver bier. A monk named Polychronius had promised to raise him by laying his statement of faith on the body. For hours he kept the paper on the corpse and whispered to it. He could not raise him, he said. The crowd cursed him as a new Simon Magus.

The council's acts tell this, the record of the side that won. They survive in Greek and Latin. The condemned reach us mostly through their opponents' records. Michael the Syrian, a Syriac Orthodox patriarch of the 1100s, sides with one will against the council.

For forty-six years or so, the pope's envoys told the council, bishops of Constantinople had taught one will in Christ. The emperor Constantine IV had written to Rome, and Pope Agatho sent envoys with a letter. The church of Rome, he wrote, "has never erred from the path of the apostolic tradition."

The emperor presided in a domed hall of the palace, from 7 November 680. The Book of the Popes counts 150 bishops, Theophanes, a Greek chronicler, 289, and Michael 250.

Macarius, patriarch of Antioch, spoke for "one will and one theandric operation," a single working at once divine and human. He named Pope Honorius among those he followed. He would not say two wills, he said, even if he were cut limb from limb and thrown into the sea. The bishops cried out to throw him out, and stripped him of his bishop's mantle.

With the teachers of one will, the council anathematized, or cursed, "Honorius who was some time Pope of Old Rome," for following Sergius of Constantinople. On 16 September 681 it defined "two natural wills and two natural operations," the human will "subject to his divine and omnipotent will."

Agatho died during the council, and his successor Leo II accepted it. He wrote to the emperor that Honorius had tried to overturn the spotless faith. To the bishops of Spain he wrote that Honorius had fed the flame of heresy by neglect. At a council in Toledo, the Spanish bishops received the acts so far as they did not depart from the earlier councils.

[warmly] Michael says the bishops signed under force, and Macarius died a prisoner in Rome. That, he wrote, was how the pernicious heresy of Maximus came in.
