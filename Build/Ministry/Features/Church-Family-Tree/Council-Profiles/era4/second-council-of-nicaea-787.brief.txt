In the church of the Holy Apostles in Constantinople, the bishops had begun to read the Scriptures while the rulers looked on. Then soldiers of the palace guard burst in with drawn swords. They threatened to kill the patriarch and the bishops. Bishops of the soldiers' party went out to them, shouting that they had won. So Theophanes the Confessor, a Greek chronicler on the side of the images, tells it. The meeting broke up, a year before the council met at Nicaea.

In 754 a council of 338 bishops at Hieria had banned holy images. Its decree survives only in the acts of 787, which read it out to answer it. Henry Percival, the English editor of the councils, gives it in a summary drawn mostly from Charles Joseph Hefele, their historian. Whoever paints Christ, it says, "either depicts the Godhead which cannot be depicted," or splits off his human body. The only true image of his humanity "is bread and wine in the holy Supper."

The empress Irene and her son, Constantine VI, called the council. Their letter says Tarasius, the new patriarch, had asked for a council with envoys from Rome and the East. The Christians of the East, he said, "decline communion with us." Pope Hadrian I sent two envoys.

The acts, the winning side's record, are told here from Percival's English extracts. The Book of the Popes gives about 350 bishops. Theophanes puts the first session on 11 October 787. Louis Duchesne, a French historian, gives eight sessions from 25 September to 22 October.

Bishops who had opposed the images read out their confessions and were received. "Shall we not weep when we see an image of our crucified Lord?" Tarasius asked. The decree ordered images set up and given "honourable reverence," but not the true worship due to God alone. "For the honour which is paid to the image passes on to that which the image represents."

Hadrian had the acts put into Latin. That translation, Duchesne says, shocked the Frankish clergy. At Frankfurt in 794, Charlemagne's bishops rejected the Greek synod. It had cursed anyone, they said, who would not give images the adoration due to the Trinity. Hefele, as Percival quotes him, blames a false translation.

Photius of Constantinople later wrote of the acts, "at the sessions of which the iconoclasts were defeated and the orthodox faith shone with increased brightness."
