In October 649, the Gospels were set out before the bishops in the church called the Constantinian, at the Lateran in Rome. Bishops and priests sat, the Book of the Popes says, and the deacons and clergy stood. Pope Martin I presided, and the acts say the synod met at his order.

Those acts survive in Latin and Greek, the record of Martin's side, and are told here from the Latin. The documents they condemned are quoted in them in Latin translation.

The emperor Heraclius had issued the Ekthesis, a statement of faith that confessed one will in Christ. Paul, patriarch of Constantinople, wrote the same to Rome. Then Heraclius's grandson, Constans II, issued the Typos. It forbade all argument over one will or two. Martin told the synod that Paul had urged the emperor to issue it, an edict that would destroy the catholic teaching.

The Book of the Popes and Bede of Jarrow count 105 bishops, and the acts list 105 at the opening, besides Martin. Theophanes, a Greek chronicler, says 150. Maurus of Ravenna sent a bishop and a priest in his place. The army and people had kept him at home, he wrote, for fear of raids. Stephen, bishop of Dor, told how Sophronius of Jerusalem had sent him to Rome. Greek abbots and monks asked to enter, and Martin let them in.

Theophanes says Maximus the Confessor had come from Africa and fired Martin's zeal. A monk named Maximus signs the monks' petition, but the acts do not say he is the Confessor.

The synod met five times that month. It heard the Ekthesis and the Typos read aloud. The Typos had a good aim, the bishops said, but its force was at odds with that aim. Their eighteenth canon cursed Cyrus of Alexandria and the patriarchs Sergius, Pyrrhus and Paul, with the Ekthesis and the Typos.

Martin asked Amandus, bishop of Maastricht, to have King Sigebert of the Franks send bishops to carry the acts to the emperor. Theophanes says Constans, in fury, had Martin and Maximus brought to Constantinople and tortured. Martin died in exile at Cherson. At Bizya, a bishop told Maximus the Roman synod had no force, since the emperor had not ordered it.

Bede says John, a Roman abbot, brought "the decision of the synod of the blessed Pope Martin and 105 bishops" to Wearmouth to be copied.
