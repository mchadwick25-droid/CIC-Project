# Sheet: new-spain-and-andes-mission-born-christianity

Working file for the tradition brief. Census entry `new-spain-and-andes-mission-born-christianity`, 1524-1650. Files in `cic/texts/`; line numbers are file lines (OCR spacing noisy; accents dropped).

**Scope.** The vendored sources are the friars' and a Jesuit's pens. The brief tells the Franciscan mission in New Spain in the 1520s-1530s as Motolinia wrote it (1536-41), and one Jesuit's view of native rites (Acosta, about 1590). It says plainly that no native Christian text is held and that the Andes appear almost only through Acosta. It does not tell Guaman Poma, the Huarochiri manuscript or the extirpation records (not held).

## Witnesses

- **W1. Toribio de Benavente, called Motolinia (d. 1568)**, Franciscan, one of the first twelve (editor, line 1328: "el ultimo de los doce primeros misioneros"). Historia de los indios de la Nueva Espana, written 1536-1541, edition of 1914: `motolinia_historia-de-los-indios-de-la-nueva-espana-spa_sanchez-garcia1914.txt`. The mission's own voice. Contemporary to the events he narrates (1520s-1530s). Counts and judgments are his. Confidence: the book exists and says what it says (Documented); his totals are his estimates (Inferential-Thin as numbers).
- **W2. Jose de Acosta**, Jesuit, Natural and Moral History of the Indies (Spanish 1590; English of 1604 by Grimston, edited by Markham 1880), Vol. II, `acosta_natural-and-moral-history-of-the-indies-v2_grimston-markham1880.txt`. A Spanish priest's view of Indian rites, about 70 years after the mission began. A hostile-view witness for native rites.

## Claims

**C1. The Franciscans came.** "En el ano del Senor de 1523, dia de la conversion de San Pablo, que es a 25 de Enero, el Padre Fray Martin de Valencia ... con once frailes sus companeros, partieron de Espana para venir a esta tierra de Anahuac ... para la conversion de los Indios" (2930-2940). W1. Documented. The year of arrival (the census gives 1524) is not stated in these lines; say "left Spain in 1523".

**C2. The plagues before the friars (Motolinia's "ten plagues").** First, smallpox, brought by a sick man on Narvaez's ship; "en las mas provincias murio mas de la mitad de la gente y en otros pocos menos" (2955-2959); they called it the great leprosy (2970). Second, the dead of the conquest, "en especial sobre Mexico" (2983-2984). "Quedo tan destruida la tierra de las revueltas y plagas ya dichas, que quedaron muchas casas yermas del todo" (3249-3252). W1, a Spanish friar's own words about what his side's arrival cost. Documented that he wrote it; the proportion is his estimate.

**C3. The friars' reading of the plagues.** Of the lords and ministers who would not accept the new law, he writes that God "remedio ... matando muchos de ellos con las plagas y enfermedades ya dichas, y otros se convirtieron; y de los que murieron han venido los senorios a sus hijos, que eran de pequenos bautizados y criados en la casa de Dios" (3408-3417). W1. This is his providential claim and his hostile reading of the native lords. Tell it in his voice only, as his claim; do not carry it as fact.

**C4. The river crossing.** The friars visited Coyxco and Tlalco from Cuauhnahuac (the fifth house); people came from the smaller town to the larger to be taught and to have their children baptized; a night of rain turned a stream into a river; the friars preached from one bank to people on the other, who would not leave without their children baptized; a raft of canes on gourds carried the children across "y alli los bautizaron con harto trabajo por ser tantos" (6872-6898). W1. His own account. Confidence: Widely Accepted as a scene he reports; he is the only witness here.

**C5. The counts.** In the same book Motolinia gives different totals. "Yo creo que ... hasta el tiempo que esto escribo, que es en el ano de 1536, mas de cuatro millones de animas se bautizaron" (6900-6903). Later in the chapter: "cerca de cinco millones" (7011-7013), and "a mi juicio y verdaderamente seran bautizados en este tiempo que digo, que seran quince anos, mas de nueve millones de animas de Indios" (7028-7031). W1. The figures are his estimates ("yo creo", "a mi juicio"). Inferential-Thin as numbers. The doubt is itself part of the story: say the figures differ and are his own count.

**C6. Acosta on native rites (Peru and Mexico).** "The Indians had an infinite number of other ceremonies and customes which resembled to the ancient law of Moses, and some to those which the Moores vse, and some approached neere to the law of the Gospel, as their bathes ... The Mexicaines had also amongst them a kind of baptisme" (3999-4008, English of 1604). He ends the chapter: "although it seeme that many of their ceremonies agree with ours, yet differ they much for the great abomination they mingle therewithal" (about 4060-4063). W2. Hostile view of native rites told openly and credited. The Andes: the contents list "Sorcerers in Peru" (about line 440) and the book treats Peru among its examples; nothing read here tells Andean Christians in their own voice.

## Quotes (Opus renderings pending)

Q1. Motolinia, 6900-6903 (about four million baptized by 1536).
Q2. Motolinia, 7028-7031 (more than nine million in fifteen years).

## Who tells it

All of it is a Spanish or Italian-style outsider's pen: a Franciscan who baptized and a Jesuit who studied rites. The native Christians speak only as reported speech inside these books. No native Christian text is held (Guaman Poma, the Huarochiri manuscript and the Nahuatl testaments are not vendored).

## Do not say

- Not that "nine million" or "four million" were baptized as a fact. They are Motolinia's own estimates and they do not agree (about four, about five, more than nine).
- Not that baptism means conversion in the listener's sense: the sources say how many were baptized, not what they believed.
- Not that the plagues were God's remedy. That is Motolinia's reading; say "he saw".
- Not "the native Christians wrote back". None of their texts is held.
- Not the arrival year 1524 as a source claim (the lines read say they left Spain in 1523).
- Not a claim about the Andes beyond what Acosta's chapter says; the Andes have no voice in this library.
- Not Las Casas's accusations as read: his Brevissima relacion is vendored but not read for this brief (OCR is poor).
- Not "friars forced baptism" or "friars did not force it" as a source claim; neither is read here.

## Leave out

- Las Casas and the Dominican dispute about baptism (not read).
- The Andean extirpation and the native authors (not held).
- The later colonial church.

## Connection to carry

What passed on: a mission that arrived in a land its own plagues and conquest had emptied; a friar who counted millions of baptisms; a Jesuit who saw native rites as imitation and abomination. The listener must understand that the sources tell what the missionaries thought and counted, and that the people baptized are heard only through them.

## Ending

The river-crossing scene (C4): people who would not leave without their children baptized, and the raft of canes.

## Opus renderings (done)
Q1 (6900-6902): "I believe more than four million souls were baptized from the time the land was won until the time I write this. The land was won in 1521. I am writing in 1536."
Q2 (7028-7031): "In my judgment, and in truth, more than nine million souls of Indians will have been baptized in the time I speak of. That time will be fifteen years."
C (7011-7013): "Up to this day, close to five million will have been baptized." (no noun in the source; it counts by priests; the nine-million count is by towns and provinces plus 500,000 later.)
