In 1536, a Franciscan friar in Mexico wrote down a number. "I believe more than four million souls were baptized from the time the land was won until the time I write this." The land had been won in 1521. The friar was Toribio de Benavente, called Motolinía.

He wrote the story of a mission, and the sources read here tell almost only his side of it. Twelve friars, he says, left Spain in January 1523 for the land of Anahuac, which the Spanish called New Spain. We hear the people they baptized only through the friars' pens. No native Christian text is held in this library.

The land the friars reached had already been struck. Smallpox came with a sick man on a Spanish ship, and in most provinces, Motolinía says, more than half the people died. War killed many more, above all around Mexico City. Many houses stood empty.

Motolinía read this as God's work. He wrote that God had ended many of the lords who would not accept the new law, through those same plagues, and that their sons, baptized as small children, then inherited their lands. That is his own reading, and it shows whose voice we are hearing.

His counts are his own, and they do not agree. In one book he gives more than four million baptized by 1536, then close to five million, then more than nine million in fifteen years: "In my judgment, and in truth, more than nine million souls of Indians will have been baptized in the time I speak of." The numbers count baptisms. They do not tell what the people believed.

A Jesuit, José de Acosta, wrote about the Indians some fifty years later, around 1590. He said many of their customs resembled the law of Moses, and some came near the Gospel, such as their washing rites. The Mexica, he wrote, even had a kind of baptism of newborn children. But he judged that these customs were mixed with abomination, and he spoke of the idols as the work of the devil's pride. For the Andes, the library holds little beyond his book.

Motolinía also told a smaller scene. Friars visited two provinces from their house at Cuauhnahuac, and people came from the small town to the larger one to hear them and to have their children baptized. One night, heavy rain turned a stream into a river. The friars preached from one bank to the people on the other, and the people would not go home until their children were baptized. So they built a poor raft of canes on gourds. They carried the children across, and the friars baptized them with great effort, he says, because there were so many.
