In July 1526, Afonso I, the Christian king of Kongo, wrote to the king of Portugal. He asked for wine and flour for the Mass. Then he warned that his land was being emptied of its people, who were being carried off to be sold. Contact with Portugal had brought Kongo both the faith and this trade, and in his letters Afonso set the one against the other.

Portuguese ships reached the mouth of the Zaire River in 1491. A report written down a century later says Kongo's king was baptized and took the name Dom João, and that his son, who took the name Afonso, soon followed.

Afonso spoke for himself in letters his scribes wrote down, and they survive, while almost everything else about Kongo's Christians was written by Portuguese and Italian outsiders. In a letter to the pope in 1512, which a Portuguese chronicler later printed, Afonso said Portuguese religious men had led his people away from idols. In 1517 he wrote that he sent his kin to Portugal to learn the faith and bring light to the blind in his kingdom. After his death, he said, they could uphold it.

Not everyone at court welcomed the faith. The report says his brother Pango stood against him, and that the rule of one wife turned some nobles and their wives against it. It says Afonso lost his province on a charge that he wanted the throne, and later won it back. When the old king died, the report adds, Afonso's mother kept the death secret for three days so that he could reach the capital in time. Then, it says, Pango brought a great army against him, and Afonso defeated it and kept the throne.

By 1526, the letters turn to a harm that came with Portugal. Afonso wrote that merchants and thieves were carrying off his people every day, including the sons of nobles and his own kin. He asked for priests and teachers for his schools, and for no merchants at all. In his words: "It is our will that in these kingdoms there be no trade in slaves, and no outlet for them." His letter is a plea. It does not tell us what came of it.

In December 1540, fourteen years on, he wrote to Portugal of an attempt on his life. He said a friar had arranged for seven or eight white men to fire at him during Easter Mass. The shot passed by the skirts of his robe, he said. It killed one man and wounded two. That charge rests on his word alone.

He wrote that letter as he wrote the others, calling the king of Portugal his brother and signing himself king.
