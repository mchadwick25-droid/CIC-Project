In the Ming court, a Chinese minister wrote to the emperor in defense of foreign priests. He called them, in the translator's English, "worthies and sages," the words China used for its own great teachers. If they were guilty, he wrote, then so was he.

The minister was Xu Guangqi, known in English as Paul Su. He was one of the scholars and officials whom Jesuit missionaries had won to Christianity in Ming China. He argued for the faith in the words of China's own teachers. His memorial is a rare convert's own voice, though it reaches us in an English translation of 1850, and its wording is the translator's.

The mission's own story was written by the Jesuit Nicolas Trigault, from Matteo Ricci's own writings. In September 1583, it tells, Ricci and a fellow Jesuit, Michele Ruggieri, reached the city of Zhaoqing. Ruggieri had stayed there once before and been sent away. Kneeling before the governor, they asked for a place to build a house and a church. He answered kindly, and told them to look about the city for a place.

The memorial answers a report from the Board of Rites, made in 1616 or 1617. The Board had impeached the Jesuits at court. Its report said their teaching was spreading, so that even men of eminence were believing in it.

Xu did not deny it. He wrote that he had talked with the Jesuits about religion for years. He said they had come thousands of miles because they heard that China's own teachers served Heaven through virtue, as theirs served the Lord of Heaven. He called Buddhism false, since it had not reformed hearts in eighteen hundred years. In his view the Jesuits' teaching could repair and strengthen the royal laws and the learning of the scholars, and even correct the Buddhists.

Then he proposed three tests. The Jesuits' books should be translated, and the emperor's ministers should judge whether they were sound. The Jesuits should debate the leading Buddhists and Taoists in writing, and scholar-officials should decide. A summary of their teaching should go before the emperor himself. If the teaching proved wicked, he wrote, the Jesuits should be sent away, and he would accept the punishment due to those who helped deceive the emperor.

Near the end, he wrote that for peace and good government, "nothing can surpass this that is taught by your majesty's courtiers." The courtiers were the Jesuits; he called them the emperor's own. If the Board had its way, he wrote, these men must at once return to their own countries. He had fasted and washed, he said, before he dared to lay their case before the throne.
