In the Ming court, a Chinese minister wrote to the emperor in defense of foreign priests. He called them, in the translator's English, "worthies and sages." Then he offered to take their punishment if he was wrong.

The minister was Xu Guangqi, whom the sources here call Paul Su. The priests were Jesuits, whose mission in China began in 1583. In September of that year, they reached the city of Zhaoqing and asked the governor for leave to stay. He answered kindly, according to the Jesuit Nicolas Trigault, and told them to look about the city for a place.

Trigault's account, drawn from Matteo Ricci's journals and printed in English in 1625, is the mission's own story. Xu Guangqi's memorial is a rare convert's voice. An English translation of it appeared in Canton in 1850, from what its editors believed was a Chinese text copied from a stone at Shanghai. Its wording is the translator's.

The memorial answers a report from the Board of Rites, dated to 1617. The Board had impeached a Jesuit named Pantoja and his colleagues. Its report said their teaching was spreading, so that even men of eminence were believing in it.

Xu did not deny it. He wrote that he had talked with the Jesuits about religion for years. He said they had come thousands of miles because they heard that China's own teachers served Heaven through virtue, as theirs served the Lord of Heaven. He called Buddhism false, since it had not reformed hearts in eighteen hundred years. In his view the Jesuits' teaching could repair and strengthen the royal teaching and the learning of the scholars.

Then he proposed tests. Their books should be translated, and the emperor's ministers should judge whether they were sound. They should debate Buddhists and Taoists in writing, and scholars should decide. Those who followed their religion should be registered, and wrongdoers punished alone. If the teaching proved wicked, he wrote, he would accept the punishment due to those who helped deceive the emperor.

He ended by saying that "nothing can surpass this that is taught by your majesty's courtiers." If the Board had its way, he wrote, these men must at once return to their own countries. He had fasted and washed, he said, before he dared to lay their case before the throne.
