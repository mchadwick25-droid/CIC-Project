# Sheet: china-mission-born-christianity

Working file. Census entry `china-mission-born-christianity`, 1583-1650. Files in `cic/texts/`; line numbers are file lines.

**Scope.** One convert voice (Xu Guangqi's memorial, an English translation of 1850) and the mission's own record (Trigault from Ricci, in Purchas's English of 1625). The brief tells the 1583 arrival and the memorial of 1617 (the date is the translator's heading). It says that the converts' own books are not held.

## Witnesses
- **W1. Xu Guangqi ("Paul Su", "Sii Kwangki"), memorial "in behalf of the Jesuit missionaries, Pantoya and others, who had been impeached by the Board of Rites in a report dated the 44th year of his reign, 7th month (A.D. 1617)"**: `chinese-repository_v19-no3-march-1850-paul-su-apology.txt`, lines 1309-1770 (file lines). English translation printed at Canton in 1850; the translator is not named; the editors say the Chinese text was copied from a marble slab at the Jesuits' church at Shanghai and is "we believe, an exact transcript" (1316-1319). A convert's own words, through a nineteenth-century translation. Confidence: Widely Accepted as his memorial; the English wording is the translator's.
- **W2. Trigault, De Christiana expeditione apud Sinas (1615, from Ricci's journals), in Purchas's English of 1625**: `purchas_hakluytus-posthumus-pilgrimes-v12_1906.txt`. The mission's own account, written by a Jesuit. Anno 1583 at about line 16060; the Jesuits "came to Sciauquin" in September 1583 (16081-16083).

## Claims
**C1. 1583.** The Jesuits set out in the beginning of September 1583 and in the same month came to Sciauquin (Zhaoqing); they were taken to the governor's palace, knelt, and asked leave to stay; he answered kindly that they should look about the city for a place and that he did not doubt he could get the viceroy's leave (16081-16090). W2. Widely Accepted as the mission's own account.

**C2. The impeachment.** Xu writes that he has seen in the Governmental Gazette the report of the Board of Rites impeaching Pantoya and others, "your majesty's European courtiers" (1344-1346). The report said their doctrines were "penetrating deep, and spreading wide, so that even men of eminence are believing in them" (1347-1349). W1. Documented as what the memorial says the report said. Date 1617 from the translator's heading (1309-1314).

**C3. His defense.** He says he is one of the ministers who have talked with the missionaries on religion for years and has studied mathematics with them (1353-1360); he knows "they are indeed worthies and sages" (1378); they came "thousands of miles eastward" because they heard the teachers of China served Heaven by personal virtue as theirs served the Lord of Heaven (1383-1391). W1. Documented as his statement.

**C4. His argument in Chinese terms.** Buddhism came to China 1,800 years before and has not reformed hearts; "the knowledge of serving Heaven, communicated by your majesty's courtiers, is truly competent to repair and augment the royal Institutes, to strengthen and maintain the arts of the literati, and to restore and correct the laws of Budha" (1433-1449). W1. Documented as his statement. His attack on Buddhism is told openly.

**C5. Three tests.** Translate the Western books and let the emperor's ministers decide if they are right (1559-1573); let the missionaries and leading Buddhists and Taoists debate in writing and let literati ministers judge (1580-1590); keep a register of those who follow the religion, and punish offenders individually (1700-1714). If their teaching is found wicked "let the said courtiers be immediately dismissed and expelled; and your majesty's servant will willingly abide the punishment appointed for those who aid and abet the deceivers" (1575-1578). W1.

**C6. The last page.** "If now the recommendation of the Board be granted, these men must at once return to their own countries" (1738-1739). He says he has fasted and performed the ablutions before laying their case before the throne (1741-1743). He says that for good government "nothing can surpass this that is taught by your majesty's courtiers" (1736-1737). W1.

## Quotes (English, 1850, quoted verbatim; no rendering)
Q1. "they are indeed worthies and sages" (1378).
Q2. "nothing can surpass this that is taught by your majesty's courtiers" (1736-1737; "courtiers" means the Jesuits at the emperor's court).

## Who tells it
Two voices: the mission's (Trigault/Ricci, Purchas) and a convert's (Xu), the second only in a translation of 1850. The converts' own books are not held. The emperor's response is not in the lines read.

## Do not say
- Not the outcome of the memorial. The lines read do not give it.
- Not that Xu Guangqi's date (1617) is the translator's/editor's heading, not the memorial's own.
- Not that Ricci died "aged 80" (the 1850 editor says so; wrong); do not use any editor's remarks about Ricci.
- Not that the Jesuits were "expelled" in 1617; the Board proposed their return, and Xu opposed it.
- Not "Confucian Christianity" as a source claim: Xu argues the teaching fits and mends the royal and literati teaching; the phrase is not his.
- Not that Xu converted from conviction alone or from politics; the memorial says he studied for years.
- Not any claim about what ordinary Chinese Christians believed.

## Leave out
Ricci's death, the Nanjing expulsion, the later Jesuit court at Beijing; the converts' own books (not held); anything after 1650.

## Connection to carry
A Chinese official defends foreign priests by speaking in the language of China's own teachers: Heaven, virtue, the royal institutes. The sources hold his case, and they hold almost nothing of what ordinary Chinese Christians thought.

## Ending
His last page (C6): the Board's recommendation would send the men home; he has fasted and washed before writing.
