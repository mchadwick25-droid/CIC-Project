A Japanese Christian stood before a governor who was leading an inquiry against the Christians. The governor told him that to follow a faith the lord had forbidden was a capital crime, and that he could still be safe if he gave it up. The man, Jean Ingoro, answered that nothing could give him more joy than to shed his blood for God. The governor said: "I have never seen anyone as happy as you seem to be."

Ingoro was one of Japan's Christians. Their church began in 1549, when Francis Xavier landed at Kagoshima and wrote from there that November. By the early 1600s its members faced prison, exile and execution. A few of them wrote about it in their own words. Most of their letters went to Jesuit priests, and missionaries chose them and printed them in their own books. A French editor translated them in 1870. So we hear these few Christians through the hands of the mission.

Asked where such joy came from, Ingoro told the governor how he had come to believe. He had read the writings of the Japanese sects, and he found nothing in them about a Creator. He heard the preachers and argued with them many times, and he thought no priest could stand up to him. Then a Christian came back from Korea to the town where Ingoro was living. He converted most of its people and nobles, and Ingoro with them, by his own account the most stubborn of all. The governor answered that he did not know whether the missionaries could be trusted, and he had Ingoro jailed.

His letter stops there. A note printed after it adds that the governor accused him of stealing the bodies of four crucified Christians, among them little Luis, to sell them at Nagasaki. Ingoro answered that he had only gathered bones that had fallen, to bury them with Christian rites.

Michel Fieoyemon wrote from prison in January 1609. He had been there four years for confessing the name of God, and had borne trials without number, of every kind. He wrote: "I have borne them with such keen joy and comfort that I seem to have been a prisoner for only one day."

In May 1614, a group of Christians sailed into exile to Tsugaru. The lord of the province, in the name of the country's ruler, ordered them to clear unworked land. They answered that they would obey in everything if they could keep their faith. In their letter to the priests, they asked that one of them visit the next year, though they knew it could not be this year.

An outsider saw the same years. Richard Cocks, an English trader at Hirado, heard in August 1615 that the king of Spain was sending the emperor a gift for his kindness to Christians. The king little knew, Cocks wrote, that the emperor had already banished all the friars, monks, Jesuits and priests.

One more exchange survives. A woman named Maria heard it said that her husband, Jean Pioyemon, had been condemned to die. She asked him to send her his reliquary, a small case for holy relics, and some of his hair to remember him by. She hoped to be there at his death, to say a last farewell. He answered that to die as a martyr was far above what he deserved. He feared he might be set free instead, and he asked at least to have his fingers cut off for God, as other Christians had at Suruga. He would gladly exchange reliquaries with her, he wrote, but she was not to speak of his hair. If by God's grace he were put to death, she would then receive the great gift she asked of him.
