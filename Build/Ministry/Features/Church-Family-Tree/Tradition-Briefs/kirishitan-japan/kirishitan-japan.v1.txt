A Japanese Christian stood before a governor with the power to kill him. The governor told him that his faith was a capital crime, and that he could still be safe if he gave it up. The man, Jean Ingoro, answered that nothing could give him more joy than to shed his blood for God. The governor, who doubted the missionaries, said: "I have never seen anyone as happy as you seem to be."

This was the church of Japan's Christians. It began in 1549, when Francis Xavier wrote from Kagoshima that he had arrived. By the early 1600s the faith was forbidden, and its members faced prison, exile and execution.

Their voices survive in letters. Most were written to Jesuit priests, and a French editor translated them in 1870, so we hear these Christians through the mission. Ingoro's letter tells how he came to believe. He had read the writings of the Japanese sects, and he found nothing in them about a Creator. He heard the preachers and argued with them, and in the end he was baptized, after a Christian back from Korea converted most of the people and nobles of his town. The governor then had him jailed.

His letter stops there. The French editor adds that the governor accused him of stealing the bodies of four crucified Christians, among them a child named Luis. Ingoro answered that he had only gathered bones that had fallen, to bury them with Christian rites.

Michel Fieoyemon wrote from prison in January 1609. He had been there four years for confessing the name of God, and he wrote: "I have borne trials without number, of every kind. I have borne them with such keen joy and comfort that I seem to have been a prisoner for only one day."

In May 1614, a group of Christians sailed into exile in Tsugaru. The lord of the province, in the name of the ruler Daifusama, ordered them to clear unworked land. They answered that they would obey in everything if they could keep their faith. They then asked for a priest to visit them the next year.

An outsider saw the same years. Richard Cocks, an English trader at Hirado, wrote in August 1615 that the emperor had banished all the friars, monks, Jesuits and priests.

One more exchange survives. A woman named Maria heard that her husband, Jean Pioyemon, was condemned to die. She asked him for a relic, and she hoped to be present and to give him a last farewell. He answered that he did not deserve to be a martyr. He hoped only to lose his fingers, as other confessors had. He would not give her his hair. If he did die, he told her, she would then receive the gift she asked of him.
