One Saturday at the start of Lent, monks from Abingdon stood at the door of the Old Minster in Winchester. Inside, the clergy were ending Mass with a psalm. It told them to serve the Lord in fear and hold to discipline, or perish. The monks took it as a call to come in, and they went in.

They were led by Æthelwold of Winchester, the new bishop. With King Edgar's leave, he was putting monks in place of the clergy. Two of his pupils tell this in Lives written as tributes to a saint. Ælfric, an abbot who had been a pupil at Winchester, wrote twenty years after his death. The other Life carries no author's name. Jean Mabillon, who printed the Lives of Benedictine saints from 1668, judged it the work of Wulfstan, a cantor at Winchester. He took Ælfric's Life to be the earlier one.

Æthelwold was born in Winchester in the days of King Edward the Elder. As a young man he served at King Æthelstan's court. Bishop Ælfheah of Winchester ordained him priest on the same day as Dunstan. He then became a monk at Glastonbury, where Dunstan was abbot.

He wanted to go abroad to learn more. Queen Eadgifu advised her son, King Eadred, not to let him leave. Eadred gave him Abingdon instead, a small old monastery that had fallen into neglect, and he became its abbot. He sent his monk Osgar across the sea to Fleury, Saint Benedict's monastery. Osgar was to learn how its monks kept the Rule of Saint Benedict, the book that ordered monastic life, and teach it at home.

Edgar chose him as bishop of Winchester. Wulfstan and the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle date his consecration to 29 November 963. Ælfric says the clergy he drove out had married, put their wives away and taken others. Out of their spite, he says, the bishop was given poison at his own table, and his faith overcame it. Æthelwold went on to set monks at Ely, Peterborough and Thorney.

Edgar also called a council at Winchester. It agreed one customary, a shared book of daily practice, for England's monks and nuns. Its preface names Edgar and Archbishop Dunstan. It does not name Æthelwold, though the cataloguer Oudin lists an English version under his name.

After Edgar died in 975, the Chronicle says, Ælfhere, a royal governor called an ealdorman, had many of Æthelwold's monasteries torn down.

He died on 1 August 984, Wulfstan says. Ælfric remembered how he loved to teach the young and explain books to them in English. Many of his pupils became abbots and bishops. One manuscript of the Chronicle, in E. E. C. Gomme's English of 1909, calls him "father of monks".
