[calmly] One Saturday at the start of Lent, monks from Abingdon stood at the door of the Old Minster in Winchester. Inside, the clergy were ending Mass with a psalm. It told them to serve the Lord in fear and hold to discipline, or perish. The monks took it as a call to come in, and they went in.

They were led by Æthelwold of Winchester, the new bishop. With King Edgar's leave, he was putting monks in place of the clergy there. Two of his pupils tell this in Lives written as tributes to a saint. Ælfric, a monk trained at Winchester, wrote twenty years after his death. The other Life carries no author's name. The scholar Jean Mabillon judged that it was by Wulfstan, a cantor at Winchester, and took Ælfric's to be the earlier one.

Æthelwold was born in Winchester in the days of King Edward the Elder. As a young man he served at King Æthelstan's court. He became a monk at Glastonbury under its abbot, Dunstan, and studied grammar, verse and Scripture there.

He wanted to go abroad to learn more. Queen Eadgifu advised her son, King Eadred, not to let him leave. Eadred gave him Abingdon, a small, neglected old monastery, and made him its abbot. Æthelwold sent his monk Osgar across the sea to Fleury, Saint Benedict's monastery, to learn its ways and teach them at home.

Edgar chose him as bishop of Winchester. Wulfstan and the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle date his consecration to 29 November 963. Ælfric says the clergy he drove out had taken wives and would not say Mass in turn. He also tells that they poisoned the bishop's drink at his own table, and that his faith overcame it. Æthelwold went on to found or restore monasteries at Ely, Peterborough and Thorney.

[warmly] He died on 1 August 984, Wulfstan says. Ælfric remembered how he loved to teach the young and to explain Latin books to them in English. Many of his pupils became abbots and bishops. One manuscript of the Chronicle, in Edward Gomme's English of 1909, calls him "father of monks".
