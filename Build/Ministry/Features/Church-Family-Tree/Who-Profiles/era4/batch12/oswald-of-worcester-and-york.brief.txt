Each Lent, Oswald of Worcester and York washed the feet of twelve poor men, singing psalms. Near the end of one February, the old archbishop knelt with his monks after the psalms. As he spoke the closing words of praise to the Trinity, he bowed and died.

A monk of Ramsey tells this in a Life of Oswald, a tribute to a saint. James Raine, who first printed it in 1879, dates it between 995 and 1005, and calls it the earliest and most trustworthy account. Oswald was an English bishop of the 900s who helped bring monks back to the churches.

His father's brother was Oda, archbishop of Canterbury. Oda bought Oswald a monastery at Winchester. Its clergy, the monk writes, gave their wealth to their wives. Oswald asked to serve God overseas, and Oda sent him to Fleury in France, where Saint Benedict's bones lay.

There Oswald stayed many years and learned the Rule of Benedict, the monks' book of laws, by heart. Oda sent for him, but as his ship neared Canterbury, Oswald heard that Oda had died. His kinsman Oscytel, archbishop of York, commended him to Bishop Dunstan. Dunstan won Oswald the empty see of Worcester from King Edgar. William Stubbs, the 1874 editor of Dunstan's Lives, dates his consecration to 961.

Oswald gathered more than twelve men to live as monks at Westbury. Then Æthelwine, ealdorman, or governor, of East Anglia, gave him an island called Ramsey. Oswald rejoiced at its woods, lakes, fish and birds.

Edgar, the Life says, set two crowns on his head, adding the see of the Northumbrians to that of the Mercians. Joseph Stevenson, editing an abbey chronicle in 1858, dates his move to York to 972. After Edgar died, Ælfhere, ealdorman of Mercia, drove out monks, and Æthelwine defended them.

Three months before his death, Oswald visited Ramsey for the last time. He blessed the monks and kissed each one. His only words, in the Life's Latin, were a prayer that the Lord would bring them all together in Paradise.
