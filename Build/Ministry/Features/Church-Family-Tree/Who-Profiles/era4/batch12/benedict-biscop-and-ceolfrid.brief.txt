On a June morning in 716, the old abbot Ceolfrid left his monks at Wearmouth, in northern England, to go to Rome. Deacons carried a golden cross and burning candles down to the river. As his boat crossed the Wear, he heard the monks singing on the bank and could not hold back his tears. He asked Christ again and again to have mercy on them and protect them. He had never known better men, he said, or readier to obey.

Ceolfrid and Benedict Biscop founded the twin monastery of Wearmouth and Jarrow. Two Lives tell their story, both written in the house as tributes to its abbots. One is anonymous. The other is by Bede of Jarrow, who was given at seven to Benedict to be educated, and afterwards to Ceolfrid.

Bede says Benedict was a nobleman who served King Oswiu of Northumbria. At about twenty-five he gave up his lands to follow Christ. He went to Rome again and again, and became a monk at Lerins, an island monastery off the coast of Provence. In 674 King Egfrid gave him land at the mouth of the Wear. Benedict brought masons from Gaul to build a stone church in the Roman style, and glassmakers, whose craft Britain did not yet know. From Rome he carried home books, relics and paintings.

The anonymous Life says Ceolfrid, also of noble birth, became a monk at about eighteen. Bishop Wilfrid of Ripon ordained him priest, and at Ripon he long served as the baker. Benedict asked for him as his partner at Wearmouth. Some nobles there could not bear his strict discipline, and he went home to Ripon. Benedict followed and begged him to come back.

In 681 or 682, by the dating of Charles Plummer, who edited the Lives in 1896, Benedict sent Ceolfrid to found Saint Paul's at Jarrow. Bede says he took about seventeen monks, and the anonymous Life says twenty-two. When plague struck Jarrow, the Life says, the only ones left who could sing the services were Ceolfrid and one small boy he had raised. Plummer says the boy can hardly be anyone but Bede.

Benedict spent his last three years paralyzed. He made Ceolfrid abbot of both houses, to keep them one monastery under one abbot. In Peter Wilcock's English of Bede, from 1818, he said he would rather the place became "a dreary unfrequented wild" than see his brother "according to the flesh" succeed him. He died on 12 January, in 689 or 690 by Plummer's reckoning.

Bede says Ceolfrid doubled the libraries of both houses. He had three great Bibles copied in Jerome's Latin. At seventy-four he set out for Rome with one of them as a gift for Saint Peter. He died on the way at Langres, in Gaul, on 25 September.

At the head of that Bible, the anonymous Life records, were verses in his name. In them Ceolfrid, abbot from the farthest ends of the English, offers it as a pledge of his love, and asks for himself and his own a place in heaven.
