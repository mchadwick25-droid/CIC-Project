A book carries a line on its first page: "This Book is for Worcester." It opens with a letter from King Alfred to Wærferth, bishop of Worcester. Henry Sweet, who printed it with his English in 1871, dates this copy to Alfred's reign.

Alfred ruled the West Saxons from 871. Asser, a Welsh churchman who became his bishop, wrote a Life of him in praise of the king. Asser says Alfred was born at Wantage in 849 and could not read until he was twelve or older.

In the letter Alfred looks back. Foreigners once came to England seeking wisdom, he writes. Now few could translate a letter from Latin. "I cannot remember a single one south of the Thames when I came to the throne." He had seen churches "filled with treasures and books" before they were ravaged and burnt. But their clergy understood little of the books, "because they were not written in their own language."

So he proposed a plan. Books "most needful for all men to know" should be put into English. Young freeborn men who could afford it should learn to read English first. He translated Pope Gregory's Pastoral Care, a book for those who teach in the church, "sometimes word by word and sometimes according to the sense." He learned it, he says, from Archbishop Plegmund, Bishop Asser and two priests. He meant to send a copy to every bishopric in his kingdom.

Asser adds that the king built a monastery at Athelney. No free Englishman would join it except children, because love of the monastic life had "utterly decayed." So Alfred brought monks from overseas.

Thomas Wright in 1841 and Henry Howorth in the 1870s doubted the Life was genuine. Albert Cook, who put it into English in 1906, judged those doubts "satisfactorily dispelled." Its editor, W. H. Stevenson, found "no anachronism or other proof" that it was written later. He dated it to 893.

The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle says Alfred died in 901. Stevenson puts his death in 899.

In his letter Alfred forbids anyone to take the book from its church, since "it is uncertain how long there may be such learned bishops as now, thanks be to God, there are nearly everywhere."
