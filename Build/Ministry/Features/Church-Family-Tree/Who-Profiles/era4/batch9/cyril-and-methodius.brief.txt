On the thirtieth of December, a priest named Constantine sailed out with George, the metropolitan of Cherson. They were looking for the body of the martyr Clement of Rome, on an island near Cherson. As they dug, suddenly, like a bright star, one of the martyr's ribs shone out. Then they found his head, and at last the anchor with which he had been thrown into the sea.

Constantine was called the Philosopher. He and his brother Methodius came from Thessalonica. The finding is told in what the historian Johann Ginzel calls the Italian Legend. He trusts it, and follows the Bollandists in tracing it to Gauderich of Velletri, a bishop of the brothers' time.

Rastislav, prince of Moravia, asked the emperor for a teacher, since his people had left their idols but had none. The emperor sent the brothers. The Legend says they taught the children letters and the services. After four and a half years they left behind all the books the church needed.

A Life of Methodius says God revealed the Slavonic letters to the Philosopher. The historian Ernst Dümmler thought a pupil of Methodius wrote it soon after his death. Ginzel, who writes against the Greek schism, calls it a schismatic's concoction.

They brought Clement's relics to Rome. Pope Hadrian II came out to meet them, and the Legend says he made them both bishops. There Constantine took the name Cyril and died forty days later, on the fourteenth of February. Methodius asked to take his body back to their monastery, as their mother had tearfully begged, should one die abroad. The Roman clergy pressed to keep him, and Cyril was buried in the basilica of Saint Clement.

Methodius went back as a bishop to the Slavs. A Salzburg account of about 873 complains of a certain Greek named Methodius. With newly invented Slavonic letters, it says, he partly cheapened the Latin Masses in the eyes of all the people. In 879 Pope John VIII repeated his ban on singing the Mass in Slavonic. In 880, after examining him in Rome, he wrote to Svatopluk, prince of Moravia. We rightly praise the Slavonic letters found by Constantine the Philosopher, he said. God, who made the three chief languages, Hebrew, Greek and Latin, made all the others too, for his praise.

Methodius died on the sixth of April 885, the Life says, and his pupils held his funeral in Latin, Greek and Slavonic.
