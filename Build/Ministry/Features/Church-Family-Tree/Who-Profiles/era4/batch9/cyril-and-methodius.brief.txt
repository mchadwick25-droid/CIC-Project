On the thirtieth of December, a priest named Constantine sailed out with George, the metropolitan of Cherson, and his clergy. They were looking for the body of the martyr Clement of Rome, on an island near Cherson. As they dug, suddenly, like a bright star, one of the martyr's ribs shone out. Then they found his head, and at last the anchor with which he had been thrown into the sea.

Constantine was called the Philosopher. He and his brother Methodius came from Thessalonica. The finding is told in the Italian Legend. Johann Ginzel, who trusts it, follows the Bollandists in tracing it to Gauderich of Velletri, a bishop of the brothers' own time.

Rastislav, prince of Moravia, sent to the emperor for a teacher. His people had left their idols, he said, but had no teacher. The emperor sent the brothers. They taught the children letters and the church services, the Legend says. After four and a half years they left behind all the books the church needed.

A Life of Methodius says God revealed the Slavonic letters to the Philosopher. Ernst Dümmler thought one of Methodius's pupils wrote it soon after his death. Ginzel calls it the work of a Greek schismatic.

They brought Clement's relics to Rome. Pope Hadrian II came out to meet them, and the Legend says he made them both bishops. There Constantine took the name Cyril, and he died forty days later, on the fourteenth of February. Methodius asked to take his body home, since their mother had begged them with tears to do so if one died abroad. The Roman clergy pressed to keep him, and Cyril was buried in the basilica of Saint Clement.

Methodius went back as a bishop to the Slavs. A Salzburg account of about 873 complains that a certain Greek named Methodius, with newly invented Slavonic letters, made the Latin Masses cheap in the eyes of all the people. In 879 Pope John VIII forbade him to sing the Mass in Slavonic. The next year, after examining him in Rome, the pope wrote to Svatopluk, prince of Moravia. We rightly praise the Slavonic letters found by Constantine the Philosopher, he said. God, who made the three chief languages, Hebrew, Greek and Latin, made all the others too, for his praise.

Methodius died on the sixth of April 885, the Life says, and his pupils held his funeral in Latin, Greek and Slavonic.
