[calmly] In 754 a council of bishops at Constantinople ruled against the holy images. Its bishops cursed three men by name. In Henry Percival's English, one cry runs: "Anathema to Mansur, who has an evil name and Saracen opinions!"

Mansur was John of Damascus, a monk and priest in Palestine. He came from a Christian family of Damascus, a city then ruled by the Muslim caliphs. The chronicler Theophanes the Confessor names him as the son of Mansur, and says he was rightly called Chrysorrhoas, golden-flowing, for the grace of his speech.

Most of his story comes from a Life headed as the work of a John, patriarch of Jerusalem. Johann Fabricius dates its author after the middle of the tenth century, and the Life says it reworks an older Life in Arabic. It tells that the caliph made John his first counselor. The emperor Leo the Isaurian, it says, forged a letter in John's hand offering to betray Damascus, and the caliph had John's right hand cut off. John prayed before an image of the Mother of God, and woke healed. Fabricius calls this a hardly credible tale, while Leo Allatius counts the healing the main thing in it. Then, the Life says, John gave away his goods and went to the monastery of Mar Saba, near Jerusalem.

Michel Le Quien, who edited John's works in 1712, says Leo began to move against the images in 726. John answered with three speeches in their defense. Le Quien dates the first to 727 or 728, and the second to 730, when Leo drove Patriarch Germanus of Constantinople from his see. Once, John wrote, no image could show God, who had no body. Now God has been seen in the flesh. I do not worship matter, he wrote, but the maker of matter, who became matter for my sake. Emperors, he said, have no power to make laws for the church.

His Exposition of the Orthodox Faith says the same in S. D. F. Salmond's English. When we see the image of Christ's crucifixion, "we fall down and worship not the material but that which is imaged."

[warmly] In 808 Stephen the Deacon wrote the Life of Stephen the Younger, who died for honoring the images. In it he recalled that John kept writing against the emperor Constantine V. The tyrant called him Mansur, Stephen wrote, but we call him holy and God-bearing.
