[calmly] At Schleswig, Rimbert saw a crowd of Christians being dragged off in chains. One of them, a nun, knelt and bowed to him from far off. To show she was a Christian, she raised her voice and began to sing psalms. Rimbert prayed for her in tears, and the chain broke from her neck. Her captors would take no price but his own horse. He jumped down at once, gave it to them, harness and all, and set her free.

Rimbert followed Anskar as archbishop of Hamburg and Bremen, and with a fellow disciple he wrote Anskar's Life. His own story is told in a Life of Rimbert by an unnamed author. G. H. Pertz says it was written while Adalgar, the next archbishop, was alive, by a cleric of Bremen or a monk of Corbie.

Anskar first saw him at Turholt, in Flanders. Among boys running to church in play, one of the smallest walked soberly, prayed with reverence and signed his forehead with the cross. Anskar asked his name and, with his parents' consent, gave him the tonsure.

Wherever the Life of Anskar speaks of a most faithful disciple, the Life of Rimbert says, it means Rimbert, who left out his own name so as not to boast. When the dying Anskar could no longer speak, it was Rimbert he told to go on saying the prayers. On the day of Anskar's burial, in 865, clergy and people chose Rimbert to follow him.

We must not delay to help all the poor, he used to say, for we do not know which one is Christ, or when he comes to us. To ransom captives he spent even the altar vessels. We can always find enough for the service of the altar, he said, but if a Christian dies in captivity, it cannot be undone.

He died in 888, Adam of Bremen writes nearly two centuries later, and was buried on 11 June.

[warmly] Once, the Life says, Rimbert asked Anskar to share his reward in the life to come. Three days later Anskar told him: I have obtained from the Lord that in the world to come, as you asked, we shall not be parted.
