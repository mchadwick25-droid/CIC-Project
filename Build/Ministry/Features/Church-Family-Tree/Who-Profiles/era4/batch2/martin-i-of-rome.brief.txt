In June 653, Pope Martin I lay sick before the altar of the Lateran church in Rome. Soldiers came in with lances, swords and bows ready. The candles fell under their weapons, he later wrote, like leaves in a winter wind. He gave himself up. He judged it better to die ten times, he said, than to let anyone's blood be shed.

He told the story in a letter to his friend Theodore. Martin was born at Todi in Tuscany and became bishop of Rome in 649.

The emperor Constans had issued an edict, the Typos, forbidding anyone to say that Christ had one will or two. Martin's own speech to his synod blames Paul, patriarch of Constantinople, for urging the edict. Paul had also overturned the Roman see's altar in that city, so its envoys could not say Mass. In October 649 Martin gathered his synod at the Lateran. Its bishops condemned Cyrus of Alexandria and Sergius, Pyrrhus and Paul, patriarchs of Constantinople, for teaching one will.

After Olympius, governor of Italy, failed to seize him, the Book of the Popes says, his successor, Theodore Calliopas, took him. Martin wrote that he spent a year on the island of Naxos.

An account sent to the West by one of his clergy says he reached Constantinople on 17 September. After ninety-three days in prison he was carried to his trial, gravely ill. Witnesses swore that he had plotted with Olympius. When Martin began to speak of the Typos, the prefect Troilus cut him off. Do not bring the faith in here, Troilus said; the charge was rebellion.

Before a crowd he was stripped of his priestly cloak and led through the city in irons. Spared death, he sailed for Cherson the next spring, on Holy Thursday. There, he wrote, people spoke of bread but never saw it. His own church of Saint Peter had forgotten him, he complained. He prayed that God would keep its people firm in the faith, and above all the shepherd who now led them.

The clergyman says Martin died on 16 September and was buried outside Cherson. Bede says a Roman abbot sent by Pope Agatho brought "the decision of the synod of the blessed Pope Martin and 105 bishops" to Wearmouth.

The Lord would care for his poor body, Martin wrote, in endless trouble or in a little relief. The Lord was near, so he need not be anxious.
