At sunset, guards took the old monk Maximus the Confessor and a companion off a ship at Constantinople, barefoot and without cloaks. Days later, before the senate, the emperor's treasurer angrily asked whether he was a Christian. By the grace of Christ, he answered, he was. The treasurer said that was not true. You say so, Maximus replied, but God knows I am a Christian and remain one.

The scene comes from his own side's record of that hearing. Louis Duchesne, editor of the Book of the Popes, dates it to 655. Maximus alone, the treasurer said, had handed Egypt, Alexandria and Africa to the Saracens.

His Greek Life, written in his honor, says he was born in Constantinople. The emperor Heraclius made him first among his secretaries. He left the court for a monastery at Chrysopolis as the teaching that Christ had one will spread.

In Africa he debated Pyrrhus, a former patriarch of Constantinople. Later the emperor Constans forbade talk of one will or two in an edict, the Typos.

His questioners asked why he shunned communion with Constantinople's church. He named the Typos and an earlier statement by the patriarch Sergius. By them, he said, its leaders had cast out the four councils and deposed themselves. One must speak of two wills and two workings in Christ, he said, since the Fathers teach that no nature exists without its own working.

The chronicler Theophanes the Confessor says Maximus went from Africa to Rome and stirred Pope Martin I to act. Martin's synod of 649 condemned Sergius and Pyrrhus.

At Bizya in Thrace, a bishop promised the Typos would be withdrawn if Maximus took communion. Maximus asked that the Roman synod's verdict be accepted first. The bishop said it lacked force without the emperor's order. If emperors' orders made synods valid, Maximus answered, the synods against the Nicene faith would count too.

A synod then condemned him with two companions, both named Anastasius. The sentence ordered their tongues cut out and their right hands cut off. They were paraded through the city and exiled among the Lazi.

There, Anastasius the envoy wrote, guards carried Maximus on a litter of woven branches to a fort near the Alans. He foretold the day of his death, the envoy adds, and died on it, 13 August.

He had told his questioners he held no teaching of his own, only the common teaching of the Catholic church.
