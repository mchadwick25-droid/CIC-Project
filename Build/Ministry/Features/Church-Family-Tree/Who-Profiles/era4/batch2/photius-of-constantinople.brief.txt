In October 869, a council sent for Photius of Constantinople, the city's former patriarch. He came under compulsion, its acts say, and stood in the last place. Asked whether he accepted the decrees of the fathers and the popes, he said nothing. Pressed, he answered that God heard his voice even when he was silent. Silence would not save him, the Roman legates said. He replied that Jesus, too, kept silent and was condemned.

This record comes from the council that condemned him, in the Latin of Anastasius, the Roman see's librarian.

Photius had risen at court as chief secretary. Nicetas the Paphlagonian, who wrote the Life of Photius's rival, the patriarch Ignatius, was an enemy too. Yet he calls Photius the first scholar of his age, in grammar, poetry, rhetoric, philosophy and medicine. He bought books in great numbers, Nicetas says, and read whole nights through, for love of glory. When Ignatius was driven out, Photius replaced him. Nicetas says he went from layman to patriarch in six days, taking the throne on Christmas Day. J. H. Freese, his English translator, dates this to 858.

Pope Nicholas I refused to accept him. He wrote to Photius that he had leapt to that honor from a layman's dress, without climbing the church's grades. Photius wrote to the Eastern patriarchs against the Latins. He listed their fasting on Saturdays and their contempt for married priests. Worst, he wrote, they tried to corrupt the Creed with added words. They taught that the Spirit proceeds from the Son as well as the Father. Cornelius Will, who printed part of the letter in Latin, dates it to 866.

Symeon the Logothete says Photius called the new emperor, Basil, a robber and a murderer at communion, and that Basil then put him out. After Ignatius died, says the chronicle that continues Theophanes, Basil lawfully restored him. In 879 a council received him, with legates of Pope John VIII. Its own acts curse anyone who adds to the Creed. Severinus Binius, in his notes on the councils, calls it a false synod.

When Leo VI followed Basil, Symeon says, he sent Photius to the monastery of Gordon, where he died.

His Library sums up 279 books he had read, for Tarasius, whom he calls his beloved brother. "It is no easy matter to undertake to read each individual work, to grasp the argument, to remember and record it", he wrote.
