[calmly] Messengers came from the city of Ani to the convent of Narek. An assembly of chiefs and clergy had sent them to bring its abbot, Gregory, to answer for his faith. He welcomed them and served roasted pigeons. They objected that it was Friday, a fast day. Then, the story goes, he told the birds to rise and go, and they flew away.

Gregory of Narek was a monk of tenth-century Armenia. Michael Chamich tells this story in his History of Armenia, printed in 1784. James Issaverdens retells it in 1875. Both were Mekhitarists, monks of an Armenian order at Venice. "We, however, report this merely as an anecdote," Issaverdens adds, "as it shews the public esteem towards the holy man."

Chamich says Gregory was raised from infancy at Narek, in the land of the Rushtunians, and in time became its abbot. Giovanni de Serpos, writing at Venice in 1786, calls Gregory the pupil of Anania, a learned abbot of Narek.

Chamich and Issaverdens say Gregory tried to unite the Armenians and the Greeks by bringing his people to accept the council of Chalcedon. Malachia Ormanian, a former Armenian patriarch of Constantinople, names their order among those that won Armenians over to the Catholic side. Serpos says Gregory worked to calm the quarrels over Chalcedon. Stepanos of Taron, of the same century, wrote Armenian history. He says teachers including Anania of Narek ruled that anyone baptized in the Chalcedonian faith be baptized again.

Chamich says Gregory prayed long to see the Virgin with her Son, and was granted the sight. Then he wrote the book of prayers called Narek. Chamich has him comment on the Song of Songs and on Job at twenty-six. Serpos says twenty, for a Bagratid king.

Two of his prayers to the Holy Spirit stand in the Armenian Liturgy, as Issaverdens translates it. In the first, said at the foot of the altar, the one leading the service prays, "we implore Thee with out-stretched arms and deep groans." In one breviary manuscript edited by F. C. Conybeare, a meditation of his follows the prayers before sleep. It begins, "Accept, O powerful Lord God, in thy loving-kindness the prayer of him that has turned again to thee."

[warmly] Chamich says he died at fifty, and Ormanian gives the year as 1003. Ormanian writes that the Narek is used for private prayer, and is "regarded as a potent talisman against all kinds of dangers."
