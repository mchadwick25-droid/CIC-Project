[calmly] 'Amr, the Arab commander, had taken Alexandria. A Christian duke named Sanutius told him about the patriarch of the Copts, a fugitive from the emperor's men. 'Amr wrote to the provinces of Egypt that the patriarch could come out safely and govern his church. When Benjamin came before him, 'Amr told his friends he had never seen a man of God like this man.

So says the History of the Patriarchs of Alexandria, a later Arabic history of Benjamin's own church. Benjamin I of Alexandria led the Egyptian church that refused the council of Chalcedon. B. Evetts, its translator, heads his life 622 to 661. He came from a rich family at Barshut and became a monk under an old man named Theonas. The patriarch Andronicus made him a priest and named him his successor.

The emperor Heraclius sent Cyrus, bishop of Phasis, to Egypt as prefect and patriarch, to bring the Egyptians over to Chalcedon. The History says an angel told Benjamin to flee and to warn his bishops to hide. He walked by night to the desert monasteries of Wadi Habib, then hid in Upper Egypt. Heraclius had Benjamin's brother Mennas tortured and drowned for refusing to call Chalcedon good.

R. H. Charles thinks John, the Coptic bishop of Nikiu whom he translated, was born about the time of the conquest. John says Benjamin came back in the thirteenth year after his flight. People said, John writes, that the Muslims won because of Heraclius's persecution through Cyrus.

Michael the Syrian, a twelfth-century patriarch of the Syriac Orthodox church, tells it differently. He found in histories that Benjamin promised the Arabs Alexandria if they would drive out Cyrus and give him back the churches.

Back in his see, the History says, Benjamin won back by gentleness most of those Heraclius had led astray. His spiritual son Agathon tells how he consecrated a church of Saint Macarius in Wadi Habib. Some of Benjamin's rules for its sanctuary end in curses. "Think not, my brethren, that I have written these anathemas for this generation," he told those around him. Eusèbe Renaudot, who wrote a Latin history of these patriarchs in 1713, calls their line heretical. He calls the account a fable of later Copts.

[warmly] Benjamin died after thirty-nine years as patriarch. Agathon, who succeeded him, remembered him as one "who understood the interpretation of the scriptures, and dwelt in the desert, and grasped many mysteries."
