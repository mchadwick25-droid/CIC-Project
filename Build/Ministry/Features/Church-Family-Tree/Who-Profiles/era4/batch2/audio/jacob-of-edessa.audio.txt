[calmly] Leaving his see at Edessa, Jacob argued with the patriarch Julian the Roman and the bishops over keeping the church's canons. No one listened, and all told him to bend to the times. So he burned the book of canons at the door of the patriarch's monastery. He cried out that he was burning, as useless, the canons they trampled underfoot.

Michael the Syrian, a twelfth-century patriarch, tells this, and so does Bar Hebraeus a century later. Both belonged to Jacob's own Syriac Orthodox church, which refused the Council of Chalcedon.

Jacob, who lived around the year 700, came from 'Ayndaba, a village in the country of Antioch. He became a monk at Kenneshre, where he studied Greek, then gathered learning in Alexandria. Back in Syria he settled at Edessa, and the patriarch Athanasius of Balad made him its bishop. After four years, troubled by clergy he had barred for their disorders, he gave up the see.

He withdrew to a monastery at Kaisum. There he wrote two tracts of reproach, against the church's shepherds and against those who break its canons. For eleven years the monks of Eusebona had him teach Greek, whose study had died out. Brothers who hated the Greeks made trouble for him, and he left. At the monastery of Tell 'Ada he spent nine years correcting the Old Testament.

In his chronicle's preface, which Michael keeps, Jacob says no one since Eusebius of Caesarea had carried his tables forward. Joseph Assemani reports that Elias of Nisibis called Jacob the first to write a grammar of Syriac.

In about 701 he revised the Syriac of Severus of Antioch's sermons against the Greek. M.-A. Kugener and his student Triffaux, who edited one, say he kept the older version wherever it needed no change.

When the next bishop died, Edessa called Jacob back. He held the see four months, then went to fetch his books and pupils. At Tell 'Ada he sent the books ahead, but died there. Its monks hurried to bring the books back before they crossed the Euphrates. Bar Hebraeus dates his death to the fifth of June 708. Michael's manuscript puts it four years earlier in one place, and Assemani gives 710.

[warmly] Kugener and Triffaux print his note on how Greek and Syriac name the days of the week. Whoever reads these words with love of the work, Jacob wrote, may perhaps understand why Severus's words vary here.
