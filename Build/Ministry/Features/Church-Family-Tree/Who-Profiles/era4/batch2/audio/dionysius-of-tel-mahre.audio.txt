[calmly] In Baghdad, in March 829, the caliph al-Ma'mun let the patriarch Dionysius of Tel Mahre come to him alone in his garden. Lazarus, a deposed bishop of Baghdad, cited the caliph's decree that any ten men of a faith could choose a leader. The Christians' fathers, Dionysius answered, had handed many cities to the Muslims on promises that their laws would stand. A law, he said, cannot be kept without a lawgiver, and the church's leadership was one of those laws. Al-Ma'mun ruled that the church could depose a sinner. It could not shut him out of church and prayer, for sinners above all must pray.

Bar Hebraeus tells this in his thirteenth-century Ecclesiastical Chronicle.

He led the Syriac Orthodox church as patriarch of Antioch from 818 until his death in 845. That church refused the Council of Chalcedon and lived under the caliphs. He was a monk of Kenneshre. In words Bar Hebraeus quotes, he tells how two monks seized him like a criminal. He pleaded, weeping, that he was unfit. The bishops made him deacon on a Friday, priest on Saturday, and patriarch on Sunday.

The emir Abdallah, son of Tahir, had gone down to Egypt, and his brother tore down new church buildings in Edessa. Dionysius sailed after the emir, and a storm drove him into Tanis. The emir scolded him for coming instead of writing, then wrote to his brother forbidding attacks on the churches. Dionysius won the people of Tanis a tax graded by wealth, in place of five dinars a head.

Later al-Ma'mun sent him, with Joseph, patriarch of Egypt, to talk rebel Christians of Lower Egypt into peace. The general Afshin refused terms, burned their villages, killed many, and shipped the rest away as captives. Dionysius told the caliph they were being crushed.

He wrote a history at the request of John, metropolitan of Dara. Michael the Syrian, a twelfth-century patriarch, keeps its preface. There Dionysius protests that John overlooked his old age and the contempt of waiting at princes' doors. He says Theophilus of Edessa, a Chalcedonian, dropped every story that named anyone of Dionysius's church.

[warmly] The history closed, Bar Hebraeus says, with words to John about the crushing taxes on Christians. He could not sleep, and wept over his life as he watched the church's children suffer. Death was the one release left to him, he wrote, and he sought it as a good thing.
