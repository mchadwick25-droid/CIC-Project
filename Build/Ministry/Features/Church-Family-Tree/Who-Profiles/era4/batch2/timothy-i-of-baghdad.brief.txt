When the head of the Church of the East died, its bishops quarreled over his seat. Two later historians of that church, Mari ibn Sulayman and Amr ibn Matta, tell how Timothy I of Baghdad won it. He promised money to the men of the school and showed them sacks that seemed full of coins. Once he was consecrated, Mari says, they asked for the money. The priesthood is not sold for money, he told them, and showed that the sacks held only stones.

Timothy came from Hazza in Adiabene. He studied under Rabban Abraham at Bashosh and became bishop of Beth Beghash when his uncle George stepped down. J.-B. Chabot, who edited the church's synods, dates his enthronement at Seleucia to May 780. Ephrem, metropolitan of Elam, refused him, and a split of about two years followed. In 790, by Chabot's date, Timothy wrote to Ephrem's province, setting rules for choosing a patriarch. He had not stayed away out of pride, he wrote. Ruined churches were being rebuilt, and he had gone before the king six times about it.

Thomas of Marga, writing about 840, tells how Timothy sent monks as bishops to Gilan and Dailom, where no preacher had gone since the apostles. E. A. Wallis Budge, who translated Thomas, places Gilan by the Caspian Sea. Timothy's letter to them, which Thomas read, said a bishop's ordination did not strictly need three persons. In their country, the Book of the Gospels could lie on the throne in place of the third. From Timothy's letters, Thomas also learned of a David, metropolitan of Beth Sinaye, which Budge glosses as China. Mari says Timothy brought the Khagan of the Turks and other kings to the faith.

'Abdisho, metropolitan of Nisibis, lists a disputation with the caliph al-Mahdi among his works. Amr says the caliph al-Hadi often called Timothy in to argue about religion. Amr tells that Harun al-Rashid once asked him which religion God approves. The one whose laws agree with the reason God keeps in dealing with his creatures, Timothy answered. The caliph said no objection could be raised to it.

The chronicler Elias of Nisibis says he died on a Friday in January 823. The historian Bar Hebraeus puts his death in the Arab year that began in June 819.

In a letter Alphonse Mingana translates, he said a hymn had no added words "among the Indians, the Chinese, the Tibetans, the Turks".
