On the Monday before Pentecost in 966, a crowd attacked the Church of the Resurrection in Jerusalem. The patriarch John had shut himself inside. The governor of Jerusalem had pressed him for more gifts than custom allowed, and John had refused. The crowd burned the church doors, and its dome fell in. The next day they found the patriarch hiding in an oil cistern in the church. They killed him, tied his body to a pillar and burned it.

Yahya ibn Said of Antioch tells this story. I. Kratchkovsky and A. Vasiliev, who edited his history in 1924, call him an Arab Christian historian of the eleventh century. He wrote his book as a sequel to the history of Sa'id ibn Bitriq, patriarch of Alexandria, also called Eutychius. Sa'id's history stops in the Arab year 326, which is 937 to 938, and Yahya takes up the story there.

His preface sets out his plan. He would record every account of past times that reached him and that he judged sound, down to his own day. He would name the patriarchs of Alexandria, Jerusalem, Antioch and Constantinople, as Sa'id had done. He wrote for a patron he does not name, who had asked him for the book.

He wrote it more than once. Chronicles he had not known came into his hands, he says, so he rewrote the whole book. Then he moved to Antioch, in the Arab year 405, which is 1014 to 1015. There he found still other chronicles and changed part of it again. He set this down so that, if other copies turned up that differed, the reason would be known.

His preface is all the held sources give of his life. None gives his birth or his death.

Frédéric Macler, translating the Armenian historian Stepanos of Taron, quotes Gustave Schlumberger. Schlumberger says the eastern historians, Yahya among them, are almost always very exactly informed about Asia, though much less about the distant war in Bulgaria. Yet only lately, he writes, has Yahya's chronicle shown that the emperor Basil's Bulgarian war lasted four whole years.

Yahya promised a middle way, neither too long nor too brief. Minds seek most eagerly, he wrote, to know the events closest to their own time.
