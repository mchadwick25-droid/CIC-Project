Around 720, an Englishwoman wrote to Boniface of Mainz, the missionary on the Continent, to give thanks for his success. God had guarded him, she wrote, "on thy way through unknown countries". The pope had smiled on his wish, and Rathbod, "that enemy of the Catholic Church", had been laid low. With the messenger she sent "fifty shillings and an altar pall".

She signed herself "Bugga, a lowly handmaiden". An earlier letter, from her mother Eangyth, an abbess, names her as "her only daughter Heaburg, called Bugga". Both letters are in Edward Kylie's English translation of 1911. Mother and daughter wrote of the souls "of different sex and age committed to us", of poverty, and of "the hostility of the king". Eangyth longed to go to Rome. Her daughter, she wrote, was still young and did not share the wish.

Bugga became an abbess herself. About 725, by Kylie's uncertain date, Boniface wrote "To the lady Bugga, abbess". She had asked whether to go abroad. He would neither forbid it nor urge it. Their sister Wiethburga had found quiet at the shrine of Saint Peter, he wrote. But she advised waiting until the dangers "from the Saracens" in Italy were over.

Bugga did go. Years later Ethelbert, king of Kent, wrote of "the venerable Abbess Bugga", his kinswoman. She had prayed at the holy places of Rome, he told Boniface, and met him there. Then she came home to "the monastery of consecrated women, which formerly she governed well under the law of the Church".

In her old age, Boniface heard of "the storms of tribulation" that had come on her. She had laid down "the greater anxiety of caring for the monasteries", he wrote, and heavier sorrows had followed.

Bregwin, archbishop of Canterbury, later wrote to Lul, who followed Boniface at Mainz. They kept the burial day of an abbess named Bugga, 27 December, he said. She had asked that Lul be told, since Boniface had been her father in Christ. The Bollandists, editors of the Acta Sanctorum, report a belief that Bugga was another name of Abbess Eadburga of Thanet. Kylie treats them as two women.

In her letter of thanks Bugga had told Boniface that "no temporal vicissitudes can move my mind from its steady guardianship of thy love."
