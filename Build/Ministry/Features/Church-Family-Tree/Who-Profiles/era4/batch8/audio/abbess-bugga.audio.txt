[calmly] Around 720, an Englishwoman wrote to Boniface of Mainz, the English missionary, to give thanks for his success. His letter had told her that Rathbod, king of the Frisians, "that enemy of the Catholic Church", had been laid low. With the messenger she sent "fifty shillings and an altar pall".

Her greeting names her "Bugga, a lowly handmaiden". Another letter, from her mother Eangyth, an abbess, names her as "her only daughter Heaburg, called Bugga". Both are in Edward Kylie's English translation of 1911. Mother and daughter wrote of the souls "of different sex and age committed to us", and of poverty.

Bugga became an abbess. About 725, by Kylie's uncertain date, Boniface wrote "To the lady Bugga, abbess". She had asked whether to go abroad. He would not urge it strongly, he wrote, but if she found no quiet at home, going seemed better. Their sister Wiethburga, at the shrine of Saint Peter, advised waiting until the dangers "from the Saracens" in Italy were over, and Boniface agreed.

In her old age, Boniface heard of "the storms of tribulation" that had come on her. She had laid down "the greater anxiety of caring for the monasteries", he wrote, and heavier sorrows had followed.

Ethelbert the Second, king of Kent, wrote to Boniface of "the venerable Abbess Bugga", his kinswoman, and Kylie takes her for the same woman. She had prayed at the holy places of Rome, the king said, and had met Boniface there. She came home to "the monastery of consecrated women, which formerly she governed well under the law of the Church".

Bregwin, archbishop of Canterbury, later wrote to Lul, who followed Boniface at Mainz. He and his church kept the burial day of an abbess named Bugga, 27 December. While she lived, she had asked that Mainz be told and keep it too, since Boniface had been her father in Christ. The Bollandists, editors of the Acta Sanctorum, report a belief that Bugga was Abbess Eadburga under another name, with the same death day. Kylie treats them as two women.

[warmly] In her letter of thanks Bugga had told Boniface that "no temporal vicissitudes can move my mind from its steady guardianship of thy love."
