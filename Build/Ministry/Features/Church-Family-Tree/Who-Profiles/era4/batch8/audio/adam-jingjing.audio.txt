[calmly] "Behold the unchangeably true and invisible, who existed through all eternity without origin." So begins the Chinese text of a stone set up in China in 781. It praises "our eternal true lord God, triune and mysterious in substance". He "appointed the cross as the means for determining the four cardinal points".

The stone is the Xi'an stele, raised by the Church of the East in Tang China. In A. Wylie's English, its text names its author: "Composed by King-Tsing, A Priest of the Syrian Church." Henri Cordier, in his notes to Henry Yule's Marco Polo, joins this Chinese name to the Syriac name Adam. Apart from one note of Cordier's, everything the sources say of Adam comes from the stone.

His text sets out his church's faith in Chinese words. The Messiah was born of a virgin in Syria, it says, and "Twenty-seven sacred books" were left. Of the church's people he wrote that "they do not keep slaves, but put noble and mean all on an equality." Then the text tells how Alopen, a man from Syria, brought the books to the capital, Chang-an, in 635, and how emperors honored him.

Lines of Syriac run down the sides of the stone. Joseph Assemani, writing in 1728, reads one as naming Adam priest and chorepiscopus. Athanasius Kircher, who published the stone in Latin, had rendered chorepiscopus once as archbishop and once as a bishop's vicar. Once he also made Adam a deacon, which Assemani calls a misreading. Henry Yule calls him "Bishop and Pope" of China.

The stone names a second Adam, a deacon, son of the priest Jazedbuzid. In the Syriac, as Assemani reads it and as Malech gives Kircher's reading, Jazedbuzid set the stone up. Cordier writes that King-tsing erected it.

Cordier adds that King-tsing appears also to have helped a Buddhist translate a Buddhist sutra, under Te-Tsung, the emperor of the stone's day. He takes this from a paper by J. Takakusu.

No source gives Adam's birth, his death or where he came from.
