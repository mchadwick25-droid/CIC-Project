[calmly] "Behold the unchangeably true and invisible, who existed through all eternity without origin." So begins the Chinese text of a stone set up in China in 781. It praises "our eternal true lord God, triune and mysterious in substance," who "appointed the cross as the means for determining the four cardinal points".

The stone is the Xi'an stele, raised by the Church of the East in Tang China. In A. Wylie's English, its text names its author: "Composed by King-Tsing, A Priest of the Syrian Church." Henri Cordier, in his notes to Henry Yule's Marco Polo, names him as Adam, with King-tsing beside it. Apart from one note of Cordier's, everything the held sources say of Adam comes from the stone.

His text sets out his church's faith in Chinese words. The Messiah was born of a virgin in Syria, it says, and "Twenty-seven sacred books" were left. It tells of baptism and the cross, and of the church's ways. Of its people he wrote that "they do not keep slaves, but put noble and mean all on an equality." Then it tells how Alopen, a man from Syria, brought the books to the capital, Chang-an, in 635. Emperors honored him and built churches. It closes with praise of the priest I-sz', who repaired old churches, clothed the naked and buried the dead.

Lines of Syriac run down the sides of the stone. Joseph Assemani, writing in 1728, reads one as naming Adam priest and chorepiscopus, and papas of China. Athanasius Kircher had rendered chorepiscopus once as archbishop and once as a bishop's vicar. In one version Kircher also made Adam a deacon, which Assemani calls a misreading. Henry Yule calls him "Bishop and Pope" of China. The stone names a second Adam too, a deacon, son of the priest Jazedbuzid, who set the stone up.

Cordier adds one thing more. It appears, he writes, that King-tsing also helped a Buddhist translate a Buddhist sutra, under the same emperor. He takes this from a paper by J. Takakusu.

No held source gives Adam's birth, his death or where he came from.
