[calmly] On the Monday before Pentecost in 966, a crowd attacked the Church of the Resurrection in Jerusalem. The governor of Jerusalem had demanded more gifts from the patriarch John than custom allowed. John paid at first, but refused a new demand at Pentecost and shut himself in the church. The governor's followers burned its doors, and its dome fell in. The next day they found the patriarch hiding in an oil cistern in the church. They killed him, tied his body to a pillar and burned it.

Yahya ibn Said of Antioch tells this story. I. Kratchkovsky and A. Vasiliev, who edited his history in 1924, call him an Arab Christian historian of the eleventh century. He wrote his book as a sequel to the history of Sa'id ibn Bitriq, patriarch of Alexandria, also called Eutychius. Sa'id's history stops in the Arab year 326, which is 937 to 938, and Yahya takes up the story there.

His preface sets out his plan. He would record every account of past times that reached him and seemed sound, down to his own day. He wrote for someone he does not name, who had asked him for the book.

Later, he says, chronicles he had not known came into his hands, so he rewrote the whole book. Then he moved to Antioch, in the Arab year 405, which is 1014 to 1015. There he found more chronicles and revised part of it again. He set this down so that, if other copies turned up that differed, the reason would be known.

His preface is all the sources used here give of his life. None gives his birth or his death.

Frédéric Macler, translating the Armenian historian Stepanos of Taron, quotes Gustave Schlumberger's history of Byzantium in that age. Schlumberger says the eastern historians, Yahya among them, are almost always very exactly informed about Asia, though much less about the distant war in Bulgaria. Yet only lately, he writes, has Yahya's chronicle shown that the emperor Basil II's Bulgarian war lasted four whole years.

[warmly] Yahya promised a middle way, neither too long nor too brief. Minds seek most eagerly, he wrote, to know the events closest to their own time.
