Boniface of Mainz, the English missionary in Germany, wrote to an abbess with a request. He asked her "to copy in gold the Epistles of my lord, Saint Peter". The Scriptures would then be honored, he wrote, "when the preacher holds them before the eyes of the heathen." She had often eased his distress already, he added, with gifts of books and vestments.

The abbess was Eadburga, who lived in the eighth century. Edward Kylie, who put Boniface's letters into English in 1911, heads the letters to her as letters to the abbess of Thanet. He dates this one to 735. None of her own letters is in his book. She is known from what others wrote to her and about her.

Boniface wrote to her over many years. About 717, by Kylie's date, he sent her an account she had asked for. A man in the convent of Abbess Milburga at Wenlock had died and come back to life. Boniface wrote down the vision the man told him. Later he thanked her for books of Scripture, which had consoled "an exile in Germany". In a hard time he asked for her prayers. The snares of false brethren, he wrote, "surpass the malice of the heathen."

Leoba, a young woman of Boniface's circle, learned verse from her. She sent Boniface four lines of her own. "The art I have learned from the teaching of Eadburga," Leoba wrote, "who continues without ceasing to search into the divine law." Kylie says Leoba had been sent to Thanet, where Eadburga taught her the art of verse. Lul, Boniface's deacon, wrote to Eadburga too. He sent her a silver stylus, storax and cinnamon, and asked for her prayers.

The Bollandists, editors of the Acta Sanctorum, report a belief that she was an abbess in Kent also called Bugga. Kylie keeps the two women apart. His Bugga is the daughter of another abbess, Eangyth, and her own name was Heaburg.

Boniface opened one letter to her this way: "To the Abbess Eadburga, beloved sister, now long bound to him in the kinship of spiritual love."
