In Jerusalem, Theodore Abu Qurrah met David, a Jacobite, at the holy places, both there to pray. David's church taught that Christ has one nature. He asked how the council of Chalcedon could call Christ two natures without making him two persons. Theodore answered in an Arabic letter, whose heading says David became orthodox.

Theodore, a Chalcedonian Christian under Muslim rule, is called bishop of Harran in most of his Arabic works. Georg Graf, a German priest who translated those works in 1910, calls him the first Christian author yet known to write in Arabic.

Graf places his life roughly between 740 and 820, and his birth at Edessa, which Theodore calls our city. Copies of his Arabic works all trace back to Mar Saba, near Jerusalem, so Graf holds that he lived in that monastery. Michel Le Quien, who edited John of Damascus in 1712, made Theodore John's pupil. Graf finds no direct proof.

A rival of his own day, the Jacobite Abu Ra'ita of Takrit, was summoned to debate him. Held captive, Abu Ra'ita sent his kinsman, the deacon Elian, to read his letter aloud three times before the debate. He calls Theodore the philosopher, and accuses him of secretly following Nestorius, whom he calls a false teacher.

Thomas, patriarch of Jerusalem, sent the Armenians a letter. Its Greek heading says Theodore dictated it in Arabic, and Michael the Syncellus, the patriarch's secretary, put it into Greek. Graf dates it to 811 to 813.

Michael the Syrian, a Jacobite chronicler who died in 1199, tells of Theodore in Armenia. We have his account only in Graf's German. Michael says the young archdeacon Nonnus of Nisibis beat him in debate. Graf calls Michael partisan and rejects the defeat.

Theodore also defended honoring images, for Christians mocked as idol-worshippers by those who claim a book revealed by God. He wrote for a bishop, Janah. In Graf's German, the close asks Janah to pray that Christ make him an image of Christ, seen in his soul as in a mirror.
