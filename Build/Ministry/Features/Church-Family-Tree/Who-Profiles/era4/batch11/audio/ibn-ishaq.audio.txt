[calmly] The Muslims who had fled Mecca stood before the Negus, the Christian king of Abyssinia, with his bishops and their books around him. Asked about Jesus, their spokesman Ja'far said he was God's servant and messenger, his spirit and his word, given to the virgin Mary. The king picked a twig off the ground. Jesus, son of Mary, he said, is not one twig more than that. He told the exiles they were safe in his land.

Ibn Ishaq tells this in his life of Muhammad, as Umm Salama, one of the exiles, remembered it. He was a Muslim collector of traditions. Gustav Weil, who put the book into German in 1864, says he lived in the first half of Islam's second century. Philip Hitti, a historian writing in 1916, says he wrote it for the caliph al-Mansur and died in 767. A note by B. T. A. Evetts, in 1895, gives 768.

The book survives only in a later reworking by Ibn Hisham. Weil says the wording was kept. Ibn Hisham himself says he left out things ugly to tell or hurtful to some.

Ibn Ishaq says Christianity first took root among the Arabs at Najran. In one tradition, from Wahb ibn Munabbih, a devout carpenter named Faymiyun, sold there as a slave, converted the town. He gives a second account and says God knows which is right. He tells how Dhu Nuwas, a Jewish king of Yemen, killed about 20,000 Christians in a trench. When Christians from Najran came to debate at Medina, he says they held, like all Christians, that Jesus is God, a son of God and the third of three.

[warmly] Weil calls him an excellent and reliable collector, whom envy and sectarian hatred tried to discredit. Weil adds that much he tells as fact belongs to legend. Axel Moberg, who edited a Syriac account of the Najran martyrs in 1924, could not see in the Arabic stories of the persecution "anything that suggests an independent Muhammadan tradition of historical value."
