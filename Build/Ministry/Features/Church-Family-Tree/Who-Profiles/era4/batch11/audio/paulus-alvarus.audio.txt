[calmly] Eulogius of Cordoba once went to the house of his teacher Alvarus. He found a Christian named Aurelius there, asking how to seek martyrdom. Alvarus told him first to weigh his strength, and whether he could bear the coming tortures rather than flee them. Eulogius, his closest friend, tells this in his Memorial of the Saints.

Paulus Alvarus was a Christian layman of Cordoba in the 800s, when Muslim emirs ruled the city. As boys, he writes, he and Eulogius studied under Abbot Speraindeo. They traded letters and rhyming verses of praise, then destroyed them when older. Eulogius rose to the priesthood, while Alvarus says he himself still crawled in the mud of pleasure.

His own life is thin. Nicolás Antonio, a Spanish scholar whose notice opens his works, finds nothing certain about his death. Even his ancestry is disputed. In a letter to Eleazar, a Christian turned Jew, Alvarus calls himself a Hebrew by faith and by race. Antonio accepts that, if the letter is his. In another, Alvarus quotes Virgil on the fearless Getae. Antonio reads that as a claim to be a Goth, perhaps in jest.

In 854 a book called the Luminous Index defended the Christians of Cordoba who offered themselves for martyrdom unasked. It is unsigned, and Antonio gives it to Alvarus from its style and date. Its author grieves that young Christians read Arabic books eagerly. Hardly one Christian in a thousand, he says, can write a decent letter to a brother.

Eulogius was beheaded on a Saturday, 11 March, in 859 by the reckoning of Ambrosio de Morales, his editor in the 1500s. Alvarus wrote his Life for the yearly feast, promising to tell only what he had seen and tested. The Christians of Cordoba, he writes, bought back the head and buried the body at St Zoilus.

[warmly] At its end he turns to his dead friend and asks him to hear his Alvarus crying out. Eulogius used to say, he recalls, that Alvarus was no other than Eulogius.
