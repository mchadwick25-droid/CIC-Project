[calmly] At the monastery of Khladsor, in the district of Derjan, the old abbot Peter spent his nights with his arms stretched out, weeping and groaning. Under his successor Basil, the brothers ate one meal a day. In Lent they drank cold water only on Saturday and Sunday. Stepanos of Taron stayed with them during Lent. There, he writes, he set down these few notes, marking the dates as well as he could.

Stepanos was an Armenian historian, also called Asoghik. He wrote a Universal History in three books at the order of the catholicos Sargis, head of the Armenian church. He finished it in the Armenian year 453. Frédéric Macler, who put its third book into French in 1917, reckons that as 1004 to 1005. Macler calls him the historian of the Bagratid kings who ruled at Ani.

His own life is thin. Macler warns that it is better to stay in near-ignorance than to fill it with bold guesses. The name Taronetsi says he came from Taron.

He writes that as a young man he saw some of the great teachers in their old age, among them Anania of Narek, and tasted the sweet food of their words. He records their ruling that people baptized in the faith of Chalcedon must be baptized again. The Chalcedonians, he says, deny that God suffered death in the body. His own church confesses that God the Word, united to a human body, suffered and was crucified for us.

Macler calls him the first Armenian writer whose dates can be trusted. No early source gives his own death. A manuscript in the Mekhitarist library at Venice, reported by Alishan, says the father of the monastery, Stephen, also Asoghik, died when the Persians took Kars. Macler fits that to 1021.

[warmly] At the end of the History, Stepanos asks his readers to remember him in their prayers, so that he too might be found worthy to sing the Trinity's praise.
