[calmly] At the monastery of Khladsor, in the district of Derjan, the old abbot Peter spent his nights with his arms stretched out, weeping and groaning. Under his successor Basil, the brothers ate one meal a day. In Lent they drank water only on Saturday and Sunday. Stepanos of Taron spent a Lent with them. There, he writes, he set down these few notes, marking the dates as well as he could.

Stepanos was an Armenian historian, also called Asoghik. He wrote a Universal History in three books at the order of the catholicos Sargis, head of the Armenian church. He finished it in the Armenian year 453. Frédéric Macler, who put its third book into French in 1917, reckons that as 1004 to 1005. Macler calls him the historian of the Bagratid kings at Ani, and the first Armenian writer whose dates can be trusted.

His own life is thin. Macler warns that it is better to stay in near-ignorance than to fill the gaps with bold guesses. J.-B. Emin, his Russian translator, took the name Taronetsi to mean he came from the district of Taron.

In his youth, Stepanos writes, he saw some great teachers in their old age. Among them was Anania of Narek. Stepanos tasted the sweet food of their words. He records their ruling that people baptized in the faith of Chalcedon must be baptized again. The Chalcedonians, he says, deny that God suffered death in the body. His own church confesses that God the Word, united to a human body, suffered and was crucified for us.

Marie-Félicité Brosset, who put an Armenian chronicle into French, wrote in 1876 that no one knows when Stepanos died. Ghevond Alishan, an Armenian scholar, reported a manuscript at Venice. It says the father of the monastery, Stephen, also Asoghik, died when the Persians took Kars. Macler fits that to 1021.

[warmly] Stepanos ends his History by asking readers to remember him in their prayers. Then he too might be found worthy to sing the Trinity's praise.
