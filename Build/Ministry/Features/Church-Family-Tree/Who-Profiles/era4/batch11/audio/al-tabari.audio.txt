[calmly] The last Persian king, Yazdegerd, lay dead in the river at Merv. In one telling al-Tabari gives, a miller had killed him in his sleep. Iliya, the metropolitan, or senior bishop, of Merv, gathered the Christians. He reminded them that the king's grandmother Shirin had been a believer and kind to them. They took the body from the river and carried its coffin on their shoulders to a tomb Iliya had built.

Al-Tabari was a Muslim chronicler of Baghdad. F. K. Ginzel, an astronomer writing for the Berlin Academy in 1887, gives his life as the years 224 to 310 of the Muslim calendar. Philip Hitti, who put al-Baladhuri into English in 1916, says he traveled in Egypt, Syria and Iraq to study, and died in Baghdad.

His annals tell how Muslim armies met Christians two centuries before his birth. At Hira in Iraq, the commander Khalid ibn al-Walid's men broke into houses and monasteries. Priests and monks shouted to the men in the castles that they alone were getting them killed. The leaders came out and agreed to pay the jizya, the tax on non-Muslims.

For Aelia, his name for Jerusalem, he gives the caliph Umar's terms. They promised the people safety for life, goods, churches and crosses. No church was to be lived in or made smaller, and no one forced in religion. The Leiden editors filled part of this text from later writers. Their leader, M. J. de Goeje, suspected some words were not genuine.

Like many of his conquest reports, the Hira story reaches him from an earlier teller, Sayf ibn Umar. He often sets versions side by side, as with three accounts of the king's burial. Ginzel says he is counted the most reliable of the Muslim annalists.

[warmly] Hitti says Arab historians did little to weigh or test their reports. He writes that al-Tabari knew many readers might object to some of his. His preface answers them, in Hitti's English. "We only transmit to others what has been transmitted to us."
