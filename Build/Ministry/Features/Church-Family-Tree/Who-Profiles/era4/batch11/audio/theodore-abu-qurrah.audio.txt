[calmly] In Jerusalem, Theodore Abu Qurrah met David, a Jacobite, at the holy places. David's church taught that Christ has one nature. He asked how the council of Chalcedon could call Christ two natures without making him two persons. Theodore answered in an Arabic letter, whose heading says David became orthodox.

Theodore, a Chalcedonian Christian under Muslim rule, is called bishop of Harran in most of his Arabic works. Georg Graf, a German priest who translated them in 1910, calls him the first Christian author yet known to write in Arabic.

Graf places his life at roughly 740 to 820, and his birth at Edessa, which Theodore calls our city. Copies of his Arabic works all trace back to Mar Saba, near Jerusalem, so Graf holds Theodore lived there. Michel Le Quien, who edited John of Damascus in 1712, made Theodore John's pupil. Graf finds no direct proof.

A rival of his own day, the Jacobite Abu Ra'ita of Takrit, was summoned to debate him. Held captive, Abu Ra'ita sent his kinsman, the deacon Elian, to read his letter aloud three times before the debate. Abu Ra'ita calls Theodore the philosopher, and a secret follower of Nestorius, whom he calls a false teacher.

Thomas, patriarch of Jerusalem, sent the Armenians a letter. Its Greek heading says Theodore dictated it in Arabic, and Michael the Syncellus, the patriarch's secretary, put it into Greek. Graf dates it 811 to 813.

Michael the Syrian, a Jacobite chronicler who died in 1199, tells of Theodore in Armenia. Graf gives Michael's account in German. Michael says the young archdeacon Nonnus of Nisibis beat Theodore in debate. Graf calls Michael partisan and rejects the defeat.

[warmly] Theodore also wrote a defense of honoring images, for Christians mocked as idol-worshippers by those who claim a book revealed by God. He wrote it for Janah, whom Graf takes to be a bishop. He closes, in Graf's German, by asking Janah to pray that Christ make him an image of Christ, seen in his soul as in a mirror.
