[calmly] One night at Beth 'Abhe, a monastery near Mosul, the catholicos lay half asleep. He saw a large eagle, "having many eaglets upon his back," fly north from the shrine of the founder, Rabban Jacob. Waking, he learned the head of the monastery had just left with seventy men and Jacob's body. They would not have a school there.

The dreamer was Ishoyahb III of Adiabene, catholicos, the head of the Church of the East. Thomas of Marga tells the story in his history of the house, dated 840 by its translator, E. A. Wallis Budge. Parts of the history, Thomas says, came down by the monastery's tradition. Ishoyahb called the monks back, Thomas says, and built the school in Kuphlana, his home village.

Ishoyahb was bishop of Nineveh, then metropolitan, the senior bishop, of Arbela and Mosul. Thomas says he joined the catholicos Ishoyahb II of Gedhala on a peace mission to the emperor Heraclius. An unnamed monk's chronicle names three other bishops. Its 1903 editor, Ignazio Guidi, dates it about 670 to 680.

Ishoyahb wrote letters against his friend Sahdona, who broke with the church's teaching about Christ. Thomas says Ishoyahb, as catholicos, expelled him. The Chronicle of Seert, an Arabic church history, says the catholicos Maremmeh did.

When Maremmeh died, says Amr ibn Matta, who wrote lives of the patriarchs in the 1300s, the bishops feared Ishoyahb would seize the throne. They left the choice to him. He took their signatures and named himself. In a letter on his election, Ishoyahb calls himself a frail man. Witnesses date his election to 647 to 650, and his death to 657 to 660.

Bar Hebraeus, of the rival Syriac Orthodox church in the 1200s, says Ishoyahb bribed judges to block that church's building at Mosul.

[warmly] One letter says the new Arab rulers honored priests and gave gifts to churches. In it, Ishoyahb blamed Simeon of Rev-Ardashir, in Fars, for cutting India off from new bishops. In Alphonse Mingana's 1926 translation, "this country has since sat in darkness."
