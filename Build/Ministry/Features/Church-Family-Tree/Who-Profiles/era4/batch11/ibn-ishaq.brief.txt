Muslims who had fled Mecca stood before the Negus, the Christian king of Abyssinia, his bishops and their books around him. Next day the king asked them about Jesus. Their spokesman, Ja'far son of Abu Talib, said Jesus was God's servant and messenger, his spirit and his word, given to the virgin Mary. The king picked up a twig. Jesus, he said, is not one twig more than that. He told the exiles they were safe.

Ibn Ishaq, a Muslim collector of traditions, tells this in his life of Muhammad, from Umm Salama, an exile. Gustav Weil, the book's German translator in 1864, places him in the first half of Islam's second century. Philip Hitti, a historian writing in 1916, says he wrote it for the caliph al-Mansur and died in 767. B. T. A. Evetts, editing a book on Egypt's churches in 1895, gives 768 in a note.

The book survives only in Ibn Hisham's reworking, which Weil says kept the wording. Ibn Hisham says he left out things ugly to tell or hurtful to some.

Ibn Ishaq gives two accounts of how Christianity first took root among the Arabs, at Najran. In one, Faymiyun, a devout carpenter sold there as a slave, converted the town. God knows which is right, Ibn Ishaq says. He tells how Dhu Nuwas, a Jewish king of Yemen, killed about 20,000 Christians in a trench. Later, Christians of Najran debated at Medina. Like all Christians, Ibn Ishaq says, they held that Jesus is God, a son of God and the third of three.

Weil says Ibn Ishaq's contemporaries and later scholars held him an excellent, reliable collector, though envy and sectarian hatred tried to discredit him. Weil adds, without blaming him, that some of what he tells as fact is legend. Axel Moberg, who edited a Syriac book on the martyrs in 1924, traces the Arabic stories of the persecution to older Christian accounts. In those stories he could not see "anything that suggests an independent Muhammadan tradition of historical value."
