The raiders demanded money from the monks of Mar Saba. When the monks said they had not even bread enough, the raiders emptied their quivers at them and wounded about thirty. As the raiders smashed the cell doors, the brothers carried the wounded into the nearest cell and pulled out the arrows. Grief, one of them writes, pierced their own hearts no less.

That writer was a monk of Mar Saba, the great laura of Saint Sabas near Jerusalem. A laura is a monastery of separate cells around a church. He says he saw the raid with his own eyes.

His account never gives his name. The Bollandists, the seventeenth-century editors of the Acta Sanctorum, a collection of saints' lives, call him Stephen. Their witness is a Life of another monk of Mar Saba, Stephen the Wonderworker, the nephew of John of Damascus. The Bollandists keep the two men apart, and say the Wonderworker died before the raid.

Stephen wrote at the order of his abbot, Basil, for reading in church, so that the courage of these men would not fade with time. He gives the year as 788. The Bollandists, by their own count of the years, make it 797. A note in their margin has him writing soon after the raid, just after Easter of 797.

Sergius of Damascus fled so that torture would not make him betray where the church's sacred vessels lay hidden. Caught, he refused to go back and was beheaded. Stephen learned Sergius's last words from a brother caught with him. Patricius gave himself up to shield six monks hiding with him. In a cave the raiders filled with smoke, eighteen died. Sergius and an old monk who refused to have his wounded hand cut off made twenty.

The monks were killed for money they did not have. Stephen argues that they were martyrs all the same.

Near the end, he asks the martyrs to remember their unworthy servant, who offers them the first fruits of his speech and sings them hymns.
