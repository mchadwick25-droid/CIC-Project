One night at Beth Abe, a monastery near Mosul, the catholicos lay half asleep. He saw a large eagle, "having many eaglets upon his back," fly north from the shrine where the founder, Rabban Jacob, was buried. On waking he learned that the abbot and seventy monks had just left with Jacob's body. They would not have a school beside their cells.

The dreamer was Ishoyahb III of Adiabene, catholicos, or head, of the Church of the East. Thomas of Marga tells the story in his 840 history of the house. Parts of it rest on legend and house tradition. Ishoyahb called the monks back, Thomas says, and built the school in his home village of Kuphlana.

Ishoyahb was bishop of Nineveh, then metropolitan, the senior bishop, of Arbela and Mosul. Thomas says he joined the catholicos Ishoyahb II of Gedhala on a peace mission to the emperor Heraclius. A chronicle from Khuzistan, written about 660, names three other bishops and leaves him out.

His friend Sahdona broke with the church's teaching about Christ. Ishoyahb wrote letters against him. Thomas says Ishoyahb, as catholicos, drove him out. The Chronicle of Seert, an Arabic church history, says the catholicos Maremmeh did.

When Maremmeh died, says Amr ibn Matta, who wrote lives of the patriarchs in the 1300s, the bishops feared Ishoyahb would seize the throne. They asked him to choose for them. He took their signatures and named himself. In a letter on his election, Ishoyahb calls himself a frail man. The witnesses date his election between 647 and 650, and his death between 657 and 660.

Bar Hebraeus, of the rival Syriac Orthodox church in the 1200s, says Ishoyahb bribed judges to block that church's building at Mosul.

One of his letters says the new Arab rulers honored priests and gave gifts to churches. He blamed Simeon of Rev-Ardashir, the metropolitan in Fars, for cutting India off from new bishops. In Alphonse Mingana's 1926 translation of the letter, "this country has since sat in darkness."
