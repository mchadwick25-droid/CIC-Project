In Rome, an old English bishop stood trial before the pope. Bede of Jarrow says the acts of an earlier synod under Pope Agatho were read aloud at the hearing. His name was found in them. The hearers asked one another "who that Bishop Wilfrid was?" A man who had been a bishop near forty years, they agreed, ought not to be condemned.

He was Wilfrid of Ripon, bishop of York in northern England, who died in 709. Stephen, a priest of his own circle, wrote his Life in Latin, from Wilfrid's side. Bede tells it more briefly.

At fourteen he went to the monks of Lindisfarne. Bede says he judged the way of the Scots, as Bede calls the Irish, not perfect, and set out for Rome. At Lyon in Gaul he wished to die with the bishop who had taken him in. The executioners spared him, Bede says, because he was a stranger, of the English nation. Back in England, Alfrid, son of King Oswy of the Northumbrians, gave him the monastery of Ripon.

At Whitby in 664 he spoke for the Roman Easter. In Bede's account he set the few "in a corner of the remotest island" against "the universal church of Christ throughout the world". King Oswy ruled for Peter, the keeper of heaven's door.

As bishop of York, Stephen says, he built great churches at Ripon and Hexham. In 678, Bede says, Oswy's son King Egfrid drove him from his see. Stephen blames the queen's envy. Wilfrid appealed to Rome, and Stephen says Agatho's synod ordered him restored. Kept out by the king's enmity, Bede says, he preached to the South Saxons, and taught the starving people to fish. Restored after Egfrid's death, he was driven out again five years later and went back to Rome.

He died at Oundle and was buried at Ripon, after forty-five years as bishop, by Bede's count. Stephen says he died as his monks, singing the psalms, reached one verse. It asks God to send forth his spirit and renew the face of the earth.
