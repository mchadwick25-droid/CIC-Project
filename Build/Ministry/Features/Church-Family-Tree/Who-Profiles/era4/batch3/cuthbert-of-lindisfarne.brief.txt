One night a young shepherd kept watch over his flock in the hills. As he prayed, he saw light pour down from the sky, and choirs of angels carry a soul up to heaven. In the morning he learned that Aidan, bishop of Lindisfarne, had died at that hour. He gave the sheep back to their owners and set out for a monastery.

The young man was Cuthbert of Lindisfarne, a monk and bishop in Northumbria, in northern England, in the seventh century. Bede of Jarrow tells this in his Latin prose Life of him. A monk of Lindisfarne wrote an earlier Life, in which the news came some days later. Both are saints' Lives, full of miracles.

He rode up to Melrose, on the river Tweed, with a spear in his hand. The prior Boisil, at the door, said only, in Bede's Latin, "Ecce servus Dei": here is a servant of God.

After the meeting at Whitby in 664, the Scots, as Bede calls the Irish, left Lindisfarne. Eata, abbot of Melrose, became its abbot too, and later moved Cuthbert there to teach the monks' rule. Then Cuthbert went to live alone on the island of Farne. The bank around his cell was so high, Bede says, that he could see nothing but heaven.

A synod at Twyford, under Archbishop Theodore of Tarsus, chose him bishop. When letters failed, King Egfrid of Northumbria crossed to Farne with Bishop Trumwine and the brothers. They knelt and wept until they drew him, weeping too, from his cell.

After two years as bishop he went back to Farne to die. Herefrid, abbot of Lindisfarne, told Bede his last words. Keep peace among yourselves, he said, and have no fellowship with those who keep Easter at the wrong time. He would rather they dug up his bones and left than bowed to schismatics.

Eleven years after his burial, Bede says, the brothers opened his grave. They found his body whole, "more like one asleep than a dead person".
