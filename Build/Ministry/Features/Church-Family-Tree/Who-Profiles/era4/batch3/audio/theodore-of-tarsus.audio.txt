[calmly] Pope Vitalian needed an archbishop for the English. Hadrian, an abbot near Naples, would not take the post, and in the end he put forward a monk he knew. Bede of Jarrow describes this monk, then in Rome, as "born at Tarsus in Cilicia" and learned in Greek and Latin. He was "venerable for age, being sixty-six years old". The pope agreed, but sent Hadrian with him. Hadrian was to see that he did not, "according to the custom of the Greeks, introduce anything contrary to the true faith".

He was Theodore of Tarsus, archbishop of Canterbury in England from 668 until he died in 690. Bede, writing about forty years later, is the main witness. Stephen, biographer of Bishop Wilfrid of York, adds more from Wilfrid's side.

Soon after reaching Canterbury he visited all the English peoples of the island, teaching the way the canons set for keeping Easter. "This was the first archbishop whom all the English church obeyed," Bede writes.

In 673 he called the bishops to Hertford. The acts speak in his own voice: "myself, Theodore, the unworthy bishop of the see of Canterbury". He showed them ten chapters he had marked in a book of canons. They covered Easter, bishops keeping to their own dioceses, and a synod each year.

A book of penances for sinners went under his name. Its compiler calls himself a disciple of the Northumbrians. Most of it, he reports, came from questions the priest Eoda put to Theodore. F. W. H. Wasserschleben, who edited it in 1851, holds that Theodore wrote no such book himself.

In 678, Bede says, King Egfrid drove Wilfrid from York, and Theodore ordained new bishops for his diocese. Stephen says the king and queen won Theodore over with gifts. In Stephen's telling, the old archbishop later confessed that he had wronged Wilfrid.

[warmly] Theodore died in 690, aged eighty-eight. Bede says he had long foretold that age to his friends, from a dream. In Bede's words, the English churches gained more in his time "than ever they had done before."
