[calmly] A writer setting out to tell the life of Saint Patrick began by doubting himself. Many had tried before him, he wrote, and none had found one sure track through the story. He had brought "the infantile row-boat of my feeble brain" into a deep and dangerous ocean. No boat had ventured there, "save only that of my father Cogitosus". He went on: "My skill is small; my authorities are uncertain".

He was Muirchu maccu Machtheni, an Irish writer of the late seventh century. He wrote at the command of Aed, bishop of Sletty. Newport White, who translated the Life in 1920, calls it the oldest Life of Patrick that survives. White dates it before Aed's death in 699, while Louis Duchesne, the French church historian, gives 698.

Of his own life little is told. White makes him a Leinster man, and says he and Aed both signed the acts of a synod at Birr, between 695 and 697. His father's name rests on a correction. Bishop Graves read the Latin words "cognito si" as the name Cogitosus, and White calls this generally accepted. White takes this father for the Cogitosus who wrote the Life of Brigid of Kildare.

Muirchu tells of Patrick's Easter fire. On that night, he says, no one might light a fire before the king's own at the palace of Temoria. Patrick lit one on the plain. The king's magicians warned that unless it was put out that night, "it will not be put out for ever." At last King Loiguire told his elders, "It is better for me to believe than to die."

White says Aed submitted to the claims of Armagh's bishop to head the Irish church. He reads the Life as a tribute from Leinster to Armagh. Professor Bury, in his book on Patrick, held that Muirchu joined two heathen festivals into one by a clever device. R. A. S. Macalister defends Muirchu's account.

[warmly] Muirchu has Patrick die on the 17th of March, after 120 years, "as it is celebrated in all parts of Ireland."
