[calmly] In the margin of a calendar he used, a bishop named Clement Willibrord wrote a short note about himself in Latin. He came over the sea to Francia in 690, he says. In 695 Pope Sergius ordained him bishop in Rome, and he calls himself unworthy of it. Louis Duchesne, editor of the early lives of the popes, prints the note.

Willibrord came from Northumbria, in northern England, and became archbishop of the Frisians, across the sea. Bede of Jarrow wrote of him in about 731, while he was still alive. Alcuin of York wrote his Life, full of miracle stories, more than fifty years after his death, by the reckoning of Froben, its editor.

Alcuin says that as soon as the boy was weaned, his father Wilgis gave him to the brothers of Ripon. He later spent twelve years in Ireland. Bede says he went to preach in Frisia with others, twelve in all.

Bede says Pippin, duke of the Franks, sent him to Pope Sergius, who consecrated him archbishop of the Frisians in 696 and named him Clement. Duchesne trusts the year in Willibrord's own note over Bede's. Pippin gave him a see at a fort that Bede says the French call Utrecht.

Alcuin tells of an island the pagans held sacred to their god Fosite. No one dared touch its cattle, and water was drawn from its spring only in silence. Willibrord baptized three men there and had the cattle killed for food. Radbod, king of the Frisians, cast lots to punish him, and one companion was put to death. Willibrord told the king his god was a devil. Radbod sent him back to Pippin.

[warmly] Alcuin says he built the monastery of Echternach and was buried there after he died on 7 November. He gives no year. In a sermon for his feast, Alcuin names the patron saints of many cities, from Rome to Paris. To the monks Willibrord gathered, he says, God gave no small help in him.
