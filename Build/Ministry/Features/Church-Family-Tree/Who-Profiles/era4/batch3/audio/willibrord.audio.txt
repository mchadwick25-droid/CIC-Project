[calmly] In the margin of a calendar he used, a bishop named Clement Willibrord wrote a short note about himself in Latin. He came over the sea to Francia in 690, he says. In 695 Pope Sergius ordained him bishop in Rome, and he calls himself unworthy of it. He was writing in 728. Louis Duchesne, editor of the early lives of the popes, prints the note.

Willibrord came from Northumbria, in northern England, and became archbishop of the Frisians, across the sea. Bede of Jarrow wrote of him in about 731, while he was still alive. Alcuin of York wrote his Life, full of miracle stories, more than fifty years after his death, by the reckoning of Froben, its editor.

Alcuin says that as soon as the boy was weaned, his father Wilgis gave him to the brothers of Ripon. At about twenty he went to Ireland for twelve years. Bede says Egbert, a priest living there as a stranger, sent him to Frisia, twelve in all. In Alcuin, he decides to go himself.

Bede says Pippin, duke of the Franks, sent him to Pope Sergius, who consecrated him archbishop of the Frisians in 696 and named him Clement. Duchesne trusts the year in Willibrord's own note over Bede's. Pippin gave him a see at a fort that Bede says the French call Utrecht.

Alcuin tells of an island the pagans held sacred to their god Fosite. No one touched its cattle or drew from its spring except in silence. Willibrord baptized three men there and had the cattle killed for food. King Radbod cast lots to punish him, and one companion was put to death. Willibrord told the king his god was a devil. Radbod sent him back to Pippin.

[warmly] Alcuin says he built the monastery of Echternach and was buried there after he died on 7 November. He gives no year. In a sermon for his feast, Alcuin says Rome, Tours and Paris each have their saints. To his own people, he says, God gave no small help in Willibrord.
