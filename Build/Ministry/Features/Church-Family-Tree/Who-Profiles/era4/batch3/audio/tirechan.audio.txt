[calmly] An Irish bishop, writing down the churches of Saint Patrick, stopped to say what moved him. His heart, he wrote in Latin, thought of love for Patrick. He saw deserters, arch-robbers and warriors of Ireland who hated Patrick's paruchia, the churches under his authority, because they had taken what was his. They feared, he said, that if Patrick's heir claimed that paruchia, he could win back almost the whole island.

The bishop was Tirechan. His notes on Patrick are kept in the Book of Armagh, which Ferdomnach, the scribe of Armagh, copied between 807 and 846. Newport White, who translated parts of them in 1920, dates the work no earlier than 664, possibly about 670. Whitley Stokes, who printed the Latin in 1887, takes the latest plagues it mentions to be those of 683, "apparently".

Tirechan tells little of himself. A heading says he wrote from the mouth or the book of Bishop Ultan, whose pupil he was. Tirechan says Ultan fostered him, and that he found four names of Patrick written in a book kept by Ultan. White makes Ultan bishop of Ardbraccan in Meath, and Tirechan a native of Tirawley, in County Mayo.

His book follows Patrick from place to place, naming the churches he founded. White says its purpose was to record what belonged to Armagh, against the claims of rival churches. In it Patrick fasts forty days on Mount Egli, "observing the discipline of Moses, and of Elijah, and of Christ." Two daughters of King Loiguire, Ethne the fair and Fedelm the golden-haired, find the bishops at a well at dawn. "Who is God?" the elder asks. "And where is God?"

Of a magician's stone near Tara he writes, in White's English, "I have seen it with mine own eyes."

[warmly] Louis Duchesne, the French church historian, saw in it little but local traditions, much embellished over two centuries of telling, with Armagh's interests at stake. To White, the book gives the impression of "an honest author who had no gift of composition".
