[calmly] A woman wrote to Boniface of Mainz, the English missionary in Germany, and greeted him as her kinsman. She asked him to pray for her father Dynne, who had died more than eight years before. Her mother Aebbe was still alive and long ill. "I am the only daughter of these my parents," she wrote. She sent a small gift, "that the bond of our love may hold firm for ever." She asked him to "correct the homely style of this letter".

Her name was Leoba, and she lived in the eighth century. Her letter survives among Boniface's letters, in Edward Kylie's English translation of 1911. It is her only letter in his book, and much of the rest of her story is Kylie's own account.

At the end of the letter she added four lines of verse. She had tried, she said, to follow "the rules of poetic art". She learned the art from Eadburga, who, she wrote, never stops searching into the divine law. Kylie calls Eadburga the abbess of Thanet and says Leoba was sent to her there.

Kylie says the friendship led her into Boniface's service, and that she ruled a convent at Bischofsheim. In Kylie's heading, Boniface writes to her as abbess there. The priest Torhthat had her consent for a certain girl to be taught for a time, if Boniface agreed. Whatever she chose, Boniface answered, had his "assent and approval". In another letter he begged Leoba, Tecla, Cynehilda and their sisters to pray that he "may not die barren without some fruit of the gospel".

When Boniface left for Frisia for the last time, Kylie writes, he told her to stay at her work in Germany. She was to be buried beside him. None of the letters in Kylie's book tells of this. The Roman Martyrology, in the Marquess of Bute's English, remembers her as Lioba on 28 September, "eminent for the gift of miracles".

[warmly] Her verses end with a prayer for Boniface: "Keep thee unharmed in His justice eternal."
