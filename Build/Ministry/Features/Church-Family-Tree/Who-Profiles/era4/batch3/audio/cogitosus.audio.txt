[calmly] In the great church of Kildare, in Ireland, two tombs stood on either side of the altar. Brigid lay in one and Bishop Conleath in the other, under crowns of gold and silver hung above them. Painted boards lined the church, and one wall bore pictures. The bishop came to the altar by one door, and the abbess with her virgins and faithful widows by another. So, the writer says, a great people in its ranks prayed with one mind to God.

The writer was Cogitosus, and his Latin Life of Brigid of Kildare is the main source for her. It is a saint's Life, mostly miracles. Migne's collection of the Latin Fathers prints him under the year 591. Basnage, whose note Migne prints, thought him much later. He did not see Brigid alive, Basnage argues, and the painted images he describes belong to later centuries.

The brothers compelled him to write, Cogitosus says, and the task was beyond his small skill. He tells a few things out of many, handed down by elders and the most learned. He has seen miracles at her tomb with his own eyes, he adds.

He makes Brigid's house the head of almost all the Irish churches, with its paruchia reaching across Ireland from sea to sea. Brigid, he says, called a hermit from the wilderness to govern the church with her as bishop. When Cogitosus wrote, an archbishop and an abbess still ruled it in unbroken succession. Kildare, he says, was a city of refuge, where no enemy was feared and kings kept their treasures.

In his Life of Patrick, Muirchu names his own father Cogitosus, on Bishop Graves's correction of the Latin. Newport White, Muirchu's translator, takes that father for Brigid's biographer, which would place him before about 700. Whitley Stokes, in an index, dates Cogitosus between 800 and 835.

[warmly] He signs the Life asking the brothers to pray for "Cogitoso nepote culpabili". Nepos can mean grandson or nephew, and Basnage could not say in what sense he used it.
