A writer setting out to tell the life of Saint Patrick began with an apology for his own skill. Many had tried before him, he wrote, and none had found one sure track of history. He had brought "the infantile row-boat of my feeble brain" into a deep and dangerous ocean. No boat had ventured there, "save only that of my father Cogitosus". He wrote, "My skill is small; my authorities are uncertain".

He was Muirchu maccu Machtheni, an Irish writer of the late seventh century. He wrote at the command of Aedh, bishop of Sletty, in Leinster. Newport White, who translated the Life in 1920, calls it the oldest Life of Patrick that survives. White dates at least the first book before Aedh's death in 699. Louis Duchesne, the French church historian, puts that death in 698.

Muirchu says almost nothing about himself. White says he and Aedh both signed the acts of Adomnán's synod at Birr, between 695 and 697. His father's name rests on a correction. Bishop Graves read the Latin words "cognito si" as the name Cogitosus, and White calls this generally accepted. White takes this father for the Cogitosus who wrote the Life of Brigid of Kildare.

Muirchu tells of Patrick's Easter fire. On that night, he says, no one might light a fire before the king's own at Temoria, which White calls Tara. Patrick lit one on the plain. The king's magicians warned that unless it was put out that night, "it will not be put out for ever." At last King Loiguire told his elders, "It is better for me to believe than to die."

Tirechan, who also wrote on Patrick, says the king was unable to believe, and White finds that account more credible. Aedh had submitted to Armagh's claim to head the Irish church, White says. He thinks the Life was probably a tribute from Leinster to Armagh.

Muirchu has Patrick die on the 17th of March, after 120 years, "as it is celebrated in all parts of Ireland."
