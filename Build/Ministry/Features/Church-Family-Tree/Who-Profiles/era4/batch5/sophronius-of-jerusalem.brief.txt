Stephen, bishop of Dor, told a synod at Rome in 649 how Sophronius had once led him to Calvary in Jerusalem. There, at the place of the cross, Sophronius bound him with an oath. You will answer to God, crucified here for us, he said, if you let his faith be put at risk. He would go himself, he said, but the Saracen invasion held him back. So Stephen was to go from end to end of the earth until he reached the apostolic see.

Sophronius of Jerusalem tells his own beginnings in a short poem, printed in Angelo Mai's Latin. He came from Damascus, and his parents were Myro and Plinthas. He never married, and became a monk in the monastery of Theodosius, near Jerusalem. John Moschus, his teacher, calls him the Sophist, a man of learning.

At Alexandria, still a monk, he opposed the patriarch Cyrus's teaching that Christ acted with one operation, or energy. Sergius, patriarch of Constantinople, wrote that Sophronius held two operations, but could not bring texts of the Fathers that said "two" in those very words. Sergius says it was agreed that he would speak no more of one or two.

He was then made patriarch of Jerusalem. In the letter he sent out on taking office, he wrote how dear quiet was to him now. The clergy, monks and people of the city, he said, had forced him into office like tyrants. In a Christmas sermon he mourned that Bethlehem lay close by, but fear of the Saracen sword kept them from it.

Theophanes, writing in the early ninth century, says Sophronius won from the caliph Umar a pledge of safety for Palestine. When Umar asked to see the temple Solomon built, Theophanes says, Sophronius wept, and called it the abomination of desolation in the holy place. Eutychius, a later patriarch of Alexandria, says he died after four years in office.

The Third Council of Constantinople, in 680 and 681, found his letter true to the faith. It ruled that his name be placed in the diptychs, the lists of names read out in the liturgy.
