When the emperor Heraclius brought the Holy Cross back to Jerusalem, the crowd was so moved that no one could sing the hymns. An Armenian history tells it so. There was weeping and sighing, it says, and a great flame in every heart, from the king and his princes to the people of the city. Heraclius set the Cross back in its place.

The history is known as the History of Heraclius, by Bishop Sebeos. Frédéric Macler put it into French in 1904. He calls Sebeos the only Armenian writer of the seventh century who tells of the first Arab invasions of Armenia. Macler says he wrote in the third quarter of that century, and saw most of what he tells.

Stephen of Taron, an Armenian historian of the tenth century, lists the History of Heraclius as written by Bishop Sebeos. The printed editions place three books under his name. Macler says the first is plainly not his. The second copies Moses of Khoren and also Stephen, whose work, Macler notes, came three centuries after Sebeos. Macler translates only the third, which he takes to be the whole of Sebeos's history. It runs to 661, when the caliph Muawiya came to the throne.

After the Greeks crossed the Jordan to fight the Arabs, Sebeos writes, a terror sent by the Lord seized their army. Macler takes him to see the Arab invasion as God's punishment of the Greeks, for not keeping one faith with the Armenians. Sebeos never says so outright, Macler adds.

For the first Arab wars, Sebeos says who his witnesses were. We learned these things, he writes, from prisoners who came from Arabia and had seen them. Of the conquests he says he will tell the evils that came in our time. A burning, deadly wind blew on us, he writes, and burned the fine young trees of the gardens. And we had deserved it, for we sinned against the Lord.

Near the end, Sebeos says he speaks in vain. He sets out his words by the feeble thought of his mind, he writes, and not by the dignity of learning.
