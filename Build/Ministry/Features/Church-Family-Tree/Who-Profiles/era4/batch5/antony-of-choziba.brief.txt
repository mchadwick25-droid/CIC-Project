A young man left his father's house in secret, with a single companion and none of his family knowing. The two meant to go to the monks of Raithou. Saracens were raiding the public roads, so on the advice of holy fathers they went to the monastery of Choziba instead. There they were received and soon given the monk's tonsure. Later his companion slipped away to Raithou, and he was left grieving.

He tells it himself. He is Antony of Choziba, a monk who lived through the Persian attack on Jerusalem. He wrote the Life of his teacher, George of Choziba, in Greek. The Bollandists edited it in 1888, with a Latin translation. In the Life, George calls him his child Antony, and when dying he asks where Antony is.

When Antony wanted to follow his companion, George took him aside one Sunday. It is not the place they look for in a monk, he said, but the way of life. And not father or mother, kin or friends, but the will. On that great and fearful day, no one would stand by us but each man's deeds.

Antony tells the Persian invasion through George. The Persians besieged the holy city, and some fled to Arabia. In a ravine the soldiers killed abba Stephen the Syrian, an old man of a hundred or more. Seeing George poor, weak and modest, they let him go.

After the invasion the monks settled in the guesthouse at Jericho, and Antony was made cellarer, the keeper of stores. There, he writes, dealing with worldly people and women, he was fiercely tempted. He went in secret to George in the holy city and told him his struggle. George prayed over him, Antony says, and by word and deed healed him and kept him near.

The same volume prints, under his name, miracles of the Mother of God at Choziba. At the end he says he is not wise, nor able in speech. Yet God, rich and generous, did not disdain to pour on him, useless and foolish, this small drop of blessing.
