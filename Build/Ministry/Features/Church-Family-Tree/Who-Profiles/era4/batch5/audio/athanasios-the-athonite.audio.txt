[calmly] The general Nikephoros Phokas was fighting the Arabs of Crete when he sent for a monk from Mount Athos. The monk tells it in the rule he wrote for his monastery. Nikephoros wrote to him many times, and when he would not come, wrote more often still. On the island he begged the monk to build a lavra, a house of monks, where he too could one day become a monk. The monk held back. He was used to living free and alone, he wrote. He thought it enough if he could save himself.

The monk was Athanasios the Athonite. Philipp Meyer, who edited his writings in 1894, tells his early life from a Life written under his successor. Meyer knew it only in a Modern Greek version of the seventeenth century. By that Life, Athanasios came from Trebizond, was orphaned young, and became a teacher in Constantinople. He became a monk under Michael Maleinos, abbot of Mount Kyminas and uncle of Nikephoros. When Michael wanted him as his successor, the Life says, he fled to Athos.

Meyer dates the visit to Crete to 960 or 961. A manuscript cited in the notes to Leo the Deacon's history says Athanasios never went, and only foretold the victory. Meyer calls that a legend.

By Meyer's reckoning, the church was half built when news came, in 963, that Nikephoros had been crowned emperor. Athanasios went to Constantinople, his rule says, and blamed him to his face. From the start, he told him, you planned one thing and promised us another. The emperor swore he would still come to the Mountain. He asked only that the church not be left half finished. He was murdered in 969, and Athanasios called his death a martyr's end.

Nikephoros's charter, which Athanasios quotes in his rule, made the house self-governing, under its own abbot. Later, the Life says, monks of Athos accused Athanasios of changing the Mountain's ways. A ruling of 972 by the emperor John Tzimiskes records their complaint, and Athanasios signed it as abbot of the greatest lavra. Meyer says his rule became the standard for the other houses of Athos.

The Life says he died with six of his monks when a dome they were finishing fell in. Meyer puts it between 997 and 1011.

[warmly] In his testament he wrote that he had spent all his days in great sorrow, knowing his weak soul unfit to lead others.
