[calmly] In the Arab year 217, a young man came to the monastery of Beth 'Abhe. He began asking the old monks about the holy men who had lived there. He tells it himself. By reason of the fervor that burned in him, he writes, he made inquiries about each of them from the old men he found there. Of one holy man he says he "did not know that I should become the historian" of his deeds, and so had not asked more.

He was Thomas of Marga, a monk of the Church of the East. E. A. Wallis Budge, who translated his Book of Governors in 1893, reckons his arrival as 832. In 837, Budge says, he was secretary to the catholicos Abraham, the head of his church. Thomas recalls copying letters "before the Patriarchal throne" in the days of his youth. Budge says Abraham made him bishop of Marga, and that he later became metropolitan of Beth Garmai.

A monk named 'Abhd-Isho' had urged him for many years to write. Thomas says he would not, for two reasons. Writing histories is severe labor, and it keeps a man from his daily work. In the end he wrote the histories of the holy men and monks of Beth 'Abhe. He names his sources. Among them are an old deacon of Shalmath, the letters of the patriarch Timothy I, and Abraham himself, who "related to me fully" one story. His stories, he writes, are "not vain imaginations of my own".

Budge's title dates the book to 840. Jérôme Labourt, the French historian, says he wrote between 832 and 850.

Mari ibn Sulayman, a later historian of that church, tells of Thomas in his life of the patriarch Theodosius. The caliph al-Mutawakkil held the patriarch in prison for three years and six months. All that time, Mari says, his brother Thomas, metropolitan of Beth Garmai, stood by him and cared for him.

[warmly] Thomas pictured his work as a harvest. He would go into the gardens of their deeds as into a field, he wrote, and gather "the choice and well-filled ears of corn" into one sheaf.
