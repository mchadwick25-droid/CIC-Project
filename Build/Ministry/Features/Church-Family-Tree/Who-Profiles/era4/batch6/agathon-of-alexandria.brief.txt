Under the emperor Heraclius, a priest of Alexandria went about the city at night in a layman's clothes. He comforted the faithful who were in hiding and gave them the sacrament. By day he carried a basket of carpenter's tools on his shoulder. He passed for a carpenter, so that his opponents would not stop him at the doors of the faithful. He kept this up for ten years, until the Muslims came.

So says the History of the Patriarchs of Alexandria, in its life of the patriarch Benjamin. The History is a later Arabic history of the Coptic church. The priest was Agathon of Alexandria, a native of Mareotis. When Benjamin came back to his see, he took Agathon as his son in governing the church. Agathon later followed him as patriarch. B. Evetts, who translated the History, heads his life 661 to 677.

As patriarch, Agathon bought captives whom Muslim raiders had brought from Sicily, and set them free, the History says.

Then Theodore, a Chalcedonian, paid Yazid, son of Muawiyah, the Muslim ruler at Damascus, for power over Alexandria and Mareotis. The Chalcedonians held to the council of Chalcedon, which Agathon's church refused. Theodore ordered that anyone who saw "the pope of the Theodosians" going out, by night or by day, might stone him to death. The Theodosians were the History's name for Agathon's own church. Agathon stayed hidden in his cell and prayed for Theodore, as the Gospel bids.

Agathon died on the 16th of the Coptic month Babah, after seventeen years, the History says. The Ethiopic Synaxarium, a book of saints' days, gives nineteen. His body was laid with Benjamin's.

In Benjamin's life, Agathon tells in his own voice how Benjamin consecrated a church of Saint Macarius in the desert. Eusèbe Renaudot was a Catholic who wrote a Latin history of these patriarchs in 1713. He calls their line heretical, and that account a fable under Agathon's name. Early in the account, Agathon writes: "And as for me, the sinner Agathon, I was the son of the Father Benjamin, and knew much of his virtues through my intimacy with him."
