For years the see of Alexandria had no patriarch. Some bishops had taken gifts from a married official who sought the see. The others prayed, and a priest of the desert named Joseph came to mind. They asked God for a sign, that they would find his door open. They found it open, and they seized him and bound him. "He is worthy, He is worthy," they cried, in E. A. Wallis Budge's English of the Ethiopic Synaxarium, a book of saints' days.

Joseph I of Alexandria, also called Yusab, came from a leading family of Manuf, says the Arabic Synaxarium of the Coptic church. Orphaned, he gave most of his fortune to the poor and became a monk. The History of the Patriarchs, a later Arabic history of the Coptic church, has a life of him. That life is not among the texts used here. The fullest account held is the Latin retelling by Eusèbe Renaudot, a Catholic scholar, in 1713. He dates Joseph's ordination to 831.

In Joseph's time the Bashmurites, Christians of Lower Egypt, rose against the tax collectors. Renaudot says Joseph wrote to tell them that whoever resists authority resists the order of God. The caliph al-Ma'mun came to Egypt with Dionysius of Tel Mahre, patriarch of the Syriac Orthodox church, and sent both patriarchs to the rebels. They failed, and the general Afshin crushed the revolt.

Bar Hebraeus, a thirteenth-century Syriac historian, quotes what Dionysius wrote of Joseph's church. Dionysius found the Copts chaste, sincere and humble. But no one could reach high office without paying two or three hundred gold pieces. When Dionysius rebuked this, Joseph said he had found the abuse already there.

Later two bishops whom Joseph's synod had cut off accused him to the governor. A man came with a sword to kill him. The sword struck a pillar and broke, both Synaxaria and Renaudot say. In the Ethiopic Synaxarium and in Renaudot, Joseph also begged the ruler to spare the two bishops.

He died after nineteen years as patriarch, both Synaxaria say.

The Ethiopic Synaxarium greets him as Joseph of the city of Manuf, "unto whom was given the knowledge of Letters."
