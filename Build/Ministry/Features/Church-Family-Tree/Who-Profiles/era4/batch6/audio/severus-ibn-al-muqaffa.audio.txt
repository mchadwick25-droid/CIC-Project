[calmly] A dog walked past while Severus stood before the chief judge. Was the dog a Christian or a Muslim, someone asked. Ask the dog, Severus said, and it will answer for itself. Today is a fast day, he went on. Christians eat no meat, and in the evening they drink a little wine. Muslims do not fast, and drink no wine. So set meat and wine before the dog. If it eats the meat, it is a Muslim. If it leaves the meat and drinks the wine, it is a Christian.

Eusèbe Renaudot, a Catholic scholar who wrote a Latin history of the Coptic patriarchs in 1713, tells this story. Severus ibn al-Muqaffa was bishop of al-Ushmunain, in Upper Egypt, and was famous, Renaudot says, for his mastery of Arabic. Under the caliph al-Mu'izz, a Jewish courtier asked to debate the patriarch Abraham. The patriarch chose Severus, who said such a debate was beneath the caliph's dignity, and it went no further. Little else of his life is recorded.

His name is joined to the History of the Patriarchs of Alexandria, and a preface in that History speaks in his voice. Severus says he asked Christian brothers to help him translate the histories written in Coptic and Greek into Arabic. Most people in Egypt, he writes, "are ignorant of the Coptic and the Greek."

How much of the History is his is not settled. B. Evetts, its translator, says no one can know how far Severus's work of translation reaches. From the seventh century, Evetts says, its lives were written by men of their own time, such as John the deacon. Renaudot names later writers who carried it on. He also doubts that Severus knew any Greek. Even his century is unsettled. Evetts placed him in the ninth century in 1895, and near the end of the tenth in 1904.

Evetts says he wrote to refute Eutychius, a Melkite patriarch of Alexandria who also wrote history.

[warmly] The preface under his name ends with a prayer: "In thy mercy be not angry with me for that which I have written, but pardon and forgive the faults therein caused by my negligence."
