[calmly] Under the emperor Heraclius, a priest of Alexandria went about the city at night in a layman's clothes. He comforted the faithful who were in hiding and gave them the sacrament. By day he carried a basket of carpenter's tools on his shoulder. He passed for a carpenter, so that no one would stop him at their doors. He kept this up for ten years, until the Muslims came.

So says the History of the Patriarchs of Alexandria, a later Arabic history of the Coptic church, in its life of the patriarch Benjamin. The priest was Agathon of Alexandria, a native of Mareotis. When Benjamin came back to his see, he took Agathon as his son in governing the church, and Agathon followed him as patriarch. B. Evetts, who translated the History, heads his life 661 to 677.

As patriarch, Agathon saw Muslim raiders bring captives from Sicily to be sold in Egypt. The History says he bought them and set them free.

Then a Chalcedonian named Theodore paid Yazid, son of Muawiyah, for power over Alexandria and Mareotis. He made Agathon pay for the sailors of the fleet. Theodore ordered that anyone who saw "the pope of the Theodosians" going out, by night or by day, might stone him to death. The Theodosians were the History's name for Agathon's own church. Agathon stayed hidden in his cell and prayed for Theodore, as the Gospel bids.

A vision told Agathon to send for John, a priest of Samannud, who would follow him. Agathon died on the 16th of the Coptic month Babah, after seventeen years, the History says. His body was laid with Benjamin's.

[warmly] In Benjamin's life, Agathon tells in his own voice how Benjamin consecrated a church of Saint Macarius in the desert. Eusèbe Renaudot, a Catholic scholar who wrote a Latin history of these patriarchs in 1713, calls that account a fable under Agathon's name. Early in it he writes: "And as for me, the sinner Agathon, I was the son of the Father Benjamin, and knew much of his virtues through my intimacy with him."
