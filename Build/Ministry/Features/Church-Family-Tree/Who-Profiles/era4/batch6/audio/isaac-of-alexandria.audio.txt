[calmly] An archbishop wanted a secretary, and he sent for a young monk of the desert of Scetis. Asked to write a letter, the monk wrote it badly on purpose, because he loved solitude. The archbishop saw through it. The second letter came out wonderfully. The monk was let go back to the desert, on condition that he spend a month each year writing the rules for the feasts.

So says the Life of Isaac by Mina, a monk of Saint Macarius who later became a bishop. Isaac of Alexandria followed John III of Samannud as patriarch. B. Evetts, who translated the History of the Patriarchs, heads his life there 686 to 689. That History, a later Arabic history of the Coptic church, gives him a short life. E. Porcher, who edited Mina's Life, says it was written to edify. It is not history as we would write it today, he says.

Mina says Isaac was a clerk before he was a monk. His parents placed him with a kinsman, Meneson, who served George, the governor of Egypt, to train as a secretary. The History says John III called him and made him his partner in governing the church.

When John died, some bishops tried to seat George, a deacon of Sakha, without asking the Amir Abd al-Aziz. Mark, the archdeacon of the city, would not take part unless they waited for Sunday, as the canons require. The Amir's officers found that John had not named George, the History says, and the Amir ordered Isaac appointed.

Isaac wrote to the king of the Abyssinians and the king of the Nubians, bidding them make peace. Slandered, he was summoned by the Amir to be killed. The History says the secretaries had swapped his letters for others, so the Amir found nothing in them. Mina names the kings of Makuria and of a land he calls Maurotania. In Mina, the apostles Peter and Mark walk beside Isaac, and the Amir sees them and is afraid.

[warmly] Mina says Isaac told the Amir that whoever afflicts the Church afflicts God.
