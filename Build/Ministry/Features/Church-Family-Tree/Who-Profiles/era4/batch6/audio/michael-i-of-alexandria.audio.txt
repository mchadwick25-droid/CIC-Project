[calmly] On the bank of the Nile, the caliph Marwan faced the patriarch Michael. "Art thou the chief of the enemies of our religion?" he asked. "I am not the chief of wicked men, but of good men," Michael answered. Marwan had the hair of his beard pulled out and thrown into the river. "I saw it with my own eyes floating on the water," writes John the deacon, who stood near him.

Michael I of Alexandria, also called Khail, was a priest at the church of Saint Macarius. B. Evetts's English of the History of the Patriarchs, a later Arabic history of the Coptic church, sets his years at 744 to 768. Michael's life in it is told by an eyewitness who shared his prisons. He calls himself "John, the sinner," and calls Moses, bishop of Wasim, his spiritual father.

Under the governor Abd al-Malik, son of Musa, Michael was jailed for the taxes on his churches. Letters from Egypt reached Cyriacus, the Christian king of Nubia, while Michael was in prison. The governor then seized Michael again, John says. The king marched north with a hundred thousand horsemen. Michael was freed and wrote the king letters of peace, and the army turned back.

When Marwan came to Egypt, his general seized Michael in Alexandria and demanded money. "There is nothing in my church; therefore I give myself instead of the money," Michael said. Led out to be beheaded, he drew his cap over his face and stretched out his neck. The general spared him. Later he lay in chains at Giza until Marwan was killed.

When the caliph made a bishop patriarch of Antioch, Michael would not accept him. Sword, fire, lions, exile or captivity did not trouble him, he said. He had signed with his own hand a ban "that no bishop shall become patriarch."

He died on the 16th of the Coptic month Barmahat, after twenty-three and a half years, by a record in the library of Saint Macarius.

[warmly] The Arabic Synaxarium is the Coptic church's book of saints' days. In its notice of a bishop who shared his sufferings, it calls Michael a martyr who shed no blood.
