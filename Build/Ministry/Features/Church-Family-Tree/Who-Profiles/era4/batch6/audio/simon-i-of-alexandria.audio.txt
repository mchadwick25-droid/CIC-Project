[calmly] The Amir Abd al-Aziz liked the man the bishops brought him. John, head of a monastery near Alexandria, was handsome, and the Amir agreed to make him patriarch. Then one bishop stood up, says the History of the Patriarchs of Alexandria. "This man shall not be our patriarch," he said. "Simon is worthy of this degree."

Simon was a Syrian, from the East. The Amir asked why they could not choose a man of their own country. "The matter belongs to God, and in the second place to thee," the bishops replied. The Amir had him ordained.

Simon I of Alexandria was patriarch from 689 to 701, in the heading of B. Evetts, who translated the History, a later Arabic history of the Coptic church. The History says Simon's parents had given him to the church as a boy, like Samuel, at John's monastery. John the deacon, who wrote a later life in the History, says Simon's archdeacon George wrote the history of these years.

Simon lived on bread, salt and herbs and kept to himself, and the people of Alexandria hated him for it. Some of his own priests poisoned his cup. It did him no harm, the History says, since he had taken communion first. Then they gave him poisoned figs while he fasted, and he lay forty days in pain. When the Amir ordered the poisoners burned, Simon fell on his face. He could not stay patriarch if they died, he said, and the priests were set free.

A priest came from India asking for a bishop. Simon would not ordain one without the Amir's command. The Gaianites, a rival sect, ordained a man anyway, and the caliph Abd al-Malik had the new bishop's hands and feet cut off. The Amir questioned Simon at night. "If a voice came from heaven, bidding me deviate from the truth, I would say no otherwise," Simon told him.

[warmly] Before he died, he asked to visit the monks of Wadi Habib, in the desert. "I shall not see them again after this time in the body," he said.
