[calmly] In Holy Week, the patriarch John was held at the house of Samad, the governor of a castle. The Amir Abd al-Aziz, governor of Egypt, had handed him over until he paid a hundred thousand denarii. John answered calmly that God's law did not command him to save anything for himself. Samad ordered a brass pan of burning coals brought for his feet, until he promised to pay.

That night, the History of the Patriarchs says, the Amir's wife fell gravely ill. She sent her eunuch to Samad: "Take heed that thou do no harm to that man of God." At cock-crow Samad told the Amir he had not put the patriarch to the torture.

This is John III of Samannud, patriarch of Alexandria after Agathon. B. Evetts, who translated the History, heads his life 677 to 686. The History is a later Arabic history of the Coptic church. The writer of John's life says he was with him at the end, "for he was his spiritual son", but gives no name.

Theodore, a Chalcedonian who had harassed Agathon, soon died of dropsy, the History says. His son became governor and was like a son to John.

In Holy Week Samad kept lowering his price, to ten thousand in the end. "The only things that I can dispose of are my garments which are upon my body," John answered. "I will not promise what I cannot perform." The secretaries of Alexandria offered to share the debt. He was freed on the Thursday of Holy Week, and he washed the feet of the people.

He rebuilt the church of Saint Mark in Alexandria in three years. He built a mill to make biscuit and fed the poor through a famine of three years. Dying, he was carried to the altar of Saint Mark's, where he stood and said the whole prayer of thanksgiving. He died on the first of the Coptic month Kiahk, after nine years.

[warmly] To Samad he had said: "My body is in thy hands, but my soul and body are both in the hands of my Lord Jesus Christ."
