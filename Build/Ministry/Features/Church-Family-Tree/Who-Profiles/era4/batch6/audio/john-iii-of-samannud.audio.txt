[calmly] In Holy Week, the patriarch John was held at the house of Samad, the governor of a castle. The Amir Abd al-Aziz, governor of Egypt, had handed him over until he paid a hundred thousand denarii. John answered calmly that God's law did not command him to save anything for himself. Samad ordered a brass pan of burning coals brought for his feet, until he promised to pay.

That night, the History of the Patriarchs says, the Amir's wife fell gravely ill. She sent her eunuch to Samad: "Take heed that thou do no harm to that man of God." At cock-crow Samad told the Amir he had not put the patriarch to the torture.

Samad came down to fifty thousand. "The only things that I can dispose of are my garments which are upon my body," John answered. Samad kept lowering his price, to ten thousand in the end. "I will not promise what I cannot perform," John said. He was freed on the Thursday of Holy Week, and he washed the feet of the people.

This is John III of Samannud, patriarch of Alexandria after Agathon. B. Evetts, who translated the History, a later Arabic history of the Coptic church, dates his patriarchate 677 to 686. The writer of John's life says he was with him at the end, "for he was his spiritual son", but gives no name.

Theodore, the Chalcedonian governor of Alexandria who had harassed Agathon, died of dropsy, the History says. The Chalcedonians held to the council of Chalcedon, which John's church refused. Theodore's son became governor and was like a son to John.

John rebuilt the church of Saint Mark in Alexandria in three years. He fed the poor through a famine of three years. Dying, he was carried to the altar of Saint Mark's, where he stood and said the whole prayer of thanksgiving. He died on the first of the Coptic month Kiahk, after nine years.

[warmly] To Samad he had said: "My body is in thy hands, but my soul and body are both in the hands of my Lord Jesus Christ."
