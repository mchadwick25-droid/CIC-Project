[calmly] On the feast of John the Baptist's birth in 1054, the monk Niketas stood before the emperor Constantine Monomachos at the Studios monastery in Constantinople. At the urging of the pope's envoys, he condemned a book against the Latin church that bore his name, and the emperor had it burned. The next day, the envoys' own record says, Niketas went to them of his own accord. He took their answers, again condemned every word and act against the see of Rome, and became their close friend.

Niketas, called Stethatos, was a monk and priest of Studios. The compilers of the Philokalia, a Greek collection on prayer, say he lived in 1030 and was the truest disciple of Symeon the New Theologian. He took on his teacher's virtues so fully, they write, that he shone beside him like a second sun.

His own three hundred chapters stand in the Philokalia. Not every tear is compunction, he writes. Tears come from regret for old sins, like boiling water that cleans the heart. Compunction comes down from the Spirit like dew, once the soul has gone deep into humility. Humility brings about the blessed life, he writes elsewhere. Holy compunction is its nurse and mother, the vision of the divine light its friend and sister, and its end the Holy Trinity.

The burned book, which survives in Latin, says the Latins use unleavened bread, which is lifeless, fast on Saturdays, and forbid their priests to marry. Cornelius Will, its editor, suspects the patriarch Michael Cerularius was behind it. The reply printed under the name of Humbert of Silva Candida, one of the envoys, ends by telling Niketas to learn at last to be silent.

Michel Le Quien, John of Damascus's editor, reasons that Niketas wrote against the Latins on the Holy Spirit after the envoys left. A Latin writer, who calls him abbot of Studios, complained that the Greeks still read his name in the liturgy.

[warmly] Unceasing prayer, Niketas writes, is not seen in lifted hands or the sound of the tongue. It is known in the inner work of the mind and the remembrance of God.
