At the end of one July, Elipandus, archbishop of Toledo, received a letter from his friend Felix. He wrote back that it came to him as if fallen from heaven, and that he thanked God with arms stretched out. He was now, he said, in worn-out old age, and had entered his eighty-second year. He still held the teaching for which both men had been condemned.

Felix was bishop of Urgell, a city the Frankish court annals place on a ridge of the Pyrenees. The council of Frankfurt, in 794, condemned the teaching as the heresy of them both, and the sources tell them together, so they share one profile. Much of their story comes from the men who fought them.

Elipandus set out his view in a letter to Alcuin of York. Christ is adoptive, he wrote, only in the form of a servant, his humanity, and not in the glory or substance of his divine nature. As God, the Son is Son by nature, not by grace. Elipandus pointed to the prayers of the Toledo mass, which spoke of the passion of the adoptive man.

His opponents heard two sons in this. Pope Hadrian likened it to Nestorius, who confessed the Son of God a mere man. Alcuin wrote that Felix divided Christ into a proper son and an adoptive one. Elipandus called Alcuin a new Arius. Beatus of Liébana, who wrote against him from Asturias, he called Antiphrasius, as if Beatus, blessed, meant its opposite.

The Frankish annals say Felix was condemned at Regensburg in 792 and renounced his teaching before Pope Hadrian at Rome. In 799 he came to the palace at Aachen, where Alcuin says he was heard, convinced, and returned to the peace of the church. Felix then wrote to his clergy that he came back with no pretence this time, as there had been once, which God knows.

Agobard of Lyon later said Felix kept teaching some of it, in a book known only from the report of Froben, Alcuin's editor. Alcuin says Elipandus still held his view in 800. No source of their own time gives the death of either man.

Elipandus had defended his friend to Alcuin. He had known Felix from early youth, he wrote, as a man of the highest charity, chaste and of good conduct, whom Alcuin now hunted in the mountains and the caves.
