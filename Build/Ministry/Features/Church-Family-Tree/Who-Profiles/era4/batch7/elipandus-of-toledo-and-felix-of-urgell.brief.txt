At the end of one July, Elipandus, archbishop of Toledo, received a letter from his friend Felix. He wrote back that it came to him as if fallen from heaven, and that he thanked God with arms stretched out. He was now, he said, in worn-out old age, and had entered his eighty-second year. He still held the teaching for which both men had been condemned.

Felix was bishop of Urgell, a city the Frankish annals place on a ridge of the Pyrenees. In 794 the council of Frankfurt condemned the teaching as the heresy of them both. The sources tell the two men together, so they share one profile. Much of their story comes from the men who fought them.

Elipandus set out his view in a letter to Alcuin of York. Christ is adoptive, he wrote, only in the form of a servant, his humanity, and not in the glory or substance of his divine nature. As God, the Son is Son by nature, not by grace. Elipandus also cited the Toledo prayers for the mass of the Lord's Supper. As he quoted them, they spoke of the passion of the adoptive man.

His opponents heard two sons in this. Pope Hadrian I likened it to Nestorius, who confessed the Son of God a mere man. Alcuin wrote that Felix divided Christ into a proper son and an adoptive one. Elipandus called Alcuin a new Arius.

The Frankish annals say Felix was condemned at Regensburg in 792 and renounced his teaching before Pope Hadrian at Rome. In 799 he came to the palace at Aachen, where Alcuin says he was heard, proved wrong, and returned to the peace of the church. Felix then wrote to his clergy that he came back with no pretense this time, as there had been once, which God knows.

Froben, Alcuin's editor, reports from a book of Agobard of Lyon that Felix still taught some people his errors, and held to them until he died. In a letter Froben dates to 800, Alcuin says Elipandus still held his view. No source of their own time gives the year either man died.

Elipandus had defended his friend to Alcuin. He had known Felix from early youth, he wrote, as a man of the highest charity, chaste and of good conduct. Yet Alcuin, he wrote, now hunted him in the mountains and the caves.
