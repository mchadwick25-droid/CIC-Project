A dying boy named Augustus, at Mérida in Spain, told a deacon what he had seen. A Lord more beautiful than all the rest, he said, had taken his hand and led him into a lovely garden, where a stream ran with water the color of glass. The deacon questioned him and told his abbot. Then he went to pray at the basilica of Saint Mary, five miles from the city. When he came back at evening, the boy was dead.

The deacon tells this himself, in his Lives of the Fathers of Mérida, written in Latin. He never gives his name in the text. The book's titles call him Paul, a deacon of Mérida, and he is known as Paulus Emeritanus, from Emerita, the city's Latin name. He calls himself only a Levite of Christ, unworthy and the first of sinners. He wrote the boy's words down, he says, in other words but with the very same sense.

The rest of his book tells of the bishops of Mérida. For these older stories he relies, he writes, on the report of many. Paul, a Greek doctor, came from the East and became bishop. His nephew Fidelis followed him. Then came Masona. Paul the deacon calls the Arian king Leovigild most savage and most cruel. He tells how Masona beat the Arian bishop Sunna in open debate. Masona refused to take the Arian faith or to hand over the tunic of Saint Eulalia, and the king sent him into exile.

The book ends with the death of Bishop Renovatus. No line of Paul's gives a year for himself or for his book. Tomás Tamayo de Vargas, who edited it in the 1600s, reasoned that Paul died soon after Renovatus. Renovatus's successor Stephen signed the Fourth Council of Toledo, and Paul does not name him.

At the close Paul asks hard readers to read his little book first and despise it afterward. He wrote, he says, driven by love of Christ and of his lady Eulalia, and told only what is true.
