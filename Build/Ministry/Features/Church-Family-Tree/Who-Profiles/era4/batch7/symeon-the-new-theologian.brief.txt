When Symeon was ordained priest, he saw the Holy Spirit come down on him, simple and formless, as light. In a passage printed with his chapters in the Philokalia, he tells it as another monk's story, with a metropolitan reading the prayer. His disciple Niketas Stethatos, in his Life of Symeon, names the patriarch Nicholas Chrysoberges as his ordainer. The vision came to him at the altar, Niketas says, through forty-eight years as a priest.

Symeon, called the New Theologian, was a monk of Constantinople. He lived about the year 1000, say the compilers of the Philokalia, a Greek collection on prayer. Niketas's Life is known through a long summary by François Combefis, a Dominican scholar.

Symeon came from Galata in Paphlagonia, Niketas says, and served young at the imperial court. Advised by a monk of Studios, Symeon called the Pious, he entered that monastery. When he would not soften his strict life, the superior put him out. His teacher sent him to the house of Saint Mamas, where he later became abbot.

His chapters speak often of light. A man may strip his whole body bare, he writes, but if a veil covers his eyes he still cannot see. So one who gives up everything will never see the spiritual light, Christ, unless he frees the eye of the soul from worldly cares.

Symeon kept a yearly feast for his teacher, with hymns and an icon. The patriarch Sergius read the hymns, Niketas says, and praised them. Sixteen years on, Stephen, once metropolitan of Nicomedia and now the patriarch's aide, began to attack the feast. Niketas puts it down to envy. A synod had the word saint blotted out on the icon, and when Symeon would not stop, he was exiled near Chrysopolis. Recalled, he was offered a high see if he would give way. He refused, and was left free to keep the feast. Sergius said, in words Niketas reports, that Symeon had not erred in the church's teaching.

Michel Le Quien, John of Damascus's editor, called him the father of the Palamite error, named for the later theologian Gregory Palamas. Combefis defended him. Gregory of Sinai tells those who seek stillness to read the New Theologian and his disciple.

Some mocked such claims, Symeon wrote. Who has seen God, they asked. Of those who take up the cross, he answered, many saw God before, and more, he thinks, see him now.
