When the Persians were masters of Egypt, Pisentius, bishop of Coptos, fled to the mountain of Djeme and hid there. "And at that time I John went with him," his disciple writes, "and I was with him in the capacity of a servant." When their water ran out, Pisentius sent John to a vessel they had forgotten. John found the vessels full to the brim, a wonder, he says, "which I myself saw with my own eyes."

John wrote the Life of Pisentius as a sermon for the bishop's feast day. He is also a witness inside it, so the two share this profile. E. A. Wallis Budge translated it in 1913 from a Coptic manuscript copied in 1006. Coptos stood on the Nile in Upper Egypt.

John marks what he only heard. The young Pisentius, "it is said," saw a pillar of fire while he kept his father's sheep. Later, as a monk in his cell, Pisentius recited the whole of Jeremiah and then Ezekiel while his brother waited outside.

When the clergy came to make him bishop, he hid. They found him at last in the region of Djeme. "O the life of peaceful contemplation ! I love it," he cried. As the men who found him told it, John writes, "it was not he who ran in pursuit of the honour, but it was the honour which ran in pursuit of him."

As bishop he gave to the poor in every town and village as far as Syene, John says. He also wrote to his people, warning that God would bring on them "a nation fierce of visage and cruel" unless they repented. The letter names no nation, and Budge takes it to mean the Persians.

He died at sunset on the thirteenth of the month Epep, which Budge reckons as the seventh of July. John gives no year. Pisentius had kept one coin since his years as a monk, to pay for his shroud.

Near the end, John asked how they would go on serving the poor if he left them nothing. Pisentius answered that "whatsoever thou givest — everything — to the poor, the Lord will give unto us twofold."
