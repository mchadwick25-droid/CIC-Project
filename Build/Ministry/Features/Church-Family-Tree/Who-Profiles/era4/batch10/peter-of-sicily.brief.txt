At Tephrike, a Paulician city on the eastern frontier, Peter of Sicily often talked with the people he had come to bargain with. He heard them boast that they would send some of their own to Bulgaria. Counting on the new preaching there, Peter writes, they thought they could sow their weeds among the hidden wheat.

The Paulicians were a sect of the Armenian borderlands, condemned as heretics by the imperial church. Peter's History says the emperor Basil I sent him to exchange prisoners of rank. Peter stayed nine months, and the exchange was made in Basil's second year. J. C. L. Gieseler, who edited the History in 1846, dates the embassy to 868. He says that is all we know of Peter for certain.

Peter wrote his book at the emperor's order and addressed it to the archbishop of the newly converted Bulgarians, without naming him. Gieseler places the writing between 868 and 871.

The History is hostile polemic. Its title calls the Paulicians Manichaeans, after the old heretic Mani. Peter says they believe in two gods, one who made this world and one who rules the world to come. He says they scorn Mary, insult the cross and reject the church's priests. He tells simple believers to flee them as from a snake.

Peter himself reports that the Paulicians curse Mani. Gieseler says Peter is mistaken that they are the same. He also argues that Peter followed a book against them by Photius, patriarch of Constantinople, often in other words. Peter never names him, Gieseler explains, because the emperor hated Photius. Montfaucon, who first printed Photius's book, thought the borrowing ran the other way.

An earlier hostile witness agrees on one charge. John of Odzun, who led the Armenian church in the early 700s, wrote that the Paulicians called honoring the cross and images idolatry.

Franjo Rački, writing in Zagreb in 1869, did not know how far the Paulicians carried out the plan. Their teaching, he wrote, reached Bulgaria all the same.
