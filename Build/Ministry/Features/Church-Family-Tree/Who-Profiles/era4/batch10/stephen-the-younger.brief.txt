On his way to the emperor Constantine V, the monk Stephen asked a devout man for a coin and hid it in his cowl. Before the emperor, his Life says, he held it up and asked whose image it bore. The emperors', said Constantine. If I tread on it, Stephen asked, will I be punished? Certainly, said those standing by. Then what of a man who tramples Christ and his mother in an image? He threw the coin down and trod on it.

Stephen the Younger was a monk of Mount Auxentius, in Bithynia, east of Constantinople. Constantine was trying to remove holy images from worship, the campaign called iconoclasm.

Stephen the Deacon, of Constantinople, wrote the Life 42 years after Stephen's death. He drew on Stephen's disciples and writes as a partisan of the images.

In 754 a council of bishops ruled against the images. Constantine sent the patrician Callistus up the mountain to have Stephen sign the ruling. Stephen refused. Holding out his cupped hand, he said that if he had only that much blood, he would pour it out for the image of Christ.

Constantine exiled him to the island of Proconnesus. After his return and the scene with the coin, Stephen was held in the prison called the Praetorium. There, the Life says, he found 342 monks, some with noses, eyes or hands cut away for refusing to sign.

After eleven months, Constantine cried out at a feast that another man was emperor, Stephen of Auxentius. A crowd ran to the prison. Stephen met them saying, I am the one you seek. They tied ropes to his chained feet and dragged him into the street. A man named Philomates struck him dead with a wooden pole. His body was thrown into the criminals' pit by Saint Pelagius's church.

The Life gives 28 November, in his fifty-third year. Theophanes the Confessor, a chronicler, gives 20 November. The bibliographer Johann Fabricius gives the year 766, and Migne's editors 767. Theophanes, and Patriarch Nikephoros of Constantinople in his Short History, say Stephen was accused of drawing people into the monastic life.

Theophanes calls him the younger protomartyr, after Stephen, the church's first martyr.
