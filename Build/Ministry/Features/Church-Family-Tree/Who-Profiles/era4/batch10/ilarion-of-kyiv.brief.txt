Prince Yaroslav of Kyiv gathered many priests at his church in Berestovoye. Among them, the Russian Primary Chronicle says, was a presbyter named Ilarion, "a virtuous man, learned and ascetic." He would walk to a wooded hill above the Dnieper. There he dug a little cave, two yards deep, to chant the hours and pray in secret.

This was Ilarion of Kyiv. In 1051, the Chronicle says, Yaroslav gathered the bishops and made him metropolitan, the chief bishop of Rus. The Laurentian copy, in Old Russian, calls him a Rusin, a man of Rus. A monk named Antony came home from Mount Athos and settled in Ilarion's cave. The Caves monastery grew from it.

The 1844 Moscow edition of his works gives these passages and says this is all that is known of him. No source used here gives his birth or death. By 1079, its foreword notes, another metropolitan, George, is named.

The chronicler does not say, the foreword notes, why Ilarion was not sent to Constantinople to be consecrated. A. N. Mouravieff, a Russian official writing in 1838, blames Yaroslav's anger at the emperor, who had blinded Rus prisoners. The 1844 foreword holds that Yaroslav did not mean to break with Constantinople.

Ilarion left a confession of faith in his own name. He signs it as a monk and presbyter, made metropolitan in Kyiv in 1051 to be its shepherd and teacher.

The foreword argues that he also wrote the Sermon on Law and Grace. In one manuscript it runs on, in one hand, into the Creed and his confession. It prays for Yaroslav and his wife Irina as living, so the foreword places it before her death in 1050.

The sermon pictures the law as Hagar the slave and grace as Sarah the free woman. The law passed, it says, as moonlight fades when the sun rises. Rome praises Peter and Paul, it says, so let us praise our teacher, Vladimir, Yaroslav's father.

Near its end the sermon calls Vladimir from his tomb. Rise, honored head, it says, and shake off sleep. See your son George, Yaroslav's Christian name. See the city shining in its greatness, the churches in flower, Christianity growing.
