On the feast of the martyr Tryphon, Emperor Leo VI asked Nicholas Mystikos, patriarch of Constantinople, to the yearly palace banquet. During the meal, Nicholas later wrote to Rome, he was seized and put on a boat. He was allowed no change of clothes, no bedding and no book, only two men who could not read. Guards with swords, bows and shields stood over him, and he slept on straw in the winter cold.

By 905, Leo's three wives had died, and Zoe Karbonopsina, his mistress, had borne him a son, Constantine. Church law in the East forbade a fourth marriage.

Nicholas writes that the bishops would baptize the boy only if Leo sent Zoe away, and Leo swore he had. Within three days of the baptism she was back in the palace, honored as an empress. Nicholas says Leo crowned her himself, as bridegroom and priest. The chronicle that carries on from Theophanes the Confessor names a priest, Thomas, who lost his office for blessing them. For this, it says, Nicholas barred Leo from church.

Nicholas writes that he begged Leo to let Zoe live apart while Rome and the other great sees judged. Leo would not part from her for a single day.

Nicholas wrote all this to win the pope over, says Carl de Boor, who edited the other side's account. That account is the Life of Euthymius, who replaced Nicholas as patriarch. It was written in 921 or later. The Life says Leo was handed a treasonous letter from Nicholas to a rebel general. De Boor thinks the charge true in substance, though he grants there is no compelling proof.

By the Life's dates, as de Boor reads them, Nicholas offered in 906 to readmit the emperor to church. He turned him away at Christmas and Epiphany. The banquet and the boat followed on 1 February 907. Legates from Rome allowed the marriage. So, de Boor finds, did legates of the eastern patriarchs.

When Leo died, Nicholas came back and helped rule for the boy Constantine. On his knees before Leo, he had told him that a king is called an unwritten law. His deeds, he said, should match the written law.
