On the feast of the martyr Tryphon, Emperor Leo VI asked Nicholas Mystikos, patriarch of Constantinople, to the yearly palace banquet. During the meal, Nicholas later wrote to Rome, he was seized and put on a boat. He was allowed no change of clothes, no bedding and no book, only two men who could not read. Armed guards stood over him in the winter cold, and he slept on straw.

By 905, Leo's three wives had died, and Zoe Karbonopsina, his mistress, had borne him a son, Constantine. Church law in the East forbade a fourth marriage.

The bishops, Nicholas writes, would baptize the boy only if Leo sent Zoe away. Leo swore he had. Within three days of the baptism she was back in the palace, honored as empress. Nicholas says Leo crowned her himself, as bridegroom and priest.

He begged Leo to let Zoe live apart while Rome and the other great sees judged. Leo would not part from her even for a day.

Nicholas wrote all this to win the pope over, says Carl de Boor, who edited the other side's account, the Life of Euthymius. Euthymius replaced Nicholas as patriarch. The Life says a treasonous letter from Nicholas to a rebel general reached Leo. De Boor thinks the charge true in substance, though he grants there is no compelling proof.

The chronicle that continues Theophanes the Confessor says Nicholas barred Leo from church, and names a priest, Thomas, deposed for blessing the marriage.

By de Boor's reading of the Life, Nicholas offered to readmit the emperor in 906, then turned him away at Christmas and Epiphany. The banquet followed on 1 February. Legates from Rome allowed the marriage. So, de Boor finds, did those of the eastern patriarchs.

When Leo died, Nicholas returned and later helped rule for the boy Constantine. Once, on his knees, he had told Leo that a king is called an unwritten law, whose deeds should match the written law.
