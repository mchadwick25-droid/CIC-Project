[calmly] On his way to the emperor Constantine V, the monk Stephen asked a devout man for a coin and hid it in his cowl. Before the emperor, his Life says, he held it up and asked whose image it bore. The emperors', said Constantine. If I tread on it, Stephen asked, will I be punished? Certainly, said the bystanders. Then what of a man who tramples Christ and his mother in an image? He threw the coin down and trod on it.

Stephen the Younger was a monk of Mount Auxentius, in Bithynia, east of Constantinople. Constantine was then removing holy images from worship, a campaign called iconoclasm.

Stephen the Deacon, of Constantinople, wrote the Life 42 years after Stephen's death. He drew on Stephen's disciples and writes as a partisan of the images.

In 754 a council of bishops ruled against the images, and Constantine sent the patrician Callistus up the mountain to have Stephen sign. Stephen refused. Holding out his cupped hand, he said if he had only that much blood, he would pour it out for the image of Christ.

After exile on the island of Proconnesus and the scene with the coin, Stephen was jailed for trial in the Praetorium. There, the Life says, he found 342 monks, some blinded or with noses or hands cut off, for refusing to sign.

After eleven months, on a feast day, Constantine cried out that another man was emperor, Stephen of Auxentius. A crowd ran to the prison. Stephen met them saying, I am the one you seek. They tied ropes to his chained feet and dragged him into the street. A man named Philomates struck him dead with a wooden pole. His body was thrown into the criminals' pit where Saint Pelagius's church had stood.

Two chroniclers name another charge. Theophanes the Confessor and Patriarch Nikephoros of Constantinople say Stephen was accused of drawing people into the monastic life.

[warmly] Theophanes calls him the younger protomartyr, after Stephen, the church's first martyr.
