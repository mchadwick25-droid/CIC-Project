[calmly] Michael Psellos says he watched the patriarch led away in disgrace, pulled about and mocked. I saw him then, Psellos writes, and wept. The patriarch did not rebuke the men who took him, or chafe at the insult.

The prisoner was Michael Keroularios, patriarch of Constantinople from 1043. Psellos, who praised him in a funeral speech, is a partisan witness. He served the emperor who arrested Michael. Konstantinos Sathas, the speech's editor in 1874, says Psellos was forced to write the charge against him.

Under Emperor Michael IV, Psellos says, plotters chose the future patriarch as emperor without telling him. He was exiled and made a monk. Later, Constantine Monomachos made him patriarch.

Psellos says Michael quarreled with Rome over the Latin teaching that the Holy Spirit comes in some way from the Son.

His letters to Peter, patriarch of Antioch, tell of Rome's envoys. They walked into the palace with a cross and staffs, he writes, and would not greet him or bow. He would not debate them. He was sure they could not be moved, and he would not hold such talks without the other patriarchs.

In July 1054 the envoys, led by Humbert of Silva Candida, laid a sentence on the altar of Hagia Sophia. The hostile sentence curses Michael and his followers unless they repent. His synod of bishops cursed the paper and its makers.

Psellos's history says Michael spoke too boldly to Isaac Komnenos, made emperor by a revolt in 1057. Isaac held his anger, then suddenly sent him out of the city.

Psellos's speech says he was brought from the island of Imbros for trial in Thrace. He died before it, forgiving the emperor and his judges. Sathas says instead he heard the charge in silence before the court, forgave them and fell dead.

[warmly] The envoys, Michael told Peter, said they had come to teach and persuade us to hold their teachings. They had not come to learn, they said, or to debate.
