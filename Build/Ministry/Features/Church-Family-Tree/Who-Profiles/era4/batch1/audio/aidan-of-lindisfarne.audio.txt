[calmly] Bede tells, from report, of a council among the elders of the Scots, as he calls the Irish. King Oswald of Northumbria had asked them for a bishop, but the first man they sent came home calling the English stubborn and barbarous. Aidan told him he had been too severe, and had not first given his hearers "the milk of more easy doctrine". The elders made Aidan a bishop and sent him.

Aidan was a monk of Iona, which Bede calls Hii, and came to the English in the 630s. Oswald gave him a see on Lindisfarne, an island at high tide. Aidan spoke little English, so the king, who had learned the Scots' tongue in exile, interpreted for him. Nearly all we know of Aidan comes from Bede, writing some eighty years later, drawing on those who had known him.

Bede says Aidan taught no otherwise than he lived. He walked through town and country, urging people to believe and to give alms. Gifts from the rich went to the poor, or to free people wrongly sold as slaves. Many of those he freed he taught and made priests. One of the twelve English boys he took to teach, Eata, later became bishop of Lindisfarne.

King Oswin of Deira gave him a fine horse, and Aidan gave it to a beggar. When the king complained, he answered, "Is that foal of a mare more dear to you than the Son of God?" Oswin later knelt for his pardon, and Aidan wept and told his priest the king would not live long. Oswin was killed soon after, and Aidan died twelve days later, on 31 August 651, Bede says.

Aidan kept Easter Sunday by his country's custom, between the fourteenth and twentieth day of the moon. Bede, who followed the Roman Easter, says he detests this. Yet he grants that Aidan always kept it on a Sunday, and that the difference was patiently borne while he lived. Charles Plummer calls it "sad" that Bede pauses to say so.

In a saint's Life of Cuthbert of Lindisfarne, an unnamed Lindisfarne monk says the young shepherd Cuthbert saw a soul carried to heaven. Days later came news that Aidan had died that hour.

[warmly] Bede praises Aidan's love of peace, his authority in rebuking the proud, and his tenderness to the afflicted and the poor. "These things I much love and admire in the aforesaid bishop," he writes.
