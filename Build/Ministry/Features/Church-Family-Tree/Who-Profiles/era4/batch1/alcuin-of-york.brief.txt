Liudger, a Frisian deacon, traveled to York to study under its master, Alcuin. Altfrid, a ninth-century bishop of Münster, tells it in his Life of Saint Liudger. A Frisian merchant killed a local count's son in a quarrel, and the Frisians hurried to leave. Alcuin sent Liudger home with them, Altfrid says, for he would rather die than see his dear son suffer deadly harm.

Alcuin was born in the province of York, of noble English stock, Jean Mabillon writes in his seventeenth-century essay. Mabillon draws on an older Life. He adds that Alcuin was raised from boyhood at York's main church and later ran its school.

In 780, Mabillon writes, Alcuin went to Rome on behalf of Eanbald, the new bishop of York. On the way back, at Parma, he met Charlemagne, who invited him to Francia. Einhard, who wrote Charlemagne's Life, calls Alcuin a deacon from Britain and a man learned in every field. Charlemagne learned rhetoric, logic and astronomy from him, Einhard says, as Mabillon quotes him.

Alcuin wrote that he came at Charlemagne's call. His teacher, he says, had charged him to defend the Catholic faith wherever new sects arose. Felix, bishop of Urgel, taught that Christ in his human nature was God's adopted Son. The Royal Frankish Annals record that teaching condemned at Regensburg in 792 and at Frankfurt in 794. The Frankfurt decrees end with Charlemagne asking the council to take Alcuin into its fellowship and prayers.

Elipandus of Toledo, who had first put the question to Felix, warned Alcuin not to corrupt the king. Alcuin answered that the king could not be corrupted, and that he had come to help him in the faith. He wrote seven books against Felix. In Charlemagne's thirty-second year as king, Alcuin says, Felix was summoned to Aachen, came willingly and confessed the true faith.

In 796, by Mabillon's reckoning, Charlemagne made Alcuin abbot of Saint Martin's at Tours. The older Life, as Mabillon reports it, puts his death in May 804. Mabillon argues instead that Alcuin lived on past 810.

From Tours Alcuin described his teaching to Charlemagne, in a letter Mabillon quotes. To some he served the honey of the Scriptures, and others he plied with the old wine of ancient learning. Some he fed on the apples of grammar. Others he longed to light up with the order of the stars, like the painted roof of a great house.
