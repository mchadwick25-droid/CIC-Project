A Latin history of the Britons begins with its writer's prologue and apology. In J. A. Giles's English, he calls himself "Nennius, disciple of St. Elbotus". He says he gathered "all that I could find" from Roman annals and the chronicles of the sacred fathers. He also drew on the annals of the Scots and Saxons, and on "our ancient traditions". He asks pardon for writing "like a chattering jay".

The book is the Historia Brittonum, and its author is disputed. A heading says it was "edited by Mark the anchorite, a holy bishop of that people". Louis Duchesne, editor of the early lives of the popes, lists a Vatican copy that gives it to Mark. He calls the writer pseudo-Nennius, that is, a false Nennius. Hugh Williams, editor of Gildas, notes that some copies give the book to that older British writer. W. H. Stevenson, editor of Asser's Life of King Alfred, calls Nennius a Welsh writer of the ninth century.

The book's own dates do not agree. The prologue says it was put together in 858, in the twenty-fourth year of Mervin, king of the Britons. Its list of years reaches 976, "the fifth year of Edmund, king of the Angles". Giles notes hardly two manuscripts give that list alike. John Williams ab Ithel, editor of the Welsh annals, dates a first edition to 674, with the genealogies added later.

Giles makes Elbotus the Elvod who was bishop of Bangor in 755 and first brought a new way of fixing Easter into the Welsh church.

The history runs from Adam to the English kings, through the British king Vortigern, Saint Patrick and the bishop Germanus of Auxerre. In this book's account, Arthur led the Britons in twelve battles, though many were more noble than he. At the hill of Badon, it says, "nine hundred and forty fell by his hand alone".

It angered him, he wrote, that his people's name should sink into oblivion. As he put it, "I had rather myself be the historian of the Britons than nobody".
