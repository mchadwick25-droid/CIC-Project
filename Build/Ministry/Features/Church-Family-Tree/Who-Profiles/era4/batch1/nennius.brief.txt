A Latin history of the Britons begins with its writer's prologue and apology. In J. A. Giles's English, he calls himself "Nennius, disciple of St. Elbotus". He says he gathered "all that I could find" from Roman annals, the chronicles of the sacred fathers, the annals of the Scots and Saxons, and "our ancient traditions". He asks pardon for writing "like a chattering jay".

The book is the Historia Brittonum, and its author is disputed. A heading says it was "edited by Mark the anchorite, a holy bishop of that people". Louis Duchesne lists a Vatican copy that gives it to Mark, and calls the writer pseudo-Nennius, as James Raine does. Hugh Williams writes of the book as Nennius's, but notes some copies give it to Gildas. W. H. Stevenson calls Nennius a Welsh writer of the ninth century.

The book's own dates do not agree. The prologue says it was put together in 858, in the twenty-fourth year of Mervin, king of the Britons. Its list of years reaches 976, "the fifth year of Edmund, king of the Angles". Giles notes hardly two manuscripts give that list alike. John Williams ab Ithel, editor of the Welsh annals, dates a first edition to 674, with the genealogies added later.

Giles makes Elbotus the Elvod, bishop of Bangor in 755, who first brought the new Easter cycle into the Welsh church. The Welsh annals put the change of Easter among the Britons in 768, made by Elbodug, whom they call a man of God.

The history runs from Adam to the English kings, through Vortigern, Saint Patrick and the bishop Germanus. In this book's account, Arthur led the Britons in twelve battles, though many were more noble than he. At the hill of Badon, it says, "nine hundred and forty fell by his hand alone".

It angered him, he wrote, that his people's name should sink into oblivion. As he put it, "I had rather myself be the historian of the Britons than nobody".
