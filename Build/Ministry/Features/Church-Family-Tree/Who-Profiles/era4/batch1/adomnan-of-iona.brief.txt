Ceolfrid, abbot of Wearmouth and Jarrow, once asked a guest why his head was shaved like that of the man Saint Peter had cursed. The guest was Adomnán of Iona, his people's envoy to King Aldfrith of Northumbria. He answered that "though I have Simon's tonsure, according to the custom of my country, yet I utterly detest and abhor the Simoniacal wickedness". Ceolfrid, who argued for the Roman tonsure, told the story in a letter that Bede copied into his History.

Wentworth Huyshe, who translated Adomnán's Life of Columba, says he was born in 624 in Donegal, of Columba's kin. By Huyshe's account he became a monk under his kinsman Seghine, abbot of Iona, and in 679 became abbot himself. Huyshe adds that the exiled Aldfrith was called his foster-son.

The Life is a saint's Life of prophecies, miracles and visions. Adomnán asks readers to attend "to the matter rather than to the words, which, as I think, seem uncultured and rude." He names his tellers. Abbot Failbeus, his predecessor, told him how King Oswald of Northumbria saw Columba in a vision before a battle. Adomnán writes that on a visit to "my friend, King Aldfrid," plague was still killing in England, yet none of his company fell ill.

Bede says Adomnán wrote a book on the holy places, from what Arculf, a bishop from Gaul, told him. A storm had driven Arculf onto Britain's west coast on his way home. In its preface, Adomnán says Arculf dictated the account to him. L. Cecil Jane, in notes to Bede, doubts that Adomnán wrote it.

In Aldfrith's kingdom, Bede says, learned men urged Adomnán not to keep an Easter against the custom of the whole Church. He changed his mind, but could not win his monks of Iona. In Ireland he brought many to the Roman Easter. He went home and died before the next year came round. Bede calls him "a good and wise man, and remarkably learned in Holy Scripture."

Newport White, who translated Patrick's writings, says Adomnán held a synod at Birr in the late 690s. A note in the Lebar Brecc, a late medieval Irish manuscript, counts Adomnán's rule among four rules of Ireland, "not to slay women".

At the close of his book on the holy places, Adomnán asks each reader to pray for Arculf. He asks them to pray for him too, the poor sinner who wrote it down.
