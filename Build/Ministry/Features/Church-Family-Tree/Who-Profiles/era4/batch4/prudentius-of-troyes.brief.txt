Wenilo, archbishop of Sens, sent Prudentius nineteen chapters taken from the book of a Scot, John Scotus Eriugena. Wenilo asked him to answer each error he found. Prudentius found the poison of Pelagius in them, he wrote, and the madness of Origen. He trembled, he confessed, to see old errors, long asleep with their authors, sprouting again in his own day.

Prudentius was bishop of Troyes in Frankish Gaul, and he died in 861. Hincmar, archbishop of Reims, names him Galindo, called Prudentius, a Spaniard by birth. In a poem on a Gospel book, Nicolás Antonio reports, Prudentius says he was born in Spain and brought to the land of the Celts.

He kept the royal annals now called the Annals of Saint-Bertin, from the 830s until his death. Georg Waitz, their editor, sets out the abbé Lebeuf's reasons for seeing a Spaniard's hand. Prudentius calls Epiphany the Apparition of the Lord, as Spaniards did. He gives Gaulish cities their old Roman names. He notes eclipses, comets, floods and wolves, and he credits good and bad alike to God.

The great quarrel of his day was over predestination. In 849, the annals record, the monk Gottschalk was flogged in public and made to burn his own books. Prudentius took the side Hincmar opposed. Under 859 he wrote that Pope Nicholas had confirmed the truth of a double predestination. Hincmar complained to Egilo, archbishop of Sens, that he had heard this from no one else and read it nowhere.

After Prudentius died, Hincmar took over the annals. His entry for 861 says Prudentius had once resisted Gottschalk, but later turned in bitterness into a fierce defender of the heresy. Antonio calls this a slander. He says Prudentius never opposed Gottschalk at all.

Troyes, Antonio adds, names him a saint in its lists of bishops.
