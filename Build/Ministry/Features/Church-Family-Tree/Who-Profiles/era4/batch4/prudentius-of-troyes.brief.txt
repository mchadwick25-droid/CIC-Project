Wenilo, archbishop of Sens, sent Prudentius of Troyes nineteen chapters from a book by John Scotus Eriugena, and asked him to answer each error. Prudentius found the poison of Pelagius in them, he wrote, and at times the madness of Origen. He trembled, he confessed, to see old errors, long asleep with their authors, sprouting again in his own day.

Prudentius was bishop of Troyes in Frankish Gaul, and he died in 861. On his death, the annals he had kept name him Galindo, called Prudentius, a Spaniard by birth. In his own verses for a Gospel book, Prudentius calls himself born in Hesperia and brought to the Celts. Nicolás Antonio, a Spanish scholar writing after 1650, takes Hesperia to be Spain.

He kept the annals of the Frankish kings' deeds, now called the Annals of Saint-Bertin, from the 830s until his death. G. H. Pertz, in Waitz's edition, gives the abbé Lebeuf's signs of his hand. Prudentius calls Epiphany the Apparition of the Lord, by the custom of his homeland. He notes eclipses, comets, floods and the ravages of wolves, and he credits good and bad alike to God.

The great quarrel of his day was over predestination. In 849, the annals record, the monk Gottschalk was flogged in public and made to burn his own books. Prudentius took the side Hincmar of Reims opposed. He taught, Antonio says, that God freely chose some for life, and by a justice past searching out destined others for punishment. Under 859 he wrote that Pope Nicholas the First had confirmed a double predestination. Hincmar complained to Egilo, archbishop of Sens, that he had heard this from no one else and read it nowhere.

After Prudentius died, Hincmar took over the annals, Pertz says. The entry for 861 says Prudentius had once resisted Gottschalk, but later turned in bitterness into a fierce defender of the heresy. Antonio calls this a slander, and blames an unnamed annalist on Hincmar's side. He says Prudentius never opposed Gottschalk.

Troyes, Antonio adds, names him a saint in its lists of bishops.
