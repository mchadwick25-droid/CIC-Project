In 882 the Northmen were burning the country around Laon and making for Reims. Its archbishop, Hincmar, fled by night with the body of Saint Remigius and the treasures of his church. He was so weak that he had to be carried in a chair. His canons, monks and nuns scattered, and he barely reached Épernay, beyond the Marne. The city had no wall and no defenders, he wrote. God's power and the merits of the saints kept the raiders out.

His annals end that year. Hincmar was archbishop of Reims in Frankish Gaul, and the held lines show him at work from 849 to 882. His instructions to the priests of his diocese survive, with many of his letters. From 861 he carried on the royal annals that Prudentius of Troyes had kept. Georg Waitz, their editor, says the author is beyond doubt, since only Hincmar could have known some of what they tell. They tell his quarrels from his own side.

He opposed Prudentius over predestination, and his notice of Prudentius's death calls him a defender of heresy.

He also quarreled with his own nephew, also named Hincmar, bishop of Laon. In 868, says Ludovicus Cellotius, the Jesuit who first printed their writings of that year, the uncle still fought for his nephew's church lands. Later the annals call the nephew a bishop in name only, a man of singular insolence, who rebelled against the king and scorned his archbishop. At Douzy in 871 a synod deposed him. A note in Mansi's council volumes says the nephew later told Pope John, in a written complaint, that he had been exiled, chained and blinded. At Troyes in 878, Hincmar wrote, the nephew's supporters led the blind man in his vestments before the pope and into the church, singing.

Waitz calls him deeply versed in church law and in the affairs of his own time. Bruno Krusch, who edited his Life of Saint Remigius, judged him vain and deceitful, and his long sentences painful to read.
