On the feast of the Lord's Passion, Julian of Toledo slipped away to a quiet room with his friend Idalius, bishop of Barcelona. They lay on separate beds and read the Gospel story of the Passion. At one passage, which Julian later could not recall, both men were shaken. They groaned and sighed, and tears came. Idalius was worn by gout, Julian wrote, but lifted far more by the hope of contemplating God.

They began to ask how the souls of the dead fare before the last resurrection. That same day, at Idalius's urging and if he remembered rightly, Julian called a secretary and set down the questions. He wrote the book, the Prognosticon, when the king rode out to war and the crowds left the city.

Julian, a seventh-century bishop of Toledo in Visigothic Spain, tells this in his letter to Idalius at the head of the work. Felix, a later bishop of Toledo who lived in his time, wrote a short Life of him. Felix says Julian was born and baptized at Toledo and taught by its bishop Eugenius.

Julian became bishop after Quiricus. His name stands first among the signatures at four councils of Toledo, from 681 to 688.

At King Ervig's command he wrote On the Proof of the Sixth Age, against Jews of his day. In the opening letter, Julian says they counted the years from Creation. By that count, they said, the world was still in its fifth age, so Christ had not yet come. He says he believes the king follows the surgeon, who cuts away rot before it reaches healthy limbs. That rot, he writes, he would call the tongues of the Jews.

In 688 the bishops met again at Toledo. Pope Benedict, now dead, had raised points against a statement of their faith two years before. One section of their answer bears Julian's name. If their critics still disagreed with the Fathers, they wrote, there was no point in arguing further.

Felix calls him a man full of the fear of the Lord.
