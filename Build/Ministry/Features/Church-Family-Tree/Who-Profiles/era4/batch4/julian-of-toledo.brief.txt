On the feast of the Lord's Passion, Julian of Toledo slipped away to a quiet room with his friend Idalius, bishop of Barcelona. They lay on separate beds and read the Gospel story of the Passion. At one passage, which Julian later could not recall, both men were shaken. They groaned and sighed, and tears came. Idalius was worn by gout, Julian wrote, but lifted far more by the hope of contemplating God.

They began to ask how the souls of the dead fare before the last resurrection. That same day Julian called a secretary and set down the questions. He finished the book, the Prognosticon, when the king rode out to war and the crowds left the city.

Julian, bishop of Toledo in Visigothic Spain, tells this in his letter to Idalius at the head of the work. Felix, a later bishop of Toledo who lived in his time, wrote a short Life of him. Felix says Julian was born and baptized at Toledo and taught by its bishop Eugenius. As a boy he was bound in friendship with a deacon named Gudila. The two wished to become monks, but God judged otherwise, Felix says.

Julian became bishop after Quiricus. His name stands first among the signatures at four councils of Toledo, from 681 to 688. He wrote a history of King Wamba's war against the rebel Paul.

For King Ervig he wrote three books arguing that Christ had already come. In the opening letter he likens the king to a surgeon who cuts away rotting flesh. The rotting limbs, he says, are the tongues of the Jews.

In 688 the bishops at Toledo answered Pope Benedict, who had faulted a statement of their faith. One section of their reply bears Julian's name. If their critics still disagreed with the Fathers, they wrote, there was no point in arguing further.

Felix calls him a man full of the fear of the Lord.
