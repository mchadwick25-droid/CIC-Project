[calmly] In 882 the Northmen were burning the country around Laon and making for Reims. Its archbishop, Hincmar, fled by night with the body of Saint Remigius and the treasures of his church. He was so weak that he had to be carried in a chair. His canons, monks and nuns scattered, and he barely reached Épernay, beyond the Marne. No wall or human hand defended the city, he wrote, but God's power and the merits of the saints kept the raiders out.

His annals end that year, the year he died, Theodor Mommsen says. Hincmar was archbishop of Reims, in Frankish Gaul. A table of dates in Mansi's council volumes puts his ordination in 845. From 861 he carried on the royal annals that Prudentius of Troyes had kept. G. H. Pertz, an earlier editor, held that Hincmar wrote them, and warned that they need care where his own affairs are concerned.

He fought for years with his nephew and namesake, Hincmar, bishop of Laon. The bishops at Douzy wrote that the uncle had raised him as an orphan, taught him his letters and made him a bishop. The annals call the nephew a bishop in name only, a man of singular insolence, who rebelled against the king and scorned his archbishop. At Douzy in 871 the synod deposed him.

The bishops wrote to Pope Hadrian II. If anyone tricked Rome into restoring him, they warned, he would do as he liked, knowing they could not send every case to Rome. The pope had blamed the uncle for leaving his nephew without help. Hincmar answered that he wished he had never seen the man, still less ordained him. Hadrian rejected the sentence, a note in Mansi says, but Pope John VIII later wrote that it was just.

In his own complaint to Pope John, the nephew says he was seized on the road and exiled. He was kept for a time in chains, he says, and after nearly two years he was blinded. At Troyes in 878, the uncle's annals say, the nephew's supporters dressed the blind man in his vestments. Without the pope's order, they led him before the pope and into the church, singing.

[warmly] Pertz calls the archbishop deeply versed in church law and in the affairs of his own time. Bruno Krusch, who edited his Life of Saint Remigius, judged him vain and deceitful, and his long sentences painful to read.
