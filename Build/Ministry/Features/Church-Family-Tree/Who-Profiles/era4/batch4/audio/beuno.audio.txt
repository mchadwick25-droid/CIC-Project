[calmly] Beuno was travelling near a ford on the river Severn when he heard a voice across the water. An Englishman was urging his dogs after a hare, shouting "Cergia". Beuno turned back at once. "My sons, put on your clothes and your shoes, and let us leave this place," he told his disciples. The man's nation "has a strange language, and is abominable", he said, and "they have invaded this place, and it will be theirs".

The story is told in the Welsh Life of Saint Beuno. W. J. Rees printed it in 1853 from two manuscripts, with an English translation. It is a late telling. Rees thought the Lives of the Welsh saints were probably compiled about the twelfth century. The few other accounts of him are late as well.

The Life makes him a Welsh clergyman and a founder of churches. A note in W. J. Rees's edition, quoting a Professor Rees, places his life in the sixth century and into the seventh. The Life says he was born to parents in old age and taught by a saint named Tangusius at Caerwent. Welsh kings and princes gave him land, estate after estate, and he built churches on it.

Cadwallon, king after his father Cadvan, gave him land in Arvon, and Beuno began to build. A woman came to him with a crying baby. The land, she said, belonged to the child's father. Beuno took her to the king. The child "who is in that woman's arms owns that land, and is heir thereof", he said, in W. J. Rees's English. The king refused, and Beuno left him under a curse. A cousin of the king gave Beuno a township called Kelynauc.

The Life also says he raised the girl Winefred from the dead, after a king cut off her head. Where her blood fell, it says, a spring rose that is called Winefred's Well. W. J. Rees places it in Flintshire.

[warmly] The same note says Beuno founded the religious house at Clynog Fawr in 616, and it counts eleven churches dedicated to him.
