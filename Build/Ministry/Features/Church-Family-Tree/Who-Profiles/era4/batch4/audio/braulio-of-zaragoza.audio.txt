[calmly] In seventh-century Visigothic Spain, Braulio, bishop of Zaragoza, was losing his sight when King Chindaswinth sent for his archdeacon, Eugenius. A part of his soul was being taken from him, he wrote, and he begged the king not to part them. The king answered that he trusted this was God's will, and that Eugenius had been born in the city that now needed a bishop. Braulio sent him. The bond between them had burst, he wrote, rather than been cut.

Ildefonsus of Toledo, a later bishop there, tells of a cleric of the royal church named Eugenius. He fled to Zaragoza to live as a monk, until the king's force brought him back to be bishop of Toledo. Ildefonsus does not name Braulio.

Ildefonsus says Braulio followed his brother John as bishop of Zaragoza and served about twenty years, under four kings. He signed at three councils of Toledo in the 630s.

He was the friend and editor of Isidore of Seville. In Ernest Brehaut's English, Braulio says that Isidore left the Etymologies unfinished. Braulio divided it into twenty books, "since he wrote it at my request."

In 638 Braulio wrote to Pope Honorius for the bishops of a council of Toledo. The pope had urged them to act more firmly against the faithless. The council had just praised King Chintila, who would let no one who was not Catholic live in his kingdom. Its canon on the Jews made every later king swear to keep it so. Braulio defended the bishops' slower way. They would bend by Christian coaxing, he wrote, those whom hard discipline could hardly move. The pope's rebuke had called them dumb dogs that cannot bark. That did not fit them at all, Braulio answered.

[warmly] Braulio once teased a priest named Taio, who answered with insults. Braulio could bite back if he wished, he wrote. He would rather follow Christ, who gave his back to the whips and did not curse in return.
