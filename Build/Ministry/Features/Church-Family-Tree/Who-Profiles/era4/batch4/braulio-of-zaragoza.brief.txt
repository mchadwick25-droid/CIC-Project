Braulio, bishop of Zaragoza, was losing his sight when King Chindaswinth sent for his archdeacon, Eugenius. A part of his soul was being taken from him, he wrote to the king, and he begged the king not to part them. The king answered that this was God's will, and that Eugenius had been born in the city that now needed a bishop. Braulio sent him. The bond between them had burst, he wrote, rather than been cut.

Ildefonsus of Toledo, a later bishop there, tells of a cleric of the royal church named Eugenius. He fled to Zaragoza to live as a monk, until the king's force brought him back to be bishop of Toledo.

Braulio lived in Visigothic Spain in the seventh century. Ildefonsus says he followed his brother John as bishop of Zaragoza and held the see for about twenty years, under four kings. His name stands among the signatures of three councils of Toledo in the 630s.

He was the friend and editor of Isidore of Seville. In Ernest Brehaut's English, Braulio says that Isidore left the Etymologies unfinished. Braulio divided it into twenty books, "since he wrote it at my request."

In 638 he wrote for the bishops of Spain to Pope Honorius. The pope had urged them to act more firmly against the faithless. Their council had just praised King Chintila, who would let no one who was not Catholic live in his kingdom. Its canon on the Jews bound every later king to uphold that law. Braulio defended the bishops' slower way. They would bend by Christian coaxing, he wrote, those whom hard discipline could hardly move. He added that the pope's line about dumb dogs that cannot bark came from Isaiah, not Ezekiel.

A priest named Taio once wrote to insult him. Braulio could bite back if he wished, he answered. He would rather follow Christ, who gave his back to the whips and did not curse in return.
