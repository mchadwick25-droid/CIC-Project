On 13 February 1327, Meister Eckhart, a Dominican friar and master of theology, climbed into the pulpit of his order's church in Cologne and preached. Brother Conrad of Halberstadt read out a paper for him. After each article, Eckhart explained it to the people, word for word, in their mother tongue. Heinrich Denifle, a Dominican scholar, printed the notary's record in 1886.

In the paper, Eckhart called God to witness that he had always hated every error in faith, as far as he was able. He publicly took back any error that might be found in his writing or preaching. Of the claim that his little finger created all things, he said he had meant the fingers of the child Jesus.

C. de B. Evans, who put his German sermons into English in the 1920s, writes that there is only "the scantiest material for a biography". Eckhart's name first appears, Evans says, on a list of Paris professors, in 1302.

In 1326, Denifle writes, the archbishop of Cologne opened a case against him. Eckhart answered forty-nine charges in a Latin defense, edited in 1923 by Augustinus Daniels, a Benedictine monk. There Eckhart wrote that he could err but not be a heretic, for error is of the mind and heresy of the will. On 24 January 1327 he appealed to the pope. On 22 February, Master Reyner, one of the judges, refused it in writing as plainly frivolous.

Pope John XXII gave judgment at Avignon on 27 March 1329, in a bull, or papal letter, called In agro dominico. It lists twenty-six articles Eckhart confessed he had preached, taught and written, and two more charged against him. It condemns the first fifteen and the last two as heresy. It calls the other eleven ill-sounding, rash and suspect of heresy, though with much explaining they could bear a Catholic sense.

The bull speaks of his life as already ended. At the end, it says, he professed the Catholic faith and took back the twenty-six articles in any heretical sense. Denifle writes that he died in 1327, having submitted to the pope. Evans holds that he "never made any recantation of his teaching", though he offered to retract any error proved against him.

In Evans's English of a sermon on the eternal birth, Eckhart asks what good it does if the birth does not happen in him. "What matters is that it shall happen in me."
