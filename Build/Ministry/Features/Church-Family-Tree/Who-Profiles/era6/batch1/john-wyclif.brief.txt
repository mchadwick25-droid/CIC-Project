In May 1382, bishops, doctors and friars met at the Black Friars in London to judge the teaching of John Wyclif. He was an Oxford doctor of theology and the parish priest of Lutterworth. Robert Vaughan, an admiring biographer, writing in 1845, says an earthquake shook the city as they began. Some feared Heaven was displeased. William Courtenay, archbishop of Canterbury, took it as a sign that the church would be purged, and twenty-four conclusions were condemned. Walter Shirley, who in 1858 edited a Carmelite collection against Wyclif, says they were drawn from his writings. In his Latin dialogue Trialogus, Wyclif called it the friars' earthquake council, and called his judges the heretics. When Christ's members fail to cry out against them, he wrote, the earth cries out.

Shirley finds Wyclif master of Balliol College at Oxford in 1361. In his book on civil lordship, Wyclif argued that no one in mortal sin has a true right to God's gift. In 1377, Shirley says, Pope Gregory XI sent letters, called bulls, that condemned nineteen of Wyclif's conclusions and ordered his arrest.

In 1381, Shirley says, Wyclif denied transubstantiation, the teaching that the bread no longer remains after the priest's words. In a Latin confession, in Vaughan's English, he called the sacrament "naturally bread and wine, but sacramentally the body and blood of Christ." A Leicester chronicle that bears the name of Henry Knighton is hostile to Wyclif. It calls John Ball, a priest among that summer's rebels, Wyclif's forerunner. Vaughan denies that Wyclif was an agent of the rising.

Josiah Forshall and Frederic Madden, who edited the English Wycliffite Bible in 1850, thought its New Testament was probably Wyclif's own translation. An early note gives much of the Old Testament to his helper Nicholas Hereford. Alfred Pollard edited fifteenth-century English prose in 1903. He says historians admit there is "substantially no evidence" that the rest is Wyclif's.

On 29 December, Shirley says, a stroke took Wyclif's speech as he heard Mass in his parish church. Vaughan reports that he was said to be giving out the bread. He died on the last day of 1384, Forshall and Madden write.

The friars, Wyclif wrote in an English tract on the pastor's office, call it heresy to write God's law in English. Christ and his apostles, he answered, taught the people in the tongue best known to them. He asked why men should not do so now.
