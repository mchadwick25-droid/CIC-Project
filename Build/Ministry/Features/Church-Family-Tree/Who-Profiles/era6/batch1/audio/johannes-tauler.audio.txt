[calmly] A book called the History and Life of the Reverend Doctor John Tauler opens in the year 1340. A Master in Holy Scripture preached often in a certain city. A layman, warned three times in his sleep, came more than thirty leagues to listen. God let him see, the History says, that the Master knew the Scriptures but was "dark as to the light of grace". Later the Master climbed a pulpit and wept so hard that he could not speak. The people called him "a downright fool".

Whether any of it happened is disputed. The History's own text never names the Master. Its title does, and so does Susanna Winkworth, its English translator in the nineteenth century, who treats it as authentic. Heinrich Denifle, a Dominican scholar, argued in 1879 that the conversion story was invented. In 1886 he called the layman, the Friend of God from the Oberland, a fiction.

Johannes Tauler was a Dominican friar of Strasbourg. Winkworth says he was born there in 1290, and C. de B. Evans, writing in the 1920s, gives 1300.

Winkworth writes that "Tauler did not himself write down his discourses, but they were compiled from notes taken by his hearers". She still thinks it highly probable that eighty-four sermons in the Strasbourg manuscripts had his own corrections.

In the struggle between the pope and the emperor Louis of Bavaria, Strasbourg lay under an interdict, a papal ban on church services. Winkworth writes that the Dominicans in general left the city in 1338. Tauler, she says, sided with the emperor and kept working. Henry Charles Lea, in his history of the Inquisition of 1888, also says Tauler supported Louis. At Basel, wrote his old friend Henry of Nördlingen, a priest from Constance, Tauler "helped me in every way he could with all fidelity".

His last months are told by Winkworth alone. In 1361, she writes, he lay ill at Strasbourg. He had himself carried to the convent where his aged sister was a nun. A writer of his school, she adds, counted this a fault. He died on 16 June 1361 and was buried in his own convent.

[warmly] In 1516 Martin Luther praised Tauler's sermons in a letter. He had seen no theology in Latin or German, he wrote, more wholesome or closer to the Gospel. "Works of love," Tauler says, in a line Winkworth quotes, "are more acceptable to God than lofty contemplation".
