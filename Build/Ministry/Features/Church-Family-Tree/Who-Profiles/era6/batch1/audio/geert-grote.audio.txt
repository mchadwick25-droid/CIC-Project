[calmly] One Lent, Geert Grote walked out from Zwolle with disciples to find them a quiet place. The wind beat against them. He told them, "I will go before you and shield you from the wind with my cloak." On a hill of Nemel he showed them where to build. "If the Lord grant me life I will be here often with you."

Thomas à Kempis tells this in his Chronicle of Mount St Agnes, the house built there. Grote, a citizen of Deventer in the Low Countries, died in a time of plague that same year, 1384. Kempis, who belonged to the movement Grote began, never saw him. He wrote Grote's Life, he says, from what men and women who knew Grote told him.

Kempis says Grote, born to rich parents, studied at Paris and held church posts. An old friend, prior of the Carthusian monks at Monichuysen, warned him that "the day and hour of death are unknown." Grote resigned his posts and put on a plain habit. Kempis's Chronicle says he gave his Deventer house to poor sisters he had gathered. He became a deacon, the order below priest, to preach, but out of awe would not be ordained priest.

Johannes Busch, a canon of Windesheim, finished a Latin account of Grote in 1464. The bishop of Utrecht, he says, licensed Grote to preach. At Kampen, Grote wrote, the friar Bartholomew taught that what God is by nature, we become by grace. Grote pressed the case, Busch says, until the bishop's court judged the friar a heretic.

Later a letter from the bishop stopped Grote's preaching. Kempis calls it an edict "craftily obtained". Grote told angry townspeople, "They are our prelates, and as is fitting and due we wish to obey their edicts".

Only Busch tells the talk that began the common life. Florens Radewijns, vicar of Deventer, asked what harm it would do if he and the copyist clerks pooled their earnings and lived in common. Grote feared the begging friars would fight it. Then he bade them begin in the Lord's name, and promised to defend them.

Busch alone says Grote caught the plague while taking the pulse of a sick friend, Lambert Stuerman. Both writers say Grote named Florens to lead the brothers.

[warmly] His own notes, at the end of Kempis's Life, put "the Gospel of Christ" first among his studies, "for therein is the Life of Christ."
