[calmly] In May 1382, bishops, doctors and friars met at the Black Friars in London to judge the teaching of John Wyclif. Robert Vaughan, an admiring biographer, writing in 1845, says an earthquake shook the city as they began. Some feared Heaven was displeased. William Courtenay, archbishop of Canterbury, took it as a sign that the church would be purged, and twenty-four points drawn from Wyclif's writings were condemned. In his Latin dialogue Trialogus, Wyclif called it the friars' earthquake council. When Christ's members fail to cry out against such heretics, he wrote, the earth cries out.

In 1858 Walter Shirley edited a Carmelite friars' collection of writings against him. He finds Wyclif at Oxford as master of Balliol College in 1361. The crown gave him the parish of Lutterworth.

In his book on civil lordship, Wyclif argued that no one in mortal sin has a true right to God's gifts. In February 1377 he was called before the bishops at St Paul's, with John of Gaunt, duke of Lancaster, at his side. That year, Shirley says, Pope Gregory XI sent bulls that condemned nineteen of his conclusions and ordered his arrest.

In 1381, Shirley says, Wyclif denied transubstantiation, the teaching that the bread no longer remains after the priest's words. In a Latin confession, in Vaughan's English, he called the sacrament "naturally bread and wine, but sacramentally the body and blood of Christ." Henry Knighton, a Leicester chronicler hostile to Wyclif, calls John Ball, a priest among that summer's rebels, Wyclif's forerunner. Vaughan rejects the charge.

Knighton also complains that Wyclif turned the gospel into English, open to lay people and to women who can read. So the gospel's pearl, he says, is trodden by swine. Josiah Forshall and Frederic Madden, who edited the Wycliffite Bible in 1850, thought its New Testament was probably Wyclif's own. An early note gives much of the Old Testament to his helper Nicholas Hereford. Alfred Pollard, in 1903, says historians admit there is "substantially no evidence" that the rest is Wyclif's.

On 29 December 1384, Shirley says, a stroke took his speech as he heard Mass in his parish church. He died two days later.

[warmly] The friars, Wyclif wrote in an English tract on the pastor's office, call it heresy to write God's law in English. Christ and his apostles, he answered, taught the people in the tongue best known to them. Why should men not do so now?
