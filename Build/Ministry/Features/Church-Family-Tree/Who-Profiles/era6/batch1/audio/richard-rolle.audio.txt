[calmly] A young man home from Oxford asked his sister for two of her tunics, one white and one gray, and for his father's rain-hood. She brought them to a wood nearby. He cut off sleeves and buttons, and dressed to look something like a hermit. "My brother is mad! My brother is mad!" she cried, and he fled.

The young man was Richard Rolle of Yorkshire, who died in 1349. The story comes from the Office of Saint Richard the hermit, the prayers and readings for his feast. Frances Comper, who put it into English in 1913, says the nuns of Hampole prepared it in the hope he would be made a saint.

The Office says he preached from a church pulpit, and that a squire, John de Dalton, then clothed him as a hermit and gave him a cell. Charles Horstman, who edited Rolle's English writings in 1895, says Rolle was not in holy orders, so was not allowed to preach from the pulpit. Josiah Forshall and Frederic Madden, editing the Wycliffite Bible in 1850, call him a chantry priest, kept to say Mass for the dead.

Rolle tells what came next in his Latin Fire of Love. Comper modernized the 1435 English of Richard Misyn, a friar. Three years "except three or four months" passed from the change of his life to "the opening of the heavenly door", Rolle writes. Nearly a year later, as he prayed in a chapel, "suddenly I felt within me a merry and unknown heat." After "half a year, three months and some weeks", singing psalms at night, he heard "the noise as it were of readers, or rather singers." Then "I burst out singing what before I said", though only in private, alone before his Maker.

The Office says he taught Margaret, once a recluse at Anderby. A recluse lived shut away in a cell. Horstman takes her for the Margaret of Rolle's Form of Living. Rolle spent his last years beside the nuns at Hampole. Horstman gives the day of his death as 29 September. The Office says he had promised Margaret that her fits would not return while he lived. When they returned, a rider went to Hampole and learned that Rolle had died shortly before.

[warmly] In Geraldine Hodgson's modern English of 1910, the Form of Living tells Margaret: "Love is a burning yearning after God, with a wonderful delight and certainty."
