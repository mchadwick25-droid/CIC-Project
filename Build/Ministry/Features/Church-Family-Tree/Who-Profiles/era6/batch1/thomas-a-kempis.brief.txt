As a schoolboy at Deventer, Thomas sang in the church choir. Florens Radewijns, vicar of Deventer, often stood there, and the boy took care not to chatter. Once Florens turned to share the boys' book and put his hands on the boy's shoulder. "I stood still, hardly daring to move," Thomas wrote.

Thomas à Kempis tells this in his Lives of the founders of the New Devotion. Geert Grote began that movement in the Low Countries before his death in 1384. Thomas's Chronicle names him Thomas Hemerken, of Kempen in the diocese of Cologne. He lived about a year in the brothers' house at Deventer. "Here indeed I learned to write," he says.

In 1399, he writes, he came to Zwolle for pardons the pope had granted there. He went on "glad at heart" to Mount St Agnes, a new house of canons regular, clergy who live under a rule. "I was received with mercy," he says. The prior was his brother John, and Thomas was clothed as a canon there in 1406.

In 1424 two brothers from Mount St Agnes visited John Vos of Heusden, prior of Windesheim. One had a dream that, the Chronicle says, foretold the prior's death. The Chronicle leaves the dreamer unnamed. Johannes Busch, a Windesheim canon of Thomas's day, names him in Latin as Brother Thomas of Kempen. This Thomas, Busch adds, wrote devout little books, among them one on the imitation of Christ.

William Atkynson's English version of the Imitation, printed in 1504, names John Gerson, chancellor of Paris. William Benham, who translated it in 1886, says Thomas copied it and his copyist's note began the mistake. Benham argues for John Gersen, an abbot of Vercelli. John Ingram, editor of early English versions in 1893, was convinced the book is Thomas's.

Later Thomas was sent to keep his aged brother John company. "I was with him and I closed his eyes," he wrote.

Thomas died in 1471, in his ninety-second year, says a notice by a brother of the house. It says he copied the whole Bible they used, and many books for sale. It calls his little books for the young "plain and simple in style, but mighty in the matter thereof".

Of the Deventer brothers he wrote: "Never before could I recollect to have seen such men, so devout and fervent were they in the love of God and of their neighbour."
