Before he became a friar, Bernardino of Siena decided to live in a wood, on grass and water. He told the story himself, preaching at Siena in 1427. He bought a Bible, and went to buy chamois skin to keep it dry. He went looking for a place near Massa Marittima, could not choose a hill, and came home. Outside a city gate he tried a meal of sow-thistle. "Chew, and chew, it would not go down!" One mouthful, he said, rid him of the whole temptation.

He was born in 1380 and joined the Franciscans in 1402, at twenty-two. For about forty-four years, writes Nazareno Orlandi, editing his sermons, he preached more often in town squares than in churches.

The Siena sermons survive because a cloth-shearer, Benedetto, son of Master Bartolomeo, stood in the square called the Campo with wax tablets. He took them down as he listened and wrote them out in his shop. Luciano Banchi printed this listener's report in 1880.

The historian Carolus Sigonius, quoted in the church annals of Odoricus Raynaldus, tells a story from Bologna. After his preaching, games were burned, and a painter of playing cards complained he had lost his living. Bernardino told him to paint this instead, and drew a sun, with the name of Jesus inside it in a new form of letters.

Nicolaus Glassberger, a friar of the strict Observant branch, wrote a chronicle about 1508. He says Dominican preachers accused Bernardino before Pope Martin V of leading people toward a kind of idolatry. The pope heard him, Glassberger goes on, found nothing wrong, and let him preach everywhere. Bernardino himself said at Siena in 1427 that at Rome some had wanted him fried, and some roasted. Banchi says he was called there to defend his teaching. In January 1432, Pope Eugenius IV quashed a charge brought by Michael, a Prague priest, and called Bernardino a fierce rooter-out of heresy.

In May 1444 he was sent to preach at L'Aquila, and he fell ill and died. His friend Vespasiano da Bisticci, a Florentine bookseller who wrote his Life, says he died before reaching the town. Glassberger places his death in its friary.

He dared his critics to say it to his face, or write it to him, "and we shall see what I have said that is wrong. I am one of those who wish to hold to that which Holy Church doth hold."
