[calmly] In a public hearing at Avignon, about 1317, Pope John XXII questioned a friar. Was he a Friar Minor? He said he was. Then why had he left them? He had not left them, the friar answered; they should be asked why they threw him out. The pope fell silent. The scene is told in the History of the Seven Tribulations, a chronicle of the Spirituals, the friars who wanted Francis's rule kept to the letter. Franz Ehrle, a Jesuit scholar, argued in 1886 that this friar was by far its most likely writer.

He was Angelo Clareno, from the March of Ancona in Italy. Livarius Oliger, a Franciscan who edited his commentary on the Rule in 1912, thinks he joined the order about 1270. In a letter Ehrle dates to 1317, Angelo names prisons he had known, first Ancona, Forano, Rome, Viterbo and Assisi.

Freed about 1290, Oliger says, he went as a missionary to the Armenian kingdom in Cilicia. In 1294 he and his leader, Liberatus of Macerata, won leave from Pope Celestine V to live apart, as the Poor Hermits of Celestine. When Pope Boniface VIII took that back, the brothers crossed to Greece about 1295 and stayed some ten years. Angelo was the last to come home.

After Liberatus died in 1307, Angelo led his band of Spirituals. At the Council of Vienne in 1311 he lived in the house of Cardinal Giacomo Colonna. At Avignon, John XXII had him held. In his letter of defense, Angelo wrote that he and his brothers were no heretics. If it was heresy to believe and practice humbly what Francis believed about keeping his rule, that heresy, he wrote, he had always held and held still.

The pope soon let him go, writes the historian Henry Charles Lea, and allowed him the Celestine habit and rule. Back in Italy, he lived mostly at Subiaco, near Rome. In 1334 John XXII ordered the arrest of an unnamed heretic who called himself head of the Fraticelli, the condemned rigorist friars. Conrad Eubel, who edited the papal letters, takes him for Angelo. Not long before his death, Ehrle thinks, the old man went to a hermitage in the Basilicata, in the south, and died there in June 1337.

[warmly] Simon Fidati of Cascia, an Augustinian preacher whom Angelo had taught, mourned him in a lament. Angelo was truly an angel in name, Simon wrote, and more truly in deed.
