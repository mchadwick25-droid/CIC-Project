[calmly] In a public hearing at Avignon, about 1317, Pope John XXII questioned a friar. Was he a Friar Minor? He said he was. Then why had he left them? I did not leave them, the friar answered; ask them why they threw me out. The pope fell silent. The scene is told in the History of the Seven Tribulations, a chronicle of the Franciscan rigorists. Franz Ehrle, a Jesuit scholar, argued in 1886 that this friar was by far its most likely writer.

He was Angelo Clareno, from the March of Ancona in Italy. Livarius Oliger, a Franciscan who edited his commentary on the Rule of Francis in 1912, thinks he joined the order about 1270. In a letter of 1317 Angelo lists the prisons he had known: Ancona, Forano, Rome, Viterbo and Assisi.

Freed about 1290, Oliger says, he went as a missionary to Armenia. In 1294 he and his leader Liberatus won leave from Pope Celestine V to live apart, as the Poor Hermits of Celestine. When Pope Boniface VIII took that back, the brothers crossed to Greece about 1295 and stayed some ten years. Angelo was the last to come home.

After Liberatus died in 1307, Angelo led the Spirituals, the friars who wanted Francis's rule kept to the letter. At the Council of Vienne in 1311 he lived in the house of Cardinal Giacomo Colonna. At Avignon, John XXII had him held. In his letter of defense, Angelo wrote that he and his brothers were no heretics, unless it was heresy to believe and practice, humbly, what Francis believed about keeping his rule. That heresy, he wrote, he had always held and held still.

The pope soon let him go, Henry Charles Lea writes, and allowed him the Celestine habit and rule. Back in Italy, he lived mostly at Subiaco. In 1334 John XXII ordered the arrest of an unnamed heretic who called himself head of the Fraticelli, the condemned rigorist friars. Conrad Eubel, who edited the papal letters, takes him for Angelo. Angelo fled to a hermitage in the Basilicata, in the south, and died there on 15 June 1337. Ehrle worked out the year from the weekday given in a note on his miracles.

[warmly] Simon Fidati of Cascia, who had first studied under him as a boy, mourned him in a lament. Angelo was truly an angel in name, Simon wrote, and more truly in deed.
