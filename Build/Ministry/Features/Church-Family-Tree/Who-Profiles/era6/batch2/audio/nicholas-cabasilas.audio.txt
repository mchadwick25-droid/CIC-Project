[calmly] In 1353 the bishops at Constantinople prayed for a new patriarch and put forward three names. Two were bishops. The third, John Kantakouzenos writes in his history, was Nicholas Cabasilas, who was still a layman. Kantakouzenos, the emperor, chose Philotheos instead.

A notice that Migne took from the Bibliotheca Graeca of Fabricius calls Cabasilas a thoroughly learned man and a nephew of Nilus Cabasilas, archbishop of Thessalonica. It says the two are often confused. Fabricius makes him the son of Nilus's brother. Wilhelm Gass, who printed the Greek of his Life in Christ in 1849, follows the chronicler George Phrantzes and makes him the son of Nilus's sister.

Kantakouzenos names him in his history several times. In 1346 Thessalonica chose Nicholas Cabasilas and a man named Pharmakes as envoys to the emperor's son Manuel at Berroia. In 1349 Cabasilas and Demetrios Kydones joined the emperor's plan to leave the world for a monastery. Both, Kantakouzenos says, had reached the height of secular learning, lived as philosophers in deed, and chose a chaste life free from the troubles of marriage. The plan was dropped. A synod's record of 1350 lists him among the emperor's household. Gass finds him named among the followers of Gregory Palamas, standing up for them.

In his book The Life in Christ, he wrote that this life grows and begins here, and is made complete in the life to come. Baptism gives us our being in Christ. The anointing with chrism puts in us the working that fits this life, and the Eucharist keeps it whole. We did not go up to God, he wrote; he came down to us. No one needs to go to far places, change his clothes or ruin his health for this life. The general leads his army, the farmer farms and the craftsman runs his work.

Whether he later became archbishop of Thessalonica is disputed. Fabricius says he succeeded his uncle. Gass finds him named archbishop no earlier than 1354. Symeon of Thessalonica, writing in the next century, calls him blessed and gives him no see.

Gentian Hervet, who put his commentary on the Liturgy into Latin in 1548, disputed his view of the moment the bread and wine are changed. Yet Hervet wrote that nothing more devout could be said than the rest of the book.

[warmly] Through these holy mysteries, Cabasilas wrote, as through windows, the Sun of Righteousness comes into this dark world.
