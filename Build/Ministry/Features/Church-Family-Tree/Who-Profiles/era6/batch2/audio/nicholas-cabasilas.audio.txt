[calmly] In 1353 the bishops at Constantinople called on the Holy Spirit and put forward three names for patriarch. Two were bishops. The third was Nicholas Cabasilas, a writer on the sacraments. He was still a layman, John Kantakouzenos writes in his history. Kantakouzenos, the emperor, chose Philotheos, bishop of Heraclea.

In 1865 the editor J.-P. Migne printed a notice from the Bibliotheca Graeca of Fabricius. It calls Cabasilas a thoroughly learned man and a nephew of Nilus Cabasilas, archbishop of Thessalonica. It says the two are often confused. Fabricius makes him the son of Nilus's brother. Wilhelm Gass, who printed the Greek of his Life in Christ in 1849, follows the chronicler George Phrantzes in making him a sister's son.

In 1346, Kantakouzenos writes, Thessalonica chose Nicholas Cabasilas and a man named Pharmakes as envoys to the emperor's son Manuel at Berroia. In 1349 Cabasilas and Demetrios Kydones joined the emperor's plan to leave the world. Both, Kantakouzenos says, had reached the height of secular learning, lived as philosophers in deed, and chose a chaste life free from the troubles of marriage. The plan was dropped. A synod's record of 1350 lists him among the emperor's household. Gass finds him named among the followers of Gregory Palamas.

The life in Christ begins and grows here, Cabasilas wrote in his book of that name, and is made complete in the world to come. Baptism gives being in Christ. The anointing with chrism puts in the newborn the working that fits that life, and the Eucharist keeps it whole. People did not go up to God, he wrote; God came down to them. No one need go to far places, change clothes or ruin his health for it. A general can still lead his army, a farmer farm and a craftsman run his work.

Fabricius says he succeeded his uncle as archbishop of Thessalonica. Gass also makes him archbishop, after Gregory Palamas held the see. Martin Jugie, editing his sermons on Mary in 1926, finds no serious proof and thinks the title came from confusing him with Nilus.

Gentian Hervet, who put his commentary on the Liturgy into Latin in 1548, disputed his view of the moment the bread and wine are changed. Yet Hervet wrote that nothing more devout could be said than the rest of the book.

[warmly] Through these holy mysteries, Cabasilas wrote, as through windows, the Sun of Righteousness comes into this dark world.
