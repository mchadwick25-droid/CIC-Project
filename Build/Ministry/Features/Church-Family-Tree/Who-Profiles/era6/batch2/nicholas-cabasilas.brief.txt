In 1353 the bishops at Constantinople called on the Holy Spirit and put forward three names for patriarch. Two were bishops. The third, John Kantakouzenos writes in his history, was Nicholas Cabasilas, a writer on the sacraments who was still a layman. Kantakouzenos, the emperor, chose Philotheos, bishop of Heraclea.

In 1865 the editor J.-P. Migne printed a notice from the Bibliotheca Graeca of Fabricius. It calls Cabasilas a thoroughly learned man and a nephew of Nilus Cabasilas, archbishop of Thessalonica. It says the two are often confused. Fabricius makes him the son of Nilus's brother. Wilhelm Gass, who printed the Greek of his Life in Christ in 1849, follows the chronicler George Phrantzes in making him a sister's son.

In 1346, Kantakouzenos writes, Thessalonica chose Nicholas Cabasilas and a man named Pharmakes as envoys to the emperor's son Manuel at Berroia. In 1349 Cabasilas and Demetrios Kydones joined the emperor's plan to leave the world for a monastery. Both, Kantakouzenos says, had reached the height of secular learning, lived as philosophers in deed, and chose a chaste life free from the troubles of marriage. The plan was dropped. A synod's record of 1350 lists him among the emperor's household. Gass finds him named among the followers of Gregory Palamas, standing up for them.

The life in Christ begins and grows here, he wrote in his book of that name, and is made complete in the world to come. Baptism gives being in Christ. The anointing with chrism puts in the newborn the working that fits that life, and the Eucharist keeps it whole. People did not go up to God; God came down to them. No one need go to far places, change clothes or ruin his health for it. A general can still lead his army, a farmer farm and a craftsman run his work.

Fabricius says he later succeeded his uncle as archbishop of Thessalonica. Gass presumes so too, and finds him named archbishop no earlier than 1354. A record of the church of Thessalonica, which Gass quotes, calls him its most holy archbishop.

Gentian Hervet, who put his commentary on the Liturgy into Latin in 1548, disputed his view of the moment the bread and wine are changed. Yet Hervet wrote that nothing more devout could be said than the rest of the book.

Through these holy mysteries, Cabasilas wrote, as through windows, the Sun of Righteousness comes into this dark world.
