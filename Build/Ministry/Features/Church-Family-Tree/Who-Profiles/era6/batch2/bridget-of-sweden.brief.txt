Early in 1373, at Naples, back from Jerusalem, a Swedish widow had a vision about the pope. The Queen of Naples and many others tell me it is impossible for him to come to Rome, she said, because the King of France and the cardinals put many obstacles in his way. The answer, her Revelations say, was that whether she would see him come was not hers to know. She died in Rome that July. Gregory XI reached Rome in January 1377.

She was Bridget of Sweden, or Birgitta. W. P. Cumming, her English editor of 1929, says her father married her at thirteen, against her wishes, to Ulf Gudmarson. Ulf died in 1344, and a few days later, Cumming writes, Christ appeared to her and chose her as his bride.

In 1346 a revelation sent her to Rome. Two or three years later she left Sweden, never to return alive. With her went two confessors, Peter, prior of Alvastra, and Peter Olafson. She wrote in Swedish, Cumming says, and her confessors put the revelations into Latin. Alphonso, a hermit and former bishop of Jaén, later edited them. Who did what is disputed. Consalvo Duranti, a bishop annotating the Latin in 1628, quotes Johannes Vastovius, who gives all eight books to Peter Olafson. C. A. Wilkens, writing in 1894, says Prior Peter and Alphonso made her Swedish into a rhetorical Latin that dimmed her rich images.

In Rome she pressed for the papacy's return from Avignon. When Urban V, who came in 1367, left again in 1370, a revelation she handed him warned that he would soon suffer a blow. Duranti notes he died after his return. That year, Cumming says, Urban approved her Order of St Saviour, for sisters and brothers.

In the English version, Christ tells the head of his church that he is "a sleer of soules". Henry Charles Lea, writing in 1888, judged that nothing Wyclif or Hus said of the clergy's depravity could exceed the bitterness of hers.

Her bones were carried home to Vadstena, her order's Swedish house. Pope Boniface IX made her a saint in 1391. Cumming says the schism of rival popes brought the canonization into question, and later popes confirmed it.

Juan de Torquemada, who defended her Revelations at the Council of Basel, tells of her first call. She feared a trick of Satan, until Christ said: I am the maker of all, not a deceiver.
