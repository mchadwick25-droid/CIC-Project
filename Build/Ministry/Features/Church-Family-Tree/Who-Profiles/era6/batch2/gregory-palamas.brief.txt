In 1347, in a civil war, the monk Gregory Palamas lay in prison inside the imperial palace at Constantinople. Her young son urged the empress Anna to make peace. She had Palamas brought out and sent him as an envoy to her rival, the emperor John Kantakouzenos. Kantakouzenos, his ally, tells this. Through all he had suffered for justice and truth, he writes, Palamas never said or did anything base.

Palamas was a monk of Mount Athos, later archbishop of Thessalonica. Philotheos, a patriarch of Constantinople who wrote a speech in his praise, says his father sat in the senate. Palamas left for Athos with his two brothers, and became a monk at Vatopedi.

Barlaam of Calabria, a scholar, attacked the hesychasts. These were monks who prayed in stillness and said they saw a divine light. Kantakouzenos says Barlaam called them cheats and navel-souls, as if they put the soul in the navel. Palamas called it plain slander, since none of them had ever said such a thing.

A synod in Hagia Sophia condemned Barlaam in 1341. Its record says the monks held that they received the uncreated grace of the Spirit. They denied sharing in God's essence, his very being. In his essence, Palamas wrote, God cannot be shared. Gregory Akindynos and the historian Nikephoros Gregoras fought him on this. Gregoras wrote that Palamas made many gods. The patriarch John Kalekas had him seized, and Philotheos says he spent four years under guard. A synod of 1347 put out the patriarch and declared Palamas sound.

Made archbishop of Thessalonica, he was kept out of the city. Its governors, Kantakouzenos says, wanted to rule it themselves, and one of them kept the Zealots, a city faction, around him. He waited on the island of Lemnos. At a synod of 1351, Kantakouzenos, presiding, found him in accord with the saints. Later, sailing to Constantinople, he was taken by the Turks and held a whole year, Philotheos says. Gregoras wonders if it was God's justice.

Philotheos says he died at sixty-three, and gives no year. The editor of his sermons, printed at Jerusalem in 1857, writes that Philotheos set his feast in 1368, six years after his death. Philotheos, he notes, counted him a saint above all for the healings at his tomb.

It is bad for the mind to dwell on bodily thoughts, Palamas wrote, but not to be in the body, since the body is not evil.
