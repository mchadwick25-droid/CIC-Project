In the summer of 1376, a woman from Siena stood before Pope Gregory XI at Avignon. She knew no Latin and he no Italian, so her confessor, Raymond of Capua, interpreted. She complained that the papal court stank of the vices of hell. The pope asked how she could know its ways after only a few days. Even in her own city, she answered, she had smelled its sins more than the men who committed them. At this, Raymond writes in his Life of her, the pope kept silent.

She was Catherine of Siena. Vida Scudder, her English translator of 1905, dates her birth to 25 March 1347. She was the youngest of twenty-five children of Jacopo Benincasa, a dyer. She took the black and white habit of the Mantellate, Dominican women who lived under a rule in their own homes. Scudder says she learned to write only three years before her death.

Florence had sent her to make peace. From Avignon, on 28 June 1376, she wrote to the Eight of War, the magistrates Florence chose when it rebelled against the pope. Gregory would receive them as sons, she said, if their case was as she had put it. "But you, with your taxes and frivolities, are spoiling all that is sown." The peace failed. To Gregory she wrote: "Come, come, come, and do not wait for time, since time does not wait for you."

Gregory reached Rome in January 1377. Scudder writes that he was swayed by Catherine. Consalvo Duranti, in his 1628 notes on Bridget of Sweden's Revelations, says her letters handled the whole matter of his return. A life of Gregory printed in Mansi's councils says one writer, Gobelinus, credited his return to Bridget's revelations.

In 1378, Scudder says, she dictated her Dialogue at Siena. Her secretary Cristofano Guidini wrote that she spoke it in a trance, every sense lost but her tongue, while three disciples took turns writing. When two rival popes split the church that year, she went to Rome at Urban VI's call and wrote to rulers for him.

Early in 1380 she wrote to Raymond of a pain in her heart so great that her tunic burst. She died in Rome on 30 April, Scudder says, among her disciples.

In Algar Thorold's English of 1896, God tells her: "I have made a Bridge of My Word, of My only-begotten Son, and this is the truth."
