On a Thursday night in late May 1328, a few friars slipped away from the papal court at Avignon. Michael of Cesena, head of the Franciscan order, led them. With him went William of Ockham, an English Franciscan and master of theology. At Aigues-Mortes they boarded a boat, then an armed galley bound for Genoa. Armando Carlini, in a 1912 study, dates the flight to the night of 26 May.

Two days later, Pope John XXII wrote that William had been accused of heresy at the court. By leaving in secret while the case was open, the pope said, he had shown himself guilty. Letters ordering his arrest went to Germany and England. In 1330 the pope added that doctors had found many articles in his writings heretical.

Michael's friars held that Christ and the apostles owned nothing. In 1323, writes Henry Charles Lea in his 1888 history of the Inquisition, the pope declared that view heretical. He also told the bishops of Bologna and Ferrara to summon an English friar named William. They were to do so if they found he had preached the friars' view. Conrad Eubel, who edited the papal letters in 1898, thinks him more likely Ockham than William of Alnwick, another Englishman.

At Pisa, William joined Michael's appeal against the pope. Michael and his companions went with the emperor, Louis of Bavaria, to Munich, writes Nicolaus Glassberger, an Observant chronicler of about 1508. For twenty years, Lea writes, William poured out works in their defense. In Lea's summary, William argued that a pope can fall into heresy, and so can a council, and that "there was nothing sure but Scripture".

In one tract, William sets out what can truly be charged against him. He had withdrawn from obedience to the church of Avignon and from most of his brother friars. He did so, he writes, only because he was sure it held open errors and heresies. He walked into persecution knowingly, he says.

When Michael died in 1342, Lea writes, he left William the order's seal. The Munich friars' book of the dead says William died on 10 April 1347. Luke Wadding, a seventeenth-century Franciscan annalist, read a papal letter of 1349 as showing him alive, returning the seal and asking pardon. Lea doubts he ever made his peace. Walter Shirley, editor in 1858 of a Carmelite collection against Wyclif, called him "the glory and the reproach of his order".
