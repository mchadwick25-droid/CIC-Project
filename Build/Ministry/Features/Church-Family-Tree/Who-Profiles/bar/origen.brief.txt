Around the year 202, the emperor Severus began to persecute the churches, and believers from all over Egypt were brought to Alexandria, where many died. This was the church of Alexandrian Christianity, and Origen's father was among those arrested. Eusebius of Caesarea says the boy longed for martyrdom. His mother hid his clothes to keep him home, so he wrote to his father in prison: "Be careful not to change your mind because of us."

His father died a martyr. In his eighteenth year Origen took charge of teaching the faith in Alexandria, because everyone else had fled. In 231 he left for Caesarea, and Jerome says he died at Tyre.

His great work was on Scripture. Jerome says he learned Hebrew, against the habit of his time. He set the Hebrew text beside the Greek translations in the Hexapla and marked where the old Greek fell short. When critics attacked Jerome's own Latin Job, Jerome defended it by pointing to those marks. Origen also taught that Scripture, like a person, has body, soul and spirit. Its plain story feeds simple believers, and its deeper senses feed those who have gone further. The Philocalia, a book of passages chosen from him, opens with those chapters on how Scripture should be read.

He held that the Son had always been with the Father, and a century later Athanasius of Alexandria, defending the council of Nicaea, called on "the hardworking Origen" as a witness. He also turned lovers of Greek learning toward the faith. Eusebius says two brothers he taught at Caesarea, Gregory and Athenodorus, both became bishops in Pontus.

Some of his ideas, above all that souls lived before they were born into bodies, drew attack. Methodius of Olympus wrote against him, and Pamphilus of Caesarea and Eusebius wrote in his defense. Three centuries after his death, the acts of a council at Constantinople list his name among condemned heretics.
