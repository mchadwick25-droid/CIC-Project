Around the year 202, the emperor Severus began to persecute the churches, and believers from all over Egypt were brought to Alexandria, where many died. Its church is the one the tree calls Alexandrian Christianity, and Origen's father was among those arrested. Origen was a boy, and Eusebius of Caesarea says he so longed for martyrdom that he rushed toward danger. His mother hid his clothes to keep him home, so he wrote to his father in prison: "Be careful not to change your mind because of us."

His father died a martyr, and the state took the family's property. In his eighteenth year Origen took charge of teaching the faith in Alexandria, because everyone else had fled. Later the bishops of Caesarea and Jerusalem ordained him a priest. Jerome says this so enraged his own bishop, Demetrius, that he wrote everywhere to ruin Origen's name. In 231 Origen left for Caesarea. Under the emperor Decius he was stretched in the stocks, and Jerome says he died at Tyre, aged sixty-nine.

His great work was on Scripture. Jerome says he learned Hebrew, against the habit of his time. He set the Hebrew text beside the Greek translations in the Hexapla and marked where the old Greek fell short. When critics attacked Jerome's own Latin Job, Jerome defended it by pointing to those marks. Origen also taught that Scripture, like a person, has body, soul and spirit. Its plain story feeds simple believers, and its deeper senses feed those who have gone further. The Philocalia, a book of passages chosen from him, opens with those chapters on how Scripture should be read.

He held that the Son had always been with the Father, and a century later Athanasius of Alexandria, defending the council of Nicaea, called on "the hardworking Origen" as a witness. He also turned lovers of Greek learning toward the faith, and Eusebius says two brothers he taught at Caesarea, Gregory and Athenodorus, both became bishops in Pontus.

Some of his ideas, above all that souls lived before they were born into bodies, drew attack. Methodius of Olympus wrote against him. Pamphilus of Caesarea, who had copied most of Origen's works by hand, wrote in his defense, and so did Eusebius. Three centuries after his death, the acts of a council at Constantinople list his name among condemned heretics.
