In 380 the Arians had held the churches of Constantinople, the eastern capital, for forty years. Those who still kept to the creed of Nicaea were led by Gregory of Nazianzus, of the church the tree calls Cappadocian Christianity. Many bishops had called him there from Asia Minor. They met in a little house made over into a house of prayer. The church historian Sozomen says it was named Anastasia, the Resurrection, because, he believes, the faith of Nicaea, buried in the city, rose again through Gregory's sermons.

His closest friend was Basil of Caesarea, from their student days in Athens. Of those years he wrote: "We seemed to have one soul living in two bodies." Yet when Basil made him bishop of Sasima, a village that a rival bishop also claimed, Gregory refused to fight for it. "Why should I fight over piglets and chickens, and ones that are not my own," he wrote to Basil, "as if I were fighting for souls and church canons?"

In Constantinople he preached the Trinity. In one sermon he said he would keep "equally far from the merging of Sabellius and from the division of Arius," the one fusing God together and the other cutting Him into unequal parts. In November 380 the emperor Theodosius ordered the Arian bishop to accept Nicaea or leave the churches, and he left. Some bishops then objected to Gregory's election, and he gave up the see. In his farewell he thanked his Anastasia for raising up a teaching once despised. The city's other churches were full again, he said, and he gave the credit to the grace that was with him.

He wrote two letters to the priest Cledonius against Apollinarius, who held that Christ had no human mind. In the first, Gregory wrote that whatever Christ did not take on, He did not heal. Whatever is joined to His divine nature, Gregory added, is also saved. Three centuries later, when the church argued over whether Christ had a human will, Pope Agatho cited Gregory to show that He did.

Jerome called him "my teacher in the Scriptures," and Rufinus of Aquileia put his works into Latin. In 553 the emperor Justinian told a council at Constantinople that he followed the holy Fathers, and among them he named Gregory the Theologian.
