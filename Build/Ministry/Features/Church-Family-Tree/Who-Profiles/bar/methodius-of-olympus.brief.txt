Socrates of Constantinople tells an odd story about Methodius, a bishop in Lycia. Methodius attacked Origen in many places, Socrates says, and then, as if taking it all back, praised him in a dialogue. Socrates defended Origen and called his critics worthless, so he is not a neutral witness, and little else about Methodius is certain.

Jerome, in his Lives of Illustrious Men, says he was bishop of Olympus and later of Tyre, and that he died a martyr at Chalcis at the end of the recent persecution. Methodius's translator William Clark puts that about the year 312. Jerome adds that others put it under the emperors Decius and Valerian. Clark rejects that, since Methodius wrote against Origen long after Origen died.

Epiphanius of Cyprus called him a learned man and a valiant defender of the truth, and Jerome praised his style. Theodoret of Cyrrhus kept a passage from his sermon on martyrs.

Jerome lists his book On the Resurrection as against Origen. Origen had taught that the soul was chained in a body after its fall. Methodius answered, Photius of Constantinople reports, that the body is no chain, and that a person is soul and body joined. He pictures sin as a wild fig tree grown into a temple's stones. Death tears the tree out, so that the temple can stand again. The flesh is then raised, he writes, "with the same parts, uninjured and immortal, while sin is utterly and entirely destroyed."
