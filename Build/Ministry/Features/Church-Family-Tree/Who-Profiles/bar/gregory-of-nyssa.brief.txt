In 378 the emperor Valens, who had driven bishops loyal to Nicaea into exile, was killed fighting the Goths, and the new emperor Gratian called the exiles home. One of them was Gregory of Nyssa, a bishop of the church the tree calls Cappadocian Christianity, in Asia Minor. Some nine months after his brother Basil of Caesarea died, Gregory left a synod at Antioch to visit their sister Macrina, and found her dying. When he told her of his exile under Valens, she answered that it was their parents' prayers that had lifted him up, since he had little of his own to explain such success.

As a young man he gave up reading the sacred books to the people and taught rhetoric. His friend Gregory of Nazianzus wrote to ask why he would rather be called a professor of rhetoric than of Christianity. He was made bishop of Nyssa by force, as Basil wrote. An imperial official, a friend of heretics in Basil's words, had him summoned on charges about church money and his ordination. Basil answered that any fault lay with those who ordained him.

After Basil died, Gregory wrote, he "inherited the controversy with Eunomius." Eunomius held, as Gregory reports him, that being unbegotten was the Father's very essence, so the Son was unlike Him in being. Gregory answered that the Deity "is above every name." He also wrote that the Holy Spirit is "of the same rank as the Father and the Son." In 381 an imperial law handed the churches to bishops who confessed one majesty in Father, Son and Spirit. It named Gregory as one whose communion marked them, though it gives no reason.

In the Great Catechism he wrote that we were made able to change, so we can keep moving toward the good without end. He looks there to a day when those now lying in sin are restored and all creation gives thanks. Christ, he writes, frees humanity from evil and heals even the one who brought evil in.

In 553 the emperor Justinian, writing to a council at Constantinople, named him among the holy Fathers whose writings on the true faith he followed. In 692 the council in Trullo set its seal on his canons, making them church law.
