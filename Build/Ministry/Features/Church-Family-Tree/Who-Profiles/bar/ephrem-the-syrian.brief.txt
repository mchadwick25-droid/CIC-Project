Around 372, famine struck Edessa, a Syriac-speaking city of upper Mesopotamia on Rome's border with Persia. Its church is the one the tree calls Syriac Christianity, and nine years before, the emperor Jovian had handed the frontier city of Nisibis to the Persians. Ephrem, a deacon of Edessa from Nisibis or its countryside, left his cell. Palladius of Helenopolis says he asked the rich why they let people die while their wealth went to ruin. They said they had no one honest enough to run the relief, so Ephrem asked what they thought of him. They said they knew him for a man of God, and he answered: "Look, on your behalf I appoint myself to run the shelter." He ran the relief for a year, with some three hundred beds, and a month after it ended, Palladius says, he died in his cell.

His years in Edessa had been spent against rival teachers. He wrote prose against Mani, Marcion and Bardaisan of Edessa, and in one hymn he tells of finding a book of Bardaisan's: "It tainted my pure ears." To deny the body's resurrection, he answered, slanders God's justice and grace.

The church historian Sozomen says Bardaisan's son Harmonius had set Greek ideas about the soul and the body's birth, death and rebirth to songs the Syrians loved. In his own homily, though, Ephrem blames Bardaisan himself. Ephrem feared the people would absorb those ideas, so he learned the same meters and wrote hymns that held the church's teaching. From that time on, Sozomen says, the Syrians sang the songs of Ephrem. His works were put into Greek in his own lifetime, and Basil of Caesarea admired him. In the next century, Theodoret of Cyrrhus says, his hymns still brightened the festivals of the martyrs. Jerome wrote that some churches read his writings aloud after the Scriptures.

Around 600, Barhadbeshabba, the historian of the school of Nisibis, wrote that Ephrem had opened a school in Edessa, and that after his death his disciples made it grow. Later, he says, Narsai founded a school at Nisibis, by then inside Persia, and most of the students of Edessa came to him. In his telling, the schools that spread through the Church of the East under Persia began with Ephrem.
