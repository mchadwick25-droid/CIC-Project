Four founders of monasteries once came over from Ireland to visit Columba of Iona on the island of Hinba, Adomnan writes. While Columba celebrated Mass at their request, one of them, Brendan Mocu Alti, saw a blazing globe of fire over his head. It rose like a pillar until the rite was done, as Brendan later told two of the others.

Wentworth Huyshe, who translated Adomnan, takes this Brendan to be Brendan of Clonfert, founder of that Irish monastery. Adomnan, a later abbot of Iona, names him once more. A devout man, Aidan son of Fergno, is said to have served him for twelve years. Huyshe gives Brendan's years as born 482, founded Clonfert 559, and died 577, without saying where they come from.

Adomnan also tells of a second Brendan, the founder of Birr, whom Huyshe warns must be kept apart from the first. The Welsh annals record the death of Brendan of Birr, and say nothing of Brendan of Clonfert.

Most of what is told of Brendan of Clonfert comes from Lives written centuries later. The Irish Life in the Book of Lismore calls him Brenainn son of Finnlug. Whitley Stokes, its editor, says the book was compiled in the later 1400s. In that Life an angel promises Brenainn the Land of Promise. He sails for years, keeps Easter on a whale's back and is shown the gate of hell. Then the nun Ita, his foster mother, tells him he will never find that holy land in boats of dead hides. So he builds a great ship, and at last he reaches it.

A Latin Life printed by W. J. Rees calls him the father of nearly three thousand monks, and breaks off as he plans his voyage. Rees thinks the Lives in his book were probably compiled about the twelfth century.

The Lismore Life likens him to Noah. As Noah raised the ark over the flood, it says, Brenainn will raise his monks and households over the fire of Doom.
