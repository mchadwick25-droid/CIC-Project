[calmly] The emperor Justinian sent two bishops to Rome to consult Pope John, called Mercurius. They came to oppose Cyrus and Eulogius, envoys of the monks called the Acoemetae. These two denied that Mary is truly and properly the Mother of God. Liberatus of Carthage writes that he was in Rome when the pope upheld this teaching by letter.

Liberatus was a deacon of the church of Carthage, in Roman Africa, under Justinian. Pope Agapetus, writing to Reparatus, bishop of Carthage, says two bishops and Liberatus the deacon came to speak for Reparatus in person. Otto Günther dates the letter to 535.

His book is the Breviarium, a short history of the quarrels over Nestorius and Eutyches. He wrote it, he says, worn out by the hardships of a journey. He wrote to answer the followers of both sides, who speak of their leaders otherwise than the truth.

Liberatus blames a later fight on Theodore, bishop of Caesarea in Cappadocia. Theodore was a fierce defender of Origen, he says. Grieved when Origen was condemned, he urged the emperor to condemn Theodore of Mopsuestia and a letter of Ibas of Edessa. These were two of the Three Chapters. So Justinian wrote a book against the Three Chapters, Liberatus says, for our sins. Garnier, whose edition Migne reprints, finds him partial to Nestorius, or at least to Theodoret of Cyrus. Theodor Mommsen calls him and Victor of Tunnuna fierce defenders of the true faith, against all who strayed from the council of Chalcedon.

Victor writes that Reparatus would not condemn the Three Chapters, and died in exile at Euchaida. Garnier takes the journey in Liberatus's preface to be this exile. He says Liberatus stayed with his bishop to the end.

[warmly] At the close he writes that bishops who agreed were rewarded with gifts, and those who refused were deposed and exiled. Some hid in flight and found a happy end in their hardship. All this is known to everyone, he says, so he thinks he should now be silent.
