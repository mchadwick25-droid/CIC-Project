[calmly] The emperor Marcian's letter was being read out in Alexandria, the History of Dioscorus says, when its narrator came up behind the reader. He saw in it the names of men he calls evil counselors. He went back to his bishop and said, in the words of the sons of the prophets, that there was death in this letter.

The narrator names himself Theopistus, a disciple of Dioscorus of Alexandria. In the book a brother calls him the deacon of Dioscorus. François Nau, who put the Syriac into French, notes his claim never to have left Dioscorus from the council's summons to his death. He tells how a heretic slapped him for begging an envoy not to mock the old man.

After the death, the book says, the archdeacon Peter wrote telling him to flee and hide. He hid in the city of Pentapolis and wrote this history there.

Nau doubts the book is still as Theopistus wrote it. In one vision a saint blesses a small child named Severus. Nau reads this as a sign that Severus will be patriarch of Antioch, which puts the present text after 512. He thinks an account by Theopistus, of the council and the exile, may have been among those written in Egypt soon after the death. After 512, he suggests, a rhetor of the school of Peter the Iberian reworked it, probably in Greek. That Greek, he thinks, was later put into Syriac with some further changes.

[warmly] Near the end the narrator testifies before God that he has written the truth.
