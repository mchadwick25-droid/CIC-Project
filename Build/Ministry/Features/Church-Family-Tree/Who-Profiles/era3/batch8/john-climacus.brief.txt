John, abbot of the monastery of Raithu, wrote to the abbot of Mount Sinai. He asked him to write down what he had seen on that mountain, as Moses once did. Such a book, he wrote, would carry those who strive for heaven up as if by the steps of a ladder. Jacob, only a keeper of sheep, had seen a ladder. A teacher of a flock that can reason, he argued, could show everyone the way to God.

The abbot answered that only the fear of casting off obedience made him take on a task beyond his strength. From his book, the Ladder, he was called John Climacus.

His life comes mainly from a short Life by Daniel, a monk of Raithu. At sixteen John gave himself to God on Sinai, under a teacher. When his teacher died, he went to live alone at a place called Tholas, five miles from the church, and stayed forty years.

Daniel tells how John, dozing in his cell, saw a man who rebuked him for sleeping safely while his disciple Moses was in danger. John rose and prayed. That evening Moses said a huge rock had nearly crushed him in his sleep. Some envied John and called him a chatterer, Daniel says. So he kept silent for a whole year, until they begged him to speak. Later the monks made him head of the monastery by force, like a second Moses.

The Ladder climbs in thirty steps, from leaving the world to faith, hope and love. Christ was baptized at about thirty, John writes at its end, and so took the thirtieth step, since God is love.

An unnamed monk of Sinai tells that his brother George died within ten months of him. Philippe Labbe, writing in the 1600s, reports a form of the tale that ties George's death to that of Peter, patriarch of Jerusalem. If the tale is true, Labbe argues, John died between 560 and 565. Yet he says hardly anything about the year is certain. Pope Gregory the Great wrote to a John, abbot of Mount Sinai, in a letter that Paul Ewald and Ludwig Hartmann date to 600. Gregory's English translator, James Barmby, takes this John to be Climacus.

Thomas à Kempis remembered John Ketel, a cook in the House of Florentius at Deventer. "Sometimes he read the book of John Climacus," Thomas writes. Ketel explained it so well, Thomas says, that wise men marveled.
