Bishops of Gaul met at Agde under Caesarius of Arles, in 506 by August Engelbrecht's date, and Ruricius of Limoges did not come. Caesarius wrote to him that this had troubled him greatly. Ruricius's letter had gone to Agde with a deacon of Verus, bishop of Tours, but Caesarius did not recall receiving it. Ruricius should, he wrote, have sent someone to sign in his place. Ruricius answered, in modern words, "Infirmity did this, not will." Ruricius reminded Caesarius that he had seen him worn out at Bordeaux. That was in winter, when Ruricius was usually stronger.

Ruricius was bishop of Limoges in Gaul. Engelbrecht, who edited his letters with those of Faustus of Riez, dates his election to 485 from one of Faustus's letters. Venantius Fortunatus wrote an epitaph for him and for his grandson, also bishop of Limoges. It joins the two to the Anicii, a great house of Rome. Sidonius Apollinaris wrote the poem for his wedding to Iberia.

Faustus, Engelbrecht says, drew him to the religious life. Ruricius's first letter to Faustus says he had long known him by fame. "My soul has clung to you, outstanding teacher," he writes, in modern words, borrowing a psalm. He asks Faustus to send him harsher food, fit for his sickness, in place of the sweet dishes sent so far. From exile Faustus rejoiced that Ruricius had turned the prow of salvation toward the harbour of religion.

Two books of his letters survive. Several speak of his poor health. Engelbrecht, with Bruno Krusch, thinks he died not long after 507, when he was too frail to reach a council at Toulouse. Fortunatus says each of the two Ruricii founded a church in his time, one to Augustine and one to Peter.

Limoges was a small city. Ruricius wrote to Caesarius, in modern words, "It is far better and far more distinguished for a city to become known by its bishop than a bishop by his city."
