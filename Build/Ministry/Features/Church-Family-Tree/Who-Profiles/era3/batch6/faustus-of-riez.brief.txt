Riochatus, a bishop and monk, was carrying books by Faustus of Riez to the Britons. Unrest among the peoples held him at Clermont, where he kept the books hidden. When he left, the bishop there, Sidonius Apollinaris, rode after him and read them. August Engelbrecht, who edited Faustus, takes them to be his work On Grace.

Faustus was a Briton by birth, Avitus of Vienne says. He was a monk of the island monastery of Lérins and its abbot from 433, by Engelbrecht's reckoning. Then he became bishop of Riez.

A priest named Lucidus drew Faustus into the dispute over grace. Lucidus later retracted the view that some are set apart for death and others predestined to life. "We walk the middle way, with Christ as our guide," Faustus wrote to him, in modern words. "After grace, without which we are nothing, we affirm the labour of dutiful service." At the request of a council at Arles he wrote two books On Grace, ready by about 474 in Engelbrecht's reckoning. They open against Pelagius, who, Faustus says, foolishly believed that human weakness could do without grace. Gennadius of Marseilles praised them for teaching "that the grace of God always invites, precedes and helps our will".

A Life of Fulgentius of Ruspe, hostile to Faustus, says he secretly favoured the Pelagians, and that Fulgentius answered him in seven books. The Princeton scholar Benjamin Warfield calls Faustus the chief champion of what he names semi-Pelagianism. He says the Council of Orange put it down in 529. Engelbrecht holds that Faustus was no semi-Pelagian and erred in good faith. A treatise of 534, which Engelbrecht credits to Caesarius of Arles, who led that council, calls him the holy bishop Faustus.

In old age Faustus was exiled by the Gothic king Euric, Engelbrecht thinks. From exile he wrote to Ruricius of Limoges, in modern words, "We are owners without a home of our own, and rich without possessions."
