One noon in Alexandria, John Moschus and his friend Sophronius, later patriarch of Jerusalem, went to study with the philosopher Stephen. He was asleep, so they waited at the Tetrapylon, where three blind men told how each had lost his sight. The third had once stripped a richly dressed corpse in its tomb. The dead man sat up, he said, and tore out his eyes. Sophronius nodded, and they left. "Truly, Abba John, let us study no more today," he said, in modern words. "We have been edified enough."

Moschus, a monk of Palestine, tells this in the Spiritual Meadow, his book of stories about monks of his time, written for Sophronius.

An old notice of his life was set before the Meadow. Daniel Papebroch, a Bollandist scholar, says Photius of Constantinople copied much of it. It says Moschus became a monk at the monastery of Saint Theodosius. He lived long in the desert by the Jordan, then at the New Laura of Saint Sabas. After the emperor Maurice was murdered and the Persians pressed in, he went to Antioch and then Alexandria. Hearing that the holy places had been taken, he sailed for Rome with Sophronius.

There, the notice says, he saw his death coming, wrote the Meadow and gave it to Sophronius. He asked to be buried on Mount Sinai, or if barbarians barred the way, at Saint Theodosius. Raiders barred the road to Sinai, so Sophronius buried him at Saint Theodosius. Papebroch reckons that year as 620.

Johann Albert Fabricius says the Meadow often attacks the followers of Severus of Antioch, who rejected the Council of Chalcedon. It has its share of fabulous tales, he adds. John of Damascus and the Second Council of Nicaea, in 787, cited it as Sophronius's book.

In the dedication, Moschus says he picked the fairest flowers from these lives and wove a crown from a meadow that never fades. "I offer it to you, and through you to all," he writes, in modern words.
