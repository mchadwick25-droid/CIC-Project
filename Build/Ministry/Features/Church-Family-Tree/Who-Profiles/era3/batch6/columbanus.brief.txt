Columbanus was writing to his monks from near the Bretons when a messenger came in. A ship was being made ready, the messenger said, to carry him back to his own country against his will. If he were thrown into the sea like Jonah, he wrote, they should pray that someone would row their Jonah back to land.

Columbanus was an Irish monk who founded monasteries in Frankish Gaul and Lombard Italy. Jonas, a monk of Bobbio, wrote his Life after his death. He names companions of the saint who told him its stories. It is a saint's Life, full of wonders, and it takes his side against his enemies.

Jonas says he was born in Leinster and entered Bangor, the monastery of Abbot Comgall. After many years he longed to live among strangers, and sailed with twelve companions.

In the Vosges wilderness he built houses at Anegray, Luxeuil and Fontaines, and wrote a rule for them, which survives. Each day, it says, a monk must fast, pray, work and read. His Penitential holds that different sins need different penances, as doctors treat wounds and sicknesses in different ways.

Jonas says he rebuked King Theuderic of Burgundy for keeping concubines. When Brunhild, the king's grandmother, brought him the sons of these unions, he refused to bless them. Jonas calls her a second Jezebel. His Irish reckoning of Easter had given offence in Gaul, James Barmby notes. Columbanus wrote to Pope Gregory the Great to defend it. "Error is manifestly ancient," he wrote, in Barmby's English, "but truth which reproves it is ever more ancient still."

About twenty years after he came, Jonas says, the king's men took him to Nantes to ship him home. The ship ran aground until his goods were put ashore, which Jonas reads as God's will that he stay. He preached among the Swabians near Bregenz, then crossed into Italy, where Agilulf, king of the Lombards, received him. The Lombard historian Paul the Deacon tells the same. Jonas says he died at his new monastery of Bobbio after a single year. Jonas's translator, Dana Munro, gives the year as probably 615.

Writing to a council of Gallic bishops, he had asked to live in silence in the Vosges woods, by the bones of seventeen dead brothers. "Let Gaul, I pray, hold us together," he wrote, in modern words, "whom the kingdom of heaven will hold, if our merits are good."
