Radegund's body was carried beneath the wall of her monastery at Poitiers. She had ruled that no living nun should leave it. So the whole community stood on the wall and wept, Baudonivia writes. In place of the psalm they gave tears, and groans in place of the alleluia.

Baudonivia was a nun of Holy Cross at Poitiers, the house Queen Radegund founded. She wrote the second book of Radegund's Life, for the abbess Dedimia and the community. The sources give nothing of her family, age or death. This account rests on her book. The German scholar Bruno Krusch dates it soon after 600, from a bishop of Autun she names.

Venantius Fortunatus had already written Radegund's Life. Baudonivia will not repeat, she says, what the apostolic man Fortunatus, the bishop, wrote. She will tell what he passed over for length. Krusch thinks the nuns missed two things in his book, the coming of the Cross and Radegund's death.

She calls herself the least of all, less learned and more devoted. Krusch notes most of her prologue is copied from Fortunatus's prologues to his Lives of Hilary of Poitiers and Marcellus of Paris. One phrase says Radegund raised her from the cradle as her own household servant. Fortunatus first wrote it of Hilary raising Pascentius, then bishop of Poitiers. There, Krusch says, it means Hilary's monastery raised Pascentius.

What Helena did in the East when she sought out the Cross, Baudonivia writes, Radegund did in Gaul. When the relic reached Poitiers, she says, the bishop and all the people wished to receive it. But the enemy of mankind worked through his agents to keep it out. Gregory of Tours tells it otherwise. Bishop Maroveus, he says, rode off to a country estate rather than receive it.

She tells the death in the voice of the whole house. On Wednesday morning, the thirteenth of August, she writes, Radegund's eyes were closed, and ours were darkened.
