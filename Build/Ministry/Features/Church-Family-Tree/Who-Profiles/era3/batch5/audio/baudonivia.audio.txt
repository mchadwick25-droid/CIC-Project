[calmly] Radegund's body was carried beneath the wall of her monastery at Poitiers. She had ruled that no living nun should leave it. So the whole community stood on the wall and wept, Baudonivia writes. In place of the psalm they gave tears, and groans in place of the alleluia.

Baudonivia was a nun of Holy Cross at Poitiers. She wrote the second book of Radegund's Life, for the abbess Dedimia and the community. No held source gives her family, her age or her death, so this account rests on her book. The German scholar Bruno Krusch dates it soon after 600, from a bishop of Autun she names.

Venantius Fortunatus had already written a Life of Radegund. Baudonivia says she will not repeat what the apostolic man Fortunatus, the bishop, wrote. She will tell what he passed over for length. Krusch thinks the nuns missed two things in his book, the coming of the Cross and Radegund's death.

She calls herself the least of all, less learned and more devoted. Krusch notes that most of her prologue is copied from Fortunatus's prologues to his Lives of Hilary of Poitiers and Marcellus of Paris. One phrase says Radegund raised her from the cradle as her own household servant. Fortunatus first wrote those words of Hilary and the abbot Pascentius, so they do not prove she was Radegund's foster-child.

What Helena did in the East when she sought out the Cross, Baudonivia writes, Radegund did in Gaul. When the relic of the Cross reached Poitiers, she says, the enemy of mankind worked through his agents to keep it out of the city. She names neither the emperor who sent it nor the bishop of Poitiers. Krusch supplies Justin and Maroveus, and Gregory of Tours names Maroveus too.

[warmly] She tells the death in the voice of the whole house. On Wednesday morning, the thirteenth of August, she writes, Radegund's eyes were closed, and ours were darkened.
