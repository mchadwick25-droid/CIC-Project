[calmly] King Reccared came among the bishops gathered at Toledo and prayed with them, the council's acts say. Not many days after his father's death, he reminded them, they had learned he had joined the Catholic faith. All he had to say of his faith was written in a book, to be read before them. The king offered it, and a notary read it out in a clear voice.

Reccared was king of the Visigoths in Spain, the son of King Leovigild. The acts date this Third Council of Toledo to the eighth of May in his fourth year. Their Latin is told here in modern words.

The book speaks as the king, though O. M. Dalton, translating Gregory of Tours, says Bishop Leander of Seville composed the speech. God had given him many peoples to rule, it says, yet he remembered he was mortal. It confesses Father, Son and Spirit as one substance.

God stirred him, burning with faith, to call back a people who served error under the name of religion. The whole Gothic people was there, it says. A great host of Sueves, brought under his kingdom, had also been called back. He offered these noble peoples to God through the bishops' hands, as a holy sacrifice. It condemns Arius and all his teaching. Then Reccared signed, holding this faith in his heart, he wrote, and affirming it with his mouth.

All the witnesses here write from the Catholic side. John of Biclaro, a chronicler of the time, had been exiled by Leovigild. He says Reccared took the throne in peace. In the tenth month of his first year, John says, Reccared became Catholic. Gregory of Tours, a Frankish bishop, tells instead of a debate between the bishops of both sides. Pope Gregory the Great credits Leander's counsel and the merits of Reccared's brother Hermenegild, whom Gregory calls a martyr.

Gregory wrote to Reccared, "I cannot express in words, most excellent son, how much I am delighted with thy work and thy life." Jews offered him money over a law against them, Gregory had heard. Reccared "preferred innocence to gold".

[warmly] Isidore of Seville, Leander's brother, wrote that Leovigild was irreligious and quick to war, and his son pious in faith and famed for peace. At the end Reccared made a public confession of penance, and died peacefully at Toledo after reigning fifteen years.
