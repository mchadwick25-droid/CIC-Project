[calmly] On his last Saturday, Columba went with his attendant Diormit to bless the granary of his monastery on Iona. He said his monks would have enough grain for the year. Then he told Diormit in secret that this day was his Sabbath, his rest. That night he would go the way of his fathers. On the way back, the white horse that carried the milk pails laid its head against his breast, shedding tears. Diormit tried to drive it away. "Let him alone, let him alone, for he loves me," Columba said.

The scene comes from Adomnán, a later abbot of Iona. His translator, Wentworth Huyshe, says Adomnán was born twenty-seven years after Columba died. Adomnán wrote from older writings and from what faithful old men told him. His book is a saint's Life, full of wonders.

Columba was an Irish monk and abbot. Adomnán says he came of noble parents, Fedilmith and Aethne. In his forty-second year, two years after a battle at Cooldrevny, he sailed from Ireland to Britain to make a pilgrimage for Christ. Huyshe puts the voyage in 563. Bede of Jarrow gives 565. Huyshe, with later Irish tradition, makes the battle the cause of his leaving. Adomnán names it only to date the voyage.

Adomnán tells how Brude, king of the Picts, kept his fortress gates shut against him. Columba made the sign of the cross on the doors and knocked, and the bolts flew back. Bede says Columba turned the northern Picts to the faith by his preaching and example, and they gave him the island for a monastery.

Adomnán says he could not let an hour pass without prayer, reading, writing or manual work. Bede adds that he had founded the Field of Oaks in Ireland. Many houses in Britain and Ireland grew from it and from Iona. The island was ruled by its abbot, a priest, and even the bishops were under him, after Columba's example. Bede, who followed the Roman Easter, says Columba's heirs kept uncertain rules for it.

[warmly] That last evening, Adomnán says, Columba was copying the Psalms. He stopped at a verse and left the rest for Baithene to write. In the night he gave his last words for the monks. Only Diormit heard them: "that ye have mutual and unfeigned charity among yourselves, with peace." He died before the altar, his hand raised to bless them. Bede says he was seventy-seven.
