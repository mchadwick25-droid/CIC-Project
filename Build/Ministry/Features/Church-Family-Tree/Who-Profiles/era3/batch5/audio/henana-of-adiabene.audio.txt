[calmly] About three hundred students walked out of the school of Nisibis, the Chronicle of Seert says. They carried Gospels and crosses on black veils, with censers, and sang the hymns of the Rogations. They were leaving in protest against their master, Henana of Adiabene.

Henana led the school of the Church of the East at Nisibis, in the Persian empire. Addai Scher, who edited the chronicle, reckons he became its master in 572. His teaching is known only through his opponents, though a pupil's praise of the man survives.

The chronicle writes against him. It says he explained Scripture against Theodore of Mopsuestia, whom his church called the Interpreter. Abdisho of Nisibis, listing Henana's books, says many were rejected because of Theodore. Here two witnesses agree.

Gregory of Kashkar, metropolitan of Nisibis, condemned his books, the chronicle says. When Sabrisho, head of the church, took Henana's side, Gregory shook the dust off his sandals at the city gate and left. The students followed.

The sharpest charges rest on Babai the Great alone, abbot on Mount Izla. In his Life of the martyr George, a monk says Henana taught that fate rules everything and denied the resurrection of the body. Babai calls him a Chaldean, which Oskar Braun reads as astrology, and a follower of Origen. Braun calls Babai passionate and speaks of his overstatements. J. Labourt calls Babai Henana's antagonist. He cannot say whether the charge about fate is false, though he finds it plausible.

Mari ibn Sulayman, in the 1100s, says Henana had eight hundred students. His pupil Barhadbeshabba wrote the Cause of the Foundation of the Schools in Henana's lifetime, Scher says. Scher thinks the pupil probably hid the truth to praise his master.

[warmly] Barhadbeshabba calls him gentle, merciful and patient, a man who does not seek his own glory. He says his master worked day and night at reading and explaining the Scriptures, and he prays that God will lengthen his days.
