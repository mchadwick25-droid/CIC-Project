[calmly] While Dorotheus of Gaza lived with the abbot Seridus, the abbot John's attendant fell sick. Seridus sent Dorotheus to serve John. Each evening, as Dorotheus left, the old man gave him a saying of the Fathers. Before each, he asked God to keep their love for one another. One night the saying was to flee these human things and be saved. Another night, it was that if we bear one another's burdens, we fulfil the law of Christ. He kept them all, he says, to guard his life. He served John nine years.

He tells this in his Teachings, printed by J.-P. Migne. Elsewhere in them he calls John the disciple of blessed Barsanuphius. Andrea Gallandi's notice calls Dorotheus a Palestinian archimandrite, the head of a monastery. The Bollandist scholars, Gallandi reports, place him late in the sixth century.

His example of cutting off one's own will is the blessed Dositheus. A Greek letter printed before the Teachings calls Dositheus his first disciple. Dositheus came, Dorotheus says, from a soft and pampered life, taught nothing of divine things. By obedience alone, he reached the height of perfection in a short time.

Once a leading man of Gaza heard the monks talking of humility, and asked how this could be. Dorotheus asked where he stood among his fellow citizens. As a noble, the man said. At Caesarea he would rank a little below its nobles, and at Antioch he would be a peasant. Before the king at Byzantium, he would be a poor beggar. Holy men are the same, Dorotheus said, and the nearer they come to God, the lower they think themselves.

A later Greek book of saints, revised by Nikodemos the Hagiorite, says he founded his own monastery after John died. The scholar François Nau instead makes him a later head of Seridus's monastery.

[warmly] Gallandi calls his twenty-four Teachings an outstanding work, most worth reading, and says Theodore the Studite approved them highly.
