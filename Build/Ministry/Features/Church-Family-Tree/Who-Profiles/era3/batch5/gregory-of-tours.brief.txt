In 573 Gregory of Tours became bishop of Tours, in the church of Merovingian Gaul, a land split among rival Frankish kings at war with one another. People lamented, he says, "Alas for our times, because the study of literature has died out among us," so he wrote ten books of History himself, though his speech, he admits, was rude.

He opened them with his creed, "so that anyone who reads may have no doubt that I hold the catholic faith," and a king put that faith to the test. Chilperic decreed that the Trinity be called only God, with no distinct Persons, and told Gregory, "I want you and the other teachers of the churches to believe this." Gregory refused. Only the Son was made man and suffered, he answered, so that He might be offered for the world, and the king must hold to "what Hilary and Eusebius taught." These were Hilary, the bishop of Poitiers, and Eusebius of Vercelli, his great helper against heresy. The king said he would find wiser men, and put the decree to Salvius, bishop of Albi, who would have torn the paper up. "So the king gave up this plan," Gregory writes. In his telling, the bishops' refusal ended a king's attempt to set the faith of his church. He does not say his stand cost him anything.

He held the same ground against the Arians of Spain. When their king's envoy Agilan argued at Tours that the Son was lesser than the Father, Gregory answered that the Father was never without wisdom or light, and so never without the Son. Agilan swore he would take no blessing from a priest of Gregory's faith, though later, Gregory reports, sickness in Spain drove him to that faith.

Two centuries later the historian Paul the Deacon sent readers to "the books of that revered man Gregory, bishop of Tours." Much of what is known of the church in Gaul under the first Frankish kings comes from those books, and for his stands against Chilperic and Agilan, his is the only account we have.
