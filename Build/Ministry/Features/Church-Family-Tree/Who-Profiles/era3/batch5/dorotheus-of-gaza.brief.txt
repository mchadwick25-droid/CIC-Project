While Dorotheus of Gaza lived with the abbot Seridus, the abbot John's attendant fell sick. The abbot sent Dorotheus to serve him. Each evening, as Dorotheus left, the old man gave him a saying of the Fathers. Before each, he asked God to keep their love for one another. One night the saying was to flee these human things and be saved. Another night, it was that if we bear one another's burdens, we fulfil the law of Christ. He kept them all, he says, to guard his life. He served John nine years.

He tells this in his Teachings, printed by J.-P. Migne in Greek and Latin. Andrea Gallandi's notice, which Migne reprints, calls him a Palestinian archimandrite, the head of a monastery. It makes John, disciple of Barsanuphius, his master. Gallandi reports that the Bollandists place him at the end of the sixth century. His death year, Gallandi says, is not clearly known. François Nau puts his birth about 540.

His example of cutting off one's own will is the blessed Dositheus. Dositheus came, he says, from a soft and pampered life, taught nothing of divine things. By obedience alone, he reached the height of perfection in a short time.

Once a leading man of Gaza heard the monks talking of humility, and asked how this could be. Dorotheus asked where he stood among his fellow citizens. As a noble, the man said. At Caesarea he would rank a little below its nobles, and at Antioch he would be a peasant. Before the king at Byzantium, he would be a poor beggar. Holy men are the same, Dorotheus said, and the nearer they come to God, the lower they think themselves.

A later Greek book of saints, revised by Nikodemos the Hagiorite, says he founded his own monastery after John died.

Gallandi calls his twenty-four Teachings an outstanding work, most worth reading, and says Theodore the Studite approved them highly.
