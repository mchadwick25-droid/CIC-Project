Pope Gregory the Great remembered where he came to know Leander. "When I knew you long since at Constantinople, my most blessed brother," he wrote. Gregory was there on the business of the see of Rome. Leander had come on an embassy "on counts touching the faith of the Wisigoths". Gregory's monks begged him to explain the book of Job, and Leander added his voice. The letter opens his finished Moralia on Job.

Leander was bishop of Seville in the Visigothic kingdom of Spain. His younger brother, Isidore of Seville, followed him as bishop and wrote of him. Through Leander's faith and work, Isidore says, the Gothic people turned from the madness of Arius's teaching to the Catholic faith. Isidore writes as Leander's brother and from his side.

Leander wrote two books against the heretics in exile abroad, Isidore says, though not where or when. Gregory the Great says King Leovigild had greatly persecuted him. Travellers from Spain told Gregory that Leander turned the king's son Hermenegild to the Catholic faith. Gregory of Tours, a Frankish bishop, credits Hermenegild's wife Ingund. Near death, Gregory the Great writes, Leovigild entrusted his other son Reccared to Leander. The historian Altamira, as O. M. Dalton reports, found no proof of this in records of the time.

Reccared became Catholic, and in 589 his bishops met at Toledo for its Third Council. John of Biclaro, a chronicler of the time, says its business lay chiefly with Leander and the abbot Eutropius. In a homily under his name, told here in modern words, Leander calls this the most solemn of feasts, because the conversion of so many peoples is new. We once groaned at their harshness, he says, and now rejoice at their faith.

He also wrote a rule for his sister Florentina. He searched for riches to leave her as his heir, he says, and found nothing under the sun worthy of her. Christ is her bridegroom, brother and friend, and her share of the inheritance. He asks her not to forget their younger brother Isidore, left a child when their parents died.

When Leander died is disputed. Isidore says only that it was under Reccared. An epitaph's date comes to 603 in Theodor Mommsen's reckoning, and he doubts it. Nicola Turchi gives 600 or 601, and Edmund Gardner 599.

Gregory once wrote to him, "I carry the image of thy countenance stamped within the bowels of my heart."
