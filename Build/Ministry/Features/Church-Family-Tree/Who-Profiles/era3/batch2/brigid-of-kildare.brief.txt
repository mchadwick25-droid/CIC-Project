In the great church at Kildare, Cogitosus writes, Brigid and Bishop Conleath lay on either side of the altar. Their tombs were adorned with gold, silver and precious stones, and crowns of gold and silver hung above them. A painted wall crossed the church. The bishop and his clergy passed through one door to the altar, and the abbess and her nuns through another.

Brigid founded the monastery of Kildare in Ireland. Cogitosus calls it the head of almost all the Irish churches. He wrote her Life in Latin at the brothers' request, from what he says elders had handed down. It is hagiography, and mostly miracles.

Cogitosus says she was born to noble Christian parents, Dubtach and Brocca. She called a holy hermit from his solitude to govern the church with her as bishop. In one story she gives the poor the foreign robes Conleath wore at the altar. When the feast comes, a two-wheeled chariot brings others just like them.

Cogitosus gives no year. He says she died on the first of February. The Welsh annals put her death in the year Columba was born. Extracts from Nennius, printed by Whitley Stokes, put four years between the two.

When Cogitosus wrote is also disputed. Migne prints the Life under the year 591. Basnage, in the note printed with it, says that placing follows others. He thought Cogitosus wrote much later, since he describes painted images and miracles at her tomb. In his Life of Patrick, Muirchu names his father Cogitosus. His editor Newport White takes the father for Brigid's biographer.

A later Irish Life, in the fifteenth-century Book of Lismore, makes her mother a bondmaid named Broicsech. It says that when Bishop Mél gave her the veil, the form for making a bishop was read over her.

On her feast, Cogitosus says, crowds came to Kildare. Some came for the food, the sick for healing, some to watch the crowds, and some with great gifts.
