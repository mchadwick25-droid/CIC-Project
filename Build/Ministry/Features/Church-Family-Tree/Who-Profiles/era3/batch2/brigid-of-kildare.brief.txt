In her great church on the plains of the Liffey, Cogitosus writes, Brigid and Bishop Conleath lay on either side of the altar. Their tombs were adorned with gold, silver and precious stones, and crowns of gold and silver hung above them. A painted wall crossed the church. The bishop and his clergy passed through one door to the altar, and the abbess and her nuns through another.

Brigid founded the monastery of Kildare in Ireland. Cogitosus calls it the head of almost all the Irish churches. He wrote her Life in Latin long after her death, at the brothers' request, from what he says elders had handed down. It is hagiography, and mostly miracles.

Cogitosus says she was born to noble Christian parents, Dubtach and Brocca. She called a holy hermit from his solitude to govern the church with her as bishop. In one story she gives the poor the foreign robes Conleath wore at the altar. When the feast comes, a two-wheeled chariot brings others just like them.

Cogitosus gives no year. He says she died on the first of February. The Welsh annals put her death in the year Saint Columcille was born. Extracts from Nennius, printed by Whitley Stokes, put four years between his birth and her death.

How long after her Cogitosus wrote is disputed. Migne prints the Life under the year 591. Basnage, in the note printed with it, says that placing follows others. He thought Cogitosus wrote much later, since he describes painted images and miracles in her church. In his Life of Patrick, Muirchu names his father Cogitosus. His editor Newport White takes the father for Brigid's biographer.

A later Irish Life, in the fifteenth-century Book of Lismore, makes her mother a bondmaid named Broicsech.

On her feast, Cogitosus says, crowds came to her church. Some came for the food, the sick for healing, some to watch the crowds, and some with great gifts.
