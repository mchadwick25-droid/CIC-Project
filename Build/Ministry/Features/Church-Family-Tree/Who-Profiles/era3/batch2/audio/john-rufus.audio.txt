[calmly] One day in Jerusalem, John Rufus walked the road from Siloam up the valley with a notable of the city. Below the mountain he saw a large old monastery falling into ruin. His companion smiled and said it had been the monastery of Juvenal, bishop of Jerusalem. It became a desert, he said, when the council of Chalcedon met. John answered that Juvenal had been the companion of Judas, as Dioscorus of Alexandria had said.

John tells this in his Plerophories, a book of testimonies against Chalcedon. François Nau, its French editor, draws John's life from the chapters where he speaks of himself. By Nau's account John was an Arab of southern Palestine, probably from Ascalon, and studied law at Beirut. Peter the Fuller, patriarch of Antioch, whose close aide he had been, made him a priest there.

When Peter the Fuller was driven out, John went to Palestine. There, Nau says, he came to know Peter the Iberian, the anti-Chalcedonian bishop of Maiuma near Gaza. In his Life of Severus, Zachariah Rhetor says that after Peter died the altar was kept for John, called Rufus. The book's heading calls John bishop of Maiuma.

Nau says John wrote in Greek soon after 512, while Severus was patriarch of Antioch. It survives in Syriac, in 89 short chapters of visions and wonders, most of them told by Peter the Iberian. In one chapter John says he checked a story with people who had seen it. Nau calls the book a new kind of collection of sayings, written to fight the council and its defenders.

A Greek Life of Peter the Iberian from about 500, held in Syriac, names no author. Its German editor, Richard Raabe, asks whether the author of the Plerophories wrote it too, and leaves the question open.

[warmly] The book's first chapter begins with a story Peter the Iberian used to tell, and John calls Peter his father and bishop.
