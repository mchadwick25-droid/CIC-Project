[calmly] Two deacons of Rome, Pelagius and Anatolius, wrote to Ferrandus, a deacon of Carthage, with a question. They asked him to answer after talking with his bishop. Facundus of Hermiane, an African bishop, tells this in a book addressed to the emperor Justinian.

Facundus says that enemies of the Council of Chalcedon wanted three things condemned. These were Theodore of Mopsuestia and his teaching, some writings of Theodoret of Cyrrhus, and a letter of Ibas of Edessa that the council had judged sound. His book defends all three, the Three Chapters of its title, and he writes as a partisan.

A short anonymous book on church writers, printed with Isidore of Seville's, says the Romans asked whether a man may be condemned after his death. Ferrandus's answer survives. What holy bishops decide in council, he writes, must be followed and not reopened. Then he asks what good it does to fight with the dead. If God has pardoned a man, our severity does him no harm. If God has prepared his punishment, our kindness does him no good.

He also wrote to Fulgentius of Ruspe, whom he calls his absent teacher. One letter asks about an Ethiopian baptized when illness had taken his mind, who died before receiving communion. He made a short digest of church laws as well.

Whether he wrote the Life of Fulgentius is disputed. Its author says he became a monk in the small monastery Fulgentius built in exile in Sardinia. Chifflet, his editor of 1649, gave the Life to him. A note in Migne's Fulgentius says others doubt it, since the author seems never to have left Fulgentius's side. Gallandi, another editor, notes that the Sardinian years come only from the Life.

Victor of Tunnuna's chronicle says Ferrandus was held in high regard in the year its editor gives as 546. [warmly] Facundus speaks of him as a man of praiseworthy memory, so Gallandi takes him to be dead by 547.
