[calmly] Around the year 500, Italy was ruled by Gothic kings. Gregory the Great tells of a young man from the province of Nursia who was sent to Rome to study. There he saw many of the students fall into loose living, so he left his books and his father's house. He was Benedict of Nursia, and the early Benedictines of Italy begin with him. Nearly all we know of his life comes from Gregory's Dialogues, written about fifty years after Benedict died, from what four of his disciples told him. Gregory wrote to show what God did through a saint, so the book is full of wonders.

Benedict built twelve monasteries near Subiaco and later moved to Cassino, where country people still worshipped Apollo. There, Gregory says, he broke the idol and by his preaching brought the people to the faith. When the Gothic king Totila came to him, Benedict told him, "You do many evil things." From then on, Gregory says, the king was less cruel.

The Rule is Benedict's own words. In it he founds "a school for the Lord's service, and in setting it up we hope to lay down nothing harsh and nothing heavy." His monks promise "stability, a changed way of life, and obedience," and divide their days between prayer, work with their hands and holy reading. The abbot must so temper all things "that the strong have something to long for and the weak do not run away." For more, the last chapter points to the Lives of the Fathers, to the Rule of Basil of Caesarea, and to John Cassian's Conferences of the Fathers.

Gregory, by then pope, praised the Rule as "outstanding for its sound judgment and clear in its language," and in 742 the first council of Carloman, duke of the Franks, told monks and nuns to live by Benedict's Rule. At the next, the abbots and monks took it up to restore the norm of the regular life. [warmly] In 802 Charlemagne had the monks at Aachen read it together, and ordered that whatever in the monasteries went against it be put right by it.
