Caesarius, bishop of Arles in Gaul, built a convent for women beside a church in his city. Then he called his sister Caesaria back from a convent at Marseille. He had sent her there, his disciples write, so that she might learn what she would teach, and be a pupil before she was a teacher. She moved into the rooms made ready for her with two or three companions.

Caesaria of Arles was the first abbess of that house. Her story comes almost wholly from her brother's Life. It was written after his death by his disciples, the bishops Cyprian of Toulon, Firminus and Viventius, with the priest Messianus and the deacon Stephen. It is a saint's Life, and no words of her own survive in it.

Bruno Krusch, who edited the Life, dates the dedication of the convent to 512. He takes the house at Marseille to be the one John Cassian founded. The Life itself names neither.

Crowds of young women came, giving up their goods and families. They were enclosed so that none might leave by the convent door until they died. Caesarius wrote a Rule for them.

He built a church where the women would be buried. Not long after, the Life says, he buried his sister there, the mother of the convent. He laid her among the women who had died before her, beside the tomb he had made ready for himself. Another Caesaria followed her as abbess. When Caesarius died, this second Caesaria led more than two hundred of them.

The two abbesses share a name and have been confused. The Life was written for the second, who was still living. A Bollandist commentary, printed in Jacques-Paul Migne's Latin edition, says some writers wrongly took it for the sister.

In the Life, the women who came to the convent sought the care of Caesarius their father and Caesaria their mother.
