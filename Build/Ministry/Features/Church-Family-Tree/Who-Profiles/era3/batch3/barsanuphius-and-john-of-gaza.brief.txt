Dorotheus of Gaza put a question to his teacher, the abbot John of Gaza. How could he hold back his too free manner and rule his tongue? By mourning, the answer said. Then how, he asked, could he keep mourning while dealing every day with people of the world? Mourning does not come through tears, the answer ran. Tears come through mourning.

The questions and answers survive among Dorotheus's writings, in a Latin translation printed by J.-P. Migne. Its heading sends them to the great old man Barsanuphius and to his disciple, the abbot John. It says John was called the Prophet for his gift of insight.

Dorotheus came to know the two old men when he entered the monastery of the abbot Seridus. So says Migne's notice on Dorotheus, citing a Life of Dositheus. The French scholar François Nau places that monastery near the village of Thabatha. Jerome put Thabatha about five miles south of Gaza.

Dorotheus later told other monks how he leaned on John. He took everything to him, he says. Once a thought told him not to trouble the old man, since he knew the answer already. Dorotheus cursed the thought and went anyway, and often John gave the very answer the thought had foreseen. When Dorotheus worried that he felt no hardship at all, he asked John by letter. Whoever gives himself to obedience, John told him, gains this rest from God.

Only the first question is marked as sent to John, so the text does not show which old man gave each later answer.

A preface that Migne prints without an author's name says there were two men named Barsanuphius and two named Dorotheus. One pair, it says, defended the errors of Severus of Antioch, and the other kept sound teaching. It calls Theodore the Studite as its witness. Theodore says the men who were condemned were others.

Barsanuphius's image, Theodore writes, stood in the Great Church beside the holy fathers Antony and Ephrem.
