Two envoys came from Constantinople to the chief priest of Rome, as Procopius of Caesarea calls the pope. They came about a point of faith that Christians quarrelled over. He knew the points well, he writes, but would not record them. "I consider it a sort of insane folly to investigate the nature of God," he explains. Of God he will say only that "He is altogether good and has all things in His power."

Procopius was born at Caesarea in Palestine, late in the fifth century, says H. B. Dewing, his English translator. He trained in law and went to Constantinople. He says himself that he was chosen as adviser to the general Belisarius in the war with Persia. He followed Belisarius to Africa and Italy, and saw much of what he tells.

His three works judge the same reign in three ways. Dewing notes the calm tone of the History of the Wars. It tells how Belisarius sent Silverius, the chief priest of Rome, away to Greece on a charge of plotting with the Goths, and put Vigilius in his place. The Buildings praises Justinian's churches. He writes that the dome of Hagia Sophia seemed "suspended from heaven by the fabled golden chain." Dewing calls the Buildings the work of a fawning flatterer, but still a storehouse of facts about the empire.

The Secret History attacks the emperor, the empress Theodora and even Belisarius. Dewing says its slander is so extreme that it cannot be believed. The unnamed English translator of 1896 thinks it exaggerates but gives a faithful picture of the court. Some have doubted that Procopius wrote it. Dewing thinks it highly probable that he did. The 1896 translator notes that the first writers to name him as its author lived centuries later.

Dewing says Procopius writes at times as a Christian and at times like a follower of the old Greek gods. The 1896 translator thinks he was a sceptic who used Christian forms under an orthodox emperor.
