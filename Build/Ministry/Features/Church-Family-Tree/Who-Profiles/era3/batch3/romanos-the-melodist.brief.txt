One Christmas night, a book of saints says, the Mother of God came to Romanos the Melodist in his sleep. She handed him a roll of paper and told him to take it and eat it. He woke full of wonder, went up into the pulpit and began to sing of the Virgin giving birth that day.

The story comes from one short notice in a Menologion, a book of saints for the church year. Cardinal Jean-Baptiste Pitra, who printed it in 1888, calls it the Menologion of the emperor Constantine Porphyrogenitus. Migne's edition of Theodore the Studite gives the same Vatican manuscript to the emperor Basil. Pitra says almost nothing more is recorded of Romanos anywhere.

The notice says Romanos came from Syria and was a deacon of the church at Berytus, now Beirut. He came to Constantinople in the time of the emperor Anastasius. He lived at a church of the Mother of God and kept night vigils at the shrine of Blachernae. He wrote about a thousand kontakia, long sung poems for the feasts, and then died. A later Greek book of saints, revised by Nikodemos the Hagiorite, names his home city as Emesa.

Pitra asks which Anastasius is meant, the first or the second, and looks for clues in one of the poems. He says it is not at all clear that Romanos lived under the second, who reigned from 713 to 716.

Pitra printed three poems from manuscripts on the island of Patmos: on Stephen the first martyr, on Joseph the chaste, and on the ten virgins. He set a Latin prose version beside the Greek, and the words here are from it. In two, Pitra's headings say, the first letters of the verses name the author as humble Romanos.

Each verse of the poem on the ten virgins ends with the plea of those left outside the bridal chamber. In Pitra's Latin it is "Aperi nobis": open to us.
