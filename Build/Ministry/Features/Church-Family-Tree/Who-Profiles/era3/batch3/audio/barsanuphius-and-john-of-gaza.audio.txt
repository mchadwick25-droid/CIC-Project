[calmly] Dorotheus of Gaza put a question to his teacher, the abbot John of Gaza. How could he hold back his too free manner and rule his tongue? By mourning, the answer said. Then how, he asked, could he keep mourning while dealing every day with people of the world? Mourning does not come through tears, the answer ran. Tears come through mourning.

J.-P. Migne prints the questions and answers among Dorotheus's writings, in Greek and Latin. Their heading sends them to the great old man Barsanuphius and his disciple, the abbot John. It says John was called the Prophet for his gift of insight.

The church historian Evagrius Scholasticus calls Barsanuphius an Egyptian, shut in a cell near Gaza. No one had seen him for over fifty years, Evagrius says. Dorotheus came to know the two old men when he entered the monastery of the abbot Seridus. So says a Life of Dositheus, as Andrea Gallandi's notice reports it.

Dorotheus later told monks how he took everything to John. When a thought told him not to trouble the old man, since he knew the answer already, Dorotheus cursed the thought and went anyway. Often John gave the very answer the thought had foreseen. When Dorotheus worried that he felt no hardship, he asked John by letter. Whoever gives himself to obedience, John told him, gains this rest from God.

Only the first question is marked as sent to John, so the text does not show which old man gave each later answer.

A preface that Migne prints without an author's name says there were two men named Barsanuphius and two named Dorotheus. One pair, it says, defended the errors of Severus of Antioch, and the other kept sound teaching. It calls Theodore the Studite as its witness. Theodore says the men who were condemned were others.

[warmly] Barsanuphius's image, Theodore writes, stood in the Great Church beside the holy fathers Antony and Ephrem.
