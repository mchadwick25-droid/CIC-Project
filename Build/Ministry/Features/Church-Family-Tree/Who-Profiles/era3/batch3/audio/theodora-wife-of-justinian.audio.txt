[calmly] Mare, the exiled bishop of Amida, sent his deacon Stephen from Petra to plead at court. John of Ephesus says God led Stephen to "Theodora who came from the brothel." She was then a patrician, married to Justinian, the emperor's nephew. She begged her husband with tears to ask his uncle for relief. An order came, and the exiles moved to Alexandria.

John wrote as her friend. R. Payne Smith, who put John's Church History into English, notes that John belonged to the party that refused the Council of Chalcedon. Procopius of Caesarea wrote as her enemy in his Secret History. He says her father kept the bears for the Green faction at Byzantium, and that she went on the stage as a girl. The rest he tells as scandal. H. B. Dewing, who translated his Wars, says we are not forced to believe it. The twelfth-century patriarch Michael the Syrian made her a priest's daughter from Daman, near Callinicus, raised in chastity.

In the Wars, Procopius gives Theodora a speech in the Nika riot of 532, as the court weighed flight by sea. She ends, "royalty is a good burial-shroud." The words are Procopius's own telling. Dewing notes his love of sounding phrases in speeches.

Evagrius of Epiphania, a later historian on Chalcedon's side, says Justinian upheld the council and Theodora backed the single nature of Christ. He offers two reasons: true conviction, or an agreement between them. Procopius's Secret History calls it a plan to divide the Christians. Evagrius adds that she persuaded Justinian to send for Severus of Antioch.

John tells what she did for his side. When Justinian raged at Anthimus, who had laid down his office as patriarch of Constantinople, she hid him in her palace until her death. She gathered no fewer than five hundred persecuted monks into the palace of Hormisdas. Every two or three days she came down to be blessed by them. In the sixteenth year of Justinian's reign, the Saracen king Hereth Bar Gabala asked her for bishops. She gave the order, and James and Theodore were made bishops, James for Edessa.

Michael the Syrian says she died in the twentieth year of Justinian's reign. E. W. Brooks, John's English translator, gives 548. John says she left the emperor a charge to care for her monks.

[warmly] John calls her the Christ-loving Theodora, "perhaps appointed queen by God to be a support for the persecuted."
