Cosmas was sailing for Further India, he writes, when his ship came near the mouth of the ocean. A great flock of birds followed it, and everyone on board was afraid. Seasoned passengers and sailors cried to the pilot: "Steer the ship to port and make for the gulf, or we shall be swept along by the currents and be carried into the ocean and be lost."

Cosmas was a merchant on the Mediterranean, the Red Sea and the Persian Gulf. Later he became a monk and wrote his Christian Topography in Greek. His English translator, J. W. McCrindle, thinks he most probably came from Alexandria. He is called Indicopleustes, the Indian Navigator.

At Adulis, the port of the Ethiopian kingdom of Axum, Cosmas copied old Greek inscriptions for the governor. The king was then preparing a war across the gulf. McCrindle dates the visit to 525 and the writing to about 547.

Photius of Constantinople, a later patriarch, sums up the book's views: the earth is a flat oblong, and the sky a vault over it. Cosmas took the tabernacle of Moses as a copy of the whole world. He learned this, he says, from Scripture and from "that most divine man and great teacher Patricius," who had become Bishop Catholic of all Persia. McCrindle takes Patricius for Mar Aba, head of the Church of the East. He thinks Cosmas most probably belonged to that church, following the earlier scholar De La Croze. Yet Cosmas once calls Mary the Mother of God, a title McCrindle says that church found abhorrent.

The book is a witness to churches across the East. In Ceylon, Cosmas says, there is "a Church of Christians, with clergy and a body of believers." At Calliana in India there is "a bishop, who is appointed from Persia."

"This I avouch to be the veritable fact," Cosmas writes of the gospel's spread, "from what I have seen and heard in the many places which I have visited."
