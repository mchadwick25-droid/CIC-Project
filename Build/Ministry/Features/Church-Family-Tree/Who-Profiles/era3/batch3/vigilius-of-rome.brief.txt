On Saint Cecilia's feast, 22 November, an officer sent by the empress Theodora found Pope Vigilius in her church in Rome. The officer took him to the Tiber and put him on a ship. As it moved off, the crowd threw stones and sticks and cried, "Your hunger go with you!"

The scene comes from the Book of the Popes, a Roman record of the popes' lives. Its life of Vigilius is hostile and reports Romans calling him a murderer. Louise Loomis, its translator, finds the life full of mistakes and the scene told nowhere else.

Vigilius, a Roman deacon, had been the pope's envoy at Constantinople. In 537 the general Belisarius sent Pope Silverius away, fearing he was plotting with the Goths. The historian Procopius of Caesarea says Belisarius "appointed another man, Vigilius by name, to the office of chief priest."

How he won the post is disputed. Liberatus of Carthage says Theodora had him promise in secret to set aside the Council of Chalcedon, and offered him gold. Liberatus and Victor of Tunnuna both copy a letter from Vigilius to three bishops Rome had condemned. In it he says he shares their faith. Theodor Mommsen, who edited Victor, says both men hated Vigilius, and with reason. Loomis, citing the French scholar Louis Duchesne, thinks the letter most likely forged; Mommsen leaves it open.

At Constantinople the emperor Justinian wanted the Three Chapters condemned. These were writings of Theodore of Mopsuestia and Theodoret of Cyrrhus, and a letter of Ibas of Edessa. Loomis says Vigilius condemned them in 548, in a ruling called the Judicatum, but held to Chalcedon.

A council met in the city in May 553 without his consent, Bishop Hefele records. On 14 May Vigilius issued his Constitutum. He did not dare condemn Theodore, he wrote, nor would he let anyone else condemn him. The council condemned them anyway.

That December, writing to Eutychius, bishop of Constantinople, Vigilius gave way and condemned the Three Chapters. Henry Percival, who prints it in English, says some have doubted it is real, but most scholars accept it.

Vigilius died of the stone in Sicily on his way home, the Book of the Popes says. Loomis reports a record of the time giving 7 June 555.

In that letter Vigilius pointed to Augustine of Hippo, who had corrected his own writings. When further study shows the truth, he wrote, "there ought to be no shame."
