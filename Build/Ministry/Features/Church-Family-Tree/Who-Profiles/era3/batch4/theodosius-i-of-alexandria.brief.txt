On the night the patriarch Timothy died, the new bishop kept watch over the body. At Alexandria, the custom was that the new bishop laid the dead man's right hand on his own head, buried him, and put on the pallium of Saint Mark. But before Theodosius could finish, the people and the monks learned what had been done. They drove him out. So says Liberatus of Carthage, a deacon who wrote while Theodosius still lived.

Theodosius I of Alexandria was patriarch of the church in Egypt that refused the council of Chalcedon, and he spent most of his time as patriarch in exile. The Syriac chronicle that goes under the name of Zachariah Rhetor calls him learned, kind and gentle, and a friend of Severus of Antioch. A rival, Gaianus, was set up in his place. The empress Theodora had Theodosius restored. Liberatus says her man Narses did it by force and fire, and that the city split into Theodosians and Gaianites.

The History of the Patriarchs of Alexandria, a later Coptic history that Eusèbe Renaudot found far from Liberatus's account, tells how Justinian offered him the governorship of Egypt too, if he would accept the Tome of Leo and Chalcedon. He refused. "The prince has no power except over my body," he says there, "but the Lord Jesus Christ, the true and great prince, has power over my soul and body together."

Summoned to Constantinople, he still would not accept Chalcedon. John of Ephesus says he and about three hundred bishops and clergy were held at Dercus, a fortress in Thrace, where John often visited them. He would not ordain outside his own see, John says, but by his mandate twelve bishops were made for Egypt. With his permission, Jacob Baradaeus went to Alexandria to make bishops there.

Two of his letters survive in that chronicle. To Anthimus of Constantinople he wrote, "For I, who am feeble, judge that it is on account of my shortcomings that I endure all the troubles which befall me." He accepted, he said, the faith of Nicaea and the formula of Zeno uniting the churches, which aimed to annul Chalcedon.

He died at Constantinople, John says, nine months after Justinian. E. W. Brooks puts that in 566; Evetts's heading in the History gives 567. The History says believers took the name Theodosians "after his name". It counts his patriarchate as thirty-two years.
