When the patriarch Timothy died, the new bishop kept watch over the body by night. By custom at Alexandria, the successor laid the dead man's right hand on his own head. Then he buried him and put on the pallium of Saint Mark. But before Theodosius could finish, the people and the monks learned what had been done. They drove him out. So says Liberatus of Carthage, a deacon on the side of Chalcedon who wrote while Theodosius still lived.

Theodosius I of Alexandria was patriarch of the church in Egypt that refused the council of Chalcedon. He spent most of his time as patriarch in exile. The Syriac chronicle under the name of Zachariah Rhetor, from his own side, calls him learned, kind and gentle, and a friend of Severus of Antioch. A rival, Gaianus, was set up in his place, though that chronicle puts Gaianus first. The empress Theodora had Theodosius restored. Liberatus says her chamberlain Narses did it by force and fire, and that the city split into Theodosians and Gaianites.

The History of the Patriarchs of Alexandria is a later history written in his own church. It tells how Justinian offered him the governorship of Egypt if he would accept the Tome of Leo. He refused. "The prince has no power except over my body," he says there, "but the Lord Jesus Christ, the true and great prince, has power over my soul and body together."

Summoned to Constantinople, he still refused Chalcedon. John of Ephesus, of his own party, says he and about three hundred bishops and clergy were held at Dercus, a fortress in Thrace. John often visited them there. He would not ordain outside his own see, John says, but by his mandate twelve bishops were made for Egypt. With his permission, Jacob Baradaeus made bishops at Alexandria.

Two of his letters survive in that chronicle. To Anthimus of Constantinople he wrote, "For I, who am feeble, judge that it is on account of my shortcomings that I endure all the troubles which befall me." He accepted, he said, the faith of Nicaea and the emperor Zeno's formula uniting the churches, which aimed to annul Chalcedon.

He died at Constantinople, John says, nine months after Justinian. E. W. Brooks, John's translator, puts that in 566. B. Evetts, who translated the History, gives 567. The History says believers took the name Theodosians "after his name".
