For ten years or more, Gildas says, he held back. "I have kept silence, I confess, with infinite sorrow of heart," he writes, "for the past ten years or even longer." At last he wrote a long letter in Latin to the rulers and clergy of Britain, "more in tears than in denunciation," as he puts it.

That letter, The Ruin of Britain, is almost all that survives from this British churchman of the sixth century. Hugh Williams, who put it into English, says it is "in no way a history". He calls Gildas "a preacher, a revivalist".

Gildas gives one fact about himself. He was born in the year of the siege of Badon Hill, almost the last great British victory over the Saxons. That year, he writes, began forty-four years before, "with one month now elapsed". Bede took the years from the coming of the Saxons. Williams follows the scholar Ussher: Gildas wrote some forty-three years after his birth. Williams puts that birth about 500, a date no early source gives. He dates the book after 540 and before 547, because a king it attacks, Maglocunus, was still alive. The Welsh annals have that king die in the great plague of 547.

"Kings Britain has, but they are as her tyrants," Gildas writes. He names five, and calls Maglocunus "the island dragon", "last in my writing, first in wickedness". Then he turns on the clergy: "Priests Britain has, but foolish ones." He spares "a very few", the church's "only real children", who he says hold up his own weakness.

Readers abroad soon knew his name. The Irish saint Columbanus wrote to Pope Gregory the Great about bishops whom "the writer Giltas" calls pests. Williams dates the letter between 595 and 600. Bede later called Gildas the Britons' "own historian".

Two Lives tell much more, but they are late. Williams dates the core of the Breton Life, by a monk of Ruys, to the ninth century. Caradoc of Llancarfan wrote the Welsh Life in the twelfth. Williams calls its Glastonbury chapters "a piece of literary fiction". The Breton Life says that Gildas, dying, asked to be laid in a boat on his stone pillow and pushed out to sea.

Alcuin of York, writing in the 790s by Williams's dates, called him the wisest of the Britons. He told his readers to learn from Gildas why the Britons lost their land.
