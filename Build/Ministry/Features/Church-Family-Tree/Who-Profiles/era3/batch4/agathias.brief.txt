In the winter after a great earthquake at Constantinople, Agathias writes, the people turned good for a while. Some left everything for a lonely life in the mountains. At night the rich walked the streets, giving food and clothes to the poor. As soon as the fear passed, most went back to their old ways. We taste good works, he says, only while need drives us and fear presses. His Greek is told here from an old Latin version.

Agathias gives his own name, town and trade. He came from Myrina in Asia, the son of Memnonius, and he practised Roman law. B. G. Niebuhr, who edited him in 1828, says he studied at Alexandria and pleaded cases in Constantinople to earn his living. He turned from love poems to history, he says, so that his life would be of some use to the public.

His five books carry on the history of Procopius of Caesarea, as Evagrius Scholasticus, writing a generation later, records. Church matters come in only in passing. He tells how Justinian raised the fallen middle of the dome of Hagia Sophia higher and made it stronger. He says the Franks are all Christians, with priests in their cities, and keep the feasts just as we do. Of peoples who worship trees and rivers he writes that those who stray from the truth deserve pity rather than hatred.

Whether he was a Christian is disputed. G. J. Vossius thought him a pagan, because he tells of the martyr Stephen only as something they say. Niebuhr thinks one of his poems, dedicated to the archangel, puts it beyond doubt that he professed the faith. But he guesses that Agathias was born to a pagan father and joined the Christians to escape the laws, not from belief.

Niebuhr thinks he died before 582. He finds the style poor, but says Agathias wrote free of attack and of flattery alike, and was a much better man than Procopius.
