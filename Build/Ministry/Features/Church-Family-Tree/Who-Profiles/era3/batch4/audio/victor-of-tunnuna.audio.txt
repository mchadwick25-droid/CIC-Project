[calmly] In his own chronicle, Victor of Tunnuna names himself as the author of this work. He tells how he was kept under guard and beaten, first in a monastery at Mandracium, then on the island of Aegimurus. Then he was sent to Alexandria with Theodore, another African bishop, for defending the Three Chapters. There he was thrown into prison in the fort of Diocletian. Theodor Mommsen, who edited the chronicle, puts this in 555.

Victor was a bishop in Roman Africa. How the name of his town should be spelled is not clear, Mommsen says. He thinks it lay perhaps not far from Carthage.

The Three Chapters were writings of three earlier churchmen. The emperor Justinian condemned them. Victor stood with the African bishops who defended them. He writes that they cut off Vigilius, bishop of Rome, for condemning them, but left him room to repent.

After fifteen days of argument in the governor's court, Victor and Theodore were sent to a monastery at Canopus, twelve miles from Alexandria. Later the emperor called them to Constantinople. They stood before Justinian and Eutychius, the bishop of the city, and would not give way. They were split up and held in the city's monasteries. Theodore died, Victor writes, in the same month and on the same day as Justinian.

Isidore of Seville says the chronicle ran from the creation of the world to the first year of the emperor Justin the Younger.

Victor writes as a partisan. To him the empress Theodora was the enemy of the council of Chalcedon, and she died covered in a cancer. Mommsen calls him and Liberatus, another African, fierce defenders of Chalcedon.

Isidore says Victor died still a prisoner in a monastery of Constantinople, as people say, he adds. No early source gives the year.

[warmly] Isidore praised the chronicle as most worthy of memory. Joseph Mesnage, a French scholar, calls it very precious for the church history of Africa in that century.
