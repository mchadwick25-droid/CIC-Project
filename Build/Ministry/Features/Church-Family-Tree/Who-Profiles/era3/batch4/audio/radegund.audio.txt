[calmly] Radegund came to Bishop Medard at Noyon and begged him to make her a nun. The king's nobles dragged the bishop back from the altar. So she went into the sacristy, put on a nun's dress and walked up to the altar. If he put off consecrating her, she told him, and feared a man more than God, the shepherd would answer for a sheep's soul. Medard laid his hand on her and made her a deacon. Her friend Venantius Fortunatus, the poet, tells it in his Life of her.

Radegund was a Thuringian princess who became a Frankish queen. Gregory of Tours says her uncle killed her father, King Berthar. When the Franks won Thuringia, King Lothar took her captive and married her. Later he had her brother killed. Then, Fortunatus writes, she came from the king to Medard.

At Poitiers she built a monastery for women, founded and enriched by Lothar, she says. She made Agnes its abbess, obeyed her, and took for the house the Rule of Caesarius of Arles. Gregory says she went to Arles to get it. Bruno Krusch, who edited her two Lives, later agreed with the scholar Arnold that Gregory was wrong.

She sent clerics to the East for wood of the True Cross. Gregory writes that Maroveus, the bishop of Poitiers, would not receive it. He rode off to a country estate. Her house came to be called Holy Cross, Krusch says.

Her letter to the bishops survives in Gregory's History. "I turned of my own will to the Rule of religion under Christ's guidance," she writes.

Three poems in her voice are printed with Fortunatus's works. Friedrich Leo, who edited them, and Krusch treat them as his, written in her name.

[warmly] Gregory was at her burial. About two hundred nuns stood round the bier, he says. The abbess showed him the book she used to read, and the spindles she spun with through long fasts and tears.
