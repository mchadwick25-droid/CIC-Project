For three years Longinus was watched in Constantinople. At last, John of Ephesus writes, "he disguised himself, and put a wig on his head,—for he was very bald ;—and taking with him two servants, he fled."

Longinus was a bishop of the party that refused the council of Chalcedon. He was sent to the Nobadae, on the eastern borders of the Thebais beyond Egypt, whom the presbyter Julian had first taught. The empress Theodora had ordered that he be made their bishop, John says. He was consecrated as soon as the patriarch Theodosius of Alexandria died. Almost all of this comes from John, of his own party.

Among the Nobadae, Longinus built a church, ordained clergy and taught them the order of worship. Their envoy came to the emperor's court. There, John says, "in the presence of myself", he praised Longinus: "Though we were Christians in name, yet we did not really know what Christianity was until Longinus came to us."

After about five years he was called north, to help make a patriarch for the anti-Chalcedonians of Alexandria. Back in Nubia, he went on to the Alodaei, a powerful people many score leagues beyond them. The Alexandrians sent word that he was a heretic and deposed. "We will receive him who baptized the Nobadæ," the Alodaei answered, "and by him will we be baptized."

John gives part of a letter Longinus wrote from that land. On the road through the desert, Longinus says, they all fell ill, and "we lost no less than seventeen camels." Then the king "came out in person to meet us, and received us with great joy." He baptized the king and his nobles. "For I desire neither silver, nor gold, nor dresses, as God is my witness," he writes.

John dates these things to a year R. Payne Smith, his translator, reckons as 580. No source tells of Longinus after that, or of his death.
