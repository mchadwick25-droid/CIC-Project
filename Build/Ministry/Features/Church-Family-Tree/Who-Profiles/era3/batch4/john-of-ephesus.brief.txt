Every son born to his parents had died before the age of two. When John fell ill too, they carried him, weeping, to Maro, a holy man who lived on a pillar. "Be quiet, be quiet," Maro told them. "John shall not die before Maro." John of Ephesus tells this himself, as his parents used to tell it to him, in his Lives of the Eastern Saints.

John was a bishop and historian of the party that refused the council of Chalcedon. E. W. Brooks, who edited his Lives, says he was born about 507 in the country around Amida, and grew up in monasteries there.

In Justinian's time, John writes, he was made teacher of the heathen in the provinces of Asia, Caria, Phrygia and Lydia. In the hills above Tralles alone he converted many thousands and built twenty-four churches. He levelled a great pagan temple at Derira and built a monastery in its place. Brooks says the emperor set one condition: that the converts be brought to the faith of Chalcedon.

His Lives record that Jacob Baradaeus made him bishop of Ephesus. Brooks finds nothing to show he ever lived there. He led his party in Constantinople, and he was jailed more than once. In one prison he told the emperor's envoys, "Even though you eat me roasted, if I be but quit of the sight of you, I am ready on these terms to be delivered to a painful death."

In his Lives he says he writes only of people he saw with his own eyes. R. Payne Smith, who put the Church History into English, joined its broken pieces with lines of his own, he says. Brooks says John is not a literary writer and that his dates are often loose.

His history ends in 585. Brooks thinks he probably died the next year. Payne Smith values the History because its writer was "a busy actor in the scenes which he describes."
