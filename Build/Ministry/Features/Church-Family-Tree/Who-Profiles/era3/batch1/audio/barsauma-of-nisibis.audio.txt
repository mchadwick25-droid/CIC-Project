[calmly] Barsauma, bishop of Nisibis, wrote to the catholicos Acacius to take back what he had done. Satan had stirred up a quarrel between him and Babai, the catholicos before Acacius, he confessed. Bishops whom Babai meant to judge had come to him, and he had given way to them. Together they held a synod at Beth Lapat and made canons out of human passion. Now he called that synod contrary to Christianity.

Nisibis was a Persian city on the Roman frontier. Jean-Baptiste Chabot, who edited the synods of the Church of the East, dates Beth Lapat to April 484. In a note he says Barsauma was first a slave of Mare of Qardu and studied at the school of Edessa. Expelled from Edessa in 457, Chabot says, he became bishop through the favour of King Peroz. Addai Scher, another editor, cites Elias and Ebedjesu of Nisibis for an earlier date, 435.

Barsauma's own letters show a bishop caught between two empires. Drought had lasted two years, he wrote, and tribes subject to Persia had raided Roman villages. He persuaded a Roman general to come to Nisibis to meet the Persian governor, and the raiders struck while they feasted. The Romans blamed Barsauma, so he could not come to the catholicos.

Later writers told his story against him. Bar Hebraeus, a Syriac Orthodox chronicler of the 1200s, says Barsauma accused Babai to Peroz as a Roman spy, and Peroz had Babai killed. Then, he says, Barsauma led Persian soldiers against those who refused his teaching, and he repeats a report that 7,700 died. Chabot calls Bar Hebraeus very inaccurate on Barsauma. Mari ibn Sulayman, a writer of the Church of the East in the 1100s, also tells of the accusation, and says Barsauma soon regretted it.

Mari says Barsauma married a nun and made marriage lawful for bishops and priests. Bar Hebraeus calls the woman a concubine. Barhadbeshabba, a historian of Barsauma's own church, praises his quick mind. He tells how Barsauma welcomed the teacher Narsai to Nisibis and helped him found its school.

Chabot says the year of his death is unknown, though it came after 491. Bar Hebraeus gives two reports. In one, nuns of Tur Abdin killed him with the keys of their cells. In the other, his tomb was shown at Nisibis.

[warmly] In his third letter, Barsauma told Acacius that Nisibis was troubled and tossed like a stormy sea.
