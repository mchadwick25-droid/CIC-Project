[calmly] One night at Edessa, a friend warned the teacher Narsai of Nisibis that he would be thrown into the fire unless he fled before morning. Narsai went to the church and found some Persians there. He asked them to carry his books, which were all his treasure, and they took them to Nisibis. Barhadbeshabba 'Arbaia tells it in a history of his church's saints, written near the end of the 500s by François Nau's dating.

Narsai was a Persian Christian, a poet and a teacher of Scripture. Barhadbeshabba says he came from the land of Ma'alta. He went to school at seven and learned the whole Psalter in nine months. He studied at Edessa, in the Roman empire, and led its school for twenty years.

His accusers there said he was a heretic who followed Theodore of Mopsuestia and Nestorius, Barhadbeshabba writes. They also said he sided with Persia and wanted to betray the city. When he left Edessa is disputed. R. H. Connolly, his translator, gives 457. Nau notes that the history names a bishop of Edessa who took office in 471, though Nau thinks it means an earlier bishop.

At Nisibis he meant to go on into Persia. Barsauma of Nisibis, the bishop, persuaded him to stay and bought him a camel stable near the church for a school. Students came from all sides. In Connolly's English of a chronicle of the school, "Edessa was darkened, but Nisibis shone forth". Connolly writes that with this school the church now called Nestorian began to take shape.

Barhadbeshabba says Barsauma's wife grew jealous of the crowds at Narsai's door and turned her husband against him. Narsai withdrew to a monastery and wrote a poem about her. When it was read at Nisibis, the bishop repented and called him back.

Narsai held to Theodore. Connolly reports that in one homily Narsai says all his skill in Scripture came from Theodore's commentaries. Jacob of Sarug, whom Barhadbeshabba calls a heretic, spread his teaching in verse. Narsai answered, Barhadbeshabba says, with a verse homily for every day of the year. Simeon of Beth Arsham, of the opposing side, calls him Narsai the leper.

Barhadbeshabba says he was buried in a good old age. Connolly, following Alphonsus Mingana, puts his death in 502; Nau reckons 503 or 504.

[warmly] In his homily on baptism he wrote of God, "instead of our clay He has made us spiritual gold."
