One night at Edessa, a friend warned the teacher Narsai that he would be thrown into the fire unless he fled before morning. Narsai went to the church and found some Persians there. He asked them to carry his books, which were all his treasure, and they took them to Nisibis. Barhadbeshabba tells it in a history of his church's saints, written near the year 600 by François Nau's dating.

Narsai of Nisibis was a Persian Christian, a poet and a teacher of Scripture. Barhadbeshabba says he came from the land of Ma'alta and recited the whole Psalter at seven. He studied at Edessa, in the Roman empire, and led its school for twenty years.

His accusers there said he was a heretic who followed Theodore and Nestorius, Barhadbeshabba writes. They also said he sided with Persia and wanted to betray the city. When he left Edessa is disputed. R. H. Connolly, his translator, gives 457. Nau says that if the history's naming of the bishop stands, Narsai stayed until after 471.

At Nisibis he meant to go on into Persia. Barsauma, the bishop, persuaded him to stay and bought him a camel stable near the church for a school. Students came from all sides. In Connolly's English of Barhadbeshabba, "Edessa was darkened, but Nisibis shone forth". Connolly writes that with this school began the church later called Nestorian.

Barhadbeshabba says Barsauma's wife grew jealous of the crowds at Narsai's door and turned her husband against him. Narsai withdrew to a monastery and wrote a poem about her. When it was read at Nisibis, the bishop repented and called him back.

Narsai held to Theodore of Mopsuestia. Connolly reports that in one homily Narsai says all his skill in Scripture came from Theodore's commentaries. When Jacob of Sarug spread his own teaching in verse, Barhadbeshabba says, Narsai answered with a verse homily for every day of the year. Simeon of Beth Arsham, of the opposing side, calls him Narsai the leper.

He died at Nisibis in old age. Connolly, following Alphonse Mingana, puts his death in 502; Nau reckons 503 or 504. The Library holds four of his homilies in Connolly's English, and Connolly notes that one of them has been doubted.

In his homily on baptism he wrote of God, "instead of our clay He has made us spiritual gold."
