After the battle of Avarayr, the Persian king held Armenian princes captive. Lazar Parpetsi, also called Lazar of Pharp, says he saw some of them with his own eyes. He writes that they set up schools in the camp to learn church teaching. They said the psalms in their minds as they took up their weapons.

Lazar was an Armenian historian. Victor Langlois, who edited the French translation of his History, places him at the end of the fifth century or the start of the sixth. Langlois says the name Parpetsi came from a long stay at the monastery of Pharp, a village in Ararat, and not from his birthplace.

Most of his life story comes from Langlois's introduction, which draws partly on a letter of Lazar's that the Library does not hold. Langlois calls him a childhood companion of Vahan Mamikonian. He says Lazar studied in Greece with leave from Sahak the Great and Mesrop Mashtots. When Vahan governed Armenia for the Persian king, he put Lazar in charge of the mother church at Etchmiadzin.

Then, Langlois says, jealous clergy accused his teaching of going against the true faith. His enemies drove him out and would not let him take the Greek books he had gathered. From Tigranocerta he wrote Vahan a long letter answering each charge. Langlois says it seems to have worked, for Vahan called him back.

Lazar says Vahan ordered him to write. His History tells of Sahak and Mesrop, the war Vardan Mamikonian led, and Vahan's revolt. It ends on the day Vahan took the government of Armenia. Lazar doubted some stories in Faustus of Byzantium, suspecting that another writer or a careless copyist had added them. He also tells that Vahan, his patron, worn down by slander, weakened in his faith at the Persian court.

He called his History the third part of Armenia's annals, after Agathangelos and Faustus, and, in his own phrase, the work of his weakness.
