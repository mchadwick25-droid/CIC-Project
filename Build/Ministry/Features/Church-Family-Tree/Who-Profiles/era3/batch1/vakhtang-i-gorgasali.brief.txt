In a dream, the Georgian royal chronicle says, King Vakhtang heard a bishop call his name three times. He would be the most faithful of all to Christ among the Persian nations, the voice said. He would build churches and set up bishops and archbishops. And he would receive the crown of martyrdom. The priest Petre and the monk Samoel read the dream: he would die in battle, but never fall into his enemies' hands.

Vakhtang the First, called Gorgasal, was king of Georgia. Marie-Félicité Brosset, who put the chronicle into French in 1849, dates his reign from 446 to 499. The chronicle says its history up to Vakhtang was written at various times. Brosset adds that even its long reigns, such as his, seem written from memory. Its Vakhtang is a giant who could run down a deer on foot.

The chronicle credits him with Georgia's first catholicos. The patriarch of Antioch, it says, consecrated twelve bishops and gave Petre that title. At Mtskheta, Vakhtang built him the great church of the Apostles, Sveti-Tskhoveli, and placed bishops across the land. Brosset finds very great difficulties in the account of the church. Reasoning from the Greek canonist Balsamon, he dates the first catholicos between 475 and 488.

The Armenian historian Lazar of Pharp, writing for the Armenian side, tells a different story. Vakhtang, he says, killed the treacherous prince Vazken, and promised to raise the Huns against Persia. A note in Victor Langlois's French edition of Lazar says that the Georgian chronicle says nothing of this. Lazar adds that Vakhtang sent the Armenians three hundred Huns, called them back, and deceived them with lies. In the battle that followed, he says, the cowardly soldiers of Iberia fled with their king and the Armenians.

The chronicle ends his life as the dream foretold. A Persian arrow struck his chest and reached the lung. He died and was buried at Mtskheta.

The faithful people, it says, envied the lot of the king killed for Christ.
