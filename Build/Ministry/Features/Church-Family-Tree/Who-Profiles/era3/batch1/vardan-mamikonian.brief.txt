At the Persian court, the princes of Armenia, Iberia and Albania begged Vardan Mamikonian to bow to the king's religion. They asked him to do it only for a time, and only in outward show. The Armenian historian Lazar of Pharp tells the scene.

In Lazar's telling, Vardan refused. He cried that he would never lie to his Creator, in outward show or in truth. Then the princes swore on the Gospel to follow him and shed their blood for the church, if he gave way for a time. Lazar says Vardan wept and agreed, to save the three nations. Some of the princes then seemed to bow in the fire temple.

Vardan was general of the Armenians and lord of the Mamikonian house. Lazar says his grandfather, the patriarch Sahak the Great, taught him the Scriptures. Charles Neumann, who put Elishe's history of the war into English, also calls him Sahak's grandson.

Back in Armenia, Elishe says, the people drove off the Magi who tried to break open a church. The princes took up arms, and Vardan led them. Vasak, prince of Siunik, went over to the Persians.

Lazar says the armies met near the village of Avarayr, on the plain of the Deghmut. Elishe gives Vardan a speech before the battle. Both historians write for the Armenian side, and his words come to us only as they wrote them. As Elishe writes it, he said, "Should the object of our lives be completed by a holy death in battle, let us meet it with a joyful heart."

Elishe says Vardan scattered the Persian right wing and its elephants. Then he fell, "found worthy of martyrdom on the spot." Elishe and Lazar both count one thousand and thirty-six Armenian dead. Lazar says the Persian king Yazdegerd grieved when he heard that the brave Vardan was dead.

The day is told two ways. Malachia Ormanian, a former Armenian patriarch of Constantinople, gives 26 May 451. Neumann's margin, and a note in Lazar's French edition, give 2 June 451. Ormanian adds that the Armenian calendar remembers these martyrs on what he calls Shrove-Thursday.

Before the court, Lazar says, Vardan had promised a deed whose fame would ring out for ever. God took that promise from the Persians, Lazar writes, and gave him instead the name of martyr, to last among all nations.
