Felix, bishop of Abbir, had been a bishop for forty-four years when King Huneric sent him into exile. He was paralysed and could neither feel nor speak. Victor of Vita writes that he and others asked that the dying man be left at Carthage. The king, as Victor reports it, answered that wild oxen should be yoked to drag him with ropes. So they carried Felix the whole way, Victor says, tied across a mule like a log.

Victor wrote the History of the Persecution in Africa under the Vandal kings Geiseric and Huneric. The manuscripts of his book call him a bishop of Vita. He calls Pampinianus, burned with red-hot iron plates, the bishop of "our city", and Karl Halm, who edited the Latin, takes that to mean Vita.

He wrote in the sixtieth year since the Vandals crossed from Spain. He calls them a cruel and savage people, and their clergy Arians. He writes as a victim and an enemy of their church, and his book is polemic.

Some of it he lived through. Four thousand nine hundred and sixty-six bishops, priests, deacons and other church members were sent into the desert, he writes. At Sicca and Lares they shouted that they were Christians and Catholics. Victor and others were let in to encourage them and celebrate the liturgy. He copies Huneric's edict into his book.

At Tipasa, Victor says, the king had the tongues and right hands of Catholics cut off, and they still spoke. Anyone who doubts it, he adds, can find one of them, the subdeacon Reparatus, at Constantinople. Procopius of Caesarea, the Greek historian of the Vandal War, also writes of many whose tongues Huneric cut out. They "even up to my time were going about in Byzantium having their speech uninjured."

Near the end he calls on the angels of God to look at Africa. Once it was held up by so many churches, he says, and now it sits a widow, cast down.
