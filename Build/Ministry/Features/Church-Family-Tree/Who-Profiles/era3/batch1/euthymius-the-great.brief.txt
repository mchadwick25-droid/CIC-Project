Cyril of Scythopolis tried hard to begin his book about Euthymius, he says, and could not write even its first words. Then he fell asleep, and Euthymius and Sabas, a monk whose Life Cyril also wrote, appeared to him. Euthymius took a silver flask from his robe, dipped a small spoon in it, and put it in Cyril's mouth. It looked like oil, Cyril writes, but to compare its taste with honey would insult it. He woke with the taste in his mouth and began.

The book is a saint's Life, full of visions and wonders. Cyril names the anchorite Cyriacus as the source of some of its stories.

Euthymius was born at Melitene in Armenia, on the Euphrates, Cyril says. After his father died, Otreius, bishop of Melitene, took the boy in and raised him.

He came to Jerusalem in about his twenty-ninth year. After five years at Pharan he left with his friend Theoctistus, and they found a great cave above a steep stream bed. A Saracen chief, Aspebetus, brought his half-paralyzed son there, and Cyril tells of the boy's healing. Cyril says Euthymius baptized the father as Peter. Louis Duchesne, in his history of the early church, says Juvenal of Jerusalem baptized him. Euthymius asked Juvenal for a bishop for the converts, Cyril says, and Peter became the first bishop of the Camps in Palestine.

The Council of Chalcedon met in Euthymius's seventy-fifth year, Cyril says. Euthymius read its definition and judged it sound. Then Theodosius, a monk Cyril calls a deceiver, won over the monks and the empress Eudocia, widow of Theodosius II. Euthymius and his monks kept apart from him. Later Eudocia sought Euthymius out. He told her to accept Chalcedon and to hold communion with Juvenal, and she did.

Cyril says he died in the sixteenth year of the emperor Leo. He called the Life fit to cheer its readers and rouse them to zeal.
