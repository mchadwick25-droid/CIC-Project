Boethius stood before King Theodoric to defend a fellow senator, Albinus, accused of writing to the emperor Justin against the king. A Latin chronicle, the Anonymus Valesianus, gives his answer, told here in modern words. The charge is false, he said, but if Albinus did it, then I and the whole Senate did it together. Its editor, Theodor Mommsen, calls its author a fierce enemy of the Arians.

Boethius was a Roman senator under Theodoric, the Ostrogothic king of Italy. He was consul in 510.

A letter Cassiodorus wrote in Theodoric's name praises him for putting Pythagoras, Ptolemy, Euclid, Plato and Aristotle into Latin.

He also wrote short books on the faith. Writing against Eutyches and Nestorius, he defined a person as "The individual substance of a rational nature." Christ, he argued, is two natures in one person, as the Catholic faith holds. He closed one with a plea: "if possible, reconcile faith and reason." Thomas Hodgkin, who put Cassiodorus's letters into English, reports that scholars of his day doubted these books were his. Hodgkin answered them from a fragment drawn from a lost work of Cassiodorus, which names a book on the Holy Trinity and one against Nestorius.

In 522 his two sons were consuls together, and he praised the king in the Senate. Soon after, he fell. Condemned, he wrote the Consolation of Philosophy, in which Philosophy visits him as a woman. He set down the charges there: "I am said to have desired the Senate's safety." "Now being conveyed five hundred miles off, dumb and defenceless, I am condemned to death," he wrote.

Marius of Avenches says he was killed in the district of Milan, in the year his editor dates to 524. The Anonymus Valesianus has him tortured with a cord twisted on his forehead and clubbed to death. The Book of the Popes says the king killed him with the sword. Procopius of Caesarea writes that Theodoric later wept for the wrong he had done.

The Consolation never names Christ, Hodgkin notes, so some have read Boethius as a pagan. His English editors, H. F. Stewart and E. K. Rand, call him without doubt a Christian, and perhaps a martyr. The Roman Martyrology, as the Bollandists print it, honors him as a martyr at Pavia.

Of his own fall he wrote that "it is the most unhappy kind of misfortune to have been happy."
