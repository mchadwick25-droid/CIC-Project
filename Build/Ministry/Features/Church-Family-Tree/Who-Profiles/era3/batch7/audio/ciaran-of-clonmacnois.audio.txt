[calmly] Ciaran planted the first stake at Clonmacnois, with Diarmait son of Cerball beside him. "Let, O warrior, thy hand be over my hand, and thou shalt be in sovranty over the men of Ireland," he told him. That night, the Life says, the king Tuathal Maelgarb was killed, and Diarmait came to rule.

The story comes from the Irish Life of Ciaran in the Book of Lismore. Whitley Stokes, its editor and translator, says the book was put together in the late 1400s, and that the Life was composed at Clonmacnois itself. It is the house's own telling of its founder, written down long after him.

Two writers from the decades around 700 confirm the man and his monastery. Tirechan, who wrote of Patrick, says the deacon Justus baptized Ciaran, the craftsman's son, from Patrick's book. Adomnan of Iona tells of Columba visiting "the Clonoensian monastery of St. Ceran", and the brethren running out to meet him.

The Life makes him the son of Beoit, a wright. His mother would not give him a cow to take to school, it says. So he blessed one, and Ciaran's Dun followed him to Findian of Clonard. Her milk fed the whole school, and her hide was later kept at Clonmacnois. The Life sends him on to Enda of Aran and other teachers before he founded his own house.

He died young, the Life says, in his thirty-third year, seven months after settling at Clonmacnois. Looking up at the sky, he said, "Awful is this way above." The Annales Cambriae note his death in their hundredth year, which their editor, John Williams ab Ithel, sets at 544. John O'Donovan, editor of the Annals of the Four Masters, gives 549.

[warmly] He is a different man from Ciaran of Saigir, and the Book of Lismore keeps the two apart. In that book's Life of Columba, Columba came to Clonmacnois with a hymn he had made for Ciaran.
