[calmly] When Count Pierius forced everyone to leave Noricum, the priest Lucillus sang the evening psalms with the monks, then had the grave of Severinus opened. "A fragrance of such sweetness surrounded us who stood by, that we fell on the earth for joy and wonder," Eugippius wrote. The monks carried the body to Italy, and it came to rest in the Lucullan castle at Naples.

Eugippius later led the monastery built there at the tomb. He calls the monk Marcianus the man "who preceded me in the headship of the monastery". Isidore of Seville calls him abbot, and those who wrote to him called him a priest.

His best-known book is the Life of Severinus, a holy man of the borderlands of the Danube. In a letter to the deacon Paschasius he tells how it began. He had read a layman's life of another monk. Then he began to tell the clergy that the great miracles of Severinus "ought not to be hidden". He gathered a memoir "filled full with testimonies from the daily narrations of the elder brethren", and sent it to Paschasius to polish. G. W. Robinson, his English translator, dates the letter to 511.

How well he knew Severinus is not clear. He writes "we" at the deathbed and at the grave, but says he drew on what the elder monks told him. Andrea Gallandi, an editor of the 1700s, calls him Severinus's disciple.

Cassiodorus says he saw him himself. He was not deeply trained in worldly learning, Cassiodorus writes, but full of the Scriptures. For a holy woman named Proba he gathered passages from Augustine into three hundred and thirty-eight chapters. Isidore says he wrote a rule for his monks, left to them at his death. No source used here gives the year of his birth or death.

[warmly] Paschasius chose to add nothing to the memoir. "The deeds of the good cannot perish with time," he wrote.
