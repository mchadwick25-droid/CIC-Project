On an estate near Sicca in North Africa, an Arian priest named Felix seized two monks and had them beaten. One, the abbot Felix, begged the men to spare his fellow monk Fulgentius, who could not bear it, and take him instead. Both were beaten, shaved and thrown out naked. When the Arian bishop of the district offered to punish the priest, Fulgentius refused, saying a Christian may not seek revenge in this world.

The Life of Fulgentius, by a Catholic monk who lived with him, tells this from what Fulgentius told him. P. Chifflet and others name its author as Ferrandus of Carthage, but a note Migne prints says others doubt it.

Fulgentius of Ruspe was a Catholic bishop under the Arian Vandal kings. The Life says his family were senators of Carthage, and he was born at Thelepte. He became a tax officer, then turned to the monks after reading Augustine of Hippo on Psalm 36. His mother, Mariana, came crying to the monastery gate for her son.

Turned back on the way to the monks of Egypt, he saw Theodoric, the Ostrogothic king, address the people of Rome. If earthly Rome shines like this, he said, how beautiful the heavenly Jerusalem must be.

He was seized and made bishop of Ruspe by force, against a royal ban, the Life says. He was soon exiled to Sardinia. Victor of Tunnuna says King Thrasamund sent a hundred and twenty bishops there.

Thrasamund called him back to Carthage to answer for the Catholic faith. To be unwilling to state the faith, Fulgentius wrote to him, is almost to deny it. The Life says the king praised his wisdom, yet was not worthy to understand the truth. At his opponents' urging, the king sent him back to Sardinia.

The unsigned preface in Migne's volume calls him a disciple of Augustine. The Life and Isidore of Seville name seven books he wrote against Faustus of Riez, now lost. To his friend Monimus he wrote that God predestined some to the punishment they deserved, but not to sin.

When Hilderic became king, the exiles came home, and crowds met Fulgentius at the shore of Carthage. The Life says he died on the first of January, in his twenty-fifth year as bishop, but gives no year. Andrea Gallandi reports that Cardinal Baronius gave 529 and Chifflet 533.

Isidore remembered him as sweet in speech and keen in teaching.
