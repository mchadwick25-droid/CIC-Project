In exile at Gangra, Philoxenus of Mabbug was shut in a room above the kitchen of a hostel, and the smoke choked him. The Syriac chronicle under the name of Zachariah Rhetor says he told of it in a letter. At last he died there.

Philoxenus, first called Akhsenaya or Xenaias, was a Syriac bishop and writer of the church that refused the Council of Chalcedon. His English translator, E. A. Wallis Budge, says he was born at Tahal in Beth Garmai between about 450 and 475. In 485, by Budge's date, Peter the Fuller, bishop of Antioch, made him bishop of Mabbug, also called Hierapolis.

Theophanes the Confessor, a later Greek chronicler loyal to Chalcedon, calls him Satan's slave. He says Philoxenus was a runaway slave, not yet baptized when he was made bishop. Budge answers from Philoxenus's own words: "For I was baptized in the name of Him that died."

He fought the teaching that Christ has two natures, which he traced to Nestorius. He put ten questions to those who held it. The first asks, "If it be a demonstrable thing that Christ hath two natures, to which of them did the Virgin give birth?" Michael the Syrian says a Syriac version of the New Testament was made at Mabbug through his care.

His long fight with Flavian II of Antioch ended in 512. The chronicle under Zachariah's name, from his own side, says his zeal urged on the monks. There were wounded and dead, and Flavian was driven out. Evagrius Scholasticus, from the other side, heard from very old men that the people of Antioch killed the monks. A great many found their grave in the Orontes. A Syriac chronicle of 846 names Philoxenus among the bishops who ordained Severus of Antioch in Flavian's place.

The Chronicle of Edessa says the emperor Justin, in his second year, drove Severus from Antioch and Philoxenus from Mabbug. A letter of his, Budge says, shows he went first to Philippopolis in Thrace, then to Gangra. No early source used here gives the year he died. The editor François Nau puts the end of his time as bishop in 523.

Palestinian monks of the other side, in a letter Evagrius copies, called him "truly a stranger to God". The chronicle of 846 calls him a great teacher and a martyr.

He wrote in his Discourses, "Faith is the foundation and the architect".
