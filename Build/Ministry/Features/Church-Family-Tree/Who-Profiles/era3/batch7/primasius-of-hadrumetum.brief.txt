Far from home in Constantinople, an African bishop met a fellow countryman, Junillus. The first thing he asked, as was his way, was whether any Greek there burned to study the holy books. Junillus tells this in Latin, in the letter that opens his handbook on the Bible. He told the bishop of a Persian named Paul, taught in the school at Nisibis. The bishop pressed him to write down Paul's rules for reading Scripture, until Junillus gave in.

The bishop was Primasius of Hadrumetum, in the African province of Byzacena. He had come, the German scholar Heinrich Kihn reckons, over the Three Chapters, writings the emperor Justinian wanted the church to condemn. Victor of Tunnuna says the emperor summoned him there with other African bishops. Victor's editor, Theodor Mommsen, gives the year as 551.

In May 553, as the Second Council of Constantinople met, he signed the ruling of Pope Vigilius on the Three Chapters. His signature survives. It names his city as Hadrumetum, also called Justinianopolis.

The rest is told only by Victor of Tunnuna, who defended the Three Chapters and tells it as betrayal. Victor says Primasius was sent to a monastery. When Boethius, the head bishop of Byzacena, died, Primasius agreed to condemn the Three Chapters so that he might take his place. Back home he persecuted those who held what he had once held. His own province's bishops condemned him, and he died an unhappy death. Kihn, writing in 1880, warned that Victor wrote as a party man.

His five books on the Revelation of John survive, as Cassiodorus describes them. In the preface, Primasius says he drew on Augustine of Hippo and also on Tyconius, a Donatist, and cut away his errors. A precious gem lying in dung, he wrote, should be picked up and restored to its worth. In the same way, wherever truth shines, it should be won back for the Catholic Church.
