Sulpicius Severus went to Tours to see its bishop, Martin. He already meant to write Martin's life. Martin gave him water to wash his hands. At evening he washed his guest's feet himself. Sulpicius says he had not the courage to stop him. Martin talked of leaving the world behind, and held up Paulinus of Nola, a rich man who had sold everything for the poor.

Gennadius of Marseilles says Sulpicius was a presbyter from Aquitaine, "distinguished by his birth," poor by choice and humble. Alexander Roberts, who translated him, says without naming a source that he gave up a name at the bar after his wife died young. Roberts puts his life at about 363 to 420. Ernest Cushing Richardson, in a note to Gennadius, gives after 353 to about 410.

The Life of Martin was done before Martin died. In a letter, Sulpicius tells of a morning dream. Martin appeared, smiling, and held out "the small treatise which I had written concerning his life." When Sulpicius woke, a servant said two monks had come from Tours, and "they have brought word that Martin is dead." Later he wrote Dialogues, where friends talk of the monks of the East and of Martin. He also wrote a Chronicle from the Creation to his own day.

The Chronicle closes with the trial of Priscillian of Abila at Trèves. Sulpicius wanted the heretics put down. Of the bishops who pressed the charges, he writes that "the accusers were as distasteful to me as the accused." Martin, he says, begged the emperor Maximus not to shed their blood.

Gennadius adds that in old age Sulpicius "was led astray by the Pelagians," the followers of Pelagius. He then kept silent until death, to atone. Roberts finds "no ground for any such conclusion" in the writings.

Paulinus of Nola, in a letter Roberts quotes, called Martin blessed for having "a historian worthy of his faith and of his life."
