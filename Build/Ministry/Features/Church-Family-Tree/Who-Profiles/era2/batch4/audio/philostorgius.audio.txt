[calmly] At twenty, Philostorgius came to Constantinople and saw Eunomius of Cyzicus, the leader of his party. In his history he praised the man's looks and speech. He admitted that Eunomius lisped, then made even the lisp a sign of grace. Photius, a later patriarch of Constantinople, summed up the history. He scorned it. Philostorgius, he says, "is not ashamed to exalt this lisp into a mark of great grace and elegance."

His book is lost. It survives through Photius's summary, and Photius was hostile throughout. He calls Philostorgius an "impious contriver of lies."

Through Photius, Philostorgius tells of his family. His mother's father, Anysius, was a priest at Borissus in Cappadocia. His mother, Eulampia, came from families who held "the consubstantial faith" of Nicaea. His father, Carterius, followed Eunomius and won her over. She then won "her brothers, then her father," and in time the rest of her family.

Photius names Aetius of Antioch and Eunomius as leaders of those who held that the Son is unlike the Father in substance. Philostorgius praised them both. He rated Aetius higher in argument, and Eunomius higher for "a style well suited to learners." He even faulted Arius, whom he praised, for teaching that God cannot be known, "not even by His own only-begotten Son." He wrote a book in praise of Eunomius, and another against Porphyry "on behalf of the Christians."

His history ran in twelve books, from the quarrel of Arius with Alexander of Alexandria to 425, by Edward Walford's date. The first letters of the books spelled his name. Walford, who translated the summary, notes his love of far lands and wonders.

A later Greek dictionary keeps a passage in his own words. In it he praises three foes of his party, Basil of Caesarea, Gregory of Nazianzus and Apollinaris of Laodicea. [warmly] They argued so well for the Nicene faith, he says, that "Athanasius must be judged a mere child in comparison with them."
