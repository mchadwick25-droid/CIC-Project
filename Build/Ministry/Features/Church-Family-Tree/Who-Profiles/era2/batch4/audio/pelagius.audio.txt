[calmly] Late in 415, fourteen bishops met at Diospolis in Palestine to judge a monk named Pelagius. His accusers, two bishops from Gaul, were absent, one of them ill. A charge was read that he had written, "A man is able, if he likes, to be without sin." Pelagius answered that this power "has been given to him by God." Denying other words laid against him, he said of those who held them, "I anathematize them as fools, not as heretics, for there is no dogma." The bishops declared him in communion with the Catholic Church.

Augustine of Hippo quoted the synod's record in a book against him. Augustine and Jerome of Strido worked together against Pelagius. Apart from his own letter and his answers at the synod, most of his story comes from their side.

Pelagius lived at Rome in the early 400s. Augustine and others call him a Briton, notes Peter Holmes, Augustine's translator. Augustine first called him "a holy man, as I am told." At Rome, he says, a bishop quoted to Pelagius a prayer from Augustine's Confessions, "Give what Thou commandest." Pelagius could not bear it and nearly quarrelled.

About 413 he wrote from the East to Demetrias, a Roman noblewoman vowed to Christ as a virgin. In the Latin letter he says he always begins by showing what human nature can do. It does no good, he explains, to call people to what they think impossible. We tell God his commands are too hard, and "we are men." "Oh, blind madness!" he answers, in the English of B. B. Warfield, editor of Augustine's books against him. Habit makes doing good hard, he adds, until sin seems to have "the force of nature."

Warfield, who takes Augustine's side, says Pelagius and his follower Caelestius fled the Goths from Rome to Africa in 411. After Diospolis, the African bishops condemned Pelagius again, and Innocent, bishop of Rome, agreed. In 418 the emperor banished the Pelagians from Rome, and Innocent's successor Zosimus condemned him.

Pelagius told Innocent to read his letter to Demetrias. There "they will find that we so commend the nature of man as always to add the help of God's grace." It almost persuaded Augustine. He decided that by grace Pelagius meant help from outside, such as teaching and Christ's example.

[warmly] Gennadius of Marseilles says that before he was proclaimed a heretic, Pelagius wrote "works of practical value for students."
