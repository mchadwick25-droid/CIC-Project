[calmly] The island off the coast lay empty. Hilary of Arles says no one lived there because it was wild and people feared its poisonous snakes. Honoratus, later bishop of Arles, went in unafraid, repeating the psalm about treading on the asp and the basilisk. The snakes gave way, Hilary says, and Honoratus set up a monastery there. Eucherius of Lyons, a friend on a nearby island, names the island Lérins and Honoratus its founder.

Almost all we know of his life comes from one sermon in his memory. Its author, Hilary, was his disciple and later his successor as bishop. It is a sermon of praise, and tells a saint's life, wonders and all.

His family, Hilary says, had risen to the consulship. His father kept him from baptism as long as he could, afraid that faith would carry him off entirely. As a young man Honoratus cut his hair short, fasted and gave to the poor. His elder brother, Venantius, followed him. The two sold their lands for the poor and left home with an old holy man, Caprasius, as their guide. They sailed for Greece, and Venantius died there, at Methone.

Honoratus came back by way of Italy. On the island he was made a presbyter, against his will, Hilary says. A church and cells for monks went up. People came to him from many lands, speaking many tongues.

Hilary was one of those he won. For Hilary's sake he went back to his home country, and wept and prayed until Hilary gave way. Later, Arles called Honoratus to be its bishop. His first care, Hilary says, was peace in a church split over the choice of a bishop. His last sermon was on the feast of the Epiphany. Edgar Gibson, who translated John Cassian, dates his death to January 429.

Hilary remembers his letters. [warmly] When one reached Eucherius on wax tablets, Eucherius said that Honoratus had given the wax its honey back.
