[calmly] A few days before Ambrose of Milan took to his bed, he was dictating the forty-third Psalm. Paulinus was taking it down. Suddenly, Paulinus writes, "a fire in the shape of a small shield covered his head," and entered his mouth. The bishop's face became like snow. Paulinus froze and could not write until the vision had passed.

Paulinus of Milan tells this in his Life of Ambrose, written at the request of Augustine of Hippo. He had lived in Ambrose's household. Later he moved to Africa, "in which we now live and write." Sister Mary Simplicia Kaniecka, who translated the Life in 1928, says little else is known of him.

Paulinus drew on men who had served Ambrose before him, and on Ambrose's sister Marcellina. He begged readers to trust him, "since indeed it is better to say nothing at all than to set forth something false." Kaniecka calls it a saint's life, written to build up the reader, and says it pays no heed to dates.

At Carthage in 412, a deacon named Paulinus brought a written charge against Caelestius, a follower of Pelagius. Marius Mercator, a Latin layman who wrote against the Pelagians, calls the accuser a deacon of Ambrose. Kaniecka takes him to be the author of the Life. Augustine quotes the council's record. Caelestius said a presbyter at Rome had denied that sin passes down from Adam, and others said the same. "Tell us their names," Paulinus pressed. "Is not one priest enough for you?" Caelestius answered. The council condemned him.

In 417 Zosimus, bishop of Rome, heard Caelestius again and called Paulinus to Rome. Paulinus sent a short petition instead, which survives in Latin. In it he thanks Zosimus for ordering Caelestius to condemn what Paulinus had charged. What he had always wanted for Caelestius, he writes, was his correction.

[warmly] He closed the Life by asking Augustine to pray for "the most humble man and sinner Paulinus."
