[calmly] In 410, by W. H. Fremantle's date, Rufinus of Aquileia was in Sicily, putting the Greek sermons of Origen of Alexandria on Numbers into Latin. The Goths had reached the strait. Rufinus wrote that "the Barbarian was within sight of us; he had set fire to the city of Rhegium." On quiet nights he kept working, "as a solace from our troubles."

Rufinus was a monk and priest from northern Italy, baptized at Aquileia. Jerome of Strido was once his close friend. Jerome wrote then that Rufinus "is inseparably bound to me in brotherly love." Later each wrote against the other, and each is a partisan witness on the other.

Rufinus stayed six years in Egypt, he says, and later two more. He learned from Didymus of Alexandria and from the desert monks. When heretics turned on him at Alexandria, he "underwent imprisonment and exile." He lived at Jerusalem with Melania the Elder, a widow from Rome who led a convent there. Palladius of Helenopolis, who took his side against Jerome, says they received pilgrims at their own cost. "A more learned man or a kinder than he was not to be found among men," Palladius writes.

He came home to Italy, and to his family, "after an absence of nearly 30 years." Friends asked him to translate Origen's On First Principles. His preface praised Jerome, without naming him, for clearing Origen of stumbling blocks as he translated. He would do the same, leaving out lines he thought heretics had added. Jerome took the praise as a trap. He wrote that "under the name of a friend I detected the snares of an enemy." Rufinus told Anastasius, the bishop of Rome, "I am not a defender or a champion of Origen."

When the Goths broke into Italy, Chromatius, the bishop of Aquileia, asked him to translate Eusebius of Caesarea's Church History. Rufinus added two books of his own, "like the two fishes to the loaves." Socrates of Constantinople built his own first books on them. Rufinus's Commentary on the Apostles' Creed passed on an old tradition. The apostles, it says, wrote the creed together, "each contributing his several sentence to one common summary."

Jerome later wrote that "the scorpion lies beneath the ground." Notes to W. H. Fremantle's edition of Jerome take the scorpion for Rufinus. [warmly] Gennadius of Marseilles says that Rufinus "opened to the Latin speaking church the greater part of the Greek literature."
