At Nitria, in Egypt, Palladius of Helenopolis heard his teacher Evagrius Ponticus wish to know what kind of man John of Lycopolis was. The next day Palladius shut his cell and set off for the Thebaid. He arrived eighteen days later, on foot and by river boat.

John, a hermit, spoke to visitors through a window. He asked if Palladius wanted to be a bishop. Palladius joked that he was bishop of the kitchen pots. "This is my bishopric, for gluttony ordained me." John smiled. "You have to be ordained bishop, and toil much and be afflicted."

Palladius tells this in his Lausiac History. He came from Galatia, he says, and lived as a monk near Alexandria and in the desert. Sozomen, a church historian, calls him "a disciple of Evagrius." Illness took him to Palestine and Bithynia, where he was made bishop, "having become embroiled in the disturbance connected with the blessed John." W. K. Lowther Clarke, its translator, takes this John to be John Chrysostom, bishop of Constantinople.

A Dialogue on Chrysostom's life tells what followed. Herbert Moore, who translated it, follows Cuthbert Butler in giving it to Palladius. Emeritus Bigot, its first editor, suggested another Palladius. There Palladius reaches Rome with a copy of an edict against John's friends. Later he is held under guard at Syene, in the far south.

Epiphanius of Salamis, in a letter Jerome of Strido put into Latin, warned that "Palladius of Galatia" taught "the heresy of Origen," the Alexandrian teacher. Jerome called him "no better than a villainous slave." Palladius wrote that Jerome's temper threw "into the shade his splendid achievements."

He wrote the Lausiac History for Lausus, a chamberlain at the emperor's court. It tells of monks and holy women he met or heard of. He made journeys of thirty days on foot to meet them. "I welcomed all the hardship of the way so long as I might meet some man that loved God."
