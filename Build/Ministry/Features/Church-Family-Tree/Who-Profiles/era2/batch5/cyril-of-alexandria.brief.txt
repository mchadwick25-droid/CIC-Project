At Ephesus in June 431, a council heard a letter read aloud. Cyril, bishop of Alexandria, had written it to Nestorius, bishop of Constantinople. Cyril asked the bishops whether he had written "rightly and blamelessly," in line with the creed of Nicaea. One by one they said he had. Then, the acts record, they cried out together, "we all anathematize Nestorius as a heretic."

Socrates of Constantinople, a church historian of those years, tells of his rise. He was a nephew of Theophilus, bishop of Alexandria. When Theophilus died in 412, a party wanted the archdeacon Timothy, and the army commander backed him. Three days later Cyril held the see, "with greater power than Theophilus had ever exercised." He at once shut the churches of the Novatians, a separate Christian body in the city.

Socrates tells of hard years. After Jews killed Christians in a night attack, Cyril led a crowd to the synagogues and drove the Jews from the city. Monks from Nitria came to fight for him, and one of them stoned Orestes, the prefect. That monk died under torture, and Cyril praised him as a martyr. Then, in March 415, a Christian crowd murdered Hypatia, a philosopher who often met Orestes. Socrates does not say Cyril ordered it. He says it brought great shame on Cyril and on the whole church of Alexandria. A. C. Zenos, who edited Socrates in English, links this verdict to Socrates's closeness to the Novatians.

At Constantinople, Socrates says, a priest close to Nestorius preached, "Let no one call Mary Theotocos," Mother of God. Nestorius backed him. Cyril wrote that the holy Fathers "ventured to call the holy Virgin, the Mother of God." They did not mean that the Word began in her. They meant that of her was born the body to which the Word was united.

Socrates says Cyril's party at Ephesus did not wait for John, bishop of Antioch. Nestorius's party met apart and deposed Cyril. When John came, he blamed Cyril for all the confusion. Nestorius, Cyril's enemy, later charged him with bribery, "the gold of iniquity." In 433 Cyril and John made peace. His letter to John begins, "Let the heavens rejoice, and let the earth be glad." He died in 444.

At Chalcedon in 451, bishops heard his letters again, and one by Leo of Rome. They cried, "Everlasting be the memory of Cyril. Leo and Cyril taught the same thing."
