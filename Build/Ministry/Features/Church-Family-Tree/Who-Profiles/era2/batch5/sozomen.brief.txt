Under the emperor Julian, Christians had to flee from city to city. "My grandfather and many of my ancestors were compelled to flee in this manner," Sozomen writes in his Church History. His grandfather was born to pagans. He and his family, with that of Alaphion, were the first in Bethelia, a town near Gaza, to become Christians. It is said, Sozomen adds, that the monk Hilarion of Gaza won them by driving a demon out of Alaphion.

His grandfather was loved in Gaza and Ascalon for explaining hard places in Scripture. Members of "the other family" founded the first churches and monasteries there. Chester Hartranft, who revised the English translation, takes this to mean Alaphion's. Sozomen saw some of its good men in his youth, "but they were then very aged."

He became an advocate. Telling of a friend healed at the Michaelium, a shrine near Constantinople, he calls him "an advocate in the same court of justice as that to which we belong." He says the shrine helped him too.

He sent his history to the emperor, the younger Theodosius, and asked him to "purify it by thy labors," adding or cutting as he saw fit. The dedication says it would run to the emperor's seventeenth consulship. Hartranft dates that to 439, and notes that the history stops at 425.

He searched laws, synod records and letters. Each party, he warns, kept only "such epistles as favored their own heresy."

Sozomen never names Socrates of Constantinople, who wrote a few years before him. Hartranft finds the use of Socrates sure, from stories both tell almost alike. One view, he reports, is that Sozomen plagiarized Socrates. Hartranft calls that charge "wholly unfair" by the custom of the time.

Sozomen also wrote of the first monks. He wanted to leave "such a record of their manner of life that others, led by their example, might attain to a blessed and happy end."
