Alexander, bishop of Alexandria, lay dying. He called for his deacon Athanasius, who was away. Another man of that name answered, but Alexander kept still. He called again. Then he cried, "O Athanasius, thou thinkest to escape, but thou wilt not escape." Sozomen, a church historian of the next century, tells this story. He took it from a writer he calls "Apolinarius, the Syrian."

Alexander led the church of Alexandria in the time of Constantine. Blomfield Jackson, who translated Theodoret of Cyrrhus, dates his years as bishop from 312 to 326. Arius was one of his priests.

The historians tell three stories of how the two fell out. Socrates of Constantinople says Alexander once taught his clergy about the Trinity in too fine a way, perhaps. Arius thought he heard the old error of Sabellius in it. Theodoret says Arius was jealous that Alexander had been made bishop. Sozomen says Alexander first let both sides argue in front of him, and seemed to lean one way and then the other. Chester Hartranft, who revised Sozomen's English, doubts this. All three agree that Alexander at last put Arius out of the church.

Some of his letters survive in the histories. In one, sent to bishops everywhere, he says that nearly a hundred bishops of Egypt and Libya met with him and condemned Arius. He told them to pay no heed to Eusebius of Nicomedia, who had taken up Arius's cause. To Alexander, bishop of Constantinople, he wrote that the Son was never made out of nothing. The Father and the Son cannot be parted, he said.

At the Council of Nicaea, Theodoret says, Athanasius came "as one of the retinue of Alexander." Theodoret has Alexander die five months after the council. Archibald Robertson, who edited Athanasius, reads an old index of the Easter letters as giving the 17th of April, 328.

Theodoret remembered him as "that admirable bishop, who had successfully withstood the blasphemies of Arius."
