At the Council of Chalcedon, the bishops stood before the altar rails and shouted, "Theodoret must now anathematize Nestorius." They wanted him to curse Nestorius, the deposed bishop of Constantinople, by name. Theodoret asked for his papers to be read. They wanted nothing read. "Unless I set forth at length my faith I cannot speak," he said. They called him a heretic. At last he said, "Anathema to Nestorius." The council's acts record the scene, and Blomfield Jackson, his translator, quotes them.

Theodoret was bishop of Cyrrhus, in Syria. His letter to the consul Nomus says his parents promised him to God before he was conceived. He lived in a monastery and was "unwillingly consecrated" bishop, in 423 by Jackson's reckoning.

He wrote that letter to answer his accusers. "From the revenues of my see I erected public porticoes; I built two large bridges," he says. He brought eight villages of Marcion's followers into the church, and he was stoned many times. To Leo of Rome he wrote, "I have done pastoral duty in eight hundred churches."

Nestorius was his close friend, Jackson says. After Rome condemned Nestorius in 430, John of Antioch asked Theodoret to answer the anathemas of Cyril of Alexandria. Theodoret held that God the Word "was not made flesh by mutation, but took flesh." Mary is the bearer of God, he wrote, "not because she gave birth in natural manner to God, but to man united to the God that had fashioned Him." Jackson judges that he and Cyril "were substantially at one."

In 449 a second council at Ephesus deposed him in his absence. Its Syriac acts have Dioscorus of Alexandria condemn him for writing against Cyril, and ask the emperors to burn his books. Theodoret wrote to Leo that the bishop of Alexandria had stabbed him "with a pen," when "I was five and thirty days’ march away." He added, "I await the sentence of your apostolic see."

Leo restored him, and Chalcedon let him in two years later. Jackson reports that Bertram, a modern scholar, thinks Theodoret then changed his views, and Möller that he kept them. Leo wrote to him of "the victory you and we together had won." In 553 a council at Constantinople condemned what "Theodoret impiously wrote" against Cyril.

His Church History, which takes up where Eusebius of Caesarea stopped, opens with painters. "Historians substitute books for panels, bright description for pigments," he wrote.
