In the Armenian district of Koghten, a monk named Mesrop Mashtots preached to people who still kept some pagan ways. But Armenian had no letters of its own, and preaching to the new believers was hard. So Mesrop prayed to God for letters for the Armenian tongue.

His pupil Koriun tells this in his Life of Mesrop, held in Victor Langlois's French edition. Koriun says he wrote of men he saw with his own eyes. He also wrote as a disciple honouring a saint, with demons put to flight. Mesrop, he says, came from Daron and studied Greek literature as a boy. A note in Langlois's edition gives his years as 361 to 441. He worked in the royal archives of the Armenian kings, then left the court to become a monk.

Mesrop took the problem to Sahak, patriarch of the Armenians. King Vramshapuh sent him to Syrian Mesopotamia. A Syrian bishop, Daniel, promised him letters, but they could not find signs for the sounds of Armenian. Then, Koriun says, the letters came to Mesrop in his heart, as if a right hand were writing them on stone. With a hermit named Rufinus, whom the text calls a man of Samos, he shaped them after the Greek alphabet.

The first book put into Armenian was the Proverbs of Solomon. Sahak and the king rejoiced, Koriun says, as if the letters were tablets from God. Koriun gives no year, only that Theodosius the Younger was then emperor. Mesrop gathered bright children with sweet voices and set up schools across the land. Koriun adds that he gave the Georgians letters and renewed those of a people called the Aghouank.

The Persian governors, Koriun says, let no Armenian under them learn Greek. So Sahak sent Mesrop to Constantinople, where its patriarch, Atticus, named him a preacher.

Koriun says Mesrop set out to fight a sect he calls the Borborides. Those who would not yield were jailed, then burned, shut away and driven out. Later a man named Theodore, cut off by the Council of Ephesus, brought books that followed Nestorius, bishop of Constantinople. The council sent letters, and Sahak and Mesrop drove him out.

Sahak died first. That same year, Koriun says, Mesrop fell ill in the New City, in Ararat. He sat up, blessed God, commended his pupils to him and died.

Langlois notes that the Armenian alphabet came to be called by his name, the Mesropian letters.
