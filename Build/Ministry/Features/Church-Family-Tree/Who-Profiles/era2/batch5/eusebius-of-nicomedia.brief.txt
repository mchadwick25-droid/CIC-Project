A bishop was leaving the Council of Nicaea for exile. Secundus of Ptolemais had refused to sign its creed. He turned to Eusebius of Nicomedia, who had signed. "You subscribed, Eusebius, in order to escape being sent into banishment," he said. Within a year, he warned, Eusebius would be sent away too. Within three months, he was.

Philostorgius tells this story. He was on Eusebius's side, but his book lives only in a hostile summary by Photius of Constantinople. Most other accounts come from the men who fought him. Among them are Alexander of Alexandria and his successor Athanasius.

He had been bishop of Berytus before he moved to Nicomedia. Sozomen says he was learned and stood high at the palace. Arius wrote to him as "my fellow-Lucianist." Philostorgius names him as one of the pupils of Lucian of Antioch.

When Alexander put Arius out, Eusebius took up his cause. Alexander told the bishops to "pay no attention to anything that Eusebius should write to you." One letter of his own survives, in the history of Theodoret of Cyrrhus. In it he asks Paulinus of Tyre to write to Alexander and win him over. The Son, he says, was not made from the Father's own being. "There is, indeed, nothing which is of His substance," he writes. All things came to be by God's will.

After Nicaea, Constantine wrote to the people of Nicomedia against him. The emperor called him "the co-operator in the cruelty of the tyrants." He said Eusebius had taken in men from Alexandria whom he had ordered seized. He sent him away with Theognis of Nicaea. Socrates of Constantinople ties their exile to Arius. He gives their plea to come home: "we subscribed the declaration of faith; we did not subscribe the anathematizing." They were brought back.

Later, Socrates says, Constantine's son, the emperor Constantius, moved him to the see of Constantinople. Philostorgius says Ulfila was made bishop of the Goths "by Eusebius and the other prelates." Louis Duchesne, a modern historian, says it was this Eusebius. Theodoret tells of Constantine's baptism but does not say who gave it. Blomfield Jackson, who translated Theodoret, adds in a note that Eusebius did. Socrates says Eusebius died soon after a synod at Antioch. He sets it in the fifth year after Constantine's death.

To Athanasius, "Eusebius and his fellows have always been the supporters and associates of the impious heresy."
