The emperor Constantius wanted Liberius, bishop of Rome, to condemn Athanasius of Alexandria. A record of their talk, which Theodoret of Cyrrhus copied into his Church History, gives his words. Asked why he stood alone, he said, "My standing alone does not make the truth a whit the weaker." Given three days to change his mind, he said, "Neither three days nor three months can change my sentiments. Send me wherever you please." He was banished to Beroea in Thrace. When the emperor sent him gold for the journey, he sent it back. The emperor, he said, needed it "to pay his troops."

Ammianus Marcellinus, a historian outside the church, tells the same refusal. He says Liberius was taken from Rome by night, for fear of the people, "among whom he was exceedingly popular." A deacon, Felix, was put in his place.

What happened in exile is disputed. Athanasius, his ally, says that after two years Liberius "gave way, and from fear of threatened death subscribed," and he blames those who forced him. Jerome says Liberius, worn out by exile, signed heresy and entered Rome as if he had won. Sozomen says he signed a creed made at Sirmium, with his own statement added. Theodoret tells of no lapse at all. Letters in which Liberius says he gave up Athanasius survive in Latin in a dossier gathered by Hilary of Poitiers. Feder, who edited the dossier, marks those letters as doubtful.

Theodoret says the noblewomen of Rome begged Constantius to bring their bishop back. The emperor proposed that Liberius and Felix share the church. The crowd cried, "One God, one Christ, one bishop." Liberius returned, and Felix withdrew.

An account of Liberius and Felix, kept in a Latin collection of papal letters, says he died on the twenty-fourth of September, in a year it names by its consuls. Chester Hartranft, Sozomen's translator, puts it in 366.

Ambrose of Milan remembered him at Christmas, giving the veil to Ambrose's sister in Saint Peter's. "You," Ambrose recalls him saying, "my daughter, have desired a good espousal."
