Four robbers once broke into a monk's cell in the Egyptian desert. Palladius of Helenopolis, in his book of holy lives, tells what followed. The monk tied them together and carried them on his back like a bundle of straw to the church of the brothers. "Since I am not allowed to hurt anyone," he asked, "what do you bid me do with these?" They knew him then as Moses, once a famous robber himself, and they too left the world.

Palladius calls him "an Ethiopian by race and black." His master, an official, drove him out for vice and robbery. People said, Palladius reports, that Moses led a robber band and had gone as far as murder. Then one of his raids struck his conscience, and he gave himself up to a monastery.

His old habits fought him. Moses himself said, as Palladius reports, that demons pressed him so hard toward lust that he almost gave up. Isidore, the priest of Scete, told him to stop struggling with the demons. "I will never cease until the appearance of the demons ceases," Moses answered. He died at Scete at seventy-five, a priest, and left seventy disciples. Sozomen, a church historian, calls him "an Egyptian" instead.

A Syriac book of desert sayings keeps more of an Abba Moses, and there were several monks of that name. W. K. Lowther Clarke, who translated Palladius, notes that Cuthbert Butler tells them apart, and Clarke takes the robber for the Moses of the sayings. But many sayings name only Abba Moses. One tells of barbarians coming to Scete, where Moses stayed and was killed with the brothers who stayed with him.

One saying calls him Abba Moses the Black. In another, when Moses was one of the clergy, his bishop had the clergy drive him out, to test him. "Get outside, O Ethiopian," they said. He told himself, "They have treated thee rightly, O thou whose skin is dark and black."
