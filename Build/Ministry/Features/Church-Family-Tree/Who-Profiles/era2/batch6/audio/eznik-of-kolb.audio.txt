[calmly] Before anything existed, the Persian magi said, there was a being called Zurvan. Eznik of Kolb retells their story in his Refutation of the Sects. For a thousand years, they said, Zurvan offered sacrifice so that a son, Ormizd, would be born to make heaven and earth. Then he doubted, and two sons were conceived: Ormizd from the sacrifices, and Ahriman from the doubt.

Eznik told the story to take it apart. If Zurvan doubted, he wrote, then he was powerless, imperfect and ignorant, and he himself was the cause of evil. Two springs never flow from one opening, he argued, one sweet and one bitter. He reports, as an opponent, a faith he attacks. The Library holds only part of one of his four books, in French.

Eznik was a pupil of Sahak the Great and of Mesrop Mashtots, who made the Armenian letters. Koriun, who had grown up with him, tells his early story. Sahak and Mesrop sent Eznik and a companion, Joseph of Baghin, to Edessa to translate the Scriptures again from Syriac. The two went on to Constantinople to study and became translators of Greek. Koriun and Leon, a priest of Vanant, joined Eznik there, as a brother they had grown up with. They came home with true copies of the Scriptures and the canons of the councils of Nicaea and Ephesus.

Moses of Khoren tells it differently. He says lying letters drew the two to Byzantium without their teachers' order. The new translations made from their copies, he adds, were found faulty in many places.

He appears once more, as bishop of Bagrevand. Elishe and Lazar of Pharp both list him among the bishops who answered a Persian edict calling Armenia to the Persian faith. Elishe places the meeting at Artashat and says the edict came from Mihr-Nerseh, the Persian governor. Lazar names King Yazdegerd as its author, and neither gives a year. The introduction to his book in Victor Langlois's collection says no one recorded his birth or death.

[warmly] The same introduction calls him, beyond question, one of the most famous writers of Armenia in the fifth century.
