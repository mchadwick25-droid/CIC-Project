[calmly] Saxons and Picts had joined forces against the Britons. Much of the British army had just been baptized at Easter. Now, Constantius of Lyon writes, Germanus, a bishop from Gaul, made himself leader of the battle. He hid the men in a valley ringed by hills. As the enemy came near, the bishops shouted Alleluia three times, and the whole army took up the cry. The enemy fled in terror.

Constantius wrote his Life of Germanus about 480 or a little later, by the date of its editor, Wilhelm Levison. It is a saint's Life, full of wonders, and Levison knows no other record of that raid.

Germanus was born at Auxerre to noble parents, Constantius says. He studied law at Rome and pleaded in the prefect's courts. The state made him a governor of provinces. Then clergy and people with one voice demanded him as their bishop, and he gave way against his will.

Why he first went to Britain is told two ways. Prosper of Aquitaine's chronicle, in an entry its editor sets in 429, says a Pelagian named Agricola was corrupting the British churches. Pope Celestine, urged by the deacon Palladius, sent Germanus in his place. Constantius never mentions Celestine. He says the Britons asked the bishops of Gaul for help, and a large synod chose Germanus and Lupus. Levison calls every attempt to join the two accounts guesswork.

When the heresy spread again, Constantius says, Germanus went back with a bishop named Severus. Bede, writing in England, tells both journeys, but Levison says Bede copied Constantius almost word for word, so he is no second witness.

At last he went to Ravenna, where Placidia ruled the empire with her son Valentinian. He fell ill there and asked that his body be sent home, which Placidia granted against her wish. He died on the seventh day of his illness.

[warmly] A Gallic chronicle of 452 says that Germanus, bishop of Auxerre, was famous for his miracles and for the strictness of his life.
