[calmly] In Rome, Marcellus, bishop of Ancyra in Galatia, wrote to Julius, bishop of the city. He had waited there a whole year and three months, he says. He wrote in his own hand and gave his creed. At the Council of Nicaea, he reminds Julius, he had refuted the Arians. He confesses the Word who always exists with the Father, and Christ's kingdom, which will have no end. The letter survives only in Greek, in a book against heresies by Epiphanius of Salamis.

His opponents' charges explain why he was in Rome. Socrates of Constantinople says Marcellus wrote against Asterius, a writer on the side of Arius. In doing so he fell into the opposite error and taught, as Paul of Samosata had, that Christ was a mere man. Sozomen says he taught that the Son began when he was born of Mary, and that his kingdom would end. Bishops meeting at Constantinople deposed him and put Basil in his place.

Julius received him, and the Western bishops at Sardica cleared him. His questions, they said, had been passed off as his own views, and he had written that Christ's kingdom had neither beginning nor end. The Eastern bishops, in a letter quoted in A. C. Zenos's notes to Socrates, called him "the most execrable of all heretics."

Whether Athanasius of Alexandria stood by him is disputed. Epiphanius once asked him. Athanasius only smiled, Epiphanius says, hinting that Marcellus was not far from error, yet treating him as cleared. The dossier of Hilary of Poitiers says Athanasius refused him communion. E. W. Watson, in his introduction to the English Hilary, says it is "notorious that he never did so."

Epiphanius writes that Marcellus had died about two years before, more or less. In 381 the Council of Constantinople condemned the Marcellians by name. Hefele, quoted in Henry Percival's edition of the council records, says Marcellus himself came back to the truth.

Epiphanius left the judgment open. [warmly] The hidden things of Marcellus's mind, he wrote, are known to God.
