[calmly] In 429, by the consuls its title names, a Latin writer named Marius Mercator gave the church of Constantinople a short warning, written in Greek. Its title says it concerned Caelestius, a follower of Pelagius. After it was read, the title goes on, Caelestius and Julian of Eclanum were driven from the city with their companions by the emperor's order. They were later condemned at the Council of Ephesus.

Little more is known of his life. Garnier, his first editor, notes that even Gennadius of Marseilles leaves him out of his list of church writers. Augustine of Hippo wrote to him as "my son." In that letter, held here in Latin, Augustine says Mercator's letters reached him at Carthage. He had not known, he says, how far Mercator had grown.

Where he came from is disputed. Garnier guessed southern Italy, since Mercator knew the private lives of Caelestius and Julian so well. Baluze, a later editor, guessed Africa. Schoenemann, a later scholar, says both are guesses. Garnier thought him a layman, since Augustine called him son and not brother. Hefele, the historian of the councils, calls him a layman living in Constantinople.

His chief work was translation. He put sermons of Nestorius, the deposed bishop of Constantinople, into Latin, with letters of Nestorius and of Cyril of Alexandria. Garnier dates his preface after the Council of Ephesus. He wanted Latin readers to know these sayings and avoid them. Hefele says Nestorius's twelve anathemas against Cyril survive only in Mercator's Latin.

He wrote as a partisan, and Garnier gathers from him rumours about Caelestius and Julian. In a book against Julian, Mercator traced the error back to Theodore of Mopsuestia.

No held line gives his death. Garnier thought he wrote nothing after the Council of Chalcedon. Baluze thought he did, since he names the followers of Eutyches.

In one preface he set out his method. He had tried to turn the Greek word for word, he wrote. [warmly] He did not want to be proved a forger rather than a true translator.
