[calmly] In 383 the emperor Theodosius called the heads of every church party to his palace. Each was to write down its faith. Socrates of Constantinople says "Eunomius himself undertook the cause of the Eunomians." The emperor shut himself up alone, prayed, and read each one. He kept only the creed that held the Son to be of one substance with the Father. Sozomen, another church historian of the next century, says he destroyed the rest.

Eunomius of Cyzicus led the Anomoeans, as their foes called them. They were charged with making the Son unlike the Father. Philostorgius, a historian of his own party who praised him, says he came from Cappadocia and studied under Aetius of Antioch. Philostorgius survives mainly in a summary by Photius, a later patriarch of Constantinople and a hostile one. Gregory of Nyssa, a foe, says Eunomius's father was a poor farmer. In winter he carved letters of the alphabet for schoolboys.

Eunomius became bishop of Cyzicus. Philostorgius puts this under the emperor Constantius, Socrates and Sozomen under Valens. His own clergy said he made the Son unlike the Father. Philostorgius says he answered them in Constantinople and won the clergy there to his side. Socrates says the people of Cyzicus grew tired of his showy talk and drove him out. Sozomen says Eudoxius, the bishop in the capital, found no fault in him. Eunomius still would not go back.

His Apology survives in his own words. God, he writes, is "the Unbegotten Substance." God has no parts, so he cannot share his being. Therefore "the Son, when he was not before, was begotten," by the Father's will. The Son made all else, and is the Father's image in what he does. To share one substance, Eunomius held, would split God. He cites Christ, "The Father, who sent me, is Greater than I."

Basil of Caesarea wrote against the Apology. Gregory of Nyssa answered Eunomius's later reply, and mocked the Apology as a speech "before an imaginary court." To Gregory, God's being lies past all words. "We have learnt to honour in silence what transcends speech and thought," he writes.

Theodosius later banished Eunomius. Sozomen says he died soon after and was buried at Dacora in Cappadocia, where he was born. Philostorgius had seen him in Constantinople at twenty. [warmly] Photius says Philostorgius "compares also to pearls the speeches which flowed from his lips," though he admits Eunomius lisped.
