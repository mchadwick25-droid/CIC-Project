[calmly] From his earliest years, Auxentius was Ulfila's pupil. His parents gave him to the bishop of the Goths, who taught him the holy writings and raised him in the faith like a son. So he says in a letter written after Ulfila died. He could not praise his teacher enough, he writes, and he did not dare stay silent.

Auxentius of Durostorum was a Homoian bishop of the late fourth century. Friedrich Kauffmann, who edited the letter in 1899, places Durostorum in Lower Moesia, at modern Silistria. He thinks Ulfila held that see first. Maximinus, an Arian bishop writing against Ambrose of Milan, kept him apart from Auxentius of Milan. Ambrose had named an Auxentius, he says, without saying which. One was alive, at Durostorum. The other, at Milan, had died without a successor.

The letter survives because Maximinus copied it into his own book, a defence of two bishops condemned at the Council of Aquileia in 381. It comes down in the margins of a fifth-century manuscript of Hilary of Poitiers, now in Paris. Kauffmann, after Ludwig Traube, dates that hand probably to the sixth century.

Auxentius tells his teacher's life in the numbers of Scripture. Ulfila became bishop at thirty, like David, Joseph and Christ. Seven years among the Goths and thirty-three in Roman lands make forty, like Moses. Kauffmann notes that writers of that time often used numbers so, yet he counts them as real years.

How much is Auxentius's own was argued in Kauffmann's day. W. Luft held that only the first part keeps his words, and the rest is Maximinus's summary of another work. Kauffmann answered that the whole fragment has one biblical style, unlike Maximinus's. He thought Auxentius sent it from Constantinople to the Arians of Moesia, soon after Ulfila's death.

Auxentius was still alive when Maximinus wrote. [warmly] He was ready, Maximinus says, to stand with Palladius of Ratiaria wherever the faith was put to a public test.
