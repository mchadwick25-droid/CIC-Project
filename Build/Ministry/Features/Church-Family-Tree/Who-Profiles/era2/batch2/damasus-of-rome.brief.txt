On one day in Rome, a hundred and thirty-seven dead bodies were found in the Basilica of Sicininus, a Christian church. Ammianus Marcellinus, a pagan soldier and historian, tells it. Two men wanted to be bishop of the city, Damasus and Ursinus. Their followers fought until men were hurt and killed, and the city's prefect was forced out to the suburbs. In the end, Ammianus says, Damasus won by the hard work of his side.

Damasus of Rome followed Liberius as bishop in 366. Ammianus blames both rivals, "both immoderately eager to obtain the bishopric." Socrates of Constantinople blames Ursinus. He says Ursinus was a deacon who could not bear his defeat, and had bishops "of little distinction" ordain him in secret. An account from the clergy who backed Ursinus tells it the other way. It is kept in Latin in a Roman book of letters, the Collectio Avellana. It says Damasus paid charioteers, arena men and gravediggers, and stormed a church held by the other side. It counts a hundred and sixty dead, men and women, and none on his own side.

As bishop, Damasus held to the creed of the Council of Nicaea. Theodoret of Cyrus kept some of his letters in the history of the church he wrote in the next century. One, from a council at Rome, condemned Auxentius of Milan. It said bishops who thought otherwise should be "deprived of the name of bishop." Another condemned Timothy, a follower of Apollinarius of Laodicea. Damasus wrote that Timothy had been condemned "by the judgment of the apostolic see."

Jerome of Strido worked for him at Rome. He later wrote that he helped Damasus with his letters, and wrote his answers to councils of the East and West. Damasus asked him to correct the Latin Gospels against the Greek. "You urge me to revise the old Latin version," Jerome's preface begins. He knew he would be called a forger for it. His comfort was that "the command is given by you who are the supreme bishop."

Sulpicius Severus, a chronicler from Aquitaine, says Priscillian of Abila and two Spanish bishops came to Rome to clear themselves before Damasus. "They were not even admitted to his presence."

He died under the emperor Theodosius, at almost eighty, Jerome says. Jerome remembered a bishop who "had a fine talent for making verses and published many brief works in heroic metre."
