In 379, about nine months after their brother Basil of Caesarea died, Gregory of Nyssa went to see his sister Macrina in Pontus. They had not met for nearly eight years. He found her lying on the floor on a sack spread over a board, worn out by fever. She raised herself on her elbow, but could not get up to meet him.

Macrina the Younger was the eldest child of a Christian family of Pontus, named for her grandmother, who had confessed Christ in the persecutions. Gregory's Life of her is a loving record, written in her honour, and it tells dreams and a healing as signs from God.

When she was about twelve, Gregory says, her father chose a young man for her to marry, but he died. She held that her father's choice already bound her, and refused all the suitors who came after. When Basil came home from his studies "puffed up beyond measure with the pride of oratory," Gregory says she drew him to a life of prayer and of work with his hands. She also persuaded their mother to give up her servants and live as an equal with her maids. W. K. Lowther Clarke, who translated the Life in 1916, reads their home as a double monastery, with the women under Macrina and the men under her brother Peter. In a famine, she took in children left by the roadside and raised them.

Gregory later wrote down their talks at her deathbed as a dialogue, On the Soul and the Resurrection. It is his written form of her teaching, and in it he calls her "the Teacher." When he doubted that the soul lives on after death, Gregory writes, "Away, she cried, with that pagan nonsense!"

Near the end, Gregory says, she stopped speaking to those around her and spoke to God in prayer. "Thou, O Lord, hast freed us from the fear of death," she began.
