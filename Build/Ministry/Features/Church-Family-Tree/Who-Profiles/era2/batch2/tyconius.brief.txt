"Of all the things that occur to me, I consider none so necessary as to write a little book of rules." So Tyconius opened his Book of Rules. His seven rules would be keys and windows into "the secret places of the law." With them, a reader crossing "the vast forest of prophecy" would not lose the way.

Tyconius was an African of the late fourth century and a Donatist. The Donatist church had split from the main body in Africa over those who lapsed in the Great Persecution. F. C. Burkitt edited the Book of Rules. He says the little known of his life comes chiefly from Augustine of Hippo, a Catholic bishop who wrote against the Donatists.

Augustine says Tyconius wrote "in defence of the Church" against his own party. He reported a Donatist council that took back those who had handed over the Scriptures, without baptizing them again. Parmenian, the Donatist bishop of Carthage, answered him and warned him not to write such things. Tyconius still kept apart from the Catholics. Augustine calls this "a most inconsistent disposition."

Augustine set out the seven rules in his guide to the Bible, On Christian Doctrine. The second rule held that the Lord's body has two parts, good and bad, joined for now. Tyconius found it in the Song of Songs, where the bride says she is dark and beautiful. Augustine renamed the rule "the mixed Church." He faulted the third rule for saying that faith does not come to us from God. The book "must be read with caution," he warned, chiefly for what its author taught as a Donatist.

Gennadius of Marseilles says Tyconius also explained the whole Apocalypse, "regarding nothing in it in a carnal sense." Burkitt says that commentary is believed to be lost, though later writers copied much of it. Augustine commended the Book of Rules to students, "for it is of very great assistance in understanding Scripture."
