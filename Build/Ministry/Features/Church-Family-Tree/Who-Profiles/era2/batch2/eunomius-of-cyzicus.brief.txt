In 383 the emperor Theodosius called the heads of every church party to his palace. Each was to write down its faith. Socrates of Constantinople says "Eunomius himself undertook the cause of the Eunomians." The emperor shut himself up alone, prayed, and read each one. He kept only the creed that held the Son to be of one substance with the Father. Sozomen, a church historian of the next century, says he destroyed the rest.

Eunomius of Cyzicus led the Anomoeans, a name their foes gave them for making the Son unlike the Father. Philostorgius, a historian of his own party who praised him, says he came from Cappadocia and studied under Aetius of Antioch. That history survives mainly in a summary by Photius, a later patriarch of Constantinople. He was hostile to it. Gregory of Nyssa, a foe, says Eunomius's father was a poor farmer. In winter he carved letters of the alphabet for schoolboys.

Philostorgius has him made bishop of Cyzicus under Constantius, and Socrates and Sozomen under Valens. Some of his clergy said he made the Son unlike the Father. Philostorgius says he answered them in Constantinople and won over the clergy there. Socrates, a Nicene opponent, says the people of Cyzicus tired of his showy talk and drove him out. Sozomen says Eudoxius, the bishop in the capital, found no fault in him, but Eunomius would not go back.

His Apology survives in his own words. God, he writes, is "the Unbegotten Substance." God has no parts and cannot share his being. So "the Son, when he was not before, was begotten," by the Father's will. The Son made all else, and is the Father's image in what he does. To share one substance, Eunomius held, would split God. He cites Christ, "The Father, who sent me, is Greater than I."

Basil of Caesarea wrote against the Apology. Gregory of Nyssa answered Eunomius's later reply, and mocked the Apology as a speech "before an imaginary court." To Gregory, God's being lies past all words. "We have learnt to honour in silence what transcends speech and thought," he writes.

Theodosius later banished Eunomius. Sozomen says he died soon after and was buried at Dacora, his birthplace in Cappadocia. Philostorgius says he was later moved to Tyana. Philostorgius had seen him in Constantinople at twenty. Photius says Philostorgius "compares also to pearls the speeches which flowed from his lips," though he admits Eunomius lisped.
