At Laodicea in Syria, the pagan teacher Epiphanius recited a hymn he had written to the god Dionysus. As the custom was, he told the uninitiated to go out. Among those who stayed were the young Apollinaris, his pupil, and his father, also named Apollinaris. Sozomen, a church historian of the fifth century, says the bishop, Theodotus, put both out of the church. After tears and fasting, Theodotus took them back.

Apollinaris of Laodicea was born in that city to a father from Alexandria, says Socrates of Constantinople. The next bishop, George, put the young man out again. Sozomen says it was for his friendship with Athanasius of Alexandria, then passing through. Socrates blames their friendship with Epiphanius.

When the emperor Julian barred Christians from the Greek classics, Socrates says father and son rewrote Scripture in Greek forms. The father put the books of Moses into epic verse, and the son turned the Gospels into dialogues like Plato's. Sozomen names only one of them. He gives him an epic of Hebrew history, plays and odes. Hans Lietzmann, who gathered his fragments in 1904, finds Sozomen the more credible.

His followers, Socrates says, kept the Nicene teaching on the Trinity. His teaching on Christ reaches us mostly through opponents. Socrates says he taught that Christ took a soul without reason, "God the Word himself being in the place of a mind." Gregory of Nazianzus, who wrote against him, reports the same. "For that which He has not assumed He has not healed," Gregory replied. A fragment kept by Gregory of Nyssa gives his own reason. If perfect God were joined to a perfect man, he wrote, there would be two sons, one by nature and one by adoption.

His followers formed their own churches, Sozomen says. Men sang his songs at their work, and women at the loom. Damasus of Rome condemned him at a Roman council, in 377 by Lietzmann's date. In 381 the council at Constantinople named the Apollinarians in its first canon.

Jerome of Strido heard him lecture at Antioch. "Although he instructed me in the holy scriptures, I never embraced his disputable doctrine as to their meaning," Jerome wrote. Lietzmann found that the church destroyed his books of doctrine wherever it could. Some survived because people who shared his views put them under trusted names, among them Athanasius and Julius of Rome. Jerome says he died under the emperor Theodosius.
