Lucifer of Cagliari would not condemn the Nicene faith. Instead he wrote a book attacking the emperor, and sent it to the emperor to read. Jerome of Strido tells it in his Lives of Illustrious Men. Lucifer was bishop of Cagliari in Sardinia. Liberius of Rome had sent him to the emperor Constantius, with the clergy Pancratius and Hilary. He went "with wonderful constancy and willingness to meet martyrdom," Jerome says.

This was the quarrel over Athanasius of Alexandria. At the Council of Milan in 354, the emperor pressed the Western bishops to condemn Athanasius. Lucifer refused, and was sent into exile from 355 to 361. He spent most of it at Eleutheropolis in Palestine, and then in the Thebaid, in Egypt. From there he sent the emperor a copy of his attack. There he wrote his pamphlets against Constantius, which survive. In them he would not grant that a Christian could obey a heretical emperor.

When Constantius died in 361, the new emperor Julian let the exiled bishops go home. Antioch was split between two orthodox parties. Lucifer went there and made Paulinus bishop of one of them. Socrates of Constantinople says he did this against the wishes of the council then meeting at Alexandria. In Robert Wallis's edition, Socrates says "a distinct sect arose under the name of 'Luciferians.'"

Lucifer returned to Sardinia. He would not accept back into office any bishop who had once given way to the Arians. His followers broke with the wider church over this. Jerome says the schism outlived him. Theodoret of Cyrrhus says it faded in time and was forgotten.

Jerome, who had been ordained by Lucifer's own man Paulinus, wrote a Dialogue against the Luciferians about 379. In it he weighs Lucifer with reluctance. He grants his courage against the Arians, then judges the schism he began. "He himself was a good shepherd," Jerome writes, "but he was leaving a vast spoil to the beasts of prey."
