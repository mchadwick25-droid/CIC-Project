Lucifer of Cagliari would not condemn the Nicene faith. Instead he wrote a book against the emperor Constantius and sent it to him to read. Jerome of Strido, in his Lives of Illustrious Men, says Lucifer wrote it "with wonderful constancy and willingness to meet martyrdom." Lucifer was bishop of Cagliari in Sardinia. Liberius of Rome had sent him to the emperor with the clergy Pancratius and Hilary.

The emperor wanted the Western bishops to condemn Athanasius of Alexandria. Lucifer refused at a council in Milan. W. H. Fremantle, who translated Jerome, says he opposed the emperor with much violence. He was exiled from 355 to 361. He spent most of it at Eleutheropolis in Palestine, and the rest in Egypt. His pamphlets from exile survive in Latin, with titles such as Of Apostate Kings.

When Constantius died in 361, the emperor Julian let the exiles go home. Theodoret of Cyrrhus says the church at Antioch was split in two, though both sides held the same creed. Instead of joining the council at Alexandria, Lucifer went straight to Antioch. There he made Paulinus bishop of one party. Eusebius of Vercelli, who had shared his exile, disapproved. Socrates of Constantinople says Lucifer took this as an insult and broke with him.

Lucifer went home to Sardinia. Fremantle says he refused church office to anyone who had given way to the Arians. Socrates writes that "a distinct sect arose under the name of 'Luciferians.'" Fremantle says it had died out by early in the next century. Jerome's Chronicle says he died in 371.

Jerome had been ordained by Paulinus, the bishop Lucifer made. About 379 he wrote a Dialogue against the Luciferians. In it he calls Lucifer "that saintly man," yet blames him for the split. "He himself was a good shepherd," Jerome writes, "but he was leaving a vast spoil to the beasts of prey."
