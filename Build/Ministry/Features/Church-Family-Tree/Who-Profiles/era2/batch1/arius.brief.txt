About the year 318, in Alexandria, Bishop Alexander was teaching his clergy about the Trinity. One of his presbyters spoke up. "If the Father begat the Son," said Arius, "he that was begotten had a beginning of existence: and from this it is evident, that there was a time when the Son was not." Socrates of Constantinople reports the words. That argument began the Arian controversy.

Arius was a presbyter of Alexandria, in charge of the church of Baucalis. Athanasius of Alexandria, his great foe, grants that he was given charge of teaching Scripture. His own letters survive in the hands of his enemies. Theodoret kept his letter to Eusebius of Nicomedia. In it Arius signs himself "unjustly persecuted by Alexander the Pope." He ends, "We are persecuted, because we say that the Son has a beginning, but that God is without beginning."

Arius taught that the Son was not eternal. God made him out of nothing, before all time, as the first and highest of creatures. To spread this, Arius wrote songs for sailors and travellers, and a book called the Thalia. Only his enemies kept any of it. Athanasius quotes its start, "According to faith of God's elect, God's prudent ones," then mocks the verses that follow.

In 325 the emperor Constantine called the Council of Nicaea, mainly to settle it. The council condemned Arius and declared the Son "consubstantial with the Father," the same in being as God. Socrates records that the emperor sent Arius into exile, and his books were burned.

Years later Constantine was persuaded to take him back. Arius came to Constantinople, and a day was set for his return to communion. The aged bishop Alexander of Constantinople prayed, Athanasius says, that "either Arius or himself might be taken away" first. The day before his return, Arius died suddenly in the city. His friends blamed magic. Alexander's side saw the hand of God.

The kind account of Arius comes from Philostorgius, who praised him. His history survives only in a hostile summary by Photius, who calls him a liar. So the attack on Arius and the defence of him both reach us through his opponents. The creed the council wrote against him became the measure of the faith for the churches that condemned him.
