Two boys were found under a tree, studying their lessons, after everyone else on their ship had been killed. So Rufinus of Aquileia tells it in his Latin Church History. They were Frumentius and Edesius, young relatives of Meropius, a philosopher from Tyre, who had taken them to what Rufinus calls India. On the way home the ship put in for water. The local people had heard that their treaty with Rome was broken, and they killed all on board. They pitied the boys and took them to their king.

Rufinus says he had the story from Edesius himself, who later became a presbyter at Tyre. The Greek historians Socrates of Constantinople and Sozomen retell it after him. Valesius, whose note A. C. Zenos prints with his translation, says Socrates translated Rufinus almost word for word. Zenos adds that the writers meant Ethiopia by India.

The king made Edesius his cup-bearer and put Frumentius in charge of his accounts. When the king died, the queen asked the two to help rule until her son grew up. Frumentius sought out Christians among the Roman merchants. Rufinus says he urged them to set up places of prayer, and gave them sites to build on.

When the prince came of age, the two went home. Frumentius went to Alexandria and asked its bishop, Athanasius of Alexandria, to send a bishop to the new Christians. Athanasius, Rufinus says, replied that they could find no better man than Frumentius himself. He consecrated him and sent him back.

A second witness does not depend on Rufinus. Athanasius later copied into his defence a letter from the emperor Constantius to the princes of Auxumis, the city of Axum. It calls Frumentius "Bishop of Auxumis." The emperor writes that Frumentius "was advanced to his present rank by Athanasius, a man who is guilty of ten thousand crimes."

Rufinus puts all this soon after Athanasius became bishop. Valesius thought it came later, under Constantius. Archibald Robertson, who edited Athanasius in English, agrees. He says the dates in the story rule out the early year. The emperor's letter, he adds, makes most sense if Frumentius had been made bishop not long before.

Chester Hartranft, who translated Sozomen, notes that Frumentius was called the Abba Salama of Axum. Blomfield Jackson, in a note to Theodoret, gives the meaning from the Dictionary of Christian Biography: "the Father of Peace."
