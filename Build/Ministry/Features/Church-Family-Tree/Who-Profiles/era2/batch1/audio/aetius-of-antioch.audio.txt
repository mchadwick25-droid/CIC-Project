[calmly] About the year 350, at Antioch, Bishop Leontius ordained a deacon named Aetius and let him preach. The choice caused such an uproar that Leontius soon had to suspend him. Aetius argued about God with a boldness that troubled even his fellow Arians. They gave him a nickname: Atheus, the godless one. Socrates of Constantinople records it.

Aetius came from Coele-Syria, in Syria, and was poor. Philostorgius, who admired him, says he worked as a goldsmith to keep himself and his mother, then turned to logic. Socrates, who did not admire him, says he leaned on Aristotle's book of Categories, and built clever arguments that fooled only himself.

Aetius taught that the Son is not like the Father. If God alone is unbegotten in his being, he reasoned, then the Son, who is begotten, cannot share that being. His short book of such claims, the Syntagmation, survives inside the work of Epiphanius of Salamis, who copied it out to refute it. Those who followed Aetius were called Anomoeans, from the Greek word for "unlike."

Even the Arians found this too much. They preferred to say the Son was like the Father, and to drop the word "being" from the quarrel. At the Council of Constantinople in 360, his friend Eudoxius gave him up, Archibald Robertson writes, as "a sacrifice to the Emperor's scruples." The council took away his office of deacon.

When the emperor Julian came to power, he recalled Aetius from exile and sent him warm letters. Philostorgius says Julian gave him land on the island of Lesbos. Aetius later lived at Constantinople, where he died. [warmly] His pupil Eunomius carried the teaching on, and in time the sect was named the Eunomians after him.
