[calmly] Palladius of Helenopolis tells of a stranger who came to the monastery of Tabennisi, in Upper Egypt, dressed as a workman. Its head, Pachomius, said he was too old for the life and would not take him in. After seven days of waiting and fasting, he was let in. Through Lent he stood in a corner weaving palm leaves, and he ate a few cabbage leaves on Sundays. The brothers told Pachomius, "Either drive him out, or know that we are all going." Pachomius prayed and learned that the stranger was Macarius of Alexandria, a famous monk. "I thank you for letting my children feel your fist," he told him, "lest they should be proud of their ascetic achievements."

Pachomius, an Egyptian monk of the fourth century, first lived as a hermit in a cave. Palladius, and the church historian Sozomen after him, say an angel told him to gather young monks around him. The angel gave him a tablet with a rule written on it. Palladius visited the Pachomian houses some forty years after Pachomius died, and he tells it as the story of a holy man.

By Palladius's account, each monk ate and fasted as his strength allowed, and those who ate more did the heavier work. Monks lived three to a cell and ate together in silence, hoods over their heads. They were sorted into twenty-four groups, each named by a Greek letter. When Pachomius said the prayers were few, the angel answered that the rule was made so "that even the little ones keep the rule and are not afflicted."

Lowther Clarke, who translated Palladius, reports that the scholar Ladeuze trusted the Greek Lives of Pachomius over this rule. Ladeuze thought the angel legendary and the letter groups Palladius's own invention. Butler disagreed, and called Palladius's version "probably the most authentic epitome."

Palladius counts seven thousand monks under the rule. At Panopolis he found three hundred monks working at every craft, and their surplus fed the women's houses and the prisons. The women lived by the same rule across the river.

An Ethiopic version of the rules also survives. George Schodde, who translated it in 1885, judged that its first part reaches back "almost to the days of Pachomius himself." Another part, of different origin, says women gathered around Pachomius's own sister, in a house he made for her.

[warmly] Schodde's introduction opens by calling Pachomius "the father of monastic institutions."
