[calmly] In Alexandria, Bishop Alexander was teaching his clergy about the Trinity. One of his presbyters spoke up. "If the Father begat the Son," said Arius, "he that was begotten had a beginning of existence: and from this it is evident, that there was a time when the Son was not." Socrates of Constantinople, writing a century later, starts the quarrel here. Archibald Robertson, who edited Athanasius in English, dates it to 318 or 319. He says no one knows for sure how it began. In Constantine's own letter, it began when Alexander asked his clergy about a passage of Scripture.

Arius was a presbyter of Alexandria, in charge of the church of Baucalis. Robertson says he also taught Scripture. His own letters survive in his enemies' books. Theodoret of Cyrus kept his letter to Eusebius of Nicomedia. In it Arius signs himself "unjustly persecuted by Alexander the Pope." Near the end he writes, "We are persecuted, because we say that the Son has a beginning, but that God is without beginning."

Arius taught that God made the Son from nothing. Yet in the same letter he says the Son "has subsisted before time, and before ages, as perfect God." Philostorgius, a later historian on his side, says Arius wrote songs for sailors, millers and travellers. Arius also wrote a book called the Thalia. Only his enemies kept any of it. Athanasius quotes its start, "According to faith of God's elect, God's prudent ones," then calls the lines that follow mockeries.

In 325 the Council of Nicaea condemned Arius and declared the Son to be of one being with the Father. Constantine sent Arius into exile and ordered his books burned, Socrates records.

Years later Arius swore to Constantine that his faith was right. Athanasius tells what came next, as the presbyter Macarius told it to him. Alexander, bishop of Constantinople, prayed that if Arius came to communion, God would "let me Thy servant depart." If not, he asked God to "take off Arius." Arius died suddenly the day before. Sozomen, as Robertson reports, gives three views. His friends blamed magic. Alexander's side saw God's judgment. Most people blamed a weak heart.

Philostorgius praised Arius, but found fault with him too. His history survives only in a hostile summary by Photius, a patriarch of Constantinople in the 800s, who calls him a liar. [warmly] So both the attack on Arius and his defence reach us through opponents.
