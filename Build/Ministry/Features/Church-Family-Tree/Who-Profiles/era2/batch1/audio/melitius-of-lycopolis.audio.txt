[calmly] Epiphanius of Salamis tells of a prison in Egypt during the Great Persecution, early in the fourth century. Peter, bishop of Alexandria, was held there with Melitius and other clergy. They split over how to treat Christians who had given way. Peter hung a cloak across the middle of the prison like a curtain. A deacon called out that Peter's friends should come to his side, and Melitius's friends to his. Most of the clergy and monks, Epiphanius says, went to Melitius.

Epiphanius wrote about seventy years later, as a foe of every heresy. Archibald Robertson, who edited Athanasius, calls this "The Meletian account of the matter."

Melitius was bishop of Lycopolis, in the Thebaid of Upper Egypt. Of his own writing, a list of his clergy survives. Two letters of the time speak against him. Four bishops in prison, among them Phileas of Thmuis, scolded him for ordaining priests in their churches. Peter wrote that Melitius had "ordained in the prison several unto himself." He told his flock to shun him.

Athanasius of Alexandria, who later held Peter's see, was Melitius's fiercest enemy. He wrote that Peter put him out of office for many crimes, among them "offering sacrifice to idols." H. Idris Bell, who published papyri of Melitius's church in 1924, doubts this. Such charges came easily after a great persecution, he says. Bell and Robertson agree the split was over church order. It was never about doctrine.

In 325 the Council of Nicaea let Melitius keep the title of bishop. It forbade him to ordain. Its letter, kept by Socrates of Constantinople, left him "a nominal dignity." Sozomen, the church historian, says that near his death he named his friend John to follow him. This broke the council's terms.

His followers called themselves the Church of the Martyrs, Epiphanius says. Later they sided with the Arians against Athanasius. [warmly] Bell traces them in Egypt into the eighth century.
