[calmly] In Milan, soon after the emperor Valentinian arrived there in 364, Hilary of Poitiers and Eusebius of Vercelli accused the city's bishop. He was Auxentius of Milan, an Arian who had held the see under the emperor Constantius. Before an imperial commission, Hilary says, he questioned Auxentius until he signed an orthodox confession. Valentinian still kept Auxentius, and Hilary was ordered out of Milan. E. W. Watson, who wrote the life in the English edition of Hilary, accepts Hilary's account. He notes that Auxentius's own report of the hearing does not contradict it.

Hilary was bishop of Poitiers in Aquitania, in Gaul. In the opening of On the Trinity, he tells how he searched for God. "I chanced upon the books which, according to the tradition of the Hebrew faith, were written by Moses and the prophets." There he found God's own name, "I Am that I Am." Watson warns that the account is too neatly shaped to read as literal history.

In 356 a council at Béziers, led by Saturninus of Arles, condemned Hilary. Watson says the charges concerned his conduct, not his faith. He was banished to Phrygia, in Asia Minor. There, Jerome of Strido records, he wrote twelve books against the Arians, the work now called On the Trinity. He also wrote On the Councils to the bishops of Gaul. Watson says it tried to show that the newer Eastern creeds could be read in an orthodox sense.

In 359, in the fourth year of his exile, Hilary was sent with the other bishops to the council at Seleucia. He followed its envoys to the emperor at Constantinople. Sulpicius Severus says he asked, in three petitions, to debate the faith there before his opponents. The Arians blocked him. He was sent back to Gaul as "a troubler of the East," with his sentence of exile still in force.

At home he held councils that condemned the creed signed at Ariminum, in Italy. Sulpicius, a Gallic writer firmly on Hilary's side, says the regions of Gaul "were set free from the guilt of heresy through the kind efforts of Hilarius alone." Hilary died, Sulpicius says, in the sixth year after his return. Watson places this in 367.

Augustine of Hippo named Hilary with Cyprian of Carthage and Lactantius. All of them, he wrote, came out of Egypt loaded with gold and silver. [warmly] He meant the learning of the pagan world, which they brought into the church.
