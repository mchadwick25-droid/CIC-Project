About six months after his parents died, a young Egyptian named Antony walked into church turning over a thought. He had been pondering how the first disciples left everything to follow Christ. Just then he heard the Gospel being read: "If thou wouldest be perfect, go and sell that thou hast and give to the poor." Antony took it as spoken to him. He gave away the family land, three hundred acres, and sold the rest. Keeping a little for his sister, he set out to live as an ascetic near his village.

This is how Antony's life is told in the Life of Antony. Its writer says he was Antony's attendant and "poured water on his hands." The church has long read the Life as the work of Athanasius of Alexandria. Archibald Robertson, who edited it, says the authorship is still disputed. He holds that "the external evidence for the Vita is too strong to allow us to set it aside as spurious." The book is a work of praise, and Robertson grants there is "idealisation, panegyric" in it.

Antony lived, by its account, from about 251 to 356, almost all of it in the Egyptian desert. He learned from an old hermit near his village, then withdrew step by step. He went to the tombs, then to a deserted fort, and at last to a mountain deep in the inner desert. There he fought the demons the Life describes at length. Others began to follow him, so that, in its words, "the desert was colonised by monks."

Under the persecution of Maximinus he came to Alexandria and served the confessors in the prisons, longing to be martyred himself. Years later he came again to denounce the Arians, "saying that their heresy was the last of all." The emperor Constantine and his sons wrote to him as to a father. Antony was unmoved. "Do not be astonished if an emperor writes to us," he told the monks, "for he is a man."

Sozomen says Antony came from Coma, in Upper Egypt. He left seven letters in Coptic, which Jerome of Strido lists among his illustrious men. He died at about a hundred and five. He asked two companions to bury him secretly, so that no one would make a shrine of his body. The Life closes on what it says his fame proved, that God "maketh His own known everywhere."
