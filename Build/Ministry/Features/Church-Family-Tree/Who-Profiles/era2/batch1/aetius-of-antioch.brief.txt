About the year 350, at Antioch, Bishop Leontius made a man named Aetius a deacon and let him preach. Flavian and Diodorus, who led the orthodox party in the city, raised such an uproar that Leontius soon had to stop him. Archibald Robertson, who edited Athanasius in English, tells the story. Aetius spoke so boldly about God, Sozomen says, that many people called him Atheus, the godless one.

Philostorgius, a later historian who admired him, says Aetius came from Coele-Syria and was poor. His history survives only in a hostile summary by Photius. In it Aetius works as a goldsmith to keep himself and his mother, then turns to logic. Socrates of Constantinople, who did not admire him, says he leaned on Aristotle's book of Categories. He built arguments, Socrates says, that deceived himself.

Aetius taught that the Son is not like the Father. If God alone is unbegotten in his being, he reasoned, then the Son, who is begotten, cannot share that being. He set this out in a short book of claims, the Syntagmation. It survives because Epiphanius of Salamis copied it out to refute it. Those who followed Aetius were called Anomoeans, from the Greek word for "unlike."

Many Arians found this too much. They liked to say the Son was like the Father, and to stop using the word "substance" at all. A council met at Constantinople in 360. There his friend Eudoxius, bishop of Antioch, gave him up. In Robertson's words, Aetius was "a sacrifice to the Emperor's scruples." The council took away his office of deacon.

When the emperor Julian came to power, he called Aetius back from exile and sent him warm letters. Philostorgius says Julian gave him land on the island of Lesbos. Aetius later lived at Constantinople, where he died. His pupil Eunomius of Cyzicus carried the teaching on, and in time the sect took his name.
