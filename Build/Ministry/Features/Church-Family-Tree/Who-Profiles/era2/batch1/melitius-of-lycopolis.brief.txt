Epiphanius of Salamis tells of a prison in Egypt during the Great Persecution, early in the fourth century. Peter, bishop of Alexandria, was held there with Melitius of Lycopolis and other clergy. They split over Christians who had given way. Peter wanted mercy for them, and Melitius a stricter line. Peter hung a cloak across the prison like a curtain. A deacon called out that Peter's friends should come to his side, and the others to Melitius. Most of the clergy and monks, Epiphanius says, went to Melitius.

Epiphanius, a foe of every heresy, wrote some seventy years later. Archibald Robertson, who edited Athanasius, calls this the Melitians' own account. H. Idris Bell, who published papyri of their church in 1924, thinks Epiphanius had good sources.

Melitius was bishop of Lycopolis, in Upper Egypt. Two letters of the time speak against him. Four bishops in prison, among them Phileas of Thmuis, scolded him for making priests in their churches. Peter wrote that Melitius had "ordained in the prison several unto himself." He told his flock to shun him.

Athanasius of Alexandria later held Peter's see. He was, in Bell's words, "an uncompromising opponent of the Meletians." He wrote that Peter put Melitius out for many crimes, among them "offering sacrifice to idols." Bell doubts this. Such charges came easily after a great persecution, he says. He and Robertson agree the split began over church order. It did not begin over doctrine.

In 325 the Council of Nicaea let Melitius stay a bishop in name. But he could not ordain. Its letter, kept by Socrates of Constantinople, left him "a nominal dignity." Sozomen, the church historian, says that near the end he named his friend John to follow him. This broke the council's terms.

His followers called themselves the Church of the Martyrs, Epiphanius says. Later they sided with the Arians against Athanasius. Bell traces them in Egypt into the eighth century.
