When Rome was taken in 410, soldiers broke into the house of an old widow named Marcella. Asked for gold, she is said to have pointed to her coarse dress to show she had none. They did not believe her, and "scourged her and beat her with cudgels." Jerome of Strido tells this in a letter to her friend Principia. He was in Palestine, and he writes, he says, "what holy men have seen."

Marcella came from a noble Roman family. She was widowed before seven months of marriage. An old nobleman named Cerealis then offered her his fortune. She told him that "a young man may indeed die early, but an old man cannot live long."

Clergy of Alexandria fled to Rome, Athanasius among them. From them Marcella heard of Antony, the desert monk, and of the monasteries Pachomius founded in Egypt. No highborn lady at Rome, Jerome says, had yet dared "publicly to call herself a nun." Marcella took up that life, and the virgin Eustochium was trained in her cell.

Jerome lived in Rome in the 380s, and Marcella questioned him about Scripture at every visit. Seventeen of his letters to her are held, several explaining Hebrew words. After he left, "recourse was had to her to settle" disputes about Scripture. She gave her answers as his or another's, because the apostle Paul had written, "I suffer not a woman to teach."

Later a Latin version of Origen's First Principles reached Rome. The notes in W. H. Fremantle's English edition name its translator as Rufinus of Aquileia. He and Jerome later wrote Apologies against each other. Marcella opposed its teachers in public. "She it was who originated the condemnation of the heretics," Jerome writes. He admits some may think him "giving vent to my own rancour."

She died a few days later, in Principia's arms. Two years later Jerome wrote of her as "Marcella the glory of her native Rome."
