Antony the hermit once came down from the desert to Alexandria to help its bishop, Athanasius, against the Arians. A blind scholar named Didymus came to see him. Jerome of Strido tells the story and places it in his own childhood. They talked about Scripture, and then Antony asked whether Didymus missed his eyes. At first Didymus was ashamed to answer. Asked a third time, he said it was a great grief to him. Antony told him a wise man should not grieve for "a faculty which he shares with ants and flies and gnats." Socrates of Constantinople, Sozomen and Rufinus of Aquileia tell the same saying, each with other animals.

Didymus of Alexandria, called the Blind, taught in that city. Palladius of Helenopolis visited him four times over ten years. Didymus told him he had lost his sight at four. He had never learned to read, Palladius says. Rufinus, who heard him speak, says he listened while others read. When the readers fell asleep, he went over it all again, like a clean animal chewing the cud.

Rufinus says he became teacher of the church's school at Alexandria, with Athanasius's approval. Socrates says he was seen as "the great bulwark of the true faith" against the Arians. Jerome put his book on the Holy Spirit into Latin.

Didymus admired Origen. Socrates says he wrote on Origen's book First Principles. Those who attack Origen, Didymus wrote, cannot grasp "the profound wisdom of that extraordinary man." In the sixth century, the historian Evagrius Scholasticus reports, the emperor Justinian condemned Didymus along with Origen.

Jerome had studied with Didymus at Alexandria. In the later quarrel over Origen, he was blamed for calling him master. He answered that a pupil may call an old and learned man master. He had much to thank him for, Jerome wrote, "for what I did not know I learned from him, and what I knew already I did not forget."
