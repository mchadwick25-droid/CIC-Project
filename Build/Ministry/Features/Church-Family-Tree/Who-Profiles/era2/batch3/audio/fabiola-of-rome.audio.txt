[calmly] In the Lateran basilica at Rome, a widow of a noble family stood among the penitents. Before the bishop, the priests and the people, she showed her "dishevelled hair, pale features, soiled hands and unwashed neck." Jerome of Strido says that all of them wept when they saw her weep.

She was Fabiola of Rome. Her story comes from Jerome's letters, above all the letter of praise he wrote after her death to Oceanus. W. H. Fremantle's English edition of Jerome dates her death to 399. Jerome says she had left her first husband for faults so terrible that he will not name them. She then married again while that husband still lived. Jerome defends the separation but calls the second marriage a fault, perhaps forced on her by need. After her second husband died, she did public penance.

Then she sold her large property for the poor. "She was the first person to found a hospital," Jerome writes, "into which she might gather sufferers out of the streets." He says she carried the sick on her own shoulders, washed their wounds and fed them by hand. She gave to monasteries on the islands and along the coast. With Jerome's friend Pammachius, she bought a house at Portus as a home for strangers.

Against her friends' advice, she sailed to Jerusalem and was for a time Jerome's guest. She studied Scripture with him and pressed him with questions. When news came that the Huns were near, she went home to Rome.

Jerome says that crowds filled the streets for her funeral, singing psalms. She had once asked him what the camps of Israel in the desert meant, and he had promised her a book. He wrote it after her death as a funeral offering. [warmly] In its pages, he wrote, "she may rejoice that she has passed through the wilderness of this world and has come at last to the land of promise."
