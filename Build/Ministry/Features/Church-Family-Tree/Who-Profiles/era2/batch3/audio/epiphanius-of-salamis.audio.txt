[calmly] In the church of an estate beside his monastery, a bishop from Cyprus gave his deacons an order. They were to seize a young monk named Paulinian "and to stop his mouth." So he could not beg in Christ's name to be let go. Paulinian had fled ordination several times. The bishop ordained him deacon, then presbyter, while he cried out that he was unworthy. The bishop was Epiphanius of Salamis, who tells it himself, defending the act in a letter to John, bishop of Jerusalem. Paulinian was the brother of Jerome of Strido, who later put the letter into Latin.

The church historian Sozomen says Epiphanius was born at Besauduc, a village near Eleutheropolis in Palestine. He trained under famous monks, mostly in Egypt. The people of Cyprus then chose him as bishop of their chief city, Salamis. Sozomen calls him "the most revered man under the whole heaven."

Epiphanius wrote against every heresy he knew of, as their enemy. His great book is the Panarion, which Jerome calls "Against all heresies." Jerome says his books were read by the learned for their matter, "and also by the plain people, on account of their language."

In the same letter he turned his charges on Origen. He says Origen taught that souls fell from heaven into bodies. He also taught, Epiphanius says, "that the devil will once more be what he at one time was."

Late in life he sailed to Constantinople. Socrates of Constantinople and Sozomen say that Theophilus, bishop of Alexandria, drew him in to move against John Chrysostom. Socrates says that "on account of his extraordinary piety" Epiphanius was "a man of simple mind and manners," and easily used. He would not pray with John unless John condemned Origen's books and sent away the Egyptian monks known as the Long Brothers. Sozomen says one of them, Ammonius, asked whether he had read any of their works. He had not, and said he judged them by reports. He was "measurably convinced," and soon sailed for home.

Both historians report a last exchange as hearsay. Epiphanius is said to have told John, "I hope that you will not die a bishop." John answered that Epiphanius would not reach home. Socrates says he cannot vouch for the story. Epiphanius died at sea, and John was soon driven from his see. [warmly] Of his tomb, Sozomen writes, "it was said that demons were expelled, and diseases healed."
