[calmly] In a hard winter at the gate of Amiens, a young soldier met a naked beggar. He had nothing left to give but his cloak. Sulpicius Severus says he cut it in two with his sword and gave the beggar half. That night, by Sulpicius's account, Christ appeared to him wearing it. "Martin, who is still but a catechumen, clothed me with this robe."

The soldier was Martin of Tours. He was born at Sabaria in Pannonia to pagan parents and grew up at Ticinum in Italy. The main record is by Sulpicius, his disciple, who visited him and questioned him for the Life. It is a saint's life, full of wonders. Alexander Roberts, its English translator, says it is "extremely difficult to decide as to the real value of his narrative." He grants that many stories are fables, and leaves the rest to the reader.

Sulpicius says Martin asked to leave the army before a battle, as the future emperor Julian paid the troops. "I am the soldier of Christ: it is not lawful for me to fight." He joined Hilary of Poitiers, who made him an exorcist. In Illyria he spoke against the Arians and was publicly beaten. Auxentius of Milan drove him from that city. He then founded a monastery near Poitiers.

The people of Tours made him their bishop. Some bishops objected that he looked contemptible, "that his clothing was mean, and his hair disgusting." He still lived as a monk, two miles outside the city, with eighty disciples who held everything in common.

His hardest test came at Trier. The bishops Ydacius and Ithacius had brought Priscillian and his followers before the emperor Maximus on charges of heresy. In his Chronicle, Sulpicius says Martin begged them to drop the charge, and begged Maximus to spare the accused. To him it was "a foul and unheard-of indignity, that a secular ruler should be judge in an ecclesiastical cause." While he stayed at Trier, the trial was put off. After he left, Priscillian was put to death.

Sulpicius was not with Martin at the end. Gregory of Tours, writing two centuries later, says he died at Candes in the eighty-first year of his life. His monks begged him not to leave them. Sulpicius writes that Martin, it is said, wept and prayed. [warmly] "O Lord, if I am still necessary to thy people, I do not shrink from toil: thy will be done."
