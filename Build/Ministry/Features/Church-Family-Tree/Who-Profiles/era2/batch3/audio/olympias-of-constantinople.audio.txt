[calmly] On the day John Chrysostom was sent into exile, the cathedral of Constantinople burned. Socrates of Constantinople says some of John's followers set the fire. The church historian Sozomen says each side blamed the other. The deaconess Olympias was brought before the prefect and asked why she had done it. "My past life ought to avert all suspicion from me," she answered, "for I have devoted my large property to the restoration of the temples of God."

Nothing could be proved, Sozomen says, so the prefect pressed her and other women to take communion with the new bishop, Arsacius. The others gave way. Olympias told him, "I will not hold communion with those from whom I ought to secede." He fined her heavily, and she left the city for Cyzicus.

Palladius of Helenopolis says she was the daughter and granddaughter of high imperial officials. She married Nebridius, a former prefect of the city, and was soon widowed. The Dialogue on John's life, which its translator Herbert Moore gives to Palladius, says the emperor Theodosius wanted her to marry his kinsman Elpidius. She refused, and he put her wealth in trust until she was thirty. She answered that he would do better to have it "dispersed among the poor, and the Churches." Later he gave it back.

Sozomen says the bishop Nectarius made her a deaconess, though she had been widowed young. She gave to anyone who asked, and John told her to give "with economy." Palladius says she "instructed many women." The Dialogue names bishops she gave land and money, Epiphanius of Salamis among them.

Seventeen of John's surviving letters are to her. In the first he writes, "there is only one thing, Olympias, which is really terrible, only one real trial, and that is sin." W. R. W. Stephens, John's translator, says nothing definite is known of her later life. [warmly] The people of Constantinople, Palladius writes, "reckon her life among the confessors."
