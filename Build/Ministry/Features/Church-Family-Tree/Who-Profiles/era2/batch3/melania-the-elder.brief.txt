A Roman noblewoman lost her husband, and before he was buried, two of her sons died. Jerome of Strido tells it to Paula of Rome. Not a tear fell, he says. She threw herself at the feet of Christ. "Henceforth, Lord," she said, "I will serve Thee more readily, for Thou hast freed me from a great burden." She gave all she had to her only son and sailed for Jerusalem.

She was Melania, called the Elder because her granddaughter shared her name. Palladius of Helenopolis says she was widowed at twenty-two and sailed first to Alexandria. In Egypt she spent half a year with the monks of Nitria. When their leaders were exiled to Palestine, she followed and fed them, dressed as a slave. The governor threw her in prison. She told him whose daughter and wife she was, "but I am Christ's slave." He apologised and released her.

She then founded a monastery in Jerusalem, Palladius writes, and led fifty women there for twenty-seven years. Rufinus of Aquileia lived there with her. They welcomed pilgrims and healed a split among some four hundred monks. Jerome's Chronicle says her humility earned her the name of Thecla, an early woman saint.

Rufinus, her companion and by then Jerome's opponent, says Jerome cut her from every copy of the Chronicle, for disapproving of some of his deeds. In 415 Jerome wrote of "her whose name bears witness to the blackness of her perfidy." The notes to W. H. Fremantle's edition read this as a pun on her name, meaning black.

At sixty, Palladius says, she sailed back to Rome to guard her family from false teaching, and urged them to give up their wealth. To senators who opposed her she said, "Little children, it was written 400 years ago, It is the last hour." She returned to Jerusalem and died within forty days, leaving "both a monastery in Jerusalem and an endowment for it."
