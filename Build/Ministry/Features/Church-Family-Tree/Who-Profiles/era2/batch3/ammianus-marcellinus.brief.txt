In Rome in the 360s, two men fought to become bishop. Damasus and Ursinus, writes Ammianus Marcellinus, formed parties, and their followers came to blows. "It is certain that on one day one hundred and thirty-seven dead bodies were found in the Basilica of Sicininus, which is a Christian church."

Ammianus calls himself "a Greek by birth, and formerly a soldier." Charles Duke Yonge, his English translator, gathers the rest from the history itself. He served under the general Ursicinus and fought in the emperor Julian's war on Persia. Later, at Rome, he wrote a history that ends with the death of the emperor Valens in 378. Even his religion is uncertain, Yonge says. The general belief is that he kept the old Roman faith.

In the same passage on Rome, Ammianus mocks its bishops, "enriched by offerings from matrons, riding in carriages, dressing splendidly." Yet he praises priests in the provinces for their plain dress and strict fasting. He says Julian let the Christian sects argue freely. Julian had found, he says, that "no wild beasts are so hostile to men as Christian sects in general are to one another." Still, he calls Julian's ban on Christian teachers of rhetoric "a cruel action, and one deserving to be buried in everlasting silence."

Ammianus says he never "knowingly perverted" the truth. Edward Gibbon, whom Yonge quotes, called him "an accurate and faithful guide." Professor Ramsay, also quoted by Yonge, says he shared his age's belief in omens. Ammianus reports that when Julian tried to rebuild the Temple at Jerusalem, "fearful balls of fire burst forth" and stopped the work. E. A. Abbott, quoted by Blomfield Jackson, judges him an impartial historian. Yet Abbott accepts only that people "generally believed" a fiery sign had stopped it.

A mob at Alexandria killed its bishop, George. Ammianus says the Christians might have saved him, "if both parties had not been equally exasperated by hatred of George."
