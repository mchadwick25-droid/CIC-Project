A man named Hermas opens his book with a memory. "He who had brought me up, sold me to one Rhode in Rome," he writes. Many years later he met her again and began to love her as a sister. One day he saw her bathing in the river Tiber, and he gave her his hand and drew her out. Soon after, walking on the road to the villages, he fell asleep. In a vision the heavens opened, and Rhode greeted him from the sky. She had been taken up, she said, to accuse him of his sins before the Lord.

So begins the Shepherd of Hermas, a book of visions, commands and parables written at Rome. What it tells of Hermas comes from inside the visions, and no other source confirms it.

The Muratorian Canon, an early list of Christian books, says Hermas wrote "very recently in our times." His brother Pius was then bishop of Rome. That puts the book in the middle of the second century. Arthur McGiffert, who translated Eusebius of Caesarea, says most scholars accepted this, though the scholar Zahn dated it to the years 97 to 100.

In the visions an old woman gives Hermas a little book. A young man tells him she is the Church, and that she is old because "she was created first of all." In the commands, a Christian who sins after "that great and holy calling," the angel of repentance tells Hermas, has "opportunity to repent but once."

Tertullian of Carthage, in his book On Modesty, would not allow adulterers even that one chance. He called the Shepherd "the only one which favours adulterers." Irenaeus of Lyon had quoted it as Scripture, but Eusebius placed it among the spurious books. By the time Jerome of Strido wrote his Lives, McGiffert notes, it was almost unknown among the Latins.
