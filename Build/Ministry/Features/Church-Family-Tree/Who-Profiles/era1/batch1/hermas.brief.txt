Around the year 150, by the earliest report we have, a Christian named Hermas lived at Rome, in the house churches of the age after the apostles. That report, a list of the church's books known as the Muratorian Canon, says he wrote his book of visions, the Shepherd, while his brother Pius was bishop of Rome. Eusebius of Caesarea and Jerome took him instead for the Hermas whom Paul greets in his letter to the Romans. Everything else about him comes from inside his own visions.

The man who raised him sold him to a woman named Rhoda. Many years later he met her again and came to love her as a sister, and one day he thought how happy he would be with such a wife. Then, in a vision, she greeted him from the sky and said she had been taken up to accuse him of his sins before the Lord.

Afraid, he asked himself how he could now be saved. Hermas had heard teachers say that the only repentance is the one made in baptism. The book takes its name from an angel dressed as a shepherd, sent to live with him. This angel agrees that the baptized ought not to sin again. Yet he teaches that the Lord knows human weakness, and in mercy has set one repentance more for those baptized before this time. In the same book, a husband must take back an unfaithful wife who repents, though not many times: "For the servants of God there is only one repentance."

Irenaeus of Lyon quoted the Shepherd as Scripture. Tertullian of Carthage, attacking a bishop who forgave adulterers, called it the one writing that favored them, and said the councils of the churches had judged it false. For the Muratorian list, the book should be read, but not to the people in church among the prophets or the apostles, because it was written so recently. Eusebius of Caesarea counted it among the rejected books, though he knew it was read in churches, since some thought beginners in the faith needed it. What became of Hermas himself, no source says.
