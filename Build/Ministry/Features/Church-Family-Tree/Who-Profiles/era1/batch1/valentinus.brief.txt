Around 140, a teacher named Valentinus came to Rome. Irenaeus of Lyon, who wrote against him, says he arrived when Hyginus was bishop and stayed through the time of two more bishops. His followers were called Valentinians, and theirs was one of the Gnostic Christianities, which claimed a knowledge higher than faith. Tertullian of Carthage, another opponent, tells how the break came. Valentinus expected to be made bishop, because he was an able and eloquent man, and when a man who had suffered for the faith was chosen instead, he broke with the church. Tertullian alone tells this, and he tells it to show a man driven by ambition and revenge.

Valentinus's own words survive only in a few passages that Clement of Alexandria quotes in order to answer them. In one letter he compares the heart to a roadside inn, fouled by careless guests and home to many demons. "But when the only good Father visits it," Valentinus wrote, "it is sanctified, and gleams with light."

Irenaeus and Clement wrote independently, and on his teaching they agree on two points. Valentinus placed the Maker of this world below a higher, unseen God, and his school held that its own members were saved by their nature, while ordinary believers needed faith. Irenaeus alone sets out the fuller system, a heaven of divine powers that came forth in pairs. One of them fell, he says, and from that fall came the world.

The school carried on under teachers the opponents name, Ptolemy and Heracleon among them. Irenaeus, who had read their books and met some of them, feared they would carry believers off, because they spoke as the church spoke while meaning something else. So he wrote his five books Against Heresies. Earlier writers had failed to refute the Valentinians, he says, because they did not know their system, and whoever overthrew them overthrew every heresy.
