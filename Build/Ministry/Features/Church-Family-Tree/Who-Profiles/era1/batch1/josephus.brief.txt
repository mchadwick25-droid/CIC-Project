Around the year 93, under the emperor Domitian, a Jewish priest named Josephus finished a history of his people at Rome. He was not a Christian. He was born in Jerusalem to a family of priests, and when the Jews rose against Rome he governed Galilee for them until the Romans took him. After Jerusalem fell, the emperor Vespasian gave him a house in Rome, citizenship and a pension. There he wrote the Antiquities of the Jews, and twice in it he speaks of Jesus.

In the shorter report the high priest Ananus brings before the judges "the brother of Jesus, who was called Christ, whose name was James," and has him stoned with some others. Origen of Alexandria, a Christian teacher of the third century, read this report in Josephus and used it against Celsus, a critic of the Christians. Here, Origen argued, was a writer who did not believe in Jesus as the Christ and still testified that James was a just man.

The longer report cannot be trusted as it stands. In our copies Josephus calls Jesus "a wise man, if it is right to call him a man," says "He was the Christ," and says that after Pilate sent him to the cross he appeared to his followers alive on the third day. Yet Origen, who knew the Antiquities, wrote that Josephus "did not accept Jesus as Christ."

Eusebius of Caesarea, early in the fourth century, quotes the passage as we have it, and he argued from it that a Hebrew historian's record convicted those who had forged acts against Jesus. Jerome, later in that century, gave Josephus a chapter in his book of the church's writers and copied the passage there, though in his version Jesus "was believed to be Christ."
