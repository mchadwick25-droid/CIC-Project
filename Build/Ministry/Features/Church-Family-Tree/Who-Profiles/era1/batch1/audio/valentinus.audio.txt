[calmly] Around 140, a teacher named Valentinus came to Rome. Other rival teachers came in those years too, Irenaeus of Lyon says: Cerdo, and Marcion after him. Irenaeus, who wrote against them all, says Valentinus arrived when Hyginus was bishop and stayed through the time of two more bishops. His followers were called Valentinians, and theirs was one of the Gnostic Christianities, which claimed a knowledge higher than faith. Tertullian of Carthage, another opponent, says Valentinus expected to be made bishop, because he was an able and eloquent man. When a man who had suffered for the faith was chosen instead, he broke with the church. Tertullian alone tells this, and he tells it to show a man driven by ambition and revenge.

Valentinus's own words survive only in a few passages his opponents quote. Most are in Clement of Alexandria, who quotes them in order to answer them. In one letter Valentinus compares the heart to a roadside inn, fouled by careless guests and home to many demons. "But when the only good Father visits it," he wrote, "it is sanctified, and gleams with light."

Clement had Valentinus's own letters before him, and on two points he and Irenaeus agree. Valentinus placed the Maker of this world below a higher, unseen God, and his school held that its own members were saved by their nature, while ordinary believers needed faith. Irenaeus sets out a fuller system, which Clement does not confirm: a heaven of divine powers that came forth in pairs. One of them fell, he says, and from that fall came the world.

The school carried on under teachers the opponents name, Ptolemy and Heracleon among them. Irenaeus, who had read their books and met some of them, feared they would carry believers off, because they spoke as the church spoke while meaning something else. So he wrote his five books Against Heresies. [warmly] Earlier writers had failed to refute the Valentinians, he says, because they did not know their system, and whoever overthrew them overthrew every heresy.
