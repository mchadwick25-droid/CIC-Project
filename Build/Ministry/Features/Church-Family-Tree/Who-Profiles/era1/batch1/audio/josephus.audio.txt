[calmly] After Jerusalem fell to Rome, Titus, the son of the emperor Vespasian, told a Jewish priest named Josephus to take whatever he wanted from the ruins of his country. In his own Life he says he asked for the freedom of his family. Titus also let him have the holy books.

Josephus came from a family of priests in Jerusalem and was born in the first year of the emperor Caius, the year 37. When the Jews rose against Rome, the leaders in Jerusalem gave him command of Galilee. The Romans trapped him in the town of Jotapata and brought him before their general, Vespasian. Josephus says he told Vespasian that he and his son would rule the world. The Roman writer Suetonius tells the same story. Vespasian did become emperor, and Josephus lived on at Rome in Vespasian's old house, as a Roman citizen with a pension.

There he wrote the Jewish War and the Antiquities of the Jews. He was not a Christian, but two passages in the Antiquities name Jesus. The first tells how the high priest Ananus had James and some others stoned. In William Whiston's translation, it calls James "the brother of Jesus, who was called Christ." Arthur McGiffert, who translated Eusebius of Caesarea in 1890, saw no good reason to doubt this line. A Christian adding to the text, he reasoned, would hardly call Jesus the "so-called Christ."

The second passage is longer, and the doubt about it is serious. In the form Eusebius quotes, it calls Jesus a wise man, "if indeed it be proper to call him a man." It says, "He was the Christ," and that he appeared alive again on the third day. Origen of Alexandria, a Christian scholar, wrote more than a century later that Josephus "did not accept Jesus as Christ." McGiffert notes that Eusebius is the first writer known to quote it, and he sets out four views. A few held it wholly genuine, which he thought could not stand. Hase, Keim and Schürer thought a Christian added all of it, and McGiffert agreed. Renan and Schaff thought a genuine core had been added to, and Ewald that a hostile line was rewritten as praise.

William Whiston, who translated Josephus in the eighteenth century, defended the whole passage as genuine. [warmly] Even so, he wrote that in his own time it had "of late been greatly questioned by many."
