[calmly] Early in the second century, Ignatius was bishop of Antioch in Syria, among the house churches of the generation after the apostles. He had been condemned for his faith, and soldiers were taking him across Asia Minor to Rome, to be thrown to wild beasts. From Smyrna he sent a letter ahead to the Christians of Rome, begging them not to save him. In it he likened the band of soldiers who guarded him to ten leopards. "I am God's wheat, and let me be ground by the teeth of the wild beasts, so that I may be found the pure bread of Christ."

On the road he wrote six more letters. Some people said that Christ only seemed to suffer, and Ignatius answered them with his own chains in his letter to the church at Smyrna: "If our Lord did these things only in appearance, then I too am bound only in appearance." Three centuries later Theodoret of Cyrrhus called on that same letter as testimony that Christ was truly crucified in the flesh.

Ignatius also stood for the bishop. He told the same church that no one should do anything that concerns the church without the bishop, so that whatever the bishop approved would be secure and valid. The sources we have do not show what came of this rule in other churches.

His letters were soon passed from church to church. Polycarp, the bishop of Smyrna, sent the church at Philippi every letter of Ignatius he had. Later in that century Irenaeus of Lyon quoted the wheat saying as the words of a fellow Christian condemned to the beasts. [warmly] Eusebius of Caesarea, two centuries later, gives his death at Rome only as a report, but he names all seven letters, and in his day many still celebrated his fame.
