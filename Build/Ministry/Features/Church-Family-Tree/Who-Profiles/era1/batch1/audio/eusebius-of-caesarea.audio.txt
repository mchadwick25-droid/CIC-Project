[calmly] In 303 the emperor Diocletian ordered the churches leveled and the Scriptures burned. Eusebius lived at Caesarea in Palestine, in the church of Roman Palestine before Constantine. Its presbyter Pamphilus, his dearest friend and a man devoted to the Scriptures, was tortured, jailed and at last put to death, and Eusebius wrote down what happened to him and to the other martyrs of Palestine.

He became bishop of Caesarea and wrote the story of the church from the apostles to his own day. "Since I am the first to take up this subject, I am trying to travel what is, so to speak, a lonely and untrodden road." He built the book from older writers and copied their own words into his pages.

Later historians built on it. A century afterward Socrates of Constantinople and Theodoret of Cyrrhus each began a church history where his ended. Yet Socrates says that in his Life of Constantine Eusebius cared more for polished writing and the emperor's praise than for an accurate account of the facts, and passed lightly over Arius.

In 325, at the council of Nicaea, Eusebius read out his own creed before Constantine. In a letter home he says the emperor approved it and urged one added word, that the Son is "of the same essence" as the Father. Eusebius held back for a short time, then signed, and told his people he had not let the word pass without asking what it meant.

That letter followed him. Some branded him a friend of Arius's views, and Socrates wrote in his defense. Athanasius of Alexandria, defending Nicaea, attached the letter to show that even Eusebius had signed its creed. Socrates himself built his account of Nicaea on Eusebius's words, treating them as an authority. [warmly] He copied the letter in full.
