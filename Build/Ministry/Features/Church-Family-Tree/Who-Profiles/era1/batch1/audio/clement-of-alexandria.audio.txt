[calmly] Around the year 200, some people spoke against Greek philosophy, as if it dragged believers away from the faith. One who answered them was Clement, a presbyter of Alexandria in Egypt. He led the Christian school of Alexandria, where converts were taught the faith. He had studied under Christian teachers from Greece to Palestine. The last and best of them he tracked down, hidden in Egypt, and with him he found rest. Eusebius of Caesarea judged that this teacher was Pantaenus, who led the school before Clement.

In his Stromata, or Miscellanies, Clement answered those critics that God is the cause of every good thing. He thought it likely that philosophy was given to the Greeks as the law was given to the Hebrews, to lead them to Christ. "Philosophy, then, was a preparation, paving the way for the one who is made perfect in Christ."

Origen was among his pupils as a boy and later led the same school. Jerome says Origen followed Clement's example and wrote Miscellanies of his own, confirming Christian teaching from Plato and Aristotle. When Jerome himself was asked why he quoted pagan writers, he pointed, among others, to Clement, whose books were drawn from the heart of philosophy.

The followers of Valentinus, as Clement reports them, left faith to simple believers and kept knowledge for themselves, since they were saved by their nature. Clement answered that knowledge is built on faith, and that if anyone were saved by nature, the commandments would be useless. The sources we have show no reply from the Valentinians.

Another pupil, Alexander, became bishop of Jerusalem. From prison he sent a letter to Antioch by Clement's hand. [warmly] In it he praised Clement for strengthening and building up the church during his stay.
