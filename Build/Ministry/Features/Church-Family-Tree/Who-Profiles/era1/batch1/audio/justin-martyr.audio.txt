[calmly] Around 150, a Christian in the Roman empire could be put to death for the name alone. In Rome a teacher named Justin, from Flavia Neapolis in Palestine, wrote to the emperor Antoninus Pius on behalf of people of every nation who were hated without cause, and he counted himself among them. He was one of the second-century Greek apologists, the Christians who wrote in Greek to defend their faith.

He had come to that faith as a philosopher. In his own telling, an old man he met in a field near the sea sent him to the Hebrew prophets. He still called himself a philosopher, and he taught that Christ is the Word of God, in whom every people has had a share. So, "those who lived with reason are Christians, even if they were thought to be atheists," and he named Socrates of Athens among them.

Justin asked the emperor to judge Christians by what they had done, since a name alone proves nothing good or bad. Eusebius of Caesarea follows the petition with an edict that he gives as the emperor's, sent to Asia after Christians there told him how they suffered. No one was to be punished for being a Christian alone. Justin expected to be plotted against, perhaps by Crescens, a Cynic philosopher. Tatian says Crescens tried to have Justin killed. Eusebius says the plot succeeded, though the surviving account of his trial never names Crescens. There, when the prefect Rusticus asks whether he is a Christian, Justin answers, "Yes, I am a Christian." He and six others refused to sacrifice to the gods and were beheaded.

Justin had also written against Marcion, who preached a god greater than the Creator. [warmly] A generation later Irenaeus of Lyon quoted that book with approval, where Justin would not have believed the Lord himself, had he announced any God but the Creator.
