Around the year 200, Clement of Alexandria was a presbyter in that city in Egypt. He led the Christian school of Alexandria, where converts were taught the faith. He had studied under Christian teachers from Greece to Palestine, and in his Stromata, or Miscellanies, he tells how he reached the last and best of them: "When I had tracked him down, hidden in Egypt, I found rest." Eusebius of Caesarea judged that this teacher was Pantaenus, who led the school before Clement.

Some people spoke against Greek philosophy, as if it dragged believers away from the faith. Clement answered that God is the cause of every good thing. He thought it likely that philosophy was given to the Greeks as the law was given to the Hebrews, to lead them to Christ. "Philosophy, then, was a preparation," he wrote, "paving the way for the one who is made perfect in Christ."

Origen was among his pupils as a boy and later led the same school. Jerome says Origen followed Clement's example and wrote Miscellanies of his own, confirming Christian teaching from Plato and Aristotle. When Jerome was asked why he quoted pagan writers himself, he pointed to Clement, whose books he said were drawn from the heart of philosophy.

The followers of Valentinus, as Clement reports them, left faith to simple believers and kept knowledge for themselves, since they were saved by their nature. Clement answered that knowledge is built on faith, and that if anyone were saved by nature, the commandments would be useless. The sources we have show no reply from the Valentinians.

Another pupil, Alexander, became bishop of Jerusalem. From prison he sent a letter to Antioch by Clement's hand, and wrote in it that Clement, while with him, had strengthened and built up the church.
