Around 150, rival teachers were at work in the church of Rome. Irenaeus of Lyon names Valentinus and Cerdo, and after them Marcion of Pontus, on the Black Sea coast of Asia Minor. The churches Marcion founded took his name and became Marcionism. When Polycarp, the old bishop of Smyrna, came to Rome, Irenaeus says, he turned many away from these teachers. Once Marcion met him and, asking to be owned as a brother, said, "Do you recognize us?" Polycarp answered, "I recognize, I recognize the first-born of Satan."

We have none of Marcion's own books, so nearly all we know of him comes from men who thought as Polycarp did, and on his teaching they agree. Justin Martyr, writing while Marcion was still alive, says he taught his followers to believe in another god, greater than the Creator. Irenaeus and Tertullian of Carthage say the same, that for Marcion the God of the law and the prophets was not the Father of Jesus. Tertullian says his chief work was to separate the law from the gospel, and both say that to fit this teaching he cut down the Gospel of Luke and the letters of Paul.

Tertullian, but neither earlier witness, tells that Marcion first belonged to the church at Rome, which put him out. His own churches spread all the same. Justin says that in Marcion's lifetime he had already won many in every nation, and Tertullian, though he mocks them, grants that the Marcionites had churches of their own.

The cut-down Scriptures drew answers. Irenaeus argued that the church has four Gospels, no more and no fewer, and named Marcion as one who made them fewer. Tertullian wrote five books against him, and in the fourth he went through Marcion's own Gospel to refute him from it.

Two centuries after Marcion, Epiphanius of Salamis wrote that his followers were still found from Rome and Egypt to Syria and Persia. A century later Theodoret of Cyrrhus, a bishop in Syria, wrote that he had led eight villages of them over to his own faith.
