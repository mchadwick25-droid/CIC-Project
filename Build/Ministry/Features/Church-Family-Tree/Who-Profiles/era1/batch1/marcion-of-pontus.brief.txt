Around 150, Marcion of Pontus was teaching in Rome. He came from Pontus, on the Black Sea coast of Asia Minor, and the churches he founded took his name and became Marcionism. Irenaeus of Lyon tells that when Polycarp, the old bishop of Smyrna, met him, Marcion asked, "Do you recognize us?" Polycarp answered, "I recognize, I recognize the first-born of Satan."

We have none of Marcion's own books, so nearly all we know of him comes from men who thought as Polycarp did, and on his teaching they agree. Justin Martyr, writing while Marcion was still alive, says he taught his followers "to believe in some other god greater than the Creator." Irenaeus and Tertullian of Carthage say the same, that for Marcion the God of the law and the prophets was not the Father of Jesus. Tertullian says his chief work was to separate the law from the gospel, and both say that to fit this teaching he cut down the Gospel of Luke and the letters of Paul.

Tertullian, but neither earlier witness, tells that Marcion first belonged to the church at Rome, which put him out, but his own churches spread all the same. Justin says that in Marcion's lifetime he had already won "many of every nation," and Tertullian, though he mocks them, grants that the Marcionites had churches of their own.

The cut-down Scriptures drew an answer. Irenaeus argued that the church has four Gospels, no more and no fewer, and named Marcion as one who made them fewer. Tertullian wrote five books against him, and in the fourth he went through Marcion's own Gospel to refute him from it.

Two centuries after Marcion, Epiphanius of Salamis wrote that his followers were still found from Rome and Egypt to Syria and Persia. In the century after that, Theodoret of Cyrrhus, a bishop in Syria, wrote that he had led eight villages of Marcionites over to his own faith.
