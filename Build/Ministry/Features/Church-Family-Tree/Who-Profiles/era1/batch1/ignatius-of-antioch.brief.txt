Early in the second century, a bishop from Syria was taken under guard across Asia to Rome, to die in the arena. His name was Ignatius, and he was bishop of Antioch. At Smyrna, where Polycarp was bishop, he wrote to the churches nearby. One letter went ahead of him to Rome. "From Syria even unto Rome I fight with beasts, both by land and sea, both by night and day," he wrote, "being bound to ten leopards, I mean a band of soldiers." He begged the Christians there not to save him. "I am the wheat of God," he wrote, "and let me be ground by the teeth of the wild beasts, that I may be found the pure bread of Christ."

Arthur McGiffert, who translated Eusebius of Caesarea, says that almost all we know of Ignatius comes from those letters. Eusebius, two centuries later, adds that he was the second bishop of Antioch after Peter. He names seven letters, four from Smyrna and three from Troas, one of them to Polycarp of Smyrna himself.

On the road Ignatius warned the churches against two dangers. Some taught that Christ only seemed to suffer, and he answered them with his own chains. "But if these things were done by our Lord only in appearance," he wrote, "then am I also only in appearance bound." Others met apart from their bishop. "Let no man do anything connected with the Church without the bishop," he told the Christians of Smyrna. In the same letter he wrote that "wherever Jesus Christ is, there is the Catholic Church."

Fifteen letters carry his name, and eight are now set aside as later forgeries. The seven that Eusebius knew survive in a longer and a shorter Greek form, and scholars long argued over which was his. In 1845 William Cureton published a Syriac version of just three letters and argued that these alone were genuine. McGiffert wrote in 1890 that the work of the scholars Zahn and Lightfoot had finally settled the matter for the shorter Greek letters.

Eusebius's Chronicle puts his death in the tenth year of the emperor Trajan, about the year 107. Zahn and Lightfoot would say only that he died under Trajan, and Harnack placed it later, under Hadrian. Before that century was out, Irenaeus of Lyon was quoting the wheat line as the words of a martyr of the church.
