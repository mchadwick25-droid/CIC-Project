A quarter of Alexandria called the Pyrucheium was under siege by a Roman army, and its bread had run out. Eusebius of Caesarea tells the story as others told it to him. A learned Christian named Anatolius was inside. His friend Eusebius, a deacon, was in the part of the city that sided with Rome, and he won the Roman commander's promise to spare anyone who came over. Anatolius asked the city's senate to make peace with Rome, and they were angry. So he asked only that the old women, the children and the old men be sent out. They agreed. Church members went first, then others of every age, many "secretly clothed in women's garments." By night they escaped to the Roman camp.

Anatolius of Alexandria was born in that city. In arithmetic, geometry, astronomy, logic and physics, Eusebius says, he "stood first among the ablest men of our time."

After the war Anatolius left Egypt. At Caesarea in Palestine the bishop, Theotecnus, ordained him a bishop, meaning him to succeed. Then a synod at Antioch, held over its bishop, Paul of Samosata, summoned him. On the way he passed through Laodicea in Syria, where his friend the deacon had been bishop and had died. The Christians there kept him.

"Anatolius did not write very many works," Eusebius says. He quotes his rules for Easter, which count the years in cycles of nineteen. Arthur McGiffert, who translated Eusebius, calls him the first Christian to use that cycle for Easter, "so far as we know."

Bede, a priest of Wearmouth and Jarrow in England, tells of a synod at Whitby in 664. Bishop Colman, with his Scottish clergy, argued over Easter with the priest Wilfrid, and both named Anatolius. Colman called him "a holy man, and much commended in church history." Wilfrid agreed he was "a most holy, learned, and commendable man." But Colman's side, he said, did not keep his cycle of nineteen years.
