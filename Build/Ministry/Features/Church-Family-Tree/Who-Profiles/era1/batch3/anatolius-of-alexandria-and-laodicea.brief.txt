Around the year 270, a synod met at Antioch in Syria to judge the city's bishop, Paul of Samosata, for his teaching about Christ. Among the bishops it called was Anatolius, a scholar from Alexandria in Egypt, the home of Alexandrian Christianity. Eusebius of Caesarea says he stood first among the ablest men of his time in arithmetic, geometry and astronomy.

Earlier, by a story Eusebius tells, Roman troops had besieged a quarter of Alexandria. When the bread ran out, Anatolius persuaded the city's council to send out the old and the children. Under that cover he got nearly all the besieged away by night. After that war he left Alexandria. On the road to the synod he passed through Laodicea in Syria, where the bishop had died, and the Christians there kept him as their own.

Eusebius says he wrote little, and copies out a passage of his on finding the date of Easter. In it Anatolius counts the years in cycles of nineteen and starts each cycle with the new moon at the spring equinox. Anyone who sets the first month before that point, he says, makes "no small or ordinary mistake."

The sources we have do not show what came of his reckoning in his own lifetime, but his name carried far. In 664, at a synod at Whitby in England, two parties argued over the date of Easter, and both called on him. Bishop Colman asked whether so holy a man had gone against the law and the Gospel. The priest Wilfrid answered that Anatolius was holy and learned. But Colman's side, he said, did not keep his cycle of nineteen years, which the whole church kept. Bede, who tells it, says the king ruled for Wilfrid because Christ had given Peter the keys of heaven.
