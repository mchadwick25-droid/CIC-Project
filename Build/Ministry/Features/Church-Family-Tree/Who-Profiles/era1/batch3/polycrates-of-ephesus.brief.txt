Victor, the bishop of Rome, asked Polycrates, bishop of Ephesus, to call together the bishops of the province of Asia. The question was the date of Easter. The churches of Asia ended their fast on the fourteenth day of the moon, the day of the Jewish Passover, whatever day of the week it fell. Most others waited for Sunday, the day of the resurrection. Polycrates wrote to Victor for them all. "We observe the exact day," he began, "neither adding, nor taking away."

Eusebius of Caesarea kept part of the letter. It is the only writing of Polycrates known, says Arthur McGiffert, who translated Eusebius. It names the "great lights" buried in Asia. Philip, "one of the twelve apostles," lay at Hierapolis. John, "who reclined upon the bosom of the Lord," lay at Ephesus, and the martyr Polycarp at Smyrna. "All these observed the fourteenth day of the passover according to the Gospel," he wrote. McGiffert thinks Polycrates confused the apostle Philip with Philip the evangelist of the book of Acts.

Then he spoke of himself. "For seven of my relatives were bishops; and I am the eighth." He had "lived sixty-five years in the Lord," and he was "not affrighted by terrifying words."

Eusebius says Victor then tried to cut off all the churches of Asia, and declared the brethren there "wholly excommunicate." Charles Joseph Hefele, historian of the councils, follows Valesius in reading this as a threat only. McGiffert holds that Victor did break off communion, as Socrates of Constantinople says. Irenaeus of Lyon, who kept Easter on Sunday, urged Victor not to cut off whole churches for an ancient custom. The break healed, McGiffert says, "just how and when we do not know," and the Roman practice won.

McGiffert says the year of his death is not known. Jerome of Strido quoted the letter "to show through a small example the genius and authority of the man."
