[calmly] Four Egyptian bishops were in prison at Alexandria during the Great Persecution. Word reached them that a fellow bishop, Meletius of Lycopolis, was making clergy "in parishes having no manner of connection with thee." They wrote to him. He had ignored, they said, "the honour of our great bishop and father, Peter." An old account kept with the letter says Meletius never answered. When the four had died as martyrs, he went to Alexandria. Peter was in hiding. Meletius set aside the priests Peter had left to visit the needy, and named men of his own. Peter wrote to his people to "hold no communion with him, until I meet him in company with some wise and discreet men."

Peter of Alexandria followed Theonas as bishop of the city, about the year 300. He led the church for less than three years before the persecution, says Eusebius of Caesarea. Eusebius calls him "a divine example of a bishop," for his life and his study of the Scriptures.

In 306, at "the fourth passover of the persecution," Peter wrote rules for Christians who had given way. Those who gave in only after long torture were to fast forty more days, and then be taken back. He would not let anyone blame those who had fled "for the safety of their life."

The quarrel with Meletius is told two ways. Athanasius of Alexandria, a later bishop, was an enemy of the Meletians. He says Peter's council removed Meletius for many crimes, "and among the rest of offering sacrifice to idols." Epiphanius of Salamis says both men were in prison together. They split over the fallen. Peter asked for mercy, and Meletius for strictness. Harold Idris Bell, who edited papyri of the Meletians in 1924, thinks the sacrifice charge "need not be taken very seriously." Charles Joseph Hefele, historian of the councils, says the first letters show neither man was then in prison.

In the ninth year of the persecution, Eusebius says, Peter was seized "for no cause and quite unexpectedly" and beheaded, many bishops of Egypt with him. Bell dates it to 25 November 311. A later Passion tells of his last night, but Arthur McGiffert, who translated Eusebius, calls it "spurious and historically quite worthless."

The split outlived him. In 325 the Council of Nicaea let Meletius keep only "a nominal dignity." [warmly] Athanasius dated the Meletians "from the times of the blessed Peter, Bishop and Martyr."
