[calmly] In the 190s Polycrates was bishop of Ephesus, in the Roman province of Asia, in western Asia Minor. His church belonged to the house churches of the age after the apostles. Like the rest of Asia, it ended the fast before Easter on the fourteenth day of the moon, the day of the Jewish Passover, whatever the day of the week. Churches elsewhere waited for Sunday, and synods of bishops from Gaul to Palestine ruled for it.

At the request of Victor, the bishop of Rome, Polycrates called the bishops of Asia together, and they chose to keep their custom. He wrote to Victor for them all, and Eusebius of Caesarea copied part of the letter.

"We keep the day without tampering with it," he wrote, "adding nothing and taking nothing away." The great dead of Asia had kept it, he said, among them John, who had leaned on the Lord's breast and was buried at Ephesus. Seven of his relatives had been bishops before him, and all had kept the day; he was the eighth. After sixty-five years in the Lord he was not frightened by threats, for greater men than he had said that we must obey God rather than men.

Victor at once tried to cut all the churches of Asia off from communion, Eusebius says, and declared them excommunicate. Other bishops rebuked him, among them Irenaeus of Lyon, who kept Easter on Sunday himself and warned him not to cut off whole churches for holding to an ancient custom. Eusebius does not say how Victor answered, and the sources we have say nothing more of Polycrates.

In 325, after the council of Nicaea ruled that all should keep Easter on one day, Constantine the Great wrote that the churches of Asia already kept it as Rome did. How or when Asia came round, no source says. Yet some held on. Two centuries after Polycrates, Socrates of Constantinople says, John Chrysostom took churches away from those in Asia who still kept the fourteenth day. [warmly] They claimed, he says, to have it from the apostle John.
