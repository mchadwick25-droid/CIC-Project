Around the year 112, when Trajan was emperor, Pliny the Younger governed Bithynia, a province in the north of Asia Minor. He was not a Christian, and he had never been present at a trial of Christians. So when people were accused before him of being Christians, he judged them his own way and then wrote to Trajan for advice.

He asked the accused whether they were Christians. Those who said yes he asked twice more under threat of punishment, and those who held firm he sent to be punished. Those who denied it he released, once they had prayed to the gods and cursed Christ.

Others said they had left the faith years before, and what Pliny reports of Christian worship comes from them. Their custom, they told him, had been "to come together on a fixed day before daylight and to sing a song to Christ as to a god, answering one another." They also swore an oath to keep from theft, robbery and adultery. To test this, Pliny had two slave women who were called deaconesses questioned under torture. He found "nothing except a perverse and excessive superstition."

Pliny writes as the judge who had already sent Christians to punishment, and he calls their faith insanity, yet his own inquiry turned up no crime. Trajan answered that Christians "are not to be searched for; if they are accused and convicted, they are to be punished."

Later Tertullian of Carthage, defending the Christians, argued from the governor's report that Pliny had found no crime in their worship. Of Trajan's ruling he said, "It forbids them to be sought out, as innocent, and it orders them to be punished, as guilty." Eusebius of Caesarea, repeating Tertullian, says the ruling partly checked the persecution, though many still died as martyrs.
