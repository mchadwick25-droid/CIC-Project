In 257 the emperor Valerian's governor banished Cyprian, bishop of Carthage in Roman North Africa, to the town of Curubis. One of his household, a man of the Latin church of North Africa, went with him by choice and later wrote his Life. On their first day there, he says, Cyprian told him a dream in which a young man led him before the governor, who wrote out a sentence without questioning him. The young man showed its meaning with a flat hand swung like a blade. A year later to the day, by his account, Cyprian was beheaded.

He wrote because the church had recorded the deaths of laypeople and learners who died as martyrs, so a bishop who had much to teach apart from his death, he says, must not be passed over. He writes as an admirer, praising Cyprian throughout, and begins only at the conversion.

He tells how the new convert sold his property and gave nearly all the price to the poor. When plague struck Carthage and people abandoned their own sick, Cyprian told his church that caring only for their own was nothing great, since God sends sun and rain on strangers. They did good to all, he says, and not only to fellow believers.

The Life ends on its writer's grief: "I rejoice greatly, very greatly, at his glory, yet I grieve more that I remained behind." It never gives his name. That rests on Jerome, about 130 years after Cyprian's death, who writes that Pontius, Cyprian's deacon, shared his exile to the end and left a notable book on his life and death. Beyond Jerome, our sources do not show who read the Life or what became of Pontius.
