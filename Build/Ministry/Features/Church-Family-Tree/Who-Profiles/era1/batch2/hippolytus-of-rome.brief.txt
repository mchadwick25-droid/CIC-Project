Around 220, the Christians of Rome were split over how the Father and the Son can be one God. Hippolytus was a teacher and writer there, in the Roman church of the third century. His fight with the city's bishop, Callistus, reaches us only in the Refutation of All Heresies, a book now read as his, and it is an enemy's account.

Callistus, it says, taught that the Father and the Son are one and the same, under two names. Hippolytus opposed him, and Callistus called him and his side worshippers of two gods. In his own book against that teaching, Hippolytus wrote that the Word is another beside the Father, yet "I do not mean that there are two Gods." When Callistus became bishop, he cut off Sabellius, who taught that Christ is the Father, and Hippolytus claims he did it out of fear of him.

In a second quarrel, over grave sins, Hippolytus had put offenders out of the church. Callistus took them in and told them their sins were forgiven, arguing that the church, like Noah's ark, holds clean and unclean alike. By Hippolytus's own account, crowds went over to Callistus, whose following still stood when he wrote and called itself a Catholic church.

The sources we have do not show how the quarrel ended. A Roman list of bishops made in 354 says that in 235 the bishop Pontianus and the presbyter Hippolytus were exiled together to Sardinia. A calendar of martyrs in the same book names the two together, in one entry, among the martyrs buried at Rome.

His books outlived the quarrel, and Jerome says his table for the date of Easter moved Eusebius of Caesarea to draw up one of his own. Two centuries after the exile, Theodoret of Cyrrhus quoted Hippolytus as a bishop and martyr, among the teachers whose words witness to the church's faith in Christ.
