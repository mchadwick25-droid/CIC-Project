In the year 235, two leaders of the church at Rome were sent into exile on the island of Sardinia. A list of Rome's bishops, made in 354, names them. One is Pontianus, "episcopus," the bishop. The other is Hippolytus, "presbiter," a presbyter.

Hippolytus of Rome wrote in Greek. Jerome of Strido lists many of his books. There are tables for finding the date of Easter, commentaries on Daniel and the Song of Songs, and a book Against all heresies. Jerome adds that in one sermon Hippolytus speaks in the church with Origen of Alexandria present. Eusebius of Caesarea calls him a bishop "over another church." Jerome could not learn which city.

A book called the Refutation of All Heresies was found at Mount Athos in 1842. It was printed in 1851 under the name of Origen. J. H. MacMahon, who translated it, says most scholars have since decided it is by Hippolytus. In it Hippolytus attacks Callistus, who became bishop of Rome after Zephyrinus, and tells his life as an enemy. Callistus, he says, was a slave who made away with money left in his bank, and was later sent to the mines of Sardinia. Hippolytus held that Callistus confused the Father with the Son. Callistus called Hippolytus and his party worshippers of two gods. Hippolytus charged him with "saying that all had their sins forgiven by himself." Callistus answered, as Hippolytus reports, that Noah's ark held clean and unclean creatures, and so should the church.

Arthur McGiffert, who translated Eusebius, calls this an account "from the pen of a very bitter opponent." He thinks it "greatly overdrawn," though true at its base. He notes that Hippolytus never recognizes Callistus as bishop, while he calls himself one. In the Refutation he names himself among the apostles' successors. A later tradition made Hippolytus bishop of Portus, the harbor of Rome. MacMahon accepts it. McGiffert calls it "quite worthless." He thinks a strict party set Hippolytus up as a rival bishop, and that he came back to the main church after Callistus died.

In 1551 a marble statue of a seated man was found on the Tiburtine Road at Rome. Its chair is carved with an Easter table and a list of books, and McGiffert says it has been shown to be his. The same book of 354 records the burial of Hippolytus on that road, and of Pontianus in the cemetery of Callistus.
