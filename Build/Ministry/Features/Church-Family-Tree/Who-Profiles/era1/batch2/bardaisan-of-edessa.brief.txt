Around 180, Edessa was a Syriac-speaking city of Mesopotamia with its own king, Abgar. Bardaisan taught there, and from him came the school called the Bardaisanites.

His pupil Philip and some friends were visiting a brother when Bardaisan came in and asked what they had been talking about. A man named Avida had asked why God did not make people unable to do wrong. The answer fills the Book of the Laws of Countries, which Philip wrote, so its words are a pupil's report of his teacher.

Bardaisan admits there that he once followed the astrologers. He grants the stars some power over health and fortune, yet he denies that they rule what a person chooses. His proof is that every nation keeps its own laws, whatever stars its people are born under. Christians keep one law in every land, he adds, so that those in Parthia do not take two wives as Parthians do. Eusebius of Caesarea praised a dialogue of his on fate, and says his many pupils put his Syriac works into Greek.

Two later writers tell his life in opposite orders. Eusebius says he began as a follower of Valentinus, left him, and never quite washed off the old error. Epiphanius of Salamis says he began as an excellent Christian, a friend of Abgar, who refused an emperor's companion when urged to deny his faith. Only later did he fall in with the Valentinians and deny the resurrection of the dead.

In the fourth century Ephrem the Syrian was still writing against him. Bardaisan declared, Ephrem reports, that the body would return to dust even if Adam had never sinned. Ephrem answered that Adam's sin undid the body's life, and that Christ died and rose in a body. Sozomen says Bardaisan's son Harmonius set ideas about the soul and the body to songs the Syrians loved, so Ephrem wrote hymns to the same melodies, and from then on the Syrians sang Ephrem.
