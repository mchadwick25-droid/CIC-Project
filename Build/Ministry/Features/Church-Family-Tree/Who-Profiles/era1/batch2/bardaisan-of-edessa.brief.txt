Philip and Baryama were calling on Shemashgram when Bardaisan came in. "What were you talking about?" he asked, "for I heard your voice outside as I was coming in." Avida, who was with them, had asked why God did not make people unable to do wrong. So begins the Book of the Laws of Countries, a dialogue in Syriac.

Bardaisan taught at Edessa, in Mesopotamia, late in the second century. In the dialogue he argues that people are free, and that the stars do not rule what they do. His proof is that each nation keeps its own laws, whatever stars its people are born under. He turns to "the new race of us Christians, whom Christ at His advent planted in every country and in every region." Christians in Parthia, he says, do not take two wives, though Parthians do.

Eusebius of Caesarea calls Bardaisan "a most skillful disputant in the Syriac tongue" and "a powerful defender of the faith." Eusebius and Epiphanius of Salamis tell his story in opposite orders. Eusebius says he began as a follower of Valentinus, the Gnostic teacher, and left him, keeping some of his errors. Epiphanius, who wrote against every heresy, says he began as an excellent Christian, close to Abgar, the ruler of Edessa. He adds that Bardaisan refused when a companion of the emperor urged him to deny that he was a Christian. Only later, Epiphanius says, did he fall into the error of Valentinus. McGiffert, who translated Eusebius, thinks Eusebius's order may well be right. With Hort, he finds no Gnostic system in his teaching.

Ephrem the Syrian attacked Bardaisan as one of the worst of heretics. McGiffert thinks Ephrem knew him only through his followers.

Sozomen, the church historian, writes that Bardaisan's son Harmonius is said to have set Syriac verse to meter and music. Ephrem learned those meters, and wrote hymns of his own for the church to the same rules.
