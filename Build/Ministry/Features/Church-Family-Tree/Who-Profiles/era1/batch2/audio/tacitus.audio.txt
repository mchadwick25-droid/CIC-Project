[calmly] In the year 64 a great fire burned Rome, and people believed that the emperor Nero had ordered it. What Nero did next is told by Cornelius Tacitus, a Roman senator and historian who wrote about fifty years later. He was not a Christian, and his chapter on the Christians treats them as criminals.

To end the rumor, Tacitus says, Nero put the blame on a group "hated for their shameful acts, whom the common people called Christians." He adds, "Christus, from whom the name came, was put to death in the reign of Tiberius by the procurator Pontius Pilate." He calls the faith a harmful superstition that was checked for a moment and then broke out again, in Judaea where it began and in Rome itself.

Those who confessed, he says, were arrested first. On their word a vast number were convicted, less for setting the fire than for hatred of the human race. Some were covered in animal skins and torn apart by dogs, some were nailed to crosses, and some were burned as lamps after dark. Tacitus thought they deserved the harshest punishment. Yet he records that people came to pity them, because they seemed to die for one man's cruelty and not for the public good. He does not say where he learned of Christus, and he names none of the shameful acts.

Around 200, Tertullian of Carthage, defending the Christians, called Tacitus a great talker of lies. His Histories had said the Jews honored the head of an ass, and that charge was later turned on Christians. Around 400, Sulpicius Severus of Aquitania in Gaul told of the fire with the same details in the same order: the animal skins and the dogs, the crosses, the bodies burned for light when the day ended. He does not name Tacitus, and he calls the victims innocent. [warmly] This, Sulpicius wrote, was how the cruelty against Christians began.
