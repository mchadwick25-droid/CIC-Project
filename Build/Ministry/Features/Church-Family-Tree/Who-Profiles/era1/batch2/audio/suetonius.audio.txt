[calmly] In his life of the emperor Claudius, the Roman writer Suetonius gives one short line about trouble at Rome. In John Rolfe's translation it reads, "Since the Jews constantly made disturbances at the instigation of Chrestus," Claudius "expelled them from Rome." Suetonius says nothing more about who Chrestus was.

Gaius Suetonius Tranquillus wrote the Lives of the Caesars, from Julius Caesar to Domitian. Rolfe notes that he tells us little about himself. His father was a Roman knight who fought for Otho at Betriacum, the battle that gave the empire to Vitellius. The letters of his friend Pliny the Younger show him practising law for a time. Later he served the emperor Hadrian as his secretary, until Hadrian dismissed him, about the year 121.

His second notice comes in the life of Nero. It sits in a list of public measures, between a limit on spending and a ban on the revels of the charioteers. There he writes that Nero "inflicted punishments on the Christians, a sort of people who held a new and impious superstition." That is the translation of Alexander Thomson, revised by T. Forester. Suetonius blames Nero for the great fire elsewhere in the same life, but he does not link the Christians to it.

Rolfe's note on the line calls Chrestus "Another form of Christus." He thinks Suetonius probably got the date wrong, though he allows that a Jew of that name may be meant. The notes in Thomson and Forester's edition also read it as Christ. They say Suetonius moved Christ from Jerusalem to Rome, and into the reign of Claudius. Their translation says "one Chrestus." Rolfe notes that the Latin has no word for "one" here, and takes that as a sign that Christ is meant.

Rolfe says that Tertullian of Carthage drew on his work about the public shows. [warmly] Paulinus, secretary to Ambrose of Milan, followed Suetonius's way of writing a life.
