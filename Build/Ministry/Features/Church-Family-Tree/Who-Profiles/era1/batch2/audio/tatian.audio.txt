[calmly] In the middle of the second century, Tatian was in Rome as a hearer of the Christian teacher Justin Martyr, who was later beheaded there for refusing to sacrifice. Tatian was born, he says, in the land of the Assyrians, and was first schooled in Greek learning. After Justin's death, Epiphanius of Salamis says, he went east and set up his school in Mesopotamia. Syriac Christianity came to read the gospel book he made.

He had been admitted to the pagan mysteries and had watched the rites of Rome, he writes, and then he went off alone to look for the truth. He came upon "some non-Greek writings, older than the teachings of the Greeks, and more divine than their error."

His Address to the Greeks argues from that discovery that Moses is older than Homer, and so the Christians' philosophy is older than anything the Greeks taught. Clement of Alexandria wrote that Tatian had treated the age of Moses accurately. Eusebius of Caesarea judged the Address the best and most useful of his works.

His later teaching comes to us through opponents. Irenaeus of Lyon says that after Justin's martyrdom Tatian left the church, proud of being a teacher. He taught unseen powers like those of Valentinus, Irenaeus says, called marriage corruption, and denied that Adam was saved. Clement, a second opponent, quotes a lost book in which Tatian calls the union of husband and wife a fellowship in corruption. Later writers tied him to the Encratites, a movement that preached against marriage. Irenaeus says the movement sprang from earlier teachers, but Eusebius reports that Tatian was said to have founded it.

Tatian also wove the four Gospels into one account, the Diatessaron. Eusebius says some still had it in his day. [warmly] The Teaching of Addai, a Syriac legend of how the church of Edessa began, shows the people gathering each day to hear the Old Testament and the Diatessaron read.
