[calmly] In the middle of the second century, Tatian was in Rome as a hearer of the Christian teacher Justin Martyr. Justin was later beheaded there for refusing to sacrifice. Syriac Christianity came to read the gospel book Tatian made.

By his own account, he was born in the land of the Assyrians and first schooled in Greek learning. He had been admitted to the pagan mysteries and had watched the rites of Rome. Then he went off alone to look for the truth, and came upon "some non-Greek writings, older than the teachings of the Greeks, and more divine than their error."

His Address to the Greeks argues from that discovery that Moses is older than Homer. So the Christians' philosophy is older than anything the Greeks taught. Clement of Alexandria found its dating of Moses accurate, and Eusebius of Caesarea judged it the best and most useful of his works.

His later life and teaching come to us through opponents. Irenaeus of Lyon says that after Justin's martyrdom Tatian left the church, proud of being a teacher. He taught unseen powers like those of Valentinus, called marriage corruption, and denied that Adam was saved. Clement, a second opponent, quotes a lost book in which Tatian calls the union of husband and wife a fellowship in corruption. Epiphanius of Salamis, a later opponent, has him go east after Justin's death and set up his school in Mesopotamia.

Later writers tied Tatian to the Encratites, a movement that preached against marriage. Irenaeus traced it to earlier teachers, but Eusebius passes on a report that Tatian founded it.

Tatian also wove the four Gospels into one account, the Diatessaron, and some still had it in Eusebius's day. [warmly] The Teaching of Addai, a Syriac legend of how the church of Edessa began, shows the people gathering each day to hear the Old Testament and the Diatessaron read.
