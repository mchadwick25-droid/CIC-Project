[calmly] On their first day of exile at Curubis, Cyprian of Carthage told his deacon a dream. A young man of unusual height had led him before the proconsul, who wrote a sentence on a tablet without asking him anything. The young man could not say what it was. Instead, "with hand expanded and flattened like a blade, he imitated the stroke of the accustomed punishment." Cyprian had chosen this deacon to share his exile, and the deacon went willingly. He wished that Cyprian "could also have chosen me to share his passion!"

The deacon was Pontius, and his Life and Passion of Cyprian is a main source for how the bishop of Carthage lived and died. The Life does not name its author, so Jerome of Strido is the witness. Pontius, he says, shared Cyprian's exile until the day of his death, and left a notable volume on his life and death. Wilhelm Hartel's Latin edition calls the Life commonly attributed to Pontius.

Pontius explains why he wrote. The church had recorded the sufferings even of lay people and of those still learning the faith, when they died as martyrs. A bishop who was also a martyr should not be passed over. He writes as an admirer and praises Cyprian at every turn, and he leaves out Cyprian's life before his conversion. He tells how Cyprian sold whole estates to help the poor. When plague struck the city, he says, Cyprian urged his people to care for the heathen sick as well as their own.

At the end Pontius tells the death. People climbed into the trees to see. Cyprian bound his own eyes, and the executioner's hand shook as he gripped the sword.

Little else is recorded of Pontius himself. He closes with his grief at being left behind. [warmly] "Much and excessively I exult at his glory; but still more do I grieve that I remained behind."
