Tatian had seen the religions of the Roman world from the inside. He says he was admitted to their mysteries and watched their rites. Then, "retiring by myself, I sought how I might be able to discover the truth." He came upon "certain barbaric writings, too old to be compared with the opinions of the Greeks, and too divine to be compared with their errors." He goes on to argue that Moses is older than Homer.

Tatian tells us he was "born in the land of the Assyrians" and first trained in Greek learning. Irenaeus of Lyon says he was a hearer of Justin Martyr, and Tatian calls his teacher "the most admirable Justin." He says he saw at Rome the statues the Greeks had brought there. He writes that the Cynic Crescens tried to have both Justin and himself put to death. His Address to the Greeks mocks their philosophers and their gods.

After Justin's death, Irenaeus says, Tatian "separated from the Church." Irenaeus writes as his opponent. He says Tatian taught a system of invisible powers, "like the followers of Valentinus," called marriage "corruption and fornication," and denied that Adam was saved. Hippolytus of Rome repeats the three points, following Irenaeus closely. Eusebius of Caesarea dates the break to about 172. Hope Hogg, who translated the Diatessaron from Arabic, says Tatian left Rome for Mesopotamia after Justin's death. Eusebius and Jerome of Strido made Tatian the founder of the Encratites, who preached against marriage. Arthur McGiffert, who translated Eusebius, notes that Irenaeus's Encratites came from Saturninus and Marcion of Pontus. So Tatian found them already in being.

Tatian also wove the four Gospels into one, called the Diatessaron. McGiffert thinks Eusebius had not seen it, since Eusebius says Tatian made it "I know not how." It would seem, Hogg writes, that at an early stage it was "very widely if not universally read in the Syriac churches." In the fifth century Theodoret of Cyrus found more than two hundred copies in his diocese, "held in respect in the churches." He replaced them with the four Gospels, McGiffert reports, because Tatian's book left out the Lord's birth. Hogg reports that a growing majority of scholars held it was first written in Syriac. McGiffert thought Harnack's case for Greek very strong.

Tatian signed his Address himself. "These things, O Greeks, I Tatian, a disciple of the barbarian philosophy, have composed for you."
