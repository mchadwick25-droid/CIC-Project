Around 165, Tatian was in Rome as a hearer of the Christian teacher Justin Martyr. He was born, he says, in the land of the Assyrians, and was first schooled in Greek learning. He belongs to Syriac Christianity, the church that grew up around Edessa and Nisibis, because of a gospel book he made.

He had been admitted to the mysteries and had watched the rites of Rome, he writes, and then he went off alone to look for the truth. He came upon "some non-Greek writings, too old to be compared with the teachings of the Greeks, and too divine to be compared with their error."

His Address to the Greeks argues from that discovery that Moses is older than Homer, and so the Christians' philosophy is older than anything the Greeks taught. Clement of Alexandria wrote that Tatian had treated the age of Moses accurately. Eusebius of Caesarea judged the Address the best and most useful of his works.

His later teaching comes to us through opponents. Irenaeus of Lyon says that after Justin's martyrdom Tatian left the church, proud of being a teacher. He taught unseen powers like those of Valentinus, Irenaeus says, called marriage corruption, and denied that Adam was saved. Clement, a second opponent, quotes a lost book in which Tatian calls the union of husband and wife a fellowship in corruption.

Tatian also wove the four Gospels into one account, the Diatessaron. Eusebius says some still had it in his day. The Teaching of Addai, a Syriac legend of how the church of Edessa began, shows the people gathering each day to hear the Old Testament and the Diatessaron read. In the fifth century Rabbula, bishop of Edessa, ordered every church to keep and read a copy of the separate Gospels. Theodoret of Cyrrhus wrote that even people who held the apostles' teaching had used Tatian's book in all simplicity as a short version, without seeing the harm in how it was made.
