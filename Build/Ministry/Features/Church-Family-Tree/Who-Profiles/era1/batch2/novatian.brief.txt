Cornelius, bishop of Rome, wrote that his rival Novatian made people swear an oath at the Lord's Supper. As Novatian gave out the bread, Cornelius says, he held each person's hands in both of his own and would not let go. "Swear to me by the body and blood of our Lord Jesus Christ that you will never forsake me and turn to Cornelius." Eusebius of Caesarea kept the letter. Cornelius wrote as an enemy, and much of what is known of Novatian comes from enemies. Robert Wallis, who translated his works, points this out.

Novatian was a presbyter at Rome in the third century. He wrote a treatise On the Trinity. It opens, "The Rule of truth requires that we should first of all things believe on God the Father and Lord Omnipotent." Arthur McGiffert, who translated Eusebius, calls the book "both able and orthodox."

Under the emperor Decius, many Christians had denied the faith to save their lives. When the persecution ended, they asked to come back. In 251 Cornelius was chosen bishop of Rome, and he would receive them after penance. A stricter party chose Novatian, and three Italian bishops consecrated him. Cornelius called those bishops "rustic and very simple men" who had been tricked. He also wrote that in the persecution Novatian would not come out of his room to help, saying he admired "another philosophy." McGiffert calls this charge calumny.

Novatian sent letters announcing his election. Dionysius of Alexandria wrote back, "If, as thou sayest, thou hast been led on unwillingly, thou wilt prove this if thou retirest willingly. For it were better to suffer everything, rather than divide the Church of God." A synod of sixty bishops at Rome cut off Novatian and his followers. Cyprian of Carthage, who took Cornelius's side, wrote that "he who is not in the Church of Christ is not a Christian."

Novatian's church spread east and west and lasted, McGiffert says, into the sixth century. Socrates of Constantinople tells a story he had from Auxanon, an aged Novatian presbyter who had gone to Nicaea with Acesius. At the Council of Nicaea, in 325, the emperor Constantine asked the Novatian bishop Acesius why he kept apart. Acesius said that those who sin gravely after baptism should be urged to repent, but must look to God alone for pardon. "Place a ladder, Acesius," Constantine replied, "and climb alone into heaven."
