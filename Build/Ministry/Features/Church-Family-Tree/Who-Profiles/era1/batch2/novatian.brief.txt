In 251 the church of Rome chose a bishop, Cornelius, after a persecution under the emperor Decius in which many Christians had sacrificed to the gods. Cornelius took them back once they had done penance. Novatian, a presbyter of Rome, refused, and bishops who agreed with him made him bishop against Cornelius. So began Novatianism, the strict churches that took his name.

His book On the Trinity answers those who called Christ the Father himself and those who called him only a man. Between them, he wrote, "the Lord is, as it were, crucified between two thieves." Jerome says many later took the book for the work of Cyprian of Carthage, who had sided with Cornelius.

For the split itself, most of what we have comes from his enemies. A letter of Cornelius, kept by Eusebius of Caesarea, charges that Novatian had long wanted the office, and that he got three country bishops drunk and forced them to lay hands on him. Socrates of Constantinople, who had a friend among the Novatians, gives the other side. Novatian, he says, wrote to all the churches that those who had sacrificed should be urged to repent but kept from communion, and that their pardon should be left to God.

A synod of sixty bishops at Rome ruled that Novatian and his followers were strangers to the church, and Cornelius wrote that people were leaving him every day. Yet Socrates says the churches in each province took sides, and that many welcomed the stricter rule. In 325 the council of Nicaea set terms for taking the clergy of his churches back. They could keep their rank if they promised in writing to share communion with those who had fallen in persecution and done penance. Two centuries after the split, Socrates says, the Novatians of Phrygia and Paphlagonia still kept their strict rule, and at Rome they had held many churches until Bishop Celestinus took them away.
