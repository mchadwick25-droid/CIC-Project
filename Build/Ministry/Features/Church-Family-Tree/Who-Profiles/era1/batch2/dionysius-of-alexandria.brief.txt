Around 250 the emperor Decius ordered a persecution, and soldiers came for Dionysius, the bishop of Alexandria in Egypt. He had been a pupil of Origen and had led the Christian school of Alexandria before he became bishop. When another bishop later spoke against him, Dionysius answered that he had not fled by his own choice. Soldiers had seized him, he wrote, until guests from a country wedding burst in and dragged him away.

Then Novatian set himself up as a rival bishop at Rome and denied any hope to Christians who had given way in the persecution. Dionysius, who had ordered that the dying among them be forgiven, wrote to him: "It would have been right to suffer anything at all rather than cut the Church of God apart." Later he could tell the bishop of Rome that the churches of the East, once divided over Novatian, were all at peace.

In Libya some bishops followed Sabellius and made the Father and the Son one and the same. Dionysius wrote against them that the Son differs from the Father as a boat differs from its builder. For this he was accused at Rome of calling the Son a creature, and he answered in four books that the charge was a slander. A century later the Arians, who did call the Son a creature, claimed him as their own. Athanasius of Alexandria defended him, because Dionysius had explained himself and died honored in the church. Basil of Caesarea judged that in straining against Sabellius he had bent too far the other way.

An Egyptian bishop, Nepos, found in the Revelation of John a thousand years of bodily pleasure on earth. Dionysius argued for three days with the village teachers who followed him, and by his account their leader gave the teaching up. He would not reject the book, but its Greek and its thought were so unlike John's Gospel that he judged another John had written it. Eusebius of Caesarea copied the argument out and himself left open which John saw the Revelation.
