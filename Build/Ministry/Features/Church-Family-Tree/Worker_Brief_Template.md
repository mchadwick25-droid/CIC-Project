## The paragraph for a writer

Before you draft, read `Who_Profile_Pattern.md` in full, all eight briefs in `Who-Profiles/bar/`, and the two Standard v1 examples there, `gregory-of-tours.brief.txt` and `benedict-of-nursia.brief.txt`. They are the standard; where the eight differ from Standard v1, the two examples govern. Do not open an earlier batch to see how a brief is written, and do not match its length, its shape or its phrasing; earlier batches are being rewritten to this same bar. Mark's definition of craft is the test: "good story telling and flow, no ai tells hanging sentences and everything is relevant to the story." Write each brief as a story an adult would hear on BBC News or read in National Geographic. Open on the setting: in the first two or three sentences give the year, the place and its region, the person's tradition as the census names it, and what was going on. Then a sourced moment. Tell the person first, then the impact: each thing they stood for, developed or defended (or for a recorder, what they recorded), followed by what came of it, with the cause sourced or said plainly to be absent, and every such stance the held sources show, ordered by weight. Name what they inherited and passed on through people the tree shows, and let the close say what they changed or defended, with its witness. Put the reason in the sentence with the act. Leave no sentence standing alone, and end where the story ends. A last line picked because it is quotable is a fault. Name a witness only where trust turns on it: the person's own words, a partisan or hostile witness, a point resting on one source, a wonder or legend ("Gregory tells that"), or a contested point. Tell documented facts plainly. Name no modern scholar in the brief: editors, editions, dating arguments and any claim resting only on a modern scholar go to the card's Sources and What is uncertain. List the brief's references below it: the card's Sources in its two groups, and what the brief draws on, as work, edition and part used, so that every unnamed claim can be traced. Invent nothing, check every quote against the vendored file, and tell a dispute as a dispute. Put quotation marks on our own English only under the two-kinds rule (`Who_Profile_Pattern.md`, "The brief", Quotes). Aim under two minutes and stretch to two and a half where needed; go shorter where the sources are thin, and past 2:30 only where the content demands it and your batch note says why. Standard v1 adds to the craft tests, the rigor rules and the readability method and replaces none of them ("All of it together" in the pattern). When you finish a draft, read it beside two of the eight and apply Mark's five craft tests from checklist item 10 in your batch note, quoting your own sentences. Then score it: grade 8.0 to 10.0 on the full text, reading ease 60 or above with names left out. If it scores below grade 8, join and explain; do not reach for longer words. In review, a fix may join sentences as well as split them.

## The paragraph for a reviewer

Read `Who_Profile_Pattern.md` in full, all eight briefs in `Who-Profiles/bar/` and the two Standard v1 examples there before you read the batch. Compare every brief to them, never to an earlier batch; where the eight differ from Standard v1, the two examples govern. Reviews follow `CLAUDE.md` (Mark, 2026-11-16: "CLAUDE.md governs (Recommended)"): Opus reviews every round, round 1 runs the full checklist, from round 2 only what changed is rechecked against the earlier findings, and the three-round cap applies. First do the blind pairing you are given: say which text of the unlabeled pair is stronger and why, and record it in the review file before you learn which is which. If you cannot tell them apart, or you prefer the new brief without a concrete reason, say so; that is a finding for the coordinator. Then run the checklist, including the Standard v1 items 15 to 17. Name in the review file the bar brief you compared against: Gregory of Tours, Benedict of Nursia, or which of the eight. For item 10, apply Mark's five craft tests to the whole brief: for each test, quote the brief's weakest sentence for that test beside the model passage from the eight, and say plainly whether the brief reaches that level. No test passes without quoted evidence. Look at the signals (joining words per 100, the share of source talk, a close on a quotation, an opening on "The X was Y", runs of short sentences). A signal tells you to read harder; it does not pass or fail a brief. A failed craft test is a blocking finding, and its fix is a rewrite of the passage. A fix may join sentences as well as split them. The batch does not go live until Mark has read its sample, which the coordinator picks.
