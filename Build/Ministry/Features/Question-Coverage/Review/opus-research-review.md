# Question Repository v0.1: research review (Opus)

Reviewer: Opus, independent. Date: 2026-10-25. Scope: the 16 clusters and 12 visitor kinds in the scratchpad draft, compared against published research on what people ask, doubt and struggle with. No Representative answers are written here. No statistics are invented: every figure below is from a source in the table, and I mark where I saw a figure only in secondary coverage.

## Summary

1. The draft is strong on the questions the research ranks highest: suffering, hypocrisy, abuse cover-ups, hell, whether Jesus was God, and whether Christianity is "too narrow".
2. The biggest gap is the plain metaphysical question: "is there a God", "who made God", "does life have a purpose". Search data puts these at the top. The draft has none of them.
3. The second gap is why people actually leave. Pew and the Great Dechurching find most leavers drift, move or stop finding it important. The draft's leaver (K5) is almost always angry or wounded.
4. Third gap: LGBTQ teaching, mental health and politics. PRRI finds these are now among the top reasons people leave (47% cite LGBTQ teaching). The draft treats sexuality as a topic, never as a visitor.
5. Grief questions miss anger at God, the sense that God is not listening, and suicide bereavement. Peer-reviewed grief research names all three.
6. The verse-reader (K10) is written around hard passages. App data shows people mostly bring comfort verses about fear and anxiety (Isaiah 41:10, Philippians 4:6).
7. Stereotyping risks: K5 as one angry voice; K7 merging "other faith" and "none" and framing them by grievance; K2 as an American evangelical; K6 as a combative atheist when most "nones" are not.
8. The research is heavily US, Protestant and evangelical, and much of it is commissioned by ministries. Pew, PRRI, Gallup and the peer-reviewed work carry the most weight.
9. One widely cited UK study, Bible Society's "The Quiet Revival" (2025), was withdrawn in March 2026 for faulty data. Do not use it.
10. I propose 40 additions (R-01 to R-40), each tied to a named study.

## Kind-by-kind findings

### K1 The curious first-timer

(a) Missing struggles. Search data's top God questions are big and blunt: "Who created God?" was the most common God question on US Google, ahead of "Why does God allow suffering?" (Stephens-Davidowitz 2015). Recent lists put "Is there a God?" and "Does life have a purpose?" first (RELEVANT 2025; UCA 2024; low weight). The draft's K1 has "who is jesus" (REAL, consistent with Google's 2007 top question "Who is God"), but nothing on whether God exists, who made God, or the point of life.

(b) Rarer or off. Many K1 questions are trivia ("what did you wear", "did you have pets", "did you go to school"). Nothing in the research says these are wrong. But no source shows first-timers mostly ask texture questions. The evidence points the other way: first-timers who type into a box about God tend to ask the largest question first.

(c) Stereotyping. The voice note "lower-case, no theology words" is a design assumption, not evidence. It is harmless as a register, but it should not shrink K1 to small questions. The research suggests a first-timer may open with "who made god".

(d) Wording. Research: "Who created God?", "Is there a God?", "Does life have a purpose?". Draft nearest: C2-01 "What did you believe about God?" (a historical question, not an existence question) and C12-25 "were you happy".

### K2 The churchgoer checking their own church

(a) Missing struggles. State of Theology 2025 finds 53% of US evangelicals agree the Holy Spirit is "a force but is not a personal being" and 47% agree "God accepts the worship of all religions". Pew 2025 finds 59% of US Catholics favour ordaining women and 84% favour allowing birth control. Pew 2019 found only 31% of Catholics hold the bread and wine "actually become" Christ's body. The draft covers most of these (C2-02, C7-07, C7-23, C8-18, C8-21, C6-11). Missing: the force-or-person question as the survey phrases it, and "does God accept every religion's worship".

(b) Rarer or off. "Would you say Jesus is your personal Lord and Savior?" (C1-15), "born again" (C4-04), "rapture" (C3-18), "tithe" (C10-02), "megachurch", "worship bands" (C15-06) are American evangelical idiom. They are real questions. But they are a large share of K2. Pew's 2025 international switching report and its 2026 Catholic/Protestant analysis show the world's churchgoers are increasingly in sub-Saharan Africa and Latin America, and Catholic and Pentecostal. Pew 2022 also found 76% of historically Black Protestants and 63% of evangelicals say we live in the end times; C3-18 and C3-20 are well placed for them.

(c) Stereotyping. K2 reads as one American evangelical voice with some Catholic add-ons. Orthodox, Anglican, Pentecostal and Global South churchgoers have few questions phrased the way they would say them. The research base cannot fill this (see Thin research), so treat it as a known gap, not a solved one.

(d) Wording. Survey items: "a force but is not a personal being"; "God accepts the worship of all religions, including Christianity, Judaism, and Islam"; "Everyone is born innocent in the eyes of God" (State of Theology 2025). Draft: C2-02 "Who or what is the Holy Spirit, to your people?"; C4-02 "are people born already guilty?". C4-02 is a good match. C2-02 is softer than the survey wording.

### K3 The pastor, teacher or group leader

(a) Missing struggles. Barna 2022: among pastors who considered quitting, the top reasons were stress (56%), loneliness and isolation (43%) and political division (38%); 29% each cited the job's effect on family, lack of optimism about their church, and vision conflict. Lifeway 2025: former pastors cite change in calling (40%), church conflict (18%), burnout (16%); 87% faced conflict in their last church. Barna/Gloo 2025: 41% of Protestant pastors use AI for Bible study prep; only 12% feel comfortable teaching on AI. The draft has C15-13 (burnout, flagged NF) and C6-07 (leader failure). It has nothing on loneliness, a divided congregation, or the cost to a leader's family.

(b) Rarer or off. C15-13 "What would you tell a pastor today who is burning out?" is flagged NF. It need not be. Every world has leaders under strain. Rephrased as "Were your leaders lonely? Who looked after them?" it becomes a question the world can answer from its own record.

(c) Stereotyping. K3 is written as a Protestant preacher ("quote on Sunday"). Catholic catechists, UK school RE teachers and small-group leaders are also in K3 and have no questions in their own terms. No research found on what RE teachers ask, so I add none.

(d) Wording. Barna's reasons: "immense stress of the job", "feeling lonely and isolated", "current political division". Draft has none of these words.

### K4 The student or scholar

(a) Missing struggles. I found no survey of what students or scholars ask historical sources or chatbots about early Christianity. Research is thin here.

(b), (c), (d). Nothing evidence-based to report. The K4 questions are reasonable on their face. One note: K4 is the kind most likely to test the "quotes verified" claim. C16-15 ("show me the actual page") and C16-17 ("which edition") are well chosen.

### K5 The person reevaluating or leaving

(a) Missing struggles.
- Drift. Pew 2025: of those who left their childhood religion, 38% say they "gradually drifted away" and 38% say religion "wasn't important in their life"; among those now with no religion the figures are 42% and 44%. The Great Dechurching (2023) finds the most common reasons are ordinary ones, such as moving. Lifeway 2019: 34% of young dropouts cited moving to college; 29% of those citing church reasons "did not feel connected". The draft has no quiet-drift question.
- LGBTQ teaching. PRRI 2024: 47% of those who left cite negative teachings about or treatment of LGBTQ people, up from 29% in 2016; 60% among unaffiliated adults under 30.
- Mental health. PRRI 2024: about a third of unaffiliated Americans say their childhood religion was bad for their mental health. Slade et al. 2023 estimate 27-33% of US adults report religious trauma at some point (low-medium weight).
- Politics. Pew 2025: 34% cite the religion's teachings on social and political issues. Lifeway 2019: about 1 in 4 dropouts left over politics.
- Clergy abuse. PRRI 2024: 31% cite clergy sexual abuse scandals; 45% of former Catholics. Pew 2025: 32% cite scandals.
- Distrust. Pew 2025: 49% of nones "don't trust religious leaders"; 50% don't like religious organisations.
- Deconstructing, not leaving. Barna 2024: 42% of US adults and 37% of current Christians say they have deconstructed the faith of their youth. Many stay.
The draft covers hypocrisy (C14-07), cover-up (C14-08, C14-17), doubt (C14-02, C14-03) and fear of hell (C3-30). It misses drift, LGBTQ teaching, mental health, politics, distrust as such, and the person deconstructing who wants to stay.

(b) Rarer or off. Nothing in K5 is wrong. But the balance is off. K5 questions are almost all about the church's crimes. The research says the most common leaver has quieter reasons.

(c) Stereotyping. The voice note "Personal, guarded, sometimes angry" and the question set together make K5 one wounded voice. That is a real voice, but the research shows at least three: the drifter, the wounded, and the questioner who wants to stay. Kinnaman (Barna 2024) argues deconstruction is often a search for depth.

(d) Wording. Research: "stopped believing in the religion's teachings", "gradually drifted away", "question a lot of religious teachings" (Pew); "church members seemed judgmental or hypocritical" (Lifeway 2019); "Christians are hypocrites" (Barna 2018); "bad for my mental health" (PRRI, paraphrased in coverage). Draft C14-07 "turned out to be hypocrites" matches well. The word "judgmental" appears nowhere in the draft, though Lifeway puts it beside "hypocritical".

### K6 The skeptic

(a) Missing struggles. Barna 2018 (teens, older fieldwork): top barriers for non-Christian teens were the problem of evil (29%), "Christians are hypocrites" (23%), "I believe science refutes too much of the Bible" (20%), "I don't believe in fairy tales" (19%), "too many injustices in the history of Christianity" (15%). Pew 2024: 60% of nones cite doubt about religious teachings; Pew 2025: 78% say they can be moral without religion. The draft covers evil (C2-20, C14-01), history (C9), miracles (C13-08), science (C5-08, C15-02). It lacks "who made God", and "can you be good without God", which is the single most widely held view among US nones in Pew's list.

(b) Rarer or off. C2-12 "three gods with a disclaimer" and C11-04 "philosophy is worthless" are probe-style traps. They are useful for testing, but the research gives no sign ordinary skeptics phrase things this way. Keep them as probes; do not let them define K6.

(c) Stereotyping. K6's voice ("Say it plainly", "Isn't it really just") is the combative atheist. Pew 2024 counts 658 atheists, 678 agnostics and 1,981 "nothing in particular" among 3,317 nones. Theos 2022 splits UK nones into "campaigning", "tolerant" and "spiritual"; only 51% of UK nones say they don't believe in God. The combative skeptic is a minority. Add a calmer, curious-doubting register.

(d) Wording. Barna's barrier items, quoted above, are short and flat. Draft C13-08 "Be honest: were the miracle stories true, or were they how you talked?" is close in spirit to "I don't believe in fairy tales". Draft has no "science refutes the Bible" line in visitor wording.

### K7 The person of another faith, or none

(a) Missing struggles. Research on what people of other faiths ask about Christianity is very thin. What exists: Springtide 2020 found 1 in 3 US young people don't trust people of other religions "very much" or "at all". Barna 2019 found 47% of practicing Christian millennials agree at least somewhat that it is wrong to share one's faith hoping someone converts. Talking Jesus 2022 (UK) found the largest group (33%) see Jesus as a prophet or spiritual leader, not God. Pew 2023 found 59% of Americans are neutral or don't know enough about Muslims. The draft lacks "was Jesus a prophet?" in K7 voice, and the conversion question from the visitor's own ethical side ("is it wrong to try to change someone's religion?").

(b) Rarer or off. C11-18 "I'm Hindu. Did you know India existed?" puts a naive line in a Hindu visitor's mouth. A Hindu visitor is more likely to know the Thomas tradition than the draft assumes. Rephrase as "Did any of your people go to India?".

(c) Stereotyping. Two problems. First, K7 merges people of another faith with people of none. The research treats these as different groups, and treats the nones as several groups (above). Second, almost every K7 question frames the visitor through grievance: would you convert me, did you harm my people, did you think I was damned. Those questions are needed (C11-05, C11-06 are historically grounded). But no study shows grievance is the main way people of other faiths approach Christianity. Add curiosity questions: what did you share with us, what did you learn from us.

(d) Wording. Talking Jesus: "prophet or spiritual leader". Draft C1-26 "Would Jesus have accepted me as I am, without converting?" has no research counterpart; keep, but it is a PREDICTED guess.

### K8 The grieving or frightened

(a) Missing struggles.
- Anger at God. Exline et al. 2011 found anger toward God common, and linked to poorer adjustment in bereavement and cancer; coverage reports about 40% of a grieving sample felt it (verify in paper). Burke and Neimeyer's work on complicated spiritual grief names resentment and doubt toward God, dissatisfaction with spiritual support, and the sense that God is not listening.
- Presence of the dead. Pew 2023: 34% of US adults felt the presence of a dead relative in the past year; 53% say they have been visited by a dead relative in a dream or other form. C13-14 fits this well.
- Reunion and relief. Pew 2021: of those who believe in heaven, 65% expect reunion with loved ones and 69% freedom from suffering. C3-12 fits.
- Suicide bereavement. Lifeway 2017 found 32% of churchgoers had lost someone close to suicide, and that stigma isolates the bereaved. The draft has no question from someone bereaved by suicide.
The draft lacks angry grief, "God isn't listening" in grief, and suicide bereavement.

(b) Rarer or off. C3-15 (unbaptised baby) is plausible and well flagged FAC, but I found no research showing how often it is asked. Treat it as PREDICTED, not evidenced.

(c) Stereotyping. K8 "wants comfort or a verdict" makes the grieving soft and asking. The grief research shows many are angry or doubting. C14-16 is the only domestic-violence question; it is a woman with a pastor telling her to submit. That is a real case. Keep it, but do not let it stand for every visitor who is unsafe at home.

(d) Wording. ICSG items include feeling angry with God and feeling God is not listening during prayer (paraphrased from the MDPI listing). Draft C14-04 "I pray and nothing happens" is a good match, but it is coded to K5 and K9, not K8.

### K9 The seeker who wants to believe

(a) Missing struggles. Barna 2024: 82% of US adults believe or are open to a spiritual dimension; 74% want to grow spiritually; they want "a safe, nonjudgmental setting". Springtide 2023: 85% of 13-25s believe in a higher power on some level; of those with sacred experiences, 69% had them in nature. Pew 2023 and Theos 2022 both find large "spiritual but not religious" groups. Open Generation 2022: roughly six in ten teens across 26 countries say they are motivated to know more about Jesus. YouVersion 2025: top search terms were "love", "anxiety", "peace". The draft's K9 has good entry questions (C7-24 "Teach me to pray", C4-23). It lacks: "can I know God personally", "I feel the sacred in nature, not church", "I believe in something, not religion", and anxiety.

(b) Rarer or off. None.

(c) Stereotyping. Low risk. The voice note "I want to..." fits.

(d) Wording. "Can I know God personally?" appears in the low-weight search lists. Draft C2-16 "Did you think God could be known, or only approached?" is the scholar's version of the same question.

### K10 The Bible reader with a verse

(a) Missing struggles. YouVersion's most-engaged verse was Isaiah 41:10 in 2020, 2022, 2023 and 2025, and Philippians 4:6 in 2024. Tanzania's national verse of 2025 was Jeremiah 29:11 (a non-US data point). Top in-app searches included "prayer", "peace", "anxiety", "love". The draft's VERSE questions are almost all hard or contested passages (Psalm 137, Matthew 25, Romans 9, Matthew 7, Luke 9:27, "Wives, submit").

(b) Rarer or off. The hard passages are real and Luke 9:27 is REAL from the pilot. But app data suggests the typical verse-bringer arrives with a comfort verse. The VERSE Library index should be built for these first.

(c) Stereotyping. The draft assumes the verse-reader is testing the world. Many are leaning on it.

(d) Wording. Research gives verse references, not visitor phrasing. Draft C5-10 ("Can we actually read something together?") is the right register for comfort verses too.

### K11 The history and culture lover

(a)-(d). I found no survey of what history enthusiasts ask about early Christianity. The REAL pilot item C16-14 (asking why famous names are missing) is the best evidence available and outranks anything predicted. Research is thin.

### K12 The interrogator of the system

(a) Missing struggles. Barna/Gloo 2025: about 30% of US adults say AI spiritual advice is as trustworthy as a pastor's (34% of practicing Christians; about 40% of younger adults); 65% of practicing Christians worry about AI acting as a substitute for God. The draft has strong "what are you" questions (C16-10 to C16-13) but none on trust ranking: "should I trust you more than my pastor?".

(b), (c). No issues. The FAC flag on all K12 items is correct.

(d) Wording. No verbatim visitor phrasing in the research.

## Cluster gaps

- C1 Jesus. Add "prophet, good teacher, or God?" (Talking Jesus 2022). Open Generation 2022 found 33% of teens across 26 countries believe Jesus rose; C1-06, C1-09, C1-18 are well placed.
- C2 God. Biggest gap in the draft: "is there a God", "who made God", "can I know God personally". Also the Spirit as force or person (State of Theology 2025). Anger at God should be coded K8 as well as K5.
- C3 Afterlife. Add reincarnation: Pew 2021 found 33% of US adults, including 30% of Christians, believe in it. Add suicide bereavement (FAC). Reunion and presence of the dead are covered.
- C4 Salvation. Add "can you be good without God" (Pew 2025, 78% of nones). "Does God accept every religion's worship" (State of Theology 2025).
- C5 Bible. Add "is the Bible reliable" in plain form, "science disproves it", and comfort verses.
- C6 Authority. Add plain distrust of religious leaders (Pew). Clergy abuse is in C14; consider cross-tagging C6.
- C7 Practice. Add prayer for anxiety (YouVersion). Transubstantiation is covered.
- C8 Women, sex. Add LGBTQ visitors in the first person (PRRI 2024; Trevor 2022: 26% of LGBTQ youth aged 18-24 say religion or spirituality is important to them). Currently sexuality is only asked about, never asked from.
- C9 Power. Add church and politics for K5 and K3 (Pew 2025; Barna 2022).
- C10 Money. No research gaps found. Research is thin here.
- C11 Other religions. Add the conversion-ethics question (Barna 2019) and the spiritual-but-not-religious visitor.
- C12 Daily life. Add "what's the point of life" (meaning). The Religious and Spiritual Struggles Scale names "ultimate meaning" as one of six struggles.
- C13 Supernatural. Covered well; Gallup 2023 shows 58% believe in the devil and 69% in angels, so C13-04 and C13-05 are mainstream, not fringe.
- C14 Suffering, leaving. Add drift, moving, disconnection, judgmental, mental health, deconstructing-to-stay, distrust, angry grief, God-not-listening.
- C15 Today. AI trust, politics, LGBTQ teaching. Several NF items (pastor burnout, AI) can be reframed so the world answers from its own record.
- C16 Evidence. Add AI trust ranking (FAC).

## Stereotyping risks

1. K5 as one angry voice. Research shows drifters outnumber the wounded. Fix by adding drift and stay-while-questioning questions, and widening the voice note.
2. K7 merges other faiths and none, and frames both by grievance. Split the voice note at least, and add curiosity questions.
3. K6 as the combative atheist. Most US and UK nones are "nothing in particular" or spiritual. Add a calmer doubting register.
4. K2 as an American evangelical. Phrases like "personal Lord and Savior", "rapture", "megachurch" dominate. Orthodox, Pentecostal, Catholic and Global South phrasings are sparse. Research cannot fully fix this; name it as a gap.
5. LGBTQ people appear only as a topic for churchgoers and skeptics, never as visitors. Trevor 2022 shows many LGBTQ young people hold faith as important.
6. K8 as soft and asking. Grief research shows anger and doubt are common.
7. K1 as only a trivia-asker. Search data suggests first-timers often start with the biggest question.
8. C11-18 gives a Hindu visitor a naive line about India. Rephrase.
9. C14-16 is the only unsafe-at-home question and is one specific scenario. Keep it; do not treat it as the whole case.
10. K3 as a Protestant preacher. Catechists and teachers are missing.

## What I could not verify

- Alpha's own list of guests' most-asked questions. I found only local church pages, not an official Alpha list. I did not use it.
- The Fuller Youth Institute doubt questions ("Does God exist? Does God love me?"). These appear in secondary summaries but not on the FYI page I fetched. Not used.
- Barna's 2024 deconstruction reasons. They are behind a paywall. Only the 42% and 37% headline figures are used.
- The share of Great Dechurching respondents who cite abuse. Not found in coverage.
- The "most-Googled faith questions" lists for 2024 and 2025 give no method or volumes and seem to repeat an apologetics book's list. Low weight only.
- Stephens-Davidowitz's figures (2015) were seen only via coverage, not the NYT original.
- Exline 2011 prevalence figures (e.g. 40% of the grieving) came from press coverage; check the paper.
- PRRI's 2024 figures came from the PDF listing and press coverage; the PDF itself was not opened.
- Pew's two "nones" figures conflict in coverage (41% vs 44% "no need"); I used only figures Pew's own pages gave.
- Lifeway 2019 judgmental/hypocritical share: 32% in most coverage, 26% in one repost.
- British Social Attitudes 2023-24 religion figures: no current release found.
- Any research on what Orthodox, African, Asian or Latin American visitors ask about early Christianity. None found in English. The Open Generation (26 countries) and Pew's 36-country switching study are the only non-US bases, and neither reports questions.
- Any research on what students, scholars or history enthusiasts ask (K4, K11). None found.
- Any data on how often grieving parents ask about unbaptised infants (C3-15). None found.

### Where the research is thin, slanted, or commissioned

- US-centric: nearly everything except Talking Jesus, Theos, Oakley, Open Generation, YouVersion and Pew's international reports.
- Evangelical or Protestant-centric: Lifeway (owned by the Southern Baptist Convention), Barna (Christian research firm, often commissioned), State of Theology (Reformed framing), Fuller, Great Dechurching, Talking Jesus. Their samples are often Protestant churchgoers.
- Commissioned by interested parties: Reviving Evangelism (Alpha USA), Open Generation (Alpha, Biblica, World Vision), Spiritually Open and AI studies (Gloo), State of the Bible (American Bible Society), YouVersion (Life.Church), Theos (Christian think tank), Talking Jesus (evangelism bodies), Slade (an advocacy-adjacent research society).
- Withdrawn: The Quiet Revival (Bible Society UK, 2025), withdrawn March 2026 after YouGov found fraudulent respondents in the 2024 data.
- Weighting advice: Pew, PRRI, Gallup and the peer-reviewed grief and struggle work should carry most weight. Ministry-commissioned studies are useful for wording and for what churchgoers say, less so for population claims.
- CiC's visitors are not only American and not only Protestant. The research can tell Mark what US and UK visitors struggle with. It cannot yet tell him what an Ethiopian Orthodox, Brazilian Pentecostal, Indian Catholic or Muslim visitor will ask. The REAL pilot data is the better guide there, and is worth growing.
