## 3. What the sources can carry, part by part

**1. Why they met.** Carried from both sides. Socrates VII.32 on Anastasius's sermon against "Theotokos" (npnf202 20696); Cyril's second letter (npnf214; Greek in the Three Epistles); Nestorius's own account of how the quarrel began (Bazaar, editors' chronology from 264; his letters to Cyril and Celestine in Loofs, 428, 1084-1085); Celestine's Roman synod of 430 and the ten-day ultimatum (npnf214 Celestine's letter; Mansi 4); the Pelagian refugees at Constantinople, which is why Rome moved (Mercator's Commonitoria, PL 48). Barhadbeshabba XXII, "the causes of the struggle between Cyril and Nestorius" (31913), gives the Church of the East's version.

**2. Who came, and who was missing.** Carried. Cyril opens on 22 June with Memnon's Ephesian bishops, before John of Antioch and the Roman legates arrive; Candidianus's protest; the sixty-eight bishops who asked for delay (Bazaar chronology 863; the acts in Mansi 4; Hefele from 476). Subscription counts should be taken from the acts, not from the later "two hundred". The Antiochenes who met apart (Mansi 5, 46711). Augustine was summoned and had died (Hefele; check before citing). Nestorius refused to attend (Socrates VII.34, npnf202 20873). The Church of the East sent no one and received the council only later and only in part (Barhadbeshabba; the Synodicon Orientale vendored by a sibling worker).

**3. What was argued.** Carried. Cyril's Five Tomes (430) and the anathemas; Nestorius's sermons in Mercator's Latin and Loofs's Greek; Theodoret's Counter-statements (npnf203 4700); Nestorius's own later defence of "two natures, one prosopon of union" in the Bazaar; Cassian for the Latin West. The limit: the council itself argued little. Session I read letters and patristic florilegia and voted; a story must say that the debate happened in writing before and after.

**4. What was decided, quoted from the source.** Carried in full. The decree deposing Nestorius (npnf214; Latin and Greek in Mansi 4); the approval of Cyril's second letter; the eight canons (npnf214; Mansi 4 at 131231; Pedalion); the condemnation of the Pelagians (canon 4 names Celestius); the ruling on Cyprus; the Messalian definition (npnf214 title). The reunion: Cyril's letter to John of Antioch with the Formula of Reunion of 433 (npnf214; Greek in the Three Epistles; Latin in Mansi 5). The counter-council's deposition of Cyril and Memnon (Mansi 5, 46711).

**5. Who disagreed, and why, in each side's own terms.** Carried, and unusually well. Nestorius speaks for himself in the Bazaar, in his letters (Loofs) and in the letter to the Constantinopolitans (Nau appendix III, 19540). The Antiochene bishops speak in their counter-council acts and in the Synodicon's letters (Mansi 5), and Theodoret in his Counter-statements and letters (npnf203). The Alexandrian bribery charge is documented by the Alexandrian side's own list of presents (Nau appendix II, 19385). The Pelagians have no voice of their own here; Mercator and the canon speak about them. The Church of the East's reading, that Nestorius was wronged, is Barhadbeshabba XX-XXX (31905-31964). Cyril's side: the Five Tomes, the Three Epistles, the acts.

**6. The legacy.** Carried. The reunion of 433 and its cost to both sides (Mansi 5; Theodoret's letters); the exile of Nestorius and his later writing (Bazaar); Chalcedon's re-reading of Cyril's letters (sister dossier); the Church of the East's adoption of "Nestorius" as a father (Barhadbeshabba; Synodicon Orientale, sibling file, 1244 for the two-natures creed of 486); Rome's memory through Mercator and Cassian; the Coptic memory of Cyril as "the young lion" (sibling Seert file, 4490). The Gallic world's `dw` records already draw on Cassian.

**7. Stories from inside the event's own sources.** Carried, each with its witness:
- Anastasius's sermon and the uproar over "Theotokos": Socrates VII.32 (20696), c. 440.
- Cyril opening the council before John arrived, Candidianus's protest, the bishops' night-long session and the city's torchlit celebration: the acts (Mansi 4) and Cyril's own letters (npnf214); the Bazaar chronology (863) gives Nestorius's version; a census site story already tells this scene.
- Nestorius's refusal to come, and his guard of soldiers: Socrates VII.34 (20873); the acts.
- John of Antioch's arrival and the counter-council: Mansi 5 (46711); npnf214 excursus (title read).
- The arrest of Cyril and Memnon by the count John, and the smuggled letter in a beggar's staff: Hefele (from 476; chapter only) and the Alexandrian correspondence in Mansi 5; locate the primary text before citing.
- The "presents" sent from Alexandria to the court: the list itself (Nau appendix II, 19385).
- Nestorius's miracles in exile and his death: the letter to Cosmas (Nau appendix I, 19027) and Barhadbeshabba XXX (31964); Church of the East memory, to be dated as such.

**8. How each tradition remembers it.** See §6 below.

## 4. The dissenting side in its own words

Who dissented: Nestorius; John of Antioch and the Eastern bishops (Theodoret, Alexander of Hierapolis, Irenaeus of Tyre and others), who deposed Cyril in return; the Pelagian exiles condemned with Nestorius; later, the Church of the East, which kept Nestorius as a father.

In their own words, as the Library now holds them: Nestorius, the Bazaar of Heracleides, whole (English 1925; French 1910); his letters to Cyril, Celestine, John of Antioch and Theodosius (Loofs, list at 428; Latin in Mercator, PL 48, and in Mansi 4 at 54999); his counter-anathemas (Loofs 502); his sermons in Mercator's Latin (PL 48 from 677) and in Cyril's quotations (Five Tomes); his letter to the Constantinopolitans (Nau 19540). The Antiochene bishops: the counter-council acts and John's letters (Mansi 5, 46711, 28921); the Synodicon adversus tragoediam Irenaei (Mansi 5, 72980), the dissenters' own letter collection from 431 to 435. Theodoret: Counter-statements (npnf203 4700) and letters.

Surviving only in opponents' quotation: Nestorius's sermons in Greek (Cyril, Mercator); the lost Tragoedia of Irenaeus (known from the Synodicon's extracts). Missing: the Pelagians' own voice at Ephesus (Julian of Eclanum and Celestius are quoted by Mercator and Augustine only); Bedjan's Syriac text of the Bazaar (not located on archive.org; the two items that claim it are a 2007 reprint and a scan of Nau's French); PG 77 (Cyril's letters in Greek) not located on archive.org.

## 5. Legends

No popular legend of the kind the Nicaea rule was written for attaches to Ephesus 431 in the Candidates list ("-"). Two stories need a date tag, not a legend note:

**Nestorius's tongue eaten by worms.** A hostile death legend. Silence checked: Socrates VII (ends before his death); the Bazaar (his own, so silent); Evagrius I.7 reports his sufferings in exile from his own letter (Greek heading at 1056; English chapter only). Earliest text we hold that carries it: not located this pass; the Coptic and Syriac Orthodox histories should be searched by a build thread (John of Nikiu 6205-6233 calls him "the scourge" but no tongue passage was found by search for "tongue" or "worm"). Do not assert a first appearance.

**Nestorius raises the dead in exile.** Church of the East counter-legend: the letter to Cosmas (Nau appendix I, 19027, "la déposition et les miracles de Nestorius") and the sibling Seert file ("Mar Nestorius ressuscita un mort dans sa tombe", located in the archive.org compilation at 9123; in the vendored PO 5 file the OCR drops the accent, search "Nestorius" there). Date each as sixth- to tenth-century memory.

## 6. Reception and memory, by tradition

- **Greek and Byzantine:** Socrates (c. 440); Evagrius I (late sixth century, Greek now held); the Pedalion's canons (1800). The Chalcedonian reading, in which Ephesus is Cyril's council and Nestorius the heresiarch.
- **Latin:** Cassian (430); Marius Mercator (430s, Constantinople, for Rome); Celestine's letters (Mansi 4-5); Leo's later use (npnf212); Hefele (1855). The Western memory binds Nestorius to Pelagius from the start.
- **Syriac Orthodox:** Chronicle of Edessa 58 (159), c. 540; Philoxenus against Nestorius (c. 500); Michael the Syrian VIII.4, "De l'époque du premier synode d'Éphèse" (sibling file `michael-the-syrian_chronique-tome2-livres-viii-xi-fra_chabot1901.txt`, 1146), twelfth century. Standpoint: Cyril's heirs who later reject Chalcedon as a Nestorian revenge (Nau's appendix III introduction says so of Philoxenus, 19540 ff.).
- **Church of the East:** Barhadbeshabba XX-XXX (c. 600; 31905-31964), the fullest account from the side that kept Nestorius; the Synodicon Orientale (sibling file: the two-natures creed of 486 at 1244); the Chronicle of Seert's Nestorius memory (sibling file; see §1). Standpoint: Nestorius wronged by "the Egyptian".
- **Coptic:** Severus al-Muqaffa, the Cyril entry (33057-33166), tenth century; John of Nikiu 6205-6233, seventh century; the sibling Seert file's Evetts fascicle (4462-4490). Standpoint: Cyril the hero, Nestorius the scourge.
- **Armenian:** none located for this council as such. The Armenian church received Ephesus with the "Tome of Proclus"; the held Moses of Khoren has no Ephesus passage (the "Efesto" hits are Hephaestus). The Armenian Book of Letters (Tiflis 1901) was not found on archive.org; a 1994 Jerusalem edition exists and is in copyright.
- **Georgian, Ethiopian, Nubian:** none located in a public-domain edition reachable here, beyond the Ethiopic John of Nikiu already named.

## 9. Gaps and escalations

- The dissenting side is present in its own words, more fully than for any other Era 2 council. Nothing here needs a ruling.
- Nestorius's Syriac (Bedjan) and Cyril's Greek letters (PG 77) are not held; the English, French, Latin and Loofs's Greek fragments carry every quotation a story needs.
- The Pelagian side at Ephesus has no voice of its own in any text held; a story must say so.
- The Armenian and Georgian reception of Ephesus is not held in any public-domain text reachable here.
- The Mansi volumes' Greek columns are only partly recovered; quote the Latin, or the Greek from the Three Epistles.
