## 3. What the sources can carry, part by part

**1. Why they met.** Strong, from both ends. Alexandria 400: Theophilus's own synodical letter (npnf206 19795) and his letter to Epiphanius (19694); Socrates VI.7 on the Anthropomorphite quarrel and the Tall Brothers (17563-17689); Sozomen VIII.11-12 (43316, 43360); Palladius on Isidore and the four brothers; Cassian on Serapion (npnf211 35878). The Oak 403: Chrysostom's letter to Innocent (npnf109 21816; Palladius 1803 on; Greek PG 52 17800); Socrates VI.15 (18234); Sozomen VIII.16-17 (43635, 43684); Theodoret V.34 (17637).

**2. Who came and who was missing.** Good. Palladius names the Oak's bishops and says they were mostly Egyptians (summons at 4984-4997; Mansi's note on their number at 105324); Sozomen VIII.17 (43684) and Socrates VI.15 (18234) describe the Egyptian majority; Theodoret V.34 (17672). Who stayed away: John himself and the forty bishops with him (Palladius; Mansi 105324). For 400, Letter 92 names the convening bishops of Egypt (19795); Letters 93-94 the Palestinian and Cypriot assent (19890, 19908).

**3. What was argued.** Strong for the doctrinal quarrel, thin for the Oak's floor. Origen's teaching as charged: Epiphanius, Panarion 64 (Greek, 31495 on); Theophilus's synodical and paschal letters (npnf206 19795, 19971, 20102, 20170); Jerome, Letter 84 (18919) and Against John of Jerusalem (40428). The defence: Rufinus's preface and prologue to Peri Archon (npnf203 45686; anf04 22273), his epilogue to Pamphilus (45205), his two Apologies (45886, 46229). The Oak itself: the charge list survives only through Photius (Mansi 109310 on, the Greek garbled, the Latin legible with long s), and in summary in Palladius (4588, 10498) and Hefele Sec. 115 (26405). No speeches from the Oak survive.

**4. What was decided, quoted from the source.** Good. Alexandria 400: Letter 92 (npnf206 19795). Rome 400: Anastasius to Simplicianus (19918) and to John of Jerusalem (npnf203 46090). The Oak: the deposition as reported in Chrysostom's letter to Innocent (npnf109 21816; Greek PG 52 17800), in Palladius, and in Photius via Mansi (110056 on); Innocent's judgment that the Oak was null (npnf109 22169, 22199; Sozomen VIII.26, 44273; Palladius 2414-2500). The imperial edict of exile is reported, not held.

**5. Who disagreed and why, in each side's own terms.** Unusually strong; see section 4 below.

**6. The legacy.** Good. The Jerome-Rufinus rupture in both men's Apologies (npnf203 46229, 50868); Rome's breach with the East and its mending (Theodoret V.34-35, 17705-17787; Socrates VII.45, 21453; John of Nikiu 6082-6118); the return of the relics in 438 (Socrates VII.45; Greek socrates tomus 2, 19810; Synaxaristes 27 January, 54500); Origen's name under a cloud until 553 (Hefele; the later condemnation is another worker's event). The Coptic church's memory of Theophilus as hero (Severus 31760 on) and the Ethiopian Synaxarium's version in which John himself gives up Origen's books (Budge I, 13850-13857) show how the losing side of 403 and the winning side of 400 were remembered together.

**7. Stories from inside the event's own sources.** Rich. Serapion weeping "they have taken my God from me" (Cassian, Conf. X.3, npnf211 35878 on); the Tall Brothers at Theophilus's feet and his words to Ammonius (Palladius; Sozomen VIII.12, 43360); Epiphanius ordaining in John's city and leaving when warned (Socrates VI.12-14, 18072-18185; Sozomen VIII.14-15, 43489-43634); Theophilus crossing the Bosphorus to the Oak and the summons read to John (Palladius 4984-4997; Greek PG 47 10370); the night John left and the people's riot (Socrates VI.16, 18311; Sozomen VIII.18, 43761); the fire in the Great Church (Sozomen VIII.22, 44018); John's letters from Cucusus to Olympias (npnf109 20251 on; Greek PG 52 20121). All told by named sources of the period.

**8. How each tradition remembers it.** See section 6.

## 4. The dissenting side in its own words

This event has losers on both sides, and both are held in their own words.

- **The Origenists (Rufinus and the Tall Brothers' party).** Rufinus speaks for himself: the preface to Peri Archon, the prologue to Macarius, the epilogue to Pamphilus, the Apology to Anastasius and the Apology against Jerome (npnf203 45686, 45205, 45886, 46229; anf04 22273). The Tall Brothers' own petition does not survive; their case is carried by Palladius (a friend) and by Sozomen VIII.12-13. Origen's own De Principiis in Rufinus's version is held (anf04 22375), with Jerome's charge that Rufinus softened it (npnf203 50868).
- **The anti-Origenists (Theophilus, Epiphanius, Jerome).** Theophilus in his own words: Letters 87, 89, 90, 92, 96, 98, 100, 113 in Jerome's Latin, English in npnf206 (19585-20170, 22446); his canonical texts in Mansi 3 (118994). Epiphanius: Letter 51 (10605) and Panarion 64 in Greek (31495 on). Jerome: Letter 84 (18919), Against John of Jerusalem (40428), the Apology (npnf203 50868).
- **John Chrysostom and his party (the losers at the Oak).** John's letter to Innocent (npnf109 21816; Palladius 1803; Greek PG 52 17800) and his letters to Olympias; Palladius's Dialogue (English held; Greek PG 47 7061 on).
- **The Oak's bishops (Theophilus's party in 403).** Their own words survive only as the list of charges and the summons preserved by Photius, printed in Mansi 3 (109310-110939), and as Theophilus's letter to Innocent reported by Palladius (1688-1700). Theophilus's own account of the Oak, which Facundus and Palladius knew, is lost. The story must say the winners of 403 are heard mostly through their victims.
- **Innocent of Rome.** His own letters, in Greek translation via Sozomen VIII.26 (npnf202 44273) and in npnf109 (22169, 22199).
- **The Anthropomorphite monks.** No text of their own. Serapion's words are reported by Cassian (npnf211 35878 on), a hostile witness; Socrates VI.7 (17684-17689) gives the name as a party label.

## 5. Legends

- **Epiphanius tears down the curtain at Anablatha.** Not a legend: it is Epiphanius's own account in Letter 51 to John of Jerusalem, 393, in Jerome's Latin (npnf206 11099-11119). Silence checked: no other held text mentions Anablatha or the curtain (grep "Anablatha", "curtain": Palladius 3154, Socrates/Sozomen 26006 and Theodoret 14143 use "curtain" in other senses only; Cassian none). The note for a story: the one source is the actor himself, writing to his opponent. Tag `Documented` as Epiphanius's claim; the iconoclast reading of it is later and not held.
- **"Again Herodias rages, again she asks for the head of John."** Reported as John's words by Socrates VI.18 (18456), Sozomen VIII.20 (43923) and Palladius (5556; the editor at 544 and 10157 doubts it). The full homily that circulates under this title is spurious and not held. Say the phrase is reported by three near-contemporaries and the homily is not from the time.
- **Epiphanius and John exchange prophecies ("you will not die a bishop"; "you will not reach your island").** Socrates VI.14 and Sozomen VIII.15 (npnf202 43630), c. 440-450; Bar Hebraeus I repeats it (8592-8600), 13th century. Palladius, the eyewitness, does not carry it (grep "prophe", "die a bishop": none). Tag `Widely Accepted` tradition, first in Socrates.
- **John's last words, "Glory to God for all things."** No held text carries them: not Palladius (grep "Glory to God for all"), not Socrates, Sozomen or Theodoret. The Synaxaristes OCR could not be searched for the Greek phrase reliably. First appearance not located in a citable public-domain text we hold.
- **Eudoxia's silver statue.** Socrates VI.18 (18422) and Sozomen VIII.20 (43923), near-contemporary; not a legend, but the connection to the "Herodias" words is theirs.

## 9. Gaps and escalations
