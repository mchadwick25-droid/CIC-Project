## 1. The six claims, with what the evidence says

1. **The wheel: against, on balance.** Apple's collection guidance: use the standard row or grid and avoid a custom layout that draws attention to itself. Apple's picker guidance allows a wheel when the hidden values are predictable, like an alphabet; eleven unfamiliar names are not. Cover Flow was removed from every Apple product within about twelve years, with no usability reason on record. Google's own carousel research found the single-large-item "hero" layout left people unsure how many items there were; layouts showing several items did not. Two Oslo studies (forty participants each) found a site without a carousel beat the same site with one. For: Google Arts and Culture fans five portrait cards and raises the one under the pointer, and it reads well in Mark's screenshots; the difference is scale and content: five cards, a sketch and a name each, no text to read while they move.
2. **The card front: half supported.** The front that works on Google's hand is a picture and one name. Our draft intros run 32 to 49 words at reading ease 20 to 55, below the project floor of 60. No study of reading text on angled or moving cards was found. On flips, no usability study exists; practitioner records agree: never flip on hover alone, flip on a button or Enter and Space, keep nothing essential only on the back, honour reduced motion.
3. **Two actions on one card: supported only if one is primary.** Today's chair carries two links (brief 05's finding). The Rijksmuseum tile shows the same shape, and its picture link has no accessible name. One face, one action; the second action in the opened state.
4. **Raise to build a table: supported with a condition.** Apple separates focus from selection: focusing lifts the item, selecting is a second act, because automatic selection "might cause a distracting context shift". Music apps that collect picks show three cues: a mark, a count, a button off until the minimum. Raise can be a fourth cue, not the only one.
5. **Era colour: weakly supported; the data is not ready.** WCAG 1.4.1 requires a second cue beside colour. The census gives ten eras three repeating ground tones; eras I and VII share one. Each world has two accent colours that disagree for nine of eleven. Colour can tint; the era name must be written.
6. **The fallback: supported by standards that already specify it.** WCAG allows a sideways scroller inside a page when each panel fits 320 px; requires a plain button for anything swiped or dragged; names reduce-motion as the way to turn interaction animation off. The ARIA carousel pattern requires previous and next buttons and warns the screen-reader experience "can be confusing and disorienting". Today's landing page lacks two of these: its strips are not keyboard-focusable and its portrait links have empty names.

## 3. Flip cards: the practitioner record (all indexed only)

- W3C WAI Interest Group list, 2020: a flip card whose front text appears only on hover or focus is never seen by people using screen magnification without a screen reader; a reply warns against trapping focus inside the tile.
- UK Department for Education design history, "Adding animated cards": a hover trigger "isn't keyboard friendly", so the team used a button to flip, and kept the card from acting as a dialog.
- City of Calgary design system, "Rotating cards": cards must be focusable, flip on Enter or Space, respect reduced motion, and must not keep critical information only on the back.
- Articulate community: a learner "isn't necessarily going to know that they should hover".
