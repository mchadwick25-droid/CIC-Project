A. The whole hand

Mark's idea at full strength. All eleven cards sit in one fanned hand. The card under the pointer rises. Previous and next roll the hand; so do the arrow keys and a sideways swipe. Click a raised card to turn it over. The back carries the next level and the two actions. A seated card keeps its rise and a mark.

What the grid says

  • Against: Apple's guidance warns off custom layouts that draw attention to themselves. Eleven unfamiliar names are not "predictable hidden values". Half the cards are hidden at any moment, which is the finding Google's own research made against single-item layouts.
  • For: it is the closest to the screenshot Mark likes, and the raise-on-focus idiom is platform-standard.
  • Fallback: reduced motion or a phone turns the hand into the list below it. Same cards, same actions.

B. A hand of five

The same hand, but it holds five cards at a time, drawn from the eleven so that each era is present. "Shuffle" brings the next five; the browser remembers what this visitor has seen. A search brings matching cards into the hand you hold. "See all" lays the whole deck out as a scroll under the hand; tap a card there and it rises into your hand. This is Theme D's amendment made literal: a rotating few, not everything on the first page.

What the grid says

  • For: five is the count Google's hand uses, and the count at which nothing has to hide. The whole page stays short.
  • Against: a visitor who wants Augustine's century must deal twice or open the grid. The random deal must never drop a seated card.
  • Fallback: as in A. The list shows the dealt five, with the grid one tap away.

C. Row and grid

What the evidence most supports. One contained row of upright cards, three eras marked, the next card peeking at the edge. Previous and next buttons, a visible scrollbar, snap on release, no motion the visitor did not start. A card turns in place for the next level. On a phone the row becomes a list with the portrait at the left. The hand is kept only as the raise on hover.

What the grid says

  • For: this is the pattern WCAG, the ARIA carousel pattern, Apple's collection guidance and both September briefs describe. Nothing is hidden behind an angle.
  • Against: it is the least like the screenshot. The character has to come from the cards and the portraits, not from the motion.
  • Fallback: the row already is its own fallback. Reduced motion only removes the turn animation.

Shared in all three

One face, one action. The front of a card is a portrait, the Representative's name and title, the world's name and its era. The intro sentence and the two actions are on the back, read at rest. "Talk with [name]" is the primary action; "Seat at the table" is the quieter one.

Seat two or three, then go. The tray at the foot counts seats and names them. "Go to the table" turns on at two. A seated card keeps a mark and a ring, so the raise is never the only cue.

Where every path lands. Each action shows the address it would open: the existing talk page with worlds and mode. The room's own welcome, disclosure and the Facilitator's safety handling follow unchanged. The chooser adds no new words before them.
