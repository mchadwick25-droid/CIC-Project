# 06 — Horizontal-scroll storytelling: can a sideways slideshow carry the Unfolding Story?

**Workstream:** Website V2 · **Kind:** research brief (input to a design pass; decides nothing) · **Date:** 2026-09-08 · **Researched by:** Fable · **Companions:** `01-narrative-invitation-design-patterns.md` (pacing; P3 "nothing hijacks the scroll"), `02-craft-and-visual-quality-bar.md` (imagery, motion budget, phone), `05-landing-page-structure-and-choice-flow.md` (the attention arithmetic this brief has to respect)

**Why this brief exists.** Mark wants the ~300-word "Unfolding Story" told in a far more visual, image-led way — real artifact photographs or commissioned illustrations carrying the narrative across twenty centuries, with short text threaded around them — instead of four paragraphs of prose. He has now asked one specific question: **can that story be a horizontal-scrolling slideshow rather than a vertical scroll?** The question sits on top of a tension already on record. Brief 05 §4.1 showed, from NN/g's eyetracking, that 74% of a visitor's attention lives in the first two screenfuls, and that the chairs — the site's one primary act — already begin around screenful two on a desktop and three or four on a phone. A rich image-led narrative naturally wants to run long; every extra screenful of story is paid for with the scarcest attention on the page. A sideways gallery that holds six pictures in the vertical space of one might resolve both goals — *if* the pattern exists in a form that works, and *if* it is the self-contained kind rather than the kind that seizes the page. So this brief answers six questions with named, real examples: whether horizontal storytelling works for narrative content at all; whether a full-page "scroll-jacked" takeover and a contained swipe gallery are meaningfully different in outcome; what touch versus trackpad and wheel do to it; what WCAG and the platform say; whether the pattern reads as current or as a 2012-era gimmick; and whether a real, named example of the *hybrid* exists — a horizontal gallery inside a normal vertical page that does not lengthen the path to what is below it. It ends with a direct recommendation.

**How the research was done, honestly.** No external page was fetched live. As in briefs 01–05, every direct fetch from this sandbox was blocked at the egress proxy — this time nngroup.com, w3.org, developer.chrome.com, css-tricks.com, siteiq.net, metabole.studio, shorthand.com, deptagency.com and intarch.ac.uk among them. Two things did work: web search, which returns indexed page text; and raw file fetches from GitHub, which supplied the browser-support data and two documentation mirrors. Roughly seventy searches were run; nothing below was written from memory alone. Each claim carries one of these tags:

- **[indexed]** — a named site's own page text, or a press description of it, as returned by web search; not seen rendered.
- **[maker's account]** — the studio, newsroom, museum, platform or author describing what they built and why.
- **[critique/research]** — a Nielsen Norman Group or Baymard usability finding, a peer-reviewed study, or a published design critique or trend retrospective.
- **[industry claim]** — a vendor's or marketing blog's number; reported because the trade repeats it, never relied on.
- **[platform data]** — browser support read from the `web-features` repository's computed status files (fetched directly, values quoted), or from a browser vendor's own blog or bug tracker via indexed text.
- **[verified: repo]** — CiC files read directly. **[inference]** marks my own reasoning or arithmetic.

Two of the starting points Mark's question named turned out not to be horizontal at all, and §3 says so. §10 lists what D-work should open in a real browser before anything is imitated.

## 1. The short answer

Five things the evidence says, in order of confidence.

1. **Full-page horizontal scrolling for reading content has failed every usability test it has been given, from 2002 to 2026.** Nielsen listed it among the top ten mistakes of 2002 and wrote "avoid horizontal scrolling on web pages" in 2005; NN/g's eyetracking put 1% of fixations to the right of the initial viewport in 2010 and *none* in the later study on larger monitors; NN/g's 2024 scrolljacking study found "the majority of participants… at least mildly disoriented"; and a 2026 peer-reviewed experiment (Voinea & Murano, n = 20) found scrolljacking significantly *lowered accuracy and satisfaction* with no gain in speed. The trade's own 2026 retrospectives call scroll-jacking "the clearest offender" and "fading" [critique/research]. Nothing here is soft.
2. **The successes are all cases where sideways *is the meaning* — distance, time, a shelf — and almost all are desktop-era artefacts.** Josh Worth's *If the Moon Were Only 1 Pixel* (2014, Webby), the British Museum's *Museum of the World* (2015, desktop-only), Apple's *Thirty Years of Mac* timeline (2014), the Van Gogh Museum's sideways homepage (2020; still flagged "unusual" in 2026). Where horizontality is not the meaning, it is decoration, and decoration that costs attention [maker's account + critique].
3. **The contained gallery is a different pattern with a different record.** Apple's own Human Interface Guidelines say a horizontal scroller inside a vertical one is fine and a same-axis nest is not; Material 3 ships four carousel layouts as a first-class component; Shorthand — the editorial-storytelling platform newsrooms use — added a "Media Gallery" precisely so a horizontal swipe could sit inside a vertical story; TimelineJS has been embedded in newsroom pages a quarter of a million times, including a Pulitzer-winning package; and Instagram's carousel — a horizontal swipe inside a vertical feed — is the highest-engagement post type on the platform and, in the vertical-screen era, the one every visitor's thumb already knows [maker's account + industry claim]. NN/g and Baymard do not exempt it from scrutiny: it is "plagued by low discoverability and sequential access" and "crucial information does not belong in" it — but their remedies (visible arrows, a peeking next item, a count, nothing essential trapped inside) are specific and cheap [critique/research].
4. **WCAG has already drawn the line this brief needs.** SC 1.4.10 (Reflow) explicitly allows a horizontally scrolling carousel whose panels each fit 320 px (technique G225) while forbidding page-level sideways scroll; SC 2.5.7 (WCAG 2.2, AA) requires that anything swipeable also work by a single tap — i.e., previous/next buttons are mandatory, not optional; SC 2.3.3 (AAA) is the criterion scroll-jacking fails; and since 2024–25 Chrome and Firefox make scrollers keyboard-focusable by default while Safari still does not, so the gallery carries `tabindex="0"` itself [critique/research + platform data].
5. **The platform has caught up to the contained pattern, not to the takeover.** CSS scroll snap and `prefers-reduced-motion` have been Baseline "widely available" since July 2022; Chrome 135 (March 2025) shipped CSS-only carousels with browser-managed accessibility, still Chromium-only; `overscroll-behavior` — the property that would keep a trackpad swipe from triggering Back — lost its Baseline status in April 2026 for inconsistent behaviour and does not stop Safari's history gesture at all. A contained gallery is buildable in CSS plus ~20 lines of vanilla JS today; the takeover still needs a scroll-hijacking library [platform data].

**The recommendation (§9): yes to horizontal, no to a takeover.** A self-paced, contained gallery of roughly six artifact panels inside the normal vertical page, with the story's spine kept in the vertical flow, visible previous/next buttons, a peeking next panel, a panel count, no autoplay and no interception of the page's scroll. Not a full-page horizontal site; not a pinned section that converts wheel motion into sideways motion. If the D-work prototype fails its checks, the fallback is the image-led *vertical* scroll (Emergence, *The Boat*) with brief 05's skip link — never the takeover.

## 3. Question 1 — Does horizontal storytelling work for narrative content?

### 3.1 First, two corrections to the starting list

- ***The Boat* (SBS, 2015) is a vertical scroller.** Its makers describe a site that "initially presents as a conventional long-scroller but quickly surprises with immersive events tied to the story"; "the viewer scrolls down, consuming the story at their own pace — although there is also an auto scroll option" [maker's account: Matt Huynh's project page; Communication Arts; SBS]. It remains the best precedent for *ink-illustrated, image-led narrative* — vertically.
- ***Riding the New Silk Road* (NYT, 2013) is vertical too:** "the route is displayed on a vertical map with points that link to accompanying photos and videos" [indexed: Berkeley AMI taxonomy; contemporary reviews]. No 2023–26 NYT horizontal-scroll narrative surfaced in indexed text at all; the Times' current visual-story vocabulary is vertical scrollytelling and 3D scenes.
- **Bruno Simon's portfolio is not horizontal scroll** — it is a Three.js world "where visitors can drive around in a red car" [maker's account: Awwwards case study]. The Awwwards "horizontal portfolio" genre is real (Patrick Heng's portfolio, "Department of Culture and Tech," the "Horizontal Layout Websites" collection) [indexed], but it is a portfolio genre, not a narrative one.

The fact that two of the three "known horizontal" starting points are vertical is itself a finding: memorable image-led storytelling is *remembered* as sideways more often than it *is*.

### 3.2 The successes — and what each one had that CiC's story may not

**Josh Worth, *If the Moon Were Only 1 Pixel* (2014; Webby, Science)** · [maker's account + indexed]. "A tediously accurate scale map of the solar system"; "the simulator uses a horizontal slider to scroll you from one end of the sun's neighborhood to the other." Worth's own words on the scrolling: "Sorry for all the scrolling on the 1px moon project. Can we convert scrolling to energy?" — and his stated reason for refusing to add auto-scroll or jump features: they "almost entirely defeat the purpose of the project by eliminating the need to actively scroll through the endless void." **Why it works:** the sideways motion *is* the argument (distance). **Take:** horizontality earns its place only when moving sideways means something the reader can feel — here, emptiness. Twenty centuries could be that meaning for CiC; six paragraphs of prose cannot.

***The Museum of the World* — British Museum + Google Cultural Institute / Arts & Culture Lab (November 2015)** · [maker's account + critique]. "An interactive timeline which spans from roughly two million years ago to the present day… divided into 'tracks' on which five different coloured sets of dots sit, representing geographic origin"; curators' audio on objects; "this WebGL experiment is for desktop only" [maker's account: Experiments with Google; Design Week]. The peer-reviewed review (Lash, *Internet Archaeology* 44, 2017) calls it "undoubtedly a success in connecting vast audiences with objects of history through a thoughtful and aesthetically pleasing microsite" while questioning "the role of the museum… and the presentation of world history." **Why it works:** artifacts on a timeline — the nearest cousin of what Mark wants — and the axis is time. **What it cost:** desktop only, WebGL, a Google lab. The Atlas already occupies this niche for CiC; the homepage story should not compete with it (brief 01 §4.4).

**Apple, *Thirty Years of Mac* (January 2014; Awwwards Site of the Day)** · [indexed]. "A scrolling timeline at the bottom separated into years, where users could click each year individually to open a detailed webpage" (MacRumors). Orientation is inferred from that description, not seen rendered [inference]. **Take:** a year-strip as a *chooser* beneath a hero, not the whole page — which is closer to the hybrid of §8 than to a takeover.

***Akademie Zubrowka* — Watson/DG for *The Grand Budapest Hotel* (February 2014; Communication Arts webpick; PRINT design image of the day)** · [maker's account + critique]. "A '60s-style series of lessons about the history of the republic," with "visual elements that mimic a microfiche machine, complete with a navigational wheel to move through the digital 'slides'"; "produced entirely in-house, working in close contact with… Wes Anderson himself." **Why it works:** the slide-by-slide form was a period prop (microfiche), so the sideways move was in character. **Take:** brief 01 P7 — form matches subject. A manuscript, a codex, a scroll, a reliquary strip *could* justify a sideways form for CiC; a generic slider cannot.

**Van Gogh Museum website — DEPT (concept/design) + Q42 (development) + the museum's digital team (relaunched 2020–21; multiple 2021 design awards)** · [maker's account + critique]. 8.5 million visits a year; "the homepage employs side-scrolling to take you through what's on display"; each page carries "one of the colours in the colour palette, inspired by Vincent's quest for colour"; the story format is "short, engaging stories, reminiscent of Stories on Instagram and Facebook, which use images, animations, highlighted elements of artworks ('hotspots') and comparisons" [maker's account: press release; DEPT; blooloop]. Numiko's 2026 museum roundup: "takes the unusual step of scrolling sideways, rather than vertically, on the homepage. Scrolling also changes the colour palette, an initially subtle yet captivating effect that invites continued exploration" [critique]. **Why it works:** a homepage that is a *shelf of things on display* — browsing, not reading. No documented user complaint was found; "unusual" is the strongest word in the record. **Take:** the museum world's one live, well-made takeover is a browse surface, and its *narrative* pieces are Instagram-style stories — vertical.

**Vention, *State of AI 2025* (Awwwards Honorable Mention, 2025)** · [indexed]. Tagged by Awwwards for "horizontal layout," "unusual navigation," "data visualization," "storytelling," with a "horizontal scroll" timeline element catalogued in its inspiration library. A current, award-noted data report in sideways form. **Take:** proof the form is alive in 2025 for *report* content with a timeline spine; no reception data.

**Knight Lab, TimelineJS (2012→)** · [maker's account]. "Passed 31 million pageviews, 250,000 deployments" by November 2013; "used by… TIME, Le Monde, and the Vancouver Sun," "deployed at LeMonde, RadioLab, Gigaom"; the Denver Post "used it in a package that covered the Aurora theater shooting — the package would later win a Pulitzer"; La Nación built "a timeline of all the popes." It is embedded "using a snip of HTML code" — an iframe inside an ordinary vertical article. **Why it works:** a horizontal *widget* with a time axis, sitting inside vertical pages, with visible arrows. **Take:** the most-deployed horizontal storytelling component in journalism is the hybrid of §8, not a takeover.

### 3.3 The failures and the contested cases

**Apple's 2013 Mac Pro page** · [critique]. Trent Walton, "Scroll Hijacking" (23 Oct 2013): hijacking "a user's scroll rate for marketing purposes" is "one of my least favorite things in web design these days." Envato's later survey names it "one of the worst offenders of bad parallax design & scroll hijacking"; a designer reported "3+ second delays while trying to explore each section." Apple's product pages are, a decade on, the pages people write browser extensions to un-hijack (iDownloadBlog, 2019). **Lesson:** even the most admired product-marketing team on the web could not make interception feel like anything but interception.

**USAToday.com's swipe-mimicking article pages** · [critique/research]. NN/g (Katie Sherwin, 27 April 2014, "Beware Horizontal Scrolling and Mimicking Swipe on Desktop"): "Users ignored the arrows on the sides of the article pages, because it was not clear what would be revealed if they clicked. When specifically asked about the arrows, one user said, 'I wouldn't use these. I want to read what I want to read.'" The article's principle: "Content hidden by a horizontal scroll is at a disadvantage because even salient visual cues don't offer strong information scent," and "in implementations where the entire page 'swipes' to reveal new content, the risk of disappointment is even higher." **Lesson:** the arrows must promise something the reader can *see* — a peeking next panel does what an abstract arrow cannot.

**Therabody and the sites in NN/g's 2024 study** · [critique/research]. "Scrolljacking 101" (31 January 2024): "the majority of study participants were at least mildly disoriented… participants did not hesitate to click away from the page or refresh it"; a Therabody participant was "surprised and disoriented that nothing happened upon scrolling." **Lesson:** the disorientation is not aesthetic; it is the reader losing the one contract a page makes — that a scroll moves the page.

**Google Web Stories (AMP Stories, 2018 → pullback 2024–25)** · [maker's account + indexed]. The open-web version of the tap-through "horizontal story" was Google's own format. In February 2024 Google "deprecated the carousel view of Web Stories in Discover," stopped showing them in image search, and turned the Search grid into a carousel (Search Engine Land; SERoundtable); by April 2025 publishers reported they were "not appearing on Google Discover" at all (Search Central community thread). Glenn Gabe: "The demise of Web Stories begins." **Lesson:** the full-screen tap-through story — Snapchat's 2013 invention, Instagram's 2016 adoption, "storyboarding a long-form story into snippets" as USA Today's audience editor put it (ONA, 2019) — thrives *inside* feeds and did not survive as a destination format on the open web.

***The Museum of the World*'s own caveat** — "desktop only" — and *Snow Fall*'s (brief 01) mobile failure are the same lesson from the two most admired horizontal and vertical spectacles respectively: takeovers are built for one device class, and the other half of the audience gets nothing.

### 3.4 What separates the two columns

| Worked | Did not | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Sideways *means* something: distance, time, a shelf of objects | Sideways is a presentation choice laid over text | Worth's void; Museum of the World's timeline; Van Gogh's "what's on display" vs. USAToday's articles, Mac Pro's marketing copy |
| The reader chooses the tempo, and there is a way to jump | The page decides the tempo | Worth's refusal of auto-scroll is the exception that proves it: he *wanted* tedium, and apologised for it |
| A widget inside a page (TimelineJS; Apple's year strip) | The whole page | TimelineJS's 250k deployments vs. Web Stories' retreat |
| Desktop-first, and honest about it | Desktop-only, silently | Museum of the World's "for desktop only" vs. *Snow Fall* on phones |
| The form is in character (microfiche wheel; a museum shelf) | The form is a trend | Akademie Zubrowka; brief 01 P7 |

For CiC: twenty centuries *is* a time axis, so the meaning test can be passed — but only if the panels are eras with real artifacts, not decorated paragraphs.

## 4. Question 2 — The scroll-jacking distinction

### 4.1 Three patterns, not two

The question as posed contrasts (a) a full-page horizontal takeover with (b) a contained swipe gallery. The trade uses a third pattern more often than either, and it must be named because it *looks* like (b) and *behaves* like (a):

- **(a) Full-page horizontal site.** The document's main axis is sideways (Van Gogh homepage; Josh Worth). Vertical wheel/trackpad input is either translated or ignored.
- **(c) Section-pinned "vertical-to-horizontal."** A section pins to the viewport while the page scrolls, and the pinned section's content slides sideways in proportion to the vertical scroll distance. This is the GSAP ScrollTrigger recipe behind most Awwwards "horizontal scroll" entries: "designers want users to be able to scroll horizontally by scrolling vertically"; it uses `pin` + `scrub`, requires `ease: "none"`, and "pinning and snapping aren't available on containerAnimation-based ScrollTriggers" [maker's account: GSAP forums and docs]. The page gets *longer* by the width of the horizontal content, and for the duration of the pin the scroll input no longer moves the page. By NN/g's definition — the page "hijacks the scroll to choreograph an animation" — this is scrolljacking, confined to a band.
- **(b) Contained gallery.** A native `overflow-x` scroller with snap points inside an ordinary vertical page. Vertical input scrolls the page; horizontal input (swipe, shift-wheel, trackpad, buttons, keys) scrolls the gallery. Nothing is intercepted.

### 4.2 The evidence against (a) and (c)

- **NN/g, "Scrolljacking 101" (2024)** · [critique/research]. Disorientation across "multiple similar sites"; threats to "user control and freedom, discoverability, attention, efficiency, and task success"; the one licensed use, carried in brief 01 §2.2: an effect that "gradually adds supporting information to lessen cognitive load and support storytelling. When it does not serve this function, it is not worth it."
- **Voinea & Murano, "A Usability and Universal Design Investigation into Scrolljacking for Web Pages" (Springer, 2026)** · [critique/research — peer-reviewed, via indexed abstract]. Two prototypes, identical but for scrolljacking; 20 participants; task-driven. "No statistically significant difference in speed… a statistically significant difference in accuracy and user satisfaction, with the scrolljacking interface showing lower accuracy and user satisfaction scores." Conclusion: scrolljacking "negatively influences the usability and the Universal Design of a web page." This is the first controlled study on the question and it lands where NN/g's observational one did.
- **Murano & Pandey, "A Usability Investigation of Parallax Scrolling for Web Pages" (HCII 2024)** · [critique/research]. The nuance worth knowing: participants "were faster with the non-parallax web site" though not significantly, and "indicated stronger preferences for the parallax web site" — people *like* what slows them, which is exactly why preference cannot be the test. The authors still conclude that WCAG 2.2 and the Universal Design principles "give clear indication that in most cases parallax scrolling should be avoided."
- **Apple's Human Interface Guidelines — Scroll views** · [indexed: Apple developer page text; third-party mirror]. "Avoid putting a scroll view inside another scroll view with the same orientation, as doing so creates an unpredictable interface that's difficult to control. However, it's alright to place a horizontal scroll view inside a vertical scroll view (or vice versa)." The mirror's "Avoid" list: "Hijacking scrolling in ways that break momentum or expected behavior." The company whose marketing pages are the canonical scroll-hijack tells its own developers not to.
- **2026 trade retrospectives** · [critique]. Bubble: "Hijacking the scroll experience entirely — as opposed to reacting to it, the way scrollytelling does — is fading for a simple reason: it's slow." Jacob Tyler: scrolljacking is "the clearest offender" among 2026 trends, because "every frame competes with the browser's ability to react to a tap" and Interaction-to-Next-Paint "plummet[s]." Brief 02 §4.2 already carries the line that draws the boundary: scrollytelling "reacts to scroll position instead of taking control away from it — the whole difference between this and scroll-jacking, which is fading."

Pattern (c) inherits all of this. It also fails CiC's specific constraint outright: it *adds* page height equal to the gallery's width, pushing the chairs down by exactly the amount the gallery was supposed to save [inference].

### 4.3 The evidence on (b) — scrutiny, not exemption

- **NN/g, "Carousels on Mobile Devices" (Raluca Budiu, 19 August 2018)** · [critique/research]. Touch carousels "are plagued by low discoverability and sequential access, and not all designs implement swipe as a carousel control." "Swiping is still less discoverable than most other ways of manipulating mobile content, so we recommend including a visible cue when people can swipe." "Arrows on carousel designs provided extra insurance against swipe ambiguity (users could always choose to tap the arrow instead of swiping)."
- **NN/g, "The Illusion of Completeness"** · [critique/research]. "Crucial information does not belong in a carousel or any other horizontally scrolling container, as users often miss even obvious navigational cues." Remedies: "show additional content bleeding off screen," "a list of headlines… salient and obvious arrow controls and slide counts," "show only parts of an item… so incomplete items signal to users that there are more."
- **Baymard, homepage carousels** · [critique/research]. 52% of top US e-commerce desktop homepages and 56% of mobile ones carry a carousel; "46% of all homepage carousels… have performance issues"; users "scroll past carousels quickly or ignore carousel content entirely"; the recommendation is "to use the carousel as a complementary way to expose products, not the main or the only one," and only if its ten requirements are met. Note the scope: *homepage promotional* carousels competing with navigation — the "banner blindness" case brief 05 §4.1 already applies to the "What's new" box. A narrative gallery that *is* the section, with a heading and a count, is a different object; the caution still transfers to anything essential placed on panel four.
- **The design systems endorse the contained form as a component.** Material 3's carousel ships "four layouts: multi-browse, uncontained, hero (start- or centre-aligned), and full-screen"; "carousel items emphasize visuals, but can also contain brief text that adapts to the item size" [maker's account: material-components-android docs, fetched from GitHub]. Apple's HIG: "make scrollability apparent by letting content peek at the edge"; "for paging, snap to each page, keep a small overlap of context, and show a page indicator"; "if you show a page control with a scroll view, disable the scrolling indicator on the same axis" [indexed]. Neither system ships a scroll-hijack component.
- **Chrome's platform team built the contained pattern into CSS** — and wrote its accessibility in: "carousel best practices are handled by the browser, thanks to the engineering and accessibility teams working together. It'd be very difficult to make a more accessible carousel than this" (Adam Argyle, "Carousels with CSS," 20 March 2025) [maker's account, via indexed text].

### 4.4 Side by side

| | (a) Full-page takeover | (c) Section-pinned convert | (b) Contained gallery |
|---|---|---|---|
| What a vertical wheel/swipe does | Moves sideways or nothing | Moves sideways while pinned | Moves the page |
| Named narrative examples | Worth (2014); Museum of the World (2015); Van Gogh homepage (2020) | Most Awwwards "horizontal scroll" entries; State of AI 2025 (inferred from its tags) | TimelineJS; Shorthand Media Gallery; Apple product-page galleries; Instagram carousel |
| Usability record | NN/g 2005/2014/2017/2024; Voinea & Murano 2026 — negative | Same body of evidence applies | NN/g 2018 and Baymard — cautions with named remedies |
| Mobile | Desktop-first or desktop-only | Fragile (pin + `100vh` + toolbars) | Native; the pattern phones were built around |
| Effect on the path to the chairs | Destroys it (no "below") | Lengthens it by the gallery's width | Neutral to shorter (§9.3) |
| WCAG | 1.4.10 fails at page level; 2.3.3 (AAA) fails | 2.3.3 fails; 1.4.10 depends | 1.4.10 via G225; 2.5.7 with buttons; 2.1.1 with `tabindex` |
| Fits Design V2 §6.3 "scroll: native" | No | No | Yes, with a scoped snap change order |

The distinction is not a matter of degree. (a) and (c) break the contract that scrolling moves the page; (b) keeps it.

## 5. Question 3 — Touch versus trackpad and wheel

### 5.1 Desktop: reluctance, and the input problem

- **Reluctance is measured.** NN/g's 2010 eyetracking: "1% of fixations were to the right of the initially viewable area on the 1024px-wide monitor"; in the later study on larger monitors "none of the users observed used horizontal scrolling" ("Horizontal Attention Leans Left," 2017) [critique/research]. Sherwin's 2014 article is titled for the desktop specifically: "even as more sites mimic swiping gestures… users remain reluctant to move sideways through content."
- **Wheel input has no sideways.** A mouse wheel scrolls vertically; horizontal movement needs Shift-plus-wheel, a horizontal wheel, a trackpad, or a button. That is why the desktop version of any horizontal gallery lives or dies by its arrows — and why NN/g's USAToday users, offered arrows with no scent, ignored them [inference from the input model + NN/g 2014].
- **Trackpads have the opposite problem: too much sideways.** A two-finger swipe that reaches the end of a horizontal scroller can chain into the browser's Back gesture. `overscroll-behavior-x: contain` is the CSS answer, but WebKit bug 240183 records that in Safari it "does not disable history navigation," a developer reports the same on Windows 11 / Chrome 128 with a touchpad, and in April 2026 `web-features` *removed* the property's Baseline status: "the `overscroll-behavior` property does not work consistently across browsers when a scroll container has no overflow" — its computed support now reads Chrome 144 / Firefox 150 and no Safari [platform data: `overscroll-behavior.yml`, `.yml.dist`; WebKit Bugzilla via indexed text]. **Design consequence:** never let the gallery be the *last* thing a trackpad user is scrolling when the page ends, and never rely on `overscroll-behavior` alone — but a gallery in the middle of a vertical page, where a chained swipe merely continues sideways into nothing, is the low-risk placement.

### 5.2 Touch: the pattern the device was built around — with two hazards

- **Swipe is native but not discoverable.** NN/g 2018: "less discoverable than most other ways of manipulating mobile content… include a visible cue." Baymard, Apple's HIG and Material's "multi-browse" layout all converge on the same cue: *the next item peeks* [critique/research + indexed].
- **Hazard 1 — swipe ambiguity with system gestures.** NN/g, "iPhone X: The Rise of Gestures": "Horizontal swipe ambiguity was particularly problematic on web pages containing embedded carousels, where a horizontal swipe could mean Back or could move the carousel." Android's Chris Banes on gesture navigation: "common examples which can cause conflicts include navigation drawers, carousels (ViewPager), sliders"; "in general, swipeable views should be moved or padded away from the edges" [maker's account]. **Design consequence:** keep the column gutter on the gallery (brief 02's 20–24 px) rather than bleeding the *swipe surface* to the screen edge, and keep arrows as "insurance."
- **Hazard 2 — sequential access.** "To access the last element in the sequence, they must pass through all the ones preceding it" (NN/g). Six panels is the practical ceiling for a narrative sequence a thumb will finish; Instagram's own guidance culture treats "the first slide [as] your hook, determining whether users will engage with the rest" [industry claim]. **Design consequence:** six panels, the first fully visible, the last one the hand-off.
- **The muscle memory is real.** Instagram carousels average "1.92% engagement rate compared to 1.74% for Reels and 1.22% for single images" (Socialinsider, 2025) and are described by the trade as "the closest thing Instagram has to native storytelling" [industry claim — vendor and marketing blogs; reported, not relied on]. The vertical-screen era did not kill horizontal storytelling; it moved it *inside* the vertical feed. ONA's 2019 newsroom report calls this "tap to advance… sometimes referred to as 'horizontal storytelling'… on a vertically-oriented screen," popularised by Snapchat Stories (2013) and Instagram Stories (2016) [indexed]. The same era's *full-screen* tap-through format on the open web — Web Stories — is the one that retreated (§3.3).

### 5.3 Net

On a phone the contained gallery is the native form and needs only the cue, the gutter and the arrows. On a desktop it needs the arrows *and* a peeking panel to give the arrows scent, and it must not sit at the page's end. A full-page horizontal takeover is the worst of both: on desktop it fights the wheel; on a phone it fights the Back gesture and the toolbar-resizing viewport.

## 6. Question 4 — Keyboard, screen readers, and WCAG

### 6.1 The four criteria that decide it

| Criterion | What it says | Applied to a horizontal story |
|---|---|---|
| **1.4.10 Reflow (AA, 2.1)** | Content presentable "without requiring scrolling in two dimensions for vertical scrolling content at a width equivalent to 320 CSS pixels," except "parts of content which require two-dimensional layout for usage or meaning" (images, maps, diagrams, video, games, presentations, data tables). **Technique G225** is listed as sufficient: "although the entire section requires horizontal scrolling to navigate between panels, each panel is designed to fit within a fixed width of 320 CSS pixels." A qualifying region's exception "does not grant permission for the rest of the page to overflow" [critique/research: W3C Understanding docs and G225 via indexed secondary summaries]. | A contained gallery whose every panel is ≤ the column width passes by G225. A full-page horizontal site fails at page level. Design V2 **R-A1** ("no horizontal scroll at 320px… any page") is the page-level rule and stays; the verifier's zero-overflow check remains the gate. |
| **2.5.7 Dragging Movements (AA, 2.2)** | "All functionality that uses a dragging movement for operation can be achieved by a single pointer without dragging." Carousel "navigation through swipe-only on touch devices" is a named failure; the fix "preserves the dragging interaction… but adds… explicit 'Previous/Next' buttons"; the alternative "can't exclusively rely on a path-based gesture, such as swiping" (2.5.1) [critique/research: W3C Understanding 2.5.7 via indexed guides]. | **Buttons are mandatory.** The Storyboard's "touch-drag, no button needed" on the chair rows is, strictly, a 2.5.7 gap already — a native scroller *is* "determined by the user agent," which is the criterion's own exception, but a *designed* gallery whose only way forward is a swipe is not. Buttons resolve it either way. |
| **2.1.1 Keyboard (A)** | Every function operable from a keyboard. A scroll region with no focusable child "cannot be reached by keyboard" — the D2 review's exact finding [verified: repo]. | **Chrome 130 → shipped in 132** makes scrollers "keyboard focusable by default if they have no keyboard focusable children"; Firefox already did; **Safari does not** (WebKit bug 277290, "AX: Scrolling containers inoperable with keyboard"; Interop issue #762 with "no active signals" from Apple) [platform data: Chrome for Developers blog; WebKit Bugzilla; via indexed text]. The axe rule `scrollable-region-focusable` still flags it. **So:** `tabindex="0"` on the scroller, always — plus the buttons, which are focusable by nature. |
| **2.3.3 Animation from Interactions (AAA)** | "Motion animation triggered by interaction can be disabled, unless… essential." Parallax and scroll-driven motion are the named examples; `prefers-reduced-motion` is the primary technique (C39) [critique/research]. | Scroll-jacking (a) and (c) fail unless fully disabled under reduced motion. A contained gallery has no interaction-triggered animation beyond the scroll itself, which is user-initiated and essential. Design V2 **R-A9** already holds the site to 2.3.3. |

Two more that apply if the gallery misbehaves: **2.2.2 Pause, Stop, Hide** (auto-advancing slides need a pause control; brief 02 §4.3 already bans autoplay) and **2.4.3 Focus Order / 1.3.2 Meaningful Sequence** (DOM order must be the story's order, which a horizontal *layout* over a normal DOM satisfies by construction) [critique/research + inference].

### 6.2 What "accessible carousel" means in practice — the two W3C references

- **WAI tutorial, Carousels** · [critique/research, via indexed summaries]. "Provide structural markup to identify the carousel on the page and each individual slide; position a heading at the start of the carousel and, if slides contain text, a lower level heading at the start of each slide"; "use `<button>` elements"; "use a WAI-ARIA live region to inform screen reader users what item is currently shown… 'Item x of y'"; move actual focus with visual focus.
- **ARIA Authoring Practices Guide, Carousel pattern** · [critique/research]. Container carries `aria-roledescription="carousel"` and an accessible name; each slide is `role="group"` with `aria-roledescription="slide"` and a name, "e.g., '3 of 10'"; previous/next are real buttons; if there is auto-rotation there must be a visible rotation control and rotation "must stop when any element within the carousel receives keyboard focus or mouse hover."

Both are satisfiable with semantic HTML, three ARIA attributes, two buttons and one live region — and every one of them can be asserted by the DOM-walking verifier Design V2 §7.3 already specifies [inference].

### 6.3 What a screen reader actually experiences

A screen reader reads the DOM, not the layout. A contained gallery with six `<section>`s (or `role="group"` slides) in reading order is read as six headed sections — the horizontal arrangement is invisible to it, which is the point. What breaks screen readers is the takeover: libraries that reorder the DOM to fake position, `aria-hidden` on off-screen panels that are then unreachable, or focus that moves to something the layout has scrolled away. The APG's rule that focus and the visible slide move together is the whole discipline [critique/research + inference].

## 7. Question 5 — Of the moment, or 2012-era gimmick?

The record has three distinct phases, and the 2025–26 sources are unusually consistent about which part of the pattern survives.

**Phase 1 — the sin (2002–2005).** Horizontal scrolling was "one of the top-ten web design mistakes of 2002"; Nielsen's 2005 "Scrolling and Scrollbars": "Avoid horizontal scrolling on web pages and minimize it elsewhere" [critique/research].

**Phase 2 — the spectacle (2013–2015).** Mac Pro's hijack (2013); *Thirty Years of Mac*, *Akademie Zubrowka*, Worth's solar system (2014); *Museum of the World* (2015). The peak of the desktop-Flash-successor era, and the source of the "2012-era gimmick" association. siteIQ's assessment of Apple's later horizontal galleries reaches back to it: "horizontal scrolling has been vilified since its inception in the early 2000s… As stylish as Apple.com's presentation is, it still contains all the usability hang-ups that plagued this design approach since the beginning" [critique].

**Phase 3 — the revival, redefined (2021→).** "Previously regarded as a web design faux-pas, horizontal scroll is having a comeback" (2021 trend roundups); Webflow's 2021 rules for it: "don't force users to navigate through horizontal content, use clear visual cues to indicate where content uses horizontal scroll, and avoid requiring horizontal scroll for text that needs to be read" [critique]. Noble Desktop's 2026 list still names horizontal scrolling as a trend, dates its return to 2021, and states its terms: it "should not contain any vital text… should not be the only way to navigate through a webpage. It's more efficient to have horizontal scrolling on certain page areas while still providing additional ways to scroll through the website" [critique]. Read that last sentence again: it is a description of the contained gallery, offered as the *definition* of the trend.

**The 2026 read, source by source** [critique]:

- **Bubble, "What's in and what's out":** scroll-jacking out ("it's slow"); horizontal scrolling in "for portfolios and gallery websites, image carousels, timeline presentations, and feature showcases where visual browsing is the primary interaction, but not with text-driven sites"; the year's theme "functional minimalism… after years of heavy parallax, autoplaying video backgrounds, and scroll-jacking."
- **Lovable, "Scrolling Designs: 8 Patterns" (2026):** "Reserve horizontal scrolling for desktop-first, visually-driven applications where you have development resources for proper accessibility support… avoid it for text-heavy content, mobile-primary applications, and any context where accessibility is a concern."
- **Metabole Studio, "Horizontal Scroll: When This Bold Pattern Actually Works":** "a usability liability in the wrong hands and a signature move in the right ones."
- **Jacob Tyler, "2026 Web Design Trends Are a Core Web Vitals Problem":** scrolljacking "the clearest offender."
- **Creative Boom, "10 trends creatives are so over in 2026":** does *not* name horizontal scroll; its target is AI homogeneity — "an endless scroll of competent, hollow, interchangeable output." Reported so the absence is on record.
- **Numiko, museum sites 2026:** the Van Gogh homepage is "unusual"; the roundup's criterion is "clarity first, wonder second" (brief 05 §3.1).
- **Awwwards (brief 01 §4.4):** 3D took 61% of Site of the Day in Q1 2026; the horizontal-scroll portfolio remains a catalogued genre. The award circuit rewards it; the usability circuit does not; the trade retrospectives split the difference exactly along the takeover/contained line.

**Verdict.** In 2025–26 a *full-page or section-hijacking* horizontal narrative reads as Phase 2 — a 2013–15 spectacle, now named in the "out" lists and measured against Core Web Vitals. A *contained* horizontal gallery reads as neither fresh nor dated; it reads as a component — the same way a figure or a pull quote does — and its craft is judged on the cue, the arrows, the count and the images, not on the sideways motion itself. That is the right register for a site whose one flourish is the Arriving mark (brief 02, checklist item 5).

## 8. Question 6 — The hybrid, named: horizontal galleries inside vertical pages

This is the pattern the whole brief turns on, so each example is checked for the one property that matters — *the page below stays exactly as reachable as it was.*

**Shorthand, "Media Gallery" section** · [maker's account, via indexed text]. Shorthand is the platform behind a large share of newsroom and university long-form; its own launch note "Introducing Shorthand Media Galleries" describes "an entirely new media section type… to embed multiple images and video in a single section." The help centre: "places a grid or carousel of images and/or videos within your page… on mobile devices, readers can swipe through the images. If there are more images than will fit within the row, arrows at the right and left allow readers to click to progress through all the images"; the gallery "expands to a full-screen slideshow." A named use: Manchester City's June 2024 digital magazine "includes a Media Gallery section following the team through the streets on its celebration parade" (Shorthand, "The best visual stories of 2025"). **Property check:** by construction — a section type in a vertical scrollytelling document; swipe and arrows are the *only* horizontal input; the story continues beneath. **This is the editorial-storytelling industry's own answer to Mark's question.**

**Knight Lab, TimelineJS** · [maker's account]. An iframe embed; arrows; a draggable time axis; used at TIME, Le Monde, the Vancouver Sun, RadioLab, the Denver Post (the Pulitzer-winning Aurora package), La Nación (every pope) — always *inside* a vertical article. **Property check:** the iframe has a fixed height; the article below is unaffected. Its 2012 visual idiom is dated; its *architecture* — horizontal time inside vertical reading — has been validated a quarter of a million times.

**Apple product pages — the "design gallery"** · [critique + indexed]. Jon Lehman's recreation of the iPhone 14 page: "Apple calls [it] the 'design gallery' based on browser inspection… it stood out to me because of its elegant grid and snappy interaction," built with CSS Grid and scroll-snap and arrow controls. siteIQ's critique ("Apple.com takes on the horizontal scroll"): the pattern was "resurrected in the enterprise arena by Apple.com," and it "still contains all the usability hang-ups" — the specific one named being a vertical scrollbar on a page region that moves sideways. **Property check:** the gallery is a band in a long vertical page; everything below it is reached by scrolling past. **Take both halves:** the craft target (snap, grid, arrows, big imagery) and the critique (the region must *say* it scrolls sideways — a peek, a count, arrows with scent).

**Instagram's carousel post** · [industry claim + maker's-adjacent]. A horizontal swipe inside a vertical feed, with dots and a count; the highest-engagement format on the platform by the trade's own numbers (§5.2). **Property check:** the feed below is untouched. **Why it matters here:** it is the version of this pattern every visitor under sixty has performed thousands of times; CiC borrows the *gesture grammar*, not the aesthetic.

**The Van Gogh Museum's "stories"** · [maker's account]. "Short, engaging stories, reminiscent of Stories on Instagram and Facebook, which use images, animations, highlighted elements of artworks ('hotspots') and comparisons" — the museum sector's most-visited site tells its *narratives* in the contained-story idiom while reserving the sideways takeover for its browse homepage. **Property check:** the stories are pages; the pattern is the step-through, not the hijack.

**Apple HIG and Material 3** · [indexed + maker's account]. Both design systems define the hybrid as a normal component ("a horizontal scroll view inside a vertical scroll view" is "alright"; Material's four carousel layouts) and both prescribe the cues: peek, page indicator, snap, no same-axis nesting. **Property check:** that is what a component *is* — a bounded region with a height.

**Chrome's CSS carousel demos (chrome.dev/carousel)** · [maker's account]. Galleries with `::scroll-button()` and `::scroll-marker()` inside ordinary pages; markers are "literal `<a>` elements that scroll to a specific carousel item"; a button scrolls "roughly 85% of the scroll container's scrollport." **Property check:** by construction; and the accessibility is the browser's.

**CiC's own era rows** · [verified: repo]. Contained, pure CSS, verified at 320–1440 px, and already criticised for the three ways such a thing goes wrong (§2). The project has *built* the hybrid once; it has not yet built it *well*.

**What none of the named hybrids does:** carry the *only* copy of an essential sentence on a middle slide. Shorthand's galleries sit between paragraphs; TimelineJS sits under a headline and a standfirst; Apple's gallery illustrates a claim made in headline type above it. NN/g's "crucial information does not belong in a carousel" is honoured by every one of them by keeping the spine in the vertical flow [inference from the examples].

## 9. Synthesis — recommendation

### 9.1 The recommendation in one paragraph

**Use horizontal — as a contained gallery, never as a takeover.** Replace the four-paragraph story with a self-paced horizontal gallery of about six panels — one per era, each a real artifact (brief 02 §3: sourced, captioned with institution · object · date · licence, or a commissioned human illustration in one print idiom) with 30–60 words beside or beneath it — inside the normal vertical page, in the story's place. Keep the story's *spine* in the vertical flow: one or two sentences of Mark's copy above the gallery (the contract and the throughline: twenty centuries, modest homes to colonial fields, honest about the failures) and one sentence below it that hands to the chairs. The gallery scrolls only when the visitor swipes, drags, presses an arrow, or uses the keyboard; the page's own scroll is never intercepted, never pinned, never converted. If the D-work prototype fails any check in §9.4, the fallback is the image-led *vertical* scroll (Emergence's "traditional vertical scroll format with ample space for images"; *The Boat*) with brief 05's skip link — not a section-pinned convert, and not a full-page horizontal site.

### 9.2 Why this pattern and not the others

1. **It is the only option that passes the meaning test *and* the attention test.** Twenty centuries is a time axis, so sideways can carry meaning (Worth; Museum of the World; TimelineJS) — and a bounded gallery holds six images in the vertical space of one, so the chairs do not move down (§9.3). A vertical image-led scroll passes the first test and fails the second by roughly four to six screenfuls on a phone; a takeover fails the second absolutely.
2. **It keeps the one contract a page makes.** Scrolling moves the page (NN/g 2024; Voinea & Murano 2026; Apple's own HIG "avoid hijacking scrolling"). Brief 01 P3's "nothing hijacks the scroll" and Design V2 §6.3's "scroll: native" are preserved.
3. **It is the version of horizontal that reads as current.** Every 2026 retrospective that keeps horizontal scrolling keeps it *for galleries, contained, with other ways to move* (Bubble; Noble Desktop; Lovable); every one that lists an "out" lists the hijack.
4. **It is the version the accessibility standards were written to permit** (G225; 2.5.7 with buttons; 2.1.1 with `tabindex`; no 2.3.3 exposure) and the version the platform now builds natively (scroll snap Baseline since 2022; CSS carousels in Chromium; Chrome/Firefox focusable scrollers).
5. **It is what the editorial and museum worlds actually do** with a horizontal image sequence inside a story: Shorthand's Media Gallery, TimelineJS, the Van Gogh stories, Apple's galleries.

### 9.3 What it does to the path to the chairs [inference — arithmetic; confirm at 360, 390, 768 and 1280 px]

- **Phone (390 × 844).** The prose today: ~300 words at ~40 characters a line ≈ 55–60 lines at ~28 px ≈ 1,550–1,700 px ≈ two screenfuls, which is where brief 05's "chairs at screenful three or four" comes from. A gallery panel with a 4:5 artifact image at ~350 px wide (≈ 440 px), a three-line caption (~70 px), a control row (44 px) and the two spine sentences (~90 px) ≈ 650 px, about three-quarters of a screenful. **Net: the chairs move up by roughly a full screenful, to about screenful two.**
- **Desktop (1280 × 800).** The prose ≈ 650–750 px. A panel with a 3:2 image at the 44 rem section width (704 px wide ≈ 470 px tall) plus caption and controls ≈ 600 px. **Net: neutral to slightly shorter.** The chairs stay at about the second screenful — right at NN/g's 74% boundary, as now.
- **Either way, the skip link under the hook (brief 05 §7.1) stays**, for the visitor who arrives already knowing.

### 9.5 Where this touches frozen rulings (for `Decision-Log.md`, not this file)

- **§6.3 "Scroll — no snap."** Recommend a *scoped* amendment: snap permitted inside the story gallery's scroller only; page scroll stays native and unsnapped; reduced-motion behaviour unchanged (snap is positional, not animated). If Mark prefers to keep the ruling whole, `proximity` snapping is the softer option and *no* snap still works — the buttons then scroll by one panel width exactly.
- **§6.3 "instant" state changes.** Preserved: button-driven scroll is instant, not smooth.
- **§6.4 figures never fade or dim.** Preserved: no panel opacity effects; the peeking panel is simply partially in view, at full opacity.
- **R-A1 reflow.** Preserved at page level; the scroller's own overflow is the G225 case and should be *named* as such in the reflow rule so the verifier does not flag it.
- **Storyboard §1.3f / §1.9 chair rows.** Brief 05 §6.4 already recommends replacing the era strips with a grid/list. If the story gets a gallery, the page should not carry *two* horizontal scrollers of different kinds within a screenful of each other; the chairs' move to a grid becomes more, not less, advisable [inference].
- **Brief 05 §7.1's skip link** — unchanged and still recommended.

### 9.6 Alternatives considered and set aside

1. **Full-page horizontal story (Van Gogh-homepage style).** Rejected: the whole body of §4.2; NN/g's 1%-then-none; it removes "below," so the chairs, the Table, the Atlas and the ask have nowhere to be; it breaks P3 and §6.3 outright; desktop-first by nature.
2. **Section-pinned vertical-to-horizontal convert (the GSAP recipe).** Rejected: it is scrolljacking by NN/g's definition and Voinea & Murano's result; it lengthens the page by the gallery's width, the opposite of the goal; fragile on phones (pin + toolbar-resizing viewport, brief 02 §6.2); it needs a scroll-hijacking library the site does not otherwise carry.
3. **Full-screen tap-through "story" (Web Stories / Stories idiom) as the homepage's opening.** Rejected as the *homepage* form: Google's own retreat from the format on the open web; sequential access with no "below"; it would be the "explaining the experience instead of having it" screen Mark ruled out, in a new costume. Worth keeping in mind for a *shareable* social cut of the story later — a different artefact.
4. **Image-led vertical scroll (Emergence; *The Boat*; SCMP's Ozzy Osbourne piece).** The strong alternative and the fallback. Craft-safe, no new pattern, the register brief 01 recommends. Its one cost is the one this brief was asked about — four to six extra phone screenfuls between the hook and the chairs — and the skip link only partly repays it. If pilot data later shows visitors *want* to linger in the story rather than reach the chairs, this is the direction to return to.
5. **Keep the prose and add one hero artifact.** Cheapest; does not answer Mark's "much more visual, image-led" ask; noted only as the zero-risk baseline for A/B comparison in the sandbox.
