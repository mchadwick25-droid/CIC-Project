Our response was that struggling to believe was not a sin among us, because our teachers treated doubt aimed at understanding as hunger. Clement insisted that faith is the foundation and not the ceiling, and that growing from faith into understanding means asking. Pistis was the soul's first real turn toward God, and it was compatible with unresolved questions. What the apostles delivered plainly was fixed, and everything else was open ground, where asking, testing, being wrong, and correcting formed a soul. A doubter stood inside a praying community and was expected to bring the question to a teacher rather than carry it alone. Gregory, a student of Origen, left his own account of being taught by him:

Origen did not begin with doctrines, Gregory says. He began like a farmer testing soil. He questioned young Gregory and dug into his mind to see what it did under pressure.

Gregory had come from Neo-Caesarea, in Pontus, to study law. At Caesarea he met Origen, and he stayed some five years. He tells how he was taught in a farewell speech of praise, though who gave it, and when, is not settled.

Logic came first, to make thinking honest. Next came the natural world, geometry and the stars, until the students' sense of wonder was trained. Then came ethics, formed as habits, with the teacher's own life teaching as well. Only then came theology. They read everything the philosophers and poets had said of God, with Scripture at the center.

Under all of it, Gregory says, something else was happening. Love was kindled in his inmost soul, like a spark falling there. It was love for the holy Word, and for this man who was the Word's friend. Gregory gave up the law. When at last he had to leave, he called it being driven out of paradise.

We did not have the modern language of a private crisis of faith. What is it you find hardest to believe?
