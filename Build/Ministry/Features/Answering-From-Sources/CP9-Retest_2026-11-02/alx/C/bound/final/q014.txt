Among us, authority passed from proven wisdom, to the bishop's judgment, and at the end to the council. Early on, teachers argued with books, lectures, and letters, and authority went to proven wisdom. A didaskalos was a teacher whose authority rested on visible wisdom, not on a title. In the middle years the bishop's judgment grew, and Demetrius condemned Origen's ordination. The bishop governed how the community was formed and guarded what it had received. The teacher was trusted because others saw that he saw, while the bishop held the office handed down. At Nicaea, bishops from everywhere ruled on the disputed confession for all, and the emperor called the council and backed it with force. After Nicaea, the bishop of Alexandria became the enforcer of conciliar orthodoxy across Egypt, with exile, letter-networks, and synods as his tools. Our best picture of deciding well is Dionysius, a teacher who had become bishop, using patient public argument. Dionysius set down his own stance in a letter: “In many other respects I approve of Nepos and love him. I do so for his faith, his hard work and his diligence in the Scriptures, and for his many psalms. Many of our people still delight in them. And I hold him in all the more reverence because he has gone to his rest before us. But the truth must be loved and honored most of all.”. Our sayings tell of Bishop Dionysius and the Arsinoite conference:

The village churches near Arsinoe loved a bishop named Nepos. He had written a book against the allegorists. The promises in Scripture, he said, meant a thousand-year kingdom of plenty on this earth, plainly read. Nepos had died, and his book now ruled whole congregations. That book is lost. We know it only from the bishop who answered it.

Our bishop Dionysius went out to the villages. He told the story himself, in his book On the Promises, and Eusebius later quoted it. He called together the elders and the teachers, and any of the people who wished to come. For three days, from morning to evening, he sat with them and examined the book, argument by argument.

He wrote afterward that he loved Nepos for his faith, his hard work in the Scriptures and his psalms. Even so, he wrote, "the truth should be loved and honored most of all." He rejoiced in the constancy and sincerity of the believers, and in their willingness to reconsider as they weighed the arguments together.

At the end, Coracion, the leader of the other side, was persuaded. He said so in front of everyone. Some of the other believers, the account says, were glad at the spirit of agreement all had shown.
