Across our years, teachers settled disputed belief first, then the bishop, and at the end the council. Early on, teachers argued with books, lectures, and letters, and authority went to proven wisdom. In the middle years the bishop's judgment grew, and Demetrius condemned Origen's ordination. At Nicaea, bishops from everywhere ruled on the disputed confession for all, and the emperor who called the council backed it with force. Through all of this, the rule of faith, the short summary of what the apostles handed down, set the bounds for reading Scripture. In today's English, Origen of Alexandria says: “So, there are many who think they hold the opinions of Christ, and some of them think differently from those who came before. Yet the teaching of the Church is still preserved. It has been handed down in orderly succession from the apostles, and it remains in the churches to this day. So only that is to be accepted as truth which differs in no respect from the tradition of the Church and the apostles.”. Our best picture of deciding well is Dionysius at Arsinoe, where patient public argument loved the man and honored the truth more. Dionysius left his own account of that case:

The village churches near Arsinoe loved a bishop named Nepos. He had written a book against the allegorists. The promises in Scripture, he said, meant a thousand-year kingdom of plenty on this earth, plainly read. Nepos had died, and his book now ruled whole congregations. That book is lost. We know it only from the bishop who answered it.

Our bishop Dionysius went out to the villages. He told the story himself, in his book On the Promises, and Eusebius later quoted it. He called together the elders and the teachers, and any of the people who wished to come. For three days, from morning to evening, he sat with them and examined the book, argument by argument.

He wrote afterward that he loved Nepos for his faith, his hard work in the Scriptures and his psalms. Even so, he wrote, "the truth should be loved and honored most of all." He rejoiced in the constancy and sincerity of the believers, and in their willingness to reconsider as they weighed the arguments together.

At the end, Coracion, the leader of the other side, was persuaded. He said so in front of everyone. Some of the other believers, the account says, were glad at the spirit of agreement all had shown.. The arguments exchanged survive only in Dionysius's summary, and Nepos's book is lost. The villagers appear only in their bishop's own account of persuading them, never in their own voice.
