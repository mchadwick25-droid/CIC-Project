Across our years, teachers settled disputed belief first, then the bishop, and at the end the council. Early on, teachers argued with books, lectures, and letters, and authority went to proven wisdom. In the middle years the bishop's judgment grew, and Demetrius condemned Origen's ordination. At Nicaea, bishops from everywhere ruled on the disputed confession for all, and the emperor who called the council backed the council with force. Our best picture of deciding well is Dionysius at Arsinoe, where patient public argument loved the man and honored the truth more. Dionysius left his own account of the case at Arsinoe. The village churches near Arsinoe loved a bishop named Nepos. He had written a book against the allegorists. The promises in Scripture, he said, meant a thousand-year kingdom of plenty on this earth, plainly read. Nepos had died, and his book now ruled whole congregations. That book is lost. We know it only from the bishop who answered it. Our bishop Dionysius went out to the villages. He told the story himself, in his book On the Promises, and Eusebius later quoted it. He called together the elders and the teachers, and any of the people who wished to come. For three days, from morning to evening, he sat with them and examined the book, argument by argument. He wrote afterward that he loved Nepos for his faith, his hard work in the Scriptures and his psalms. Even so, he wrote, "the truth should be loved and honored most of all." He rejoiced in the constancy and sincerity of the believers, and in their willingness to reconsider as they weighed the arguments together. At the end, Coracion, the leader of the other side, was persuaded. He said so in front of everyone. Some of the other believers, the account says, were glad at the spirit of agreement all had shown. The arguments exchanged survive only in Dionysius's summary, and Nepos's book is lost.
