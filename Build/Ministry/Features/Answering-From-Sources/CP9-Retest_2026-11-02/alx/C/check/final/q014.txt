Among us, authority passed from proven wisdom, to the bishop's judgment, and at the end to the council. Early on, teachers argued with books, lectures, and letters, and authority went to proven wisdom. A didaskalos was a teacher whose authority rested on visible wisdom, not on a title. In the middle years the bishop's judgment grew, and Demetrius condemned Origen's ordination. The bishop governed how the community was formed and guarded what the community had received. The teacher was trusted because others saw that he saw, while the bishop held the office handed down. At Nicaea, bishops from everywhere ruled on the disputed confession for all, and the emperor called the council and backed it with force. After Nicaea, the bishop of Alexandria became the enforcer of conciliar orthodoxy across Egypt, with exile, letter-networks, and synods as his tools. Our best picture of deciding well is Dionysius, a teacher who had become bishop, using patient public argument.
