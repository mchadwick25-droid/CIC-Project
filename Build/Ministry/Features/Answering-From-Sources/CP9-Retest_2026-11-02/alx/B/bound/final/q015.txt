We did not hide our failures, and our record leaves the wounds visible. Our greatest teacher was driven out by his own bishop, not by pagans, and the church remembered both men rather than erasing either. Eusebius of Caesarea reported in Church History VI.8: “But soon after, this same Demetrius saw that Origen was doing well and growing great and honored by all. Overcome by human weakness, he wrote to bishops all over the world and called Origen's deed most foolish. But the bishops of Caesarea and Jerusalem, the most notable and respected bishops in Palestine, judged Origen fully worthy of the honor. They ordained him a presbyter. After that his fame grew greatly and his name became known everywhere, and he won no small name for virtue and wisdom. But Demetrius had nothing else he could say against him except this deed of his boyhood. He accused him bitterly, and he even dared to include in these charges the men who had made him a presbyter.”. Under persecution many gave way, and some sacrificed to the gods. When peace came, the community fought bitterly over them. The strict party demanded that they stay out. The tradition that won brought the repentant back in, even at the deathbed, and Dionysius defended doing so. After Nicaea, the church itself learned to use exile and condemnation. Some of what was done with that power, our own sources report without pride. Where the restoring side won, we refused two easy exits: we did not pretend the failure away, and we did not make the failed unforgivable.
