Among us, who settled a dispute changed over our years: first the teachers, then the bishop, and at the end the council. Early on, teachers argued with books, lectures, and letters, and authority went to proven wisdom. In the middle years, the bishop's judgment grew, and Demetrius condemned Origen's ordination. At the end, bishops from everywhere met at Nicaea and ruled on the disputed confession for all, with an emperor who called the council and backed it with force. All through those years, the rule of faith set the bounds within which questions about Scripture could range. Our best picture of deciding well is Dionysius at Arsinoe, and our harder picture is the same power with an empire behind it. Dionysius left his own account of that work, and we give it as he told it:

The village churches near Arsinoe loved a bishop named Nepos. He had written a book against the allegorists. The promises in Scripture, he said, meant a thousand-year kingdom of plenty on this earth, plainly read. Nepos had died, and his book now ruled whole congregations. That book is lost. We know it only from the bishop who answered it.

Our bishop Dionysius went out to the villages. He told the story himself, in his book On the Promises, and Eusebius later quoted it. He called together the elders and the teachers, and any of the people who wished to come. For three days, from morning to evening, he sat with them and examined the book, argument by argument.

He wrote afterward that he loved Nepos for his faith, his hard work in the Scriptures and his psalms. Even so, he wrote, "the truth should be loved and honored most of all." He rejoiced in the constancy and sincerity of the believers, and in their willingness to reconsider as they weighed the arguments together.

At the end, Coracion, the leader of the other side, was persuaded. He said so in front of everyone. Some of the other believers, the account says, were glad at the spirit of agreement all had shown.

That account is our main evidence for the process, and it is the bishop's own report of himself. The arguments exchanged survive only in his summary, and the villagers speak only through his report of their patience and honesty. The book he answered is lost, and we know it only through the man who refuted it. Dionysius also wrote of the late bishop Nepos: “In many other respects I approve of Nepos and love him. I do so for his faith, his hard work and his diligence in the Scriptures, and for his many psalms. Many of our people still delight in them. And I hold him in all the more reverence because he has gone to his rest before us. But the truth must be loved and honored most of all.”
