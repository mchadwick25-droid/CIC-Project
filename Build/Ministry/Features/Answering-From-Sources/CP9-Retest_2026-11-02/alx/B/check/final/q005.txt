Among us, who settled a dispute changed over our years: first the teachers, then the bishop, and at the end the council. Early on, teachers argued with books, lectures, and letters, and authority went to proven wisdom. In the middle years, the bishop's judgment grew, and Demetrius condemned Origen's ordination. At the end, bishops from everywhere met at Nicaea and ruled on the disputed confession for all, with an emperor who called the council and backed it with force. Our best picture of deciding well is Dionysius at Arsinoe, and our harder picture is the same power with an empire behind it. Bishop Dionysius left his own account of his three days with the village churches near Arsinoe, and we give it as Dionysius told it: The village churches near Arsinoe loved a bishop named Nepos. He had written a book against the allegorists. The promises in Scripture, he said, meant a thousand-year kingdom of plenty on this earth, plainly read. Nepos had died, and his book now ruled whole congregations. That book is lost. We know it only from the bishop who answered it. Our bishop Dionysius went out to the villages. He told the story himself, in his book On the Promises, and Eusebius later quoted it. He called together the elders and the teachers, and any of the people who wished to come. For three days, from morning to evening, he sat with them and examined the book, argument by argument. He wrote afterward that he loved Nepos for his faith, his hard work in the Scriptures and his psalms. Even so, he wrote, "the truth should be loved and honored most of all." He rejoiced in the constancy and sincerity of the believers, and in their willingness to reconsider as they weighed the arguments together. At the end, Coracion, the leader of the other side, was persuaded. He said so in front of everyone. Some of the other believers, the account says, were glad at the spirit of agreement all had shown. The arguments exchanged survive only in Dionysius's summary, and the villagers speak only through Dionysius's report of their patience and honesty. The book of Nepos that Dionysius answered is lost, and we know the book only through the man who refuted it.
