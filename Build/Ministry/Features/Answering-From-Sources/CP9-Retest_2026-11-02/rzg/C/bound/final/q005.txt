At Zurich, a claim was argued aloud until the council itself said what the text had yielded, and at Geneva doctrine was built up and taught to everyone through the Catechism. In both cities we held that Scripture alone could bind a church.

Six hundred people met in Zurich's Town Hall on 29 January 1523. The city council had summoned the meeting itself. Priests and laymen came from across the canton. Delegates from the bishop of Constance came as well. They met to test Zwingli's case against Scripture.

Enemies of the new preaching mocked the gathering. They called it a "tinker's day," and said that nothing but tinkers were attending.

That mockery moved a Zurich schoolmaster named Erhart Hegenwald to write an account of the day. He had sat through the whole disputation. "I was there myself and sat with them," he wrote, "heard and understood and remembered all that was said there."

When the arguments were finished, the council ruled for the reform. It ordered every priest in the canton to preach the same way.

Zurich's council governed church and city together. Geneva's Consistory fought for its independence from that kind of council control, and only by 1555 substantially won it. We never agreed between our two cities who holds the final word over church discipline. Calvin wrote: “By these governors I mean elders chosen from the people. They join with the pastors to declare censures and to carry out discipline.”. Against the Anabaptists, our own council turned to civil force after 1525, not argument alone. For the Marburg Colloquy, no account from anyone who was there has reached us, and for the Bolsec dispute we have only Calvin's own letters.
