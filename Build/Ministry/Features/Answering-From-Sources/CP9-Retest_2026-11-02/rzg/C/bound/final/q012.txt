Scripture alone was our authority, and no pope, council, or custom could set aside what it yields. That did not mean each believer decided doctrine alone from private study. In today's English, Huldrych Zwingli says: “I, Ulrich Zwingli, declare that I have preached the articles and opinions below in the good city of Zurich. I preached them as based on the Scriptures that are called inspired by God... Where my present understanding of these Scriptures is not correct, I will let myself be taught better. But I will be taught only from these same Scriptures.”. We tell of the city's first test of his case against Scripture:

Six hundred people met in Zurich's Town Hall on 29 January 1523. The city council had summoned the meeting itself. Priests and laymen came from across the canton. Delegates from the bishop of Constance came as well. They met to test Zwingli's case against Scripture.

Enemies of the new preaching mocked the gathering. They called it a "tinker's day," and said that nothing but tinkers were attending.

That mockery moved a Zurich schoolmaster named Erhart Hegenwald to write an account of the day. He had sat through the whole disputation. "I was there myself and sat with them," he wrote, "heard and understood and remembered all that was said there."

When the arguments were finished, the council ruled for the reform. It ordered every priest in the canton to preach the same way.. At Geneva the same conviction was built up article by article and taught to everyone through the Catechism. We read these as one authority, enacted twice, and not as one arrangement. Zurich's council governed church and city together, and Geneva's Consistory fought for its independence until about 1555.
