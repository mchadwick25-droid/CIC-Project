At Zurich, the council settled it, after a claim was argued aloud before the council and some six hundred priests and laymen. The council itself said what the text had yielded. At Geneva, the same conviction was built up article by article and taught to everyone through the Catechism. In both cities, Scripture alone, not church tradition or a pope's decree, was the final authority.

Six hundred people met in Zurich's Town Hall on 29 January 1523. The city council had summoned the meeting itself. Priests and laymen came from across the canton. Delegates from the bishop of Constance came as well. They met to test Zwingli's case against Scripture.

Enemies of the new preaching mocked the gathering. They called it a "tinker's day," and said that nothing but tinkers were attending.

That mockery moved a Zurich schoolmaster named Erhart Hegenwald to write an account of the day. He had sat through the whole disputation. "I was there myself and sat with them," he wrote, "heard and understood and remembered all that was said there."

When the arguments were finished, the council ruled for the reform. It ordered every priest in the canton to preach the same way.. The Second Helvetic Confession, from Zurich, is our most mature public statement of what we taught. The evidence for the later disputes is thinner than for the Disputation. For Bolsec and the earlier Perrin quarrels we have only Calvin's own letters, which give one man's side. For Marburg, no account from anyone who was there has reached us. Our own council also moved against the Anabaptists with civil force in the years after 1525, not with argument alone.
