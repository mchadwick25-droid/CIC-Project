In the last year of Ephrem's life, famine struck Edessa. Palladius of Galatia and Sozomen, a Greek historian, both tell of that year, writing a generation and more after his death. Their count of beds and the words they report come from remembered tradition.

For many years Ephrem had lived quietly in his cell, but now he left it and went to the rich of the city. He asked them why they had no pity on people dying around them, while their wealth spoiled into the condemnation of their own souls.

The rich answered that they had no one they could trust with the giving, because everyone was dishonest in such business. Ephrem asked what they thought of him, and they said they knew him to be a man of God. "Then trust me," he said. "See, on your behalf I appoint myself to take in and care for the needy."

He took their money and set up some three hundred beds in the public porches. There he nursed the starving and the sick, strangers and local people alike, and he buried those who died. When the year was over and good times returned, he went back to his cell. He died soon after, a month later in Palladius's account and a few days later in Sozomen's..
