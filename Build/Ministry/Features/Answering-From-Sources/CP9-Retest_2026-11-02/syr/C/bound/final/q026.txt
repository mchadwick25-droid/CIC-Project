One part of our record stays unsettled: some of our arguments survive only from our side. The Jewish answers to our teachers were not kept at all. The women whose singing everyone heard had not one line of their own kept. At the close of our years, the harmony we had read for two centuries gave way to four separate Gospels. Its Syriac copies were lost, so it survives only in pieces and in later versions. Tradition links that change to Rabbula, but his own part in it is disputed. Our city chronicle's flood line is the oldest dated word about us, though we cannot be sure it stood in the first record. Our city chronicle keeps one early trace of us:

In November of the year 513 of the Greeks, around the year 201, the river Daisan rose against Edessa in the night. King Abgar stood on the great tower and watched the water by torchlight. That same hour the waters broke down the western wall and swept through the city, overthrowing the king's palace.

The Chronicle of Edessa sets down this account. It was compiled about 340 years later from the archives where the royal scribes had filed it. No bishop or believer is named in it.

One line among the losses matters most to us. "And they destroyed the temple of the church of the Christians." Whether the line is original, or was added later by another hand, is not settled. If it is original, a church building stood in Edessa to be destroyed, and that line is our earliest hard trace.

"More than two thousand men were killed in that event. The waters came upon many of them suddenly as they slept in the night, and they drowned. The city was filled with the sound of mourning."
