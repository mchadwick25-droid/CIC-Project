No, we were not hiding in catacombs, which is another city's story. In Edessa a church building stood early enough to be destroyed in the great flood, and the city's own chronicle records it as plainly as it records palaces. On the frontier, believers gathered openly on the first day of the week. That chronicle line is the oldest dated word about us, though we cannot be sure it stood in the first record and was not added later. On the Persian side the danger was real: being known as a Christian made you a suspect, then you were taxed, then hunted. It fell hardest on clergy and on members of the covenant. When Rome's emperor became a Christian, the church on the Persian side paid for it, as the charge laid against Simeon shows. Of the church the flood destroyed, almost nothing is left, because the modern city sits directly on the ancient one. At Nisibis a baptistery still stands, and its own inscription dates it securely to the years of our bishop and the siege.
