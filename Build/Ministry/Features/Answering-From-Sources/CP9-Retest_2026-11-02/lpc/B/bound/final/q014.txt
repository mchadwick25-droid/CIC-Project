As we understand ourselves, a bishop's authority began with one plain fact: a particular people was placed in his care, and he would be asked what became of them. His flock could stand before his seat and refuse to leave until he heard them. Cyprian named two things that put a man in the office, our suffrage and God's judgment. We keep the account of how Cyprian came to the office:

Our deacon Pontius wrote our bishop Cyprian's life and chose one fact as proof enough of the good he did. In 248 Cyprian was chosen bishop of Carthage "by the judgment of God and the favour of the people." He had only recently been baptized, and he stepped back from the office, but a crowd of believers crowded outside the house where he was. For us, the people's favour was the way we saw God's judgment at work. In 251 Cyprian wrote to us that some presbyters had long hated him as bishop, which meant hating our choice and God's judgment.

A church already organized enough to hold factions was organized enough to carry an election over a faction's opposition. Authority between bishops ran another way, for Cyprian held that no bishop stands over another and each answers to Christ. Augustine held that small councils yield to wider ones, and that even the widest can be corrected. Augustine wrote about what the office is for: “Let it be the duty of love to feed the Lord’s flock, if it was the mark of fear to deny the Shepherd. Some feed the flock of Christ with this aim: to have the sheep as their own, and not as Christ’s. They are shown to love themselves, and not Christ. They act from the wish to boast, or to hold power, or to make gain. They do not act from the love of obeying, serving and pleasing God.”
