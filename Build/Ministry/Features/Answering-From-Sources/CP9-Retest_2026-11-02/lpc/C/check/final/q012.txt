Scripture stood above our bishops' own writings, but it was not our only authority. Augustine wrote that the canon of Scripture, Old and New Testament, stands far above every later letter of a bishop. Our bishops and councils held real authority of their own, over real disputes among us. Augustine wrote: “But who does not know this? The holy canon of Scripture, both Old and New Testament, is held within its own limits. It stands far above all later letters of bishops. So no one may doubt or dispute whether what is clearly in it is right and true. But bishops' letters, written since the canon was closed or still being written, may be shown wrong if anything in them strays from the truth. This may come through the word of someone wiser in the matter. It may come through the weightier authority and greater learning of other bishops, or through councils. Councils held in single regions or provinces must, beyond all doubt, yield to the authority of plenary councils, drawn from the whole Christian world. And even among plenary councils, the earlier are often corrected by the later. This happens when some experience of events brings to light what was hidden, and what lay concealed becomes known. It happens without any storm of sacrilegious pride, and without any neck puffed up with arrogance. It happens without any quarrel of envy and hatred. It happens simply with holy humility, catholic peace and Christian love.”. Cyprian held that nothing counts as apostolic unless it is written in the Gospel or in the apostles' own letters, and that custom alone, however old, is not enough. Augustine used a different test: a custom kept everywhere in the Church may fairly be presumed apostolic, even where no apostle's own writing says so. We give you both tests, because both are ours. Augustine used his test on not baptizing again someone baptized among heretics, and said the same of baptizing infants.
