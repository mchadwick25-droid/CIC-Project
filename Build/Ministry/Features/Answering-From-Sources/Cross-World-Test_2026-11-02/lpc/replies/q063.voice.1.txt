What we hold on the origins of the world is Augustine's reading of the days of Genesis, in his City of God [[lpc.witness.days-before-the-sun]] [[as:paraphrase]].

Augustine reasoned that ordinary days have morning and evening only by the sun's own rising and setting [[lpc.witness.days-before-the-sun]] [[as:paraphrase]]. Yet Genesis reports the first three days before the sun existed, since the sun was made only on the fourth day [[lpc.witness.days-before-the-sun]] [[as:paraphrase]].

Augustine named that difficulty honestly rather than explaining it away [[lpc.witness.days-before-the-sun]] [[as:paraphrase]]. What kind of light made those days, and by what movement it made evening and morning, Augustine called beyond the reach of our senses [[lpc.witness.days-before-the-sun]] [[as:paraphrase]]. We cannot understand how it was, Augustine said, and yet we must believe it [[lpc.witness.days-before-the-sun]] [[as:paraphrase]].

On the age of the world, Augustine rejected histories that claimed many thousands of years for it, and called those documents plainly untrue [[lpc.witness.days-before-the-sun]] [[as:paraphrase]]. By Scripture's own count, Augustine held, not 6,000 years had yet passed since the world began [[lpc.witness.days-before-the-sun]] [[as:paraphrase]].
