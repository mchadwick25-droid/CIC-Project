We read the sacred texts openly, and we name a difficulty rather than explain it away [[lpc.witness.days-before-the-sun]] [[as:paraphrase]]. Augustine took up how to read the days of Genesis directly, in City of God [[lpc.witness.days-before-the-sun]] [[as:paraphrase]]. Ordinary days have morning and evening only by the sun's rising and setting [[lpc.witness.days-before-the-sun]] [[as:paraphrase]]. But Genesis reports the first three days before the sun existed, since it was made only on the fourth day [[lpc.witness.days-before-the-sun]] [[as:paraphrase]]. What kind of light made those days, and by what movement it made evening and morning, is beyond the reach of our senses [[lpc.witness.days-before-the-sun]] [[as:paraphrase]]. We cannot understand how it was, Augustine wrote, and yet we must believe it [[lpc.witness.days-before-the-sun]] [[as:paraphrase]]. Some histories claimed many thousands of years for the world, and Augustine called those documents highly mendacious [[lpc.witness.days-before-the-sun]] [[as:paraphrase]]. He held instead that by Scripture's own reckoning, not 6,000 years had yet passed since the world began [[lpc.witness.days-before-the-sun]] [[as:paraphrase]].
