Record lpc.force.decian-persecution-libelli-system [formation confidence: Documented]
(1) The Decian Persecution and the *libelli* System (250) The Decian persecution was the first empire-wide persecution to be enforced in a planned way.
(2) It worked through certificates recording that the holder had sacrificed.
(3) It did not mainly demand that Christians give up their faith.
(4) It demanded a recorded act of compliance.
(5) A person could get one by performing the sacrifice, or by paying to have it recorded.
(6) This is documented in Cyprian's crisis correspondence, which carries the higher citation grade (A), and in his De Lapsis, which carries the lower grade (B).
(7) It was felt less as an attack from outside than as a table emptied one certificate at a time.
(8) The demand reached each person alone.
(9) It sorted the congregation into those who had stood and those who had not.
(10) Both groups still belonged, still in the room.
(11) Cyprian wrote that when the flock is wounded, it is the shepherd who is wounded most.
(12) This force made common the group that the whole penitential system exists to deal with.
(13) In one year, many lapsed and many confessors stood in one congregation.
(14) The certificate was a new way to fall.
(15) The pattern was not new, though.
(16) Tertullian, in On Modesty, already tells of sinners who ask imprisoned martyrs for peace.
(17) So the force produced two things at once.
(18) One was the ongoing contest over members who had failed, and the discipline for restoring them.
(19) The other was the confessors' rival claim to grant peace, which pushes against it.
(20) It also sharpened the bishop's answerability, which is sharpest when his people fail.
(21) And it gave this world its densest crisis vocabulary: the lapsed, reconciliation, confessor, and the certificates of both kinds.
(22) The later Valerianic persecution repeated this test.
(23) By then the community had built a discipline to meet it.

Record lpc.force.confessors-claim-to-grant-peace [formation confidence: Documented]
(1) The Confessors' Claim to Grant Peace People who survived interrogation without denying their faith issued written requests that named lapsed persons be received back.
(2) Cyprian wrote that thousands of certificates were being given every day, against the law of the Gospel.
(3) This is documented.
(4) Both sides spoke in earnest, and that was the difficulty.
(5) One side said: I stood before the magistrate and did not deny Him, and I say this man may come back.
(6) The other answered: the peace of the church is not any man's to give out of his own suffering, however real.
(7) This force is the one tension of its kind in this world's record: the confessors' authority set against the peace that the bishop regulates.
(8) It also explains why the penitential discipline takes such an insistent, often-restated form.
(9) Penance was not made in a vacuum.
(10) It was built against a working parallel system that already circulated documents on a large scale.
(11) That parallel system is why the contest over the lapsed had to be settled by a formal process, not by a bishop's word alone.
(12) The Decian edict made confessors a large class with a claim.
(13) The force belongs to Cyprian's phase alone.
(14) That limit rests on a check, not on an assumption from silence.
(15) The stem "confessor" appears twenty times across all eight Augustine volumes in the project's library, and never in this sense.
