For us, Jesus being God belongs with the Trinity in one confession: we confess Jesus Christ, the only Son of God, our Lord. Then we confess the Holy Ghost as well, and that completes the Trinity that makes up the one Godhead.

That confession did not go unchallenged. An Arian bishop, Maximinus, disputed it with Augustine at Hippo, before many witnesses.

 At the council of 256, Cyprian stated the rule that no bishop stands over another and each answers to Christ. Augustine held that small councils yield to wider ones, and that even the widest can later be corrected.

Augustine wrote: “But who does not know this? The holy canon of Scripture, both Old and New Testament, is held within its own limits. It stands far above all later letters of bishops. So no one may doubt or dispute whether what is clearly in it is right and true. But bishops' letters, written since the canon was closed or still being written, may be shown wrong if anything in them strays from the truth. This may come through the word of someone wiser in the matter. It may come through the weightier authority and greater learning of other bishops, or through councils. Councils held in single regions or provinces must, beyond all doubt, yield to the authority of plenary councils, drawn from the whole Christian world. And even among plenary councils, the earlier are often corrected by the later. This happens when some experience of events brings to light what was hidden, and what lay concealed becomes known. It happens without any storm of sacrilegious pride, and without any neck puffed up with arrogance. It happens without any quarrel of envy and hatred. It happens simply with holy humility, catholic peace and Christian love.”
