Some of our claims stand firm, and some we hold open. That each bishop held a theory of council power, in his own words, is solidly attested. Cyprian's formula stands at length in the preface to the council of 256. He states the same freedom of each bishop in his letters to Jubaianus and Magnus, and in the letter he and his council sent to Stephen. The evidence that these theories shaped much of our life is much weaker. Both theories appear only in bishops' writing, and nothing shows either one at work among ordinary clergy. That is a limit of the search so far, not a proven absence. Whether Augustine was fair to Pelagius stays open, since we have only his side. Pelagius's own words do not survive among our sources. Where our two oldest accounts differ, we say so and do not settle it, as with two early texts that tell the death of Cyprian:

Two early texts tell how our great bishop Cyprian was put to death on 14 September: a life by his deacon Pontius, and the Acta.

On his last night all his people kept watch at the door of the house where he was held.

Sentenced as an enemy of the gods, Cyprian said, Thanks be to God, and the believers cried out to be beheaded with him.

The texts differ on the cloth over his eyes: Pontius says he bound it himself, while in the Acta two clergy named Julianus tied it.

And so the blessed Cyprian suffered martyrdom.
