Among us a person who failed was met with a road back, walked in public and in steps under the bishop. The road had an order, the order took time, and everyone could see it.

The Decian persecution made failure common, through certificates that recorded the holder had sacrificed. A person could get one by performing the sacrifice, or by paying to have it recorded. The persecution sorted the congregation into those who had stood and those who had not, and both groups still belonged. Cyprian wrote that when the flock is wounded, the shepherd is wounded most.

Confessors issued written requests that named lapsed persons to be received back. The contest over the lapsed had to be settled by a formal process, not by a bishop's word alone.

 Cyprian wrote to his presbyters and deacons: “I think we must come to the help of our brothers in this way. Some have received letters of peace from the martyrs, and may be helped by the martyrs' standing with God. If misfortune and the danger of sickness seize them, they should not wait for me to be present. They should be able to confess their sin before any presbyter who is there. If no presbyter can be found and death begins to draw near, they may confess before even a deacon. Then hands are laid on them for repentance. In this way they may come to the Lord with the peace the martyrs asked for them in their letters to us.”

Augustine wrote to Honoratus, a bishop in Mauritania, in 428 or 429. When danger fell on all alike, Augustine held, ministers must not leave those who depend on them, so that the dying can be baptized and reconciled. Augustine also held that when someone who has sinned against you asks forgiveness, and you forgive from the heart, that much fulfils what the Lord's Prayer asks.

We can tell you what was decided about a person, how carefully, and by whom. What an ordinary believer felt walking up to the altar is not ours to give you.
