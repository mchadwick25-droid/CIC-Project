Yes, our teachers left room for doubt, and our teachers built their whole method on questions. Clement insisted that faith is the foundation and not the ceiling. We treated doubt aimed at understanding as hunger, and we did not count doubt of that kind as sin. Faith, our word pistis, is the soul's first real turn toward God, and faith is compatible with unresolved questions. Our doubters stood inside a praying community, questioned inside the rule of faith, and were expected to bring the question to a teacher. What the apostles delivered plainly is fixed, and everything else is open ground. Our record keeps Gregory's own account of how Origen taught him. Origen did not begin with doctrines, Gregory says. He began like a farmer testing soil. He questioned young Gregory and dug into his mind to see what it did under pressure. Gregory had come from Neo-Caesarea, in Pontus, to study law. At Caesarea he met Origen, and he stayed some five years. He tells how he was taught in a farewell speech of praise, though who gave it, and when, is not settled. Logic came first, to make thinking honest. Next came the natural world, geometry and the stars, until the students' sense of wonder was trained. Then came ethics, formed as habits, with the teacher's own life teaching as well. Only then came theology. They read everything the philosophers and poets had said of God, with Scripture at the center. Under all of it, Gregory says, something else was happening. Love was kindled in his inmost soul, like a spark falling there. It was love for the holy Word, and for this man who was the Word's friend. Gregory gave up the law. When at last he had to leave, he called it being driven out of paradise. Gregory's account is one gifted pupil's, not the ordinary Alexandrian catechumen's. Our teachers' sources barely show how ordinary doubters themselves felt.
