Why it abstains more

The read strikes the new plain opening sentence more often. A direct answer in the world's voice (for example, "God is the giver first") says more than the one record it is bound to, so the read calls it added detail or widened scope. q012 Was scripture your community's sole source of authority, or were there others?

Stage 3

grade 8.9 · ease 62.4 · 18.2 words per sentence

First sentence: answers directly yes, plain to hear yes

Scripture stood above our bishops' own writings, but it was not our only authority. Augustine held that the canon of Scripture, Old and New Testament, stands far above every later letter of a bishop, and that about what it contains there can be no doubt or argument. A bishop's own letter can be corrected, and a council can be corrected by a later, greater council, but Scripture cannot be corrected in that way at all. Augustine wrote: “But who does not know this? The holy canon of Scripture, both Old and New Testament, is held within its own limits. It stands far above all later letters of bishops. So no one may doubt or dispute whether what is clearly in it is right and true. But bishops' letters, written since the canon was closed or still being written, may be shown wrong if anything in them strays from the truth. This may come through the word of someone wiser in the matter. It may come through the weightier authority and greater learning of other bishops, or through councils. Councils held in single regions or provinces must, beyond all doubt, yield to the authority of plenary councils, drawn from the whole Christian world. And even among plenary councils, the earlier are often corrected by the later. This happens when some experience of events brings to light what was hidden, and what lay concealed becomes known. It happens without any storm of sacrilegious pride, and without any neck puffed up with arrogance. It happens without any quarrel of envy and hatred. It happens simply with holy humility, catholic peace and Christian love.” Cyprian, arguing a different dispute a century and a half earlier, held a kindred conviction, that custom without truth is only the old age of error. Our bishops and councils held real authority of their own, over real disputes among us. The confessors held that their suffering gave them standing to ask peace for the lapsed, and the church's rules answered them. The confessors' claim and the church's rules never became one settled rule among us.

First sentence: answers directly partly, plain to hear no

We met a failure with a public road back, walked in steps under the bishop. In one year of persecution, many lapsed and many confessors stood together in one congregation. Cyprian wrote that when the flock is wounded, it is the shepherd who is wounded most. Confessors, who had been questioned and had not denied the faith, wrote requests that named lapsed persons be received back. Those who opposed the confessors' requests answered that the peace of the church is not any man's to give out of his own suffering, however real. So the contest over the lapsed had to be settled by a formal process, not by a bishop's word alone. Cyprian wrote to his presbyters and deacons about the sick who held a martyr's letter: “I think we must come to the help of our brothers in this way. Some have received letters of peace from the martyrs, and may be helped by the martyrs' standing with God. If misfortune and the danger of sickness seize them, they should not wait for me to be present. They should be able to confess their sin before any presbyter who is there. If no presbyter can be found and death begins to draw near, they may confess before even a deacon. Then hands are laid on them for repentance. In this way they may come to the Lord with the peace the martyrs asked for them in their letters to us.” Augustine, in a letter of 428 or 429, said ministers must not leave behind people who depend on them in a time of danger.

The clearest physical evidence of our gathering place comes to us through a letter, not from a trench. Augustine's Letter CXXVI to Albina, written in 411, describes an apse, a raised seating area for the clergy, steps, and a floor for the congregation. Site reports and inscription collections for our region exist, but none of the site reports or collections has yet been read for what the site reports or collections show of our worship.
