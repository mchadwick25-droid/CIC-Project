Medium · 184 words

Celerinus, who had confessed Christ, wrote to Lucian, another confessor. It seemed plain that his sister had sacrificed and fallen from Christ. He asked Lucian to grieve with him, for to him she was dead in spirit, and he would weep until help came through Lucian or the martyrs.

Two other women, Numeria and Candida, had sinned greatly but repented and done good works, so he asked the martyrs to pardon them.

Yet Candida had given gifts so as not to sacrifice. She seems to have turned back at the Tria Fata, on her way to sacrifice, so he knew she had not sacrificed.

Lucian answered from prison, grieving over so great a crime. Though they were to die there of hunger and thirst, he wrote, they had now reached the brightness itself. Before he died, the martyr Paulus had told him to grant this pardon, called peace, in his name to anyone who asked.

He granted it in the name of Paulus and the other martyrs if each case went before the bishop and confession was made. Then the women would have peace.

Long · 613 words

Celerinus, who had confessed Christ, was one of our confessors at Rome. Lucian, one of the Carthaginian confessors, was in prison, and not one letter had come from him since the two were last together.

Then news reached Rome that Lucian had been tried and had confessed Christ before the magistrates. Celerinus was glad, and he wrote asking Lucian to pray that Celerinus too might be worthy to be crowned as a martyr with him.

But Celerinus was in great trouble too. His sister had fallen away from Christ, and it seemed plain to him that she had sacrificed and angered our Lord. To Celerinus she was dead in spirit, and he asked Lucian to grieve with him.

It was Easter, a time of joy, yet Celerinus wept day and night in sackcloth and ashes. He would go on weeping until the Lord came to her help, through Lucian or the martyrs who had already died.

Two other women weighed on him, Numeria and Candida, whom Lucian knew. They too had sinned, but they had repented and done good works for our banished colleagues. So Celerinus trusted that Christ would have mercy on them if Lucian and the other confessors asked Him.

He begged Lucian to take the case to the other confessors. Whichever of them was first to die as a martyr should forgive the two women their great sin.

With Candida it was not so simple, for she had given gifts so that she would not have to sacrifice. She seems to have gone only as far as the Tria Fata, a spot on her way up to sacrifice, and then come back down. So Celerinus knew she had not sacrificed.

The clergy in charge had lately heard both cases and told the women to wait as they were until a new bishop was appointed.

Statius, Severianus and the other confessors who had come from Lucian joined the plea. The two women had met them at the harbour and brought them into the city. They had cared for sixty-five.

When Lucian answered from his prison, he said the letter had filled him with so much joy that it almost overcame him. Lucian had confessed trembling, before only the lesser magistrates. But when Celerinus confessed, Lucian said, his words had driven back the emperor himself.

Lucian grieved for the women too, and wished he could speak of them without remembering so great a crime.

He explained why he could grant the pardon, which the confessors called peace. Before the blessed martyr Paulus died, he called Lucian to him. He said, "If any one, after my being called away, shall ask for peace from you, grant it in my name." The confessors dying with Lucian had all agreed, and their letters had given peace to all.

By the emperor's command they were to die of hunger and thirst, shut up in two cells to wear them down. The fire of their torture was more than anyone could bear. He went on, "But now we have attained the brightness itself."

So Numeria and Candida would have peace by the order of Paulus and the other martyrs, and Lucian listed those martyrs by name. Many had already died of hunger in that prison, and he expected to join them within a few days.

But he set one condition: each case was first to be set before the bishop, and confession made. Then he asked that these women have peace, along with all whom Celerinus knew to be dear to the confessors.

He sent greetings to many by name but left out the rest because he was already weary, and he asked them to forgive him.

Medium · 183 words

Our bishop Cyprian was executed by the sword, and afterward his deacon, Pontius, recorded his life. There was too much to tell, so he chose one fact as proof enough of the good Cyprian did.

In 248 Cyprian was chosen bishop of Carthage "by the judgment of God and the favour of the people." He had only recently been baptized, and people considered him a novice.

When the whole people rushed forward to honour him, he humbly stepped back and gave way to older men. Even so, a crowd of believers surrounded the house where he was.

In our own reading, the people's favour was the way we saw God's judgment at work.

By 251 certain presbyters were renewing their old attack on him, so Cyprian wrote to us. These men had long hated him as bishop, he wrote, and that meant they hated our choice and God's judgment too.

Our great bishop Cyprian was put to death on 14 September, in the year 258, and two early texts tell how he died. One is a saint's life by his deacon, Pontius, who was with him on his last night. The other is the Acta, a Christian compilation built around the records of his hearings.

The proconsul sent an officer with soldiers to take Cyprian at his gardens, but the hearing was put off until the next day. So Cyprian spent one night under guard in the officer's house, and his friends stayed with him as usual, with Pontius among them.

Outside, all his people kept watch before the officer's door, because they wanted nothing done in the night without their knowing. When Cyprian learned that all the believers were out in the street, he had the young women guarded.

The next day dawned bright and clear, and as Cyprian came out of the house, a crowd closed in around him on every side. A great crowd also gathered at the place of Sextus, as the proconsul Galerius Maximus had ordered.

At the court the proconsul had not yet come out, so Cyprian sat down to wait. He was soaked with sweat after the long journey, and an officer who had once been a Christian offered him dry clothes. Cyprian replied that there was no point easing a discomfort that would probably be gone that day.

He was brought in and asked if he was Thascius Cyprian, and he said he was. In the Acta, the proconsul told him the emperors had ordered him to worship the gods, but Cyprian said he would not. When he was told to think of his own good, he said the matter was so clearly just that there was nothing to decide.

Both texts give the sentence in nearly the same words. The proconsul spoke with his council, then named Cyprian a standard-bearer of the Christian sect and an enemy of the gods. He would be a warning to his own people, and his blood would uphold the law. The proconsul read from a tablet that Thascius Cyprian must die by the sword, and Cyprian said, Thanks be to God.

At that, the crowd of believers kept crying out to be beheaded with him too. An uproar rose, and a great crowd followed him. Pontius calls these people blessed, because they shared in their bishop's suffering by speaking out.

He was led out to the field of Sextus, where the ground was level, with trees planted thickly all around. People at the back could not see, so they climbed into the branches. Pontius says Cyprian was watched from the trees, as in the story of Zacchaeus.

In the Acta, Cyprian took off his cloak, knelt, and bowed down in prayer to the Lord. He gave his tunic to the deacons and stood in his linen garment, waiting for the executioner. When the executioner came, Cyprian had his people give the man twenty-five gold pieces. The believers laid linen cloths and hand towels in front of Cyprian.

Here our two texts differ, and we do not choose between them: Pontius says Cyprian blindfolded himself with his own hands. The Acta says he covered his eyes with his hand but could not tie the cloth strips himself. So the presbyter Julianus and the subdeacon Julianus tied them for him.

Pontius tells the last moment his own way. Cyprian tried to hurry the slow executioner, whose fingers shook so that he could barely grip the sword. Then the time came for Cyprian's glorious death, and power from above steadied the man's hand.

And so the blessed Cyprian suffered martyrdom.

Possidius, a bishop, was Augustine's friend for nearly forty years, and he was there at the end.

In those last years, the Vandals crossed the sea from Spain, together with the Alans and some Goths, and they invaded Africa. They burned, robbed and killed across the provinces, and they spared no one, young or old, not even priests. They did not spare the churches either. Augustine wept over it every day.

Then the same enemy came to besiege Hippo itself, where Count Boniface was defending the city with an army of Goths under his command. The siege went on for almost fourteen months, and the enemy blocked the city from the sea as well.

Possidius and other bishops from nearby took refuge in the city, and they stayed through the whole siege. Often they talked together about the ruin in front of them, and they saw it as God's judgment, terrible but just. In their shared grief they wept and prayed to God to hold them up through it.

One day, as they sat at table, Augustine told them what he was asking of God. He asked God to free the city, or, if God willed otherwise, to make the people who served Him brave enough to bear it. If not, he asked at least that God would take him from this life. The bishops joined him in praying the same prayer.

In the third month of the siege, Augustine came down with the fever that would be his last illness.

He had lived seventy-six years, and for almost forty of them he had served as a priest or bishop. In private conversation he often told his friends that even after baptism, good Christians and priests should not die without proper repentance.

Now, in his own last illness, he did what he had taught. He had the shortest of the psalms of repentance copied out, and the sheets were hung on the wall. Through the days of his sickness he lay in bed looking at them and reading them, and he wept "freely and constantly."

About ten days before the end, he asked those with him to let no one in, so that no one would distract him. People could come in only when the doctors came to examine him, or when his food was brought. They did as he asked, and he had all that time free for prayer.

Until that last illness, he never stopped preaching in the church, and he preached with force, with a clear mind and sound judgment.

At the end his body was still sound, and so were his sight and hearing. He died while Possidius and the others stood by, watching and praying. With his friends present, a service was offered to God for him, and then he was buried.

Prosper also recorded the death in his chronicle, in the year we count as 430, on 28 August. He adds that at the very end of his days, while the Vandals pressed their attacks on the city, Augustine was still answering books by a man named Julian.

Augustine left no will, because he was a poor man of God and had nothing to leave. Again and again he had given one order: the church library and all its books must be carefully kept for those who came after.
