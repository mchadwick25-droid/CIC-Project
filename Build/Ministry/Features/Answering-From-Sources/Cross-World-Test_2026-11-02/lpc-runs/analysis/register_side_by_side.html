The generations did change. Stage 3 sentences are about six words longer and about three grades harder, and many more open on a name. First sentences from a blind Opus rater (it did not know which version each came from).

Your hypothesis: it does not answer directly

The data points elsewhere. Stage 3’s first sentences answer the question no less often than the old ones (3 yes and 5 partly, against 2 and 3, with no outright misses). What changed is how they sound: only 3 of 8 are plain to hear, against 7 of 8 before. They run long, carry a “because” clause, and open on a name.

Across the 30 answers Opus read, first sentences answer yes 12, partly 13, no 4, plus 1 abstention. Opus’s own “addresses the question” notes for the same 30 say yes 13, partly 13, no 4. The two counts agree. So about 40% answer fully in the first sentence, and about 43% answer only in part. That part of your idea is also true, just a smaller share of the problem. In the 30, 16 first sentences are not plain to hear.

Which directive lines push toward formal

LineEffect
“name the person or thing in every sentence; never use he, she, it, they, this”Strongest. Every sentence opens on a name or “X says”. Named openers rise from 15% to 37%; pronouns fall from 23% to 5%. It reads like a report, not a community speaking.
“closely restates what those records say, in the author’s name”Strong. It tells the voice to report what an author said, which fights the lpc voice (“we, our, among us”).
“keep the hedge, concession and date of the bound records”Moderate. It adds clauses and lengthens sentences.
“every sentence bound to a record”Moderate. No room for a plain opening line or a connecting thought.
“add no person, place, title, date or detail”Weak. It limits content more than tone.

A plain-writing clause alone (12 to 20 words, one idea each, everyday words) fixes the length and the grade: 8.1 and 66.3 on all 28 sealed drafts, against 9.9 and 62.3. It does not touch the naming habit: named openers stay at 40%.

Proposed change at the source

The first sentence answers the question plainly, in the world’s own voice (we, our, among us), short, with no author’s name needed. The sentences after it give the evidence and the texture, each bound to its record. Where the sources do not answer directly, the first sentence says what the world does hold on the matter, in plain words. It is not a hedge and not a limit.

Alongside it: a sentence may say “we” or “our” as its subject, and “name the person” would apply only where the sentence could otherwise be read for the wrong person. A plain-writing clause (12 to 20 words, one idea, everyday words) goes in too. Binding, no added detail, hedge and date, and every read criterion stay exactly as they are.

The open point is the first sentence. It is the one sentence with no record bound to it as its own claim. It needs a rule: it must still be a paraphrase of the records the rest of the answer cites, so the read can check it.
