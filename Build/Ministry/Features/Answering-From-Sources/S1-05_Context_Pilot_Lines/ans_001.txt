## anf05_hippolytus-cyprian-caius-novatian/0001
1. Title page of the Cyprian section of this volume, naming the Rev. Ernest Wallis as translator.
2. Opening of the editor's introductory notice to Cyprian, which compares him with Hippolytus and Ignatius and begins a list of propositions about his view of the bishop's office.
3. The introductory notice continues its propositions: Cyprian's teaching on bishops is set against the later claim of a supreme pope and is described as opposed to it.
4. Editor's footnotes to the introductory notice, giving scriptural references and citing modern writers such as Guettée and Milman.
5. The introductory notice goes on to say that later churchmen who resisted papal power, Gregory the Great among them, built on Cyprian's view of bishops, and it closes by calling for honest research into early practice.
6. A second introduction, taken from the Edinburgh editor, sketches Cyprian's early life: born about 200, trained as a rhetorician, and converted around 246 through a presbyter.
7. The introduction lists Cyprian's writings from before he was bishop, among them a letter to Donatus and three books of Testimonies against the Jews, and begins to order and date his letters.
8. The introduction dates Cyprian's letters by events, linking Epistles LVI, LVII and LIX to the banishment of the bishops Cornelius and Lucius in the years 252 and 253.
9. The translator's note on method: the letters follow the order of the Migne edition, with the Oxford edition's numbers given in notes and other readings recorded.
10. Opening of the Life and Passion of Cyprian by Pontius the Deacon, in which Pontius says why he is writing a short account of Cyprian's life and martyrdom.
11. Editor's bracketed footnotes to Pontius's life of Cyprian, with scriptural references and cross-references to other pages of the series.
12. Pontius continues his opening, saying he feels unequal to the greatness of Cyprian's deeds while his readers press him to tell everything.

## anf05_hippolytus-cyprian-caius-novatian/0002
1. Section 2 of Pontius's life of Cyprian: he begins the story with Cyprian's faith and new birth, passing over his earlier secular pursuits and learning.
2. Pontius says that as soon as Cyprian learned from Scripture he gave away his goods, even whole estates, to relieve the poor and gave up worldly ambition.
3. Section 3: Pontius says Cyprian showed that faith can advance faster than time, though the apostle warns against putting new converts forward, and compares the eunuch baptized by Philip.
4. Pontius holds up Job and other good men as examples Cyprian copied, so that he in turn became worth copying.
5. Section 4: Pontius names the presbyter Cæcilius, who led Cyprian from his worldly errors to the true God and whom Cyprian honoured as the parent of his new life.
6. Pontius tells how the people, moved by God, called for Cyprian as their bishop while he humbly stepped back in favour of older men.
7. Section 6: Pontius describes Cyprian's bearing as bishop, his face, manner and dress, a mix of strictness and kindness, and says the bishop's chair did not make him what he already was.
8. Section 7: Pontius says Cyprian was marked out by the pagans and called for by the lions, and asks who else would have taught prayer and answered the Gentiles had he died early.
9. Pontius goes on asking who would have comforted the grieving, taught mercy and patience, and raised martyrs and confessors, and says Cyprian hid because the Lord told him to.
10. Section 8: Pontius defends Cyprian's withdrawal during a violent persecution as divinely ordered rather than cowardly, since the church still needed him.
11. Section 9: Pontius describes a dreadful plague in which people fled the sick and left bodies in the streets, and ends on the plea that God's children should show their Father's goodness.
12. Section 10: Pontius says he must leave out much, turns to what a Christian people should do, with giving shared by rich and poor alike, and mentions Tobias burying the slain.

## anf05_hippolytus-cyprian-caius-novatian/0003
1. Section 11: banishment follows Cyprian's good works, Pontius says the Acts record his answer to the proconsul, and he notes that Cyprian is shut out of the city he had helped.
2. Pontius argues that banishment suffered for virtue is a glory and not a punishment, and that God's priest needed none of the comforts of the place.
3. Section 12: Pontius says God gave Cyprian a quiet, suitable dwelling in exile, and begins to tell of a vision of a youth writing a sentence on a tablet.
4. The account of the vision continues: the youth shows by a sign the stroke of execution, and Cyprian asks for one day's delay to arrange his property.
5. Section 13: Pontius explains that the vision foretold what followed, the one day asked for standing for a year, and that Cyprian was crowned on the very day it came round.
6. Pontius says everyone knew Cyprian's suffering was near but not the day, compares Zacharias made dumb, and says Cyprian arranged his affairs before the morrow.
7. Section 14: a messenger comes from Xistus, officers are expected any day, and eminent friends urge Cyprian to withdraw, but he wishes to die while speaking of God.
8. Section 15: the proconsul's officer arrives with soldiers at Cyprian's gardens, which he had sold at the start of his faith and God had restored to him.
9. Pontius describes the crowds, Cyprian held for one night in the officer's house, and the people keeping watch outside the door.
10. Section 16: the promised day dawns, Cyprian is brought before the proconsul and asked his name, and he answers only who he is.
11. Section 17: the judge reads the sentence, calling Cyprian a standard-bearer of the sect and an enemy of the gods whose blood would establish discipline, and Pontius finds it true.
12. Section 18: Cyprian is led out to the place of execution among soldiers and a crowd, some climbing trees to see, and the faithful wish to suffer with him.
