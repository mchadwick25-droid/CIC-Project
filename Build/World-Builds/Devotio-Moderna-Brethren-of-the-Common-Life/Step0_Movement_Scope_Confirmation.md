## 1. Candidate identification

Atlas V.3, "Devotio Moderna / Brethren of the Common Life," c. 1380–1517, Low Countries. Geert Groote gave up a wealthy career to gather devout men and women into households that lived in common without ever taking vows, supporting themselves by copying manuscripts. Their practice of keeping a personal notebook of gathered readings (a *rapiarium*) produced Thomas à Kempis's *Imitation of Christ*, the most copied Christian book after the Bible. Their schools shaped Erasmus (educated at Deventer) and touched a year of Luther's own schooling (Magdeburg), and the same disciplined, interior devotion fed into Jesuit spiritual formation a century later. The houses themselves mostly dissolved during the Reformation; the *Imitation* never stopped circulating.

## 2. Section A — Movement-Scope Eligibility

**Framing note**, as for every candidate in this project's Step 0 documents: this document's "clears" are a considered signal, not a self-administered disposition.

### A1 — The floor: clears, on an absence of complication and the movement's own relationship to authority

Quoted directly: floorNote "No question." *The Imitation of Christ* and Kempis's *Founders of the New Devotion* raise no Trinitarian or Christological complication. Groote's own *Publica protestatio* (Ch. XVIII of *Founders*, in his own first-person voice) is positive evidence of general Catholic orthodoxy, not merely an absence of complication: "in regard to those things that are of faith, I have steadfastly preached and defended that faith which is certain, pure, and Catholic, resting upon Jesus Christ Himself Who is the chief corner Stone... subject always to the judgement of the Holy Roman Church, to whom with all humility I everywhere and always submit myself." This is a general profession of Catholic orthodoxy and submission to Rome — it has no Trinitarian or Christological content in the specific sense Article 4's five commitments name. The floor clearance does not rest on the Protestatio for that content; it rests on the absence of any complication across the vendored corpus (the census's own "No question"), with the Protestatio standing as corroborating evidence of the movement's own orthodoxy in the broader sense.

Revision 4 adds positive evidence from Kempis's own sermons, read in the new English and Latin volumes. Kempis, setting John the Baptist beside Christ, says to Christ: "Thou the only-begotten Son of God, co-eternal with God the Father" (`cic:kempis_meditation-on-the-incarnation-sermons-life-passion_scully1907.txt:line2063`, running to line 2064). The Latin in Pohl's volume 3 reads "tu unigenitus filius Dei coaeternus Deo Patri" (`cic:kempis_opera-omnia-v3-meditatio-incarnatione-sermones-lat_pohl1904.txt:line1810`, running to line 1811). A later passage in the same English volume reads: "I believe that Jesus is the only-begotten Son of God: and the first-born of thy fruitful virginity. Him I acknowledge my God, Creator and Redeemer: born to day for my salvation" (`cic:kempis_meditation-on-the-incarnation-sermons-life-passion_scully1907.txt:line4868`, running to line 4872; the scan breaks "acknow" and "ledge" across two lines). These lines speak to commitments (2) and (3). They are a sample of Kempis's words in a 1907 English translation and the 1904 Latin edition, **Documented** as what the texts say, and they are not a full reading. They confirm the floor finding and do not change it. Commitments (1), (4) and (5) rest, as before, on the absence of complication across the corpus.

The movement's relationship to church authority was not free of friction. Kempis's *Founders* records "many prelates of the Church were opposed to him... he was forbidden to preach by an edict craftily obtained" — Ch. XVIII is itself a defensive profession of faith written against accusations of unorthodoxy, and a friend's letter to the Bishop of Utrecht, written on Groote's behalf and preserved in the same volume, pleads for his suspended preaching license to be restored. The census confirms this: Groote was "forbidden to preach the year before for attacking the clergy's concubines." The movement submitted to church authority rather than breaking from it — a real distinction from Lollardy and the Hussites — but its relationship to that authority was genuinely contested, not absent of friction. Article 4 itself concerns Trinitarian and Christological commitments, not ecclesiastical authority; the floor clearance above does not rest on this authority relationship in either direction.

The reading behind this document's floor finding is sampling — "the opening pages and a mid-document sample," per the headers of the first three works — not a full reading. The corpus is now 22 files and about 17.5 million characters (§3 B1). The files added since the first pass were checked at their headers and at the passages quoted in this document, not read through for doctrine. Groote and Kempis were checked for the floor. The women's side is now on the shelf: Opzoomer's edition of the Diepenveen convent book (Middle Dutch, 1886) prints lives of the Diepenveen Sisters. This pass read its preface and the opening of the first life only. It has not been read for doctrine. The floor finding for the Sisters' houses therefore rests on the census's own "No question," not on any text in their own words. The *Chronicle of Mount St Agnes* also records the Diepenveen Sisters' investiture in 1408 and other sisters' houses, from the canons' side (see §3 B2).

Corpus size, counted two ways with the locale stated: Python `len()` on the UTF-8 text, and `wc -m` and `wc -w` under `LC_ALL=C.UTF-8`. The two methods agree. Two files carry the wrong description in the corpus-map and in their own headers. `kempis_opera-omnia-sermones-de-compunctione-lat_pohl1902.txt` is volume 2, the Latin *Imitation* with its apparatus (title page: "VOLVMEN ALTERVM", file line 102), not *De vera compunctione*. `kempis_opera-omnia-tractatus-lat_pohl1902.txt` is volume 5 ("VOLVMEN QVINTVM", file line 114), not *Tractatus prioris pars prior*. - **The Sisters.** Opzoomer's 1886 edition of the Diepenveen convent book, Middle Dutch with a Dutch preface and no English. Acquoy's own introduction (c. line 891) describes the source codex as containing 66 letters, of which 64 are attributable to Groote — the 19th and 20th are by William of Salvarvilla, cantor of Paris, not Groote. Acquoy's own edition, the one vendored here, covers 14 of them: 14 of the 64 letters attributable to Groote (of 66 total in the codex).

Groote's own voice is not Latin-only. The vendored *Founders* volume already contains his own writing in public-domain English: his *Publica protestatio* (Ch. XVIII, first person) and his "Resolutions and Intentions... not confirmed by vows" (the census's own named self-rule text). It also contains a letter to the Bishop of Utrecht pleading for Groote's suspended preaching license to be restored — written on his behalf, not by him: the heading itself reads "A Letter to the Bishop of Utrecht on behalf of Master Gerard Groote," and Acquoy's own introduction names the author as an unnamed friend — the scan reads "qoidam Girardi amicoa, cojos nomea non ad nos pervenit," OCR artifacts for the normalized Latin "quidam Gerardi amicus, cujus nomen non ad nos pervenit." Langenberg's volume prints his tract against simony in Middle Dutch and Low German (see above). Langenberg rests the attribution to Groote on the manuscript heading, which is in the same hand as the tract, on a sixteenth-century catalogue entry, and on style (`cic:langenberg_quellen-und-forschungen-niederdeutschen-mystik-gml_1902.txt:line2159`, running to line 2200). The vendored corpus holds no second scholar on that attribution, so Doc_02 must tag it.

Groote's Latin letters are primary evidence, not a second witness. Mark ruled on 2026-09-25 (`Build/worlds/_cross-world/LIBRARY-DECISION-LOG.md`) that a clean public-domain original-language text can be primary evidence, on the same footing as an English translation, when it is primary to the world it's assigned to — language does not decide primacy; credibility and truth to the movement do. `cic/texts/INTAKE.md` states this as the current rule. The corpus-map's Groote entry applies the ruling directly: the letters are Groote's own surviving voice, not reported through Kempis's later biography, and the scan is not on OCR ruling "a"'s garbled list. Each quote is verified against the scan, and the editor's introductions are noticeably noisier than the letter bodies — unlike the 1606 Jesuit Constitutions and Nadal's 1595 *Adnotationes*, both of which are held to second-witness status under Mark's separate OCR ruling on scan quality, so the Jesuit-precedent framing an earlier revision leaned on no longer holds and isn't needed here regardless. Under this project's practice, a quote record holds the verified Latin text; the modern English a Representative speaks is an Opus rendering of the original, independently Opus-checked; and *Founders*' own English texts (the Protestatio, the Resolutions) serve as a cross-check where they overlap with the letters, not a precondition for using them.

The Imitation's own authorship is disputed in its own front matter. The vendored Benham preface itself (c. lines 490–640) calls Kempis's authorship "the erroneous notion that he was its author" and reports the case made by "the advocates of the Gersen authorship" for a 13th-c. "Abbot John," also identified in the manuscript record as Gersen, closing with "Whoever the author may be." Kempis authorship remains the mainstream modern view, but this world's own central vendored edition disputes the attribution in its own introduction — worth a `formation_confidence` tag at Doc_02, not silence. The shelf now holds more witnesses on the question, all editors' positions rather than the author's. Pohl's volume 2 prints the Latin *Imitation* from the Brussels autograph. Its bibliography lists a work on the dispute "inter Thomae Kempensis Canonici Regularis & Joannis Gersenis Ordinis S. Benedicti Abbatis Patronos" (`cic:kempis_opera-omnia-sermones-de-compunctione-lat_pohl1902.txt:line16419`, running to line 16420), and its epilegomena report an argument that Kempis is the author of all thirteen tractates finished in 1441 (lines 17968 to 17971). Ingram's 1893 introduction to the Middle English version reports that the translator Atkinson calls the work "compyled in Latin by the right worshypfulle doctor master lohn Gerson" (`cic:kempis_earliest-english-translation-de-imitatione-christi-enm_ingram1893.txt:line1177`, running to line 1178). Ingram rejects that: "I believe neither in the authorship of the Chancellor Gerson, nor in that of Gersen (who, indeed, appears to be an apocryphal person)" (lines 1183 to 1185). These passages show the dispute is real and long-running. They do not settle it. Kempis authorship stays the mainstream modern view, and Doc_02 still has to tag the claim.

The "documented" influence claims need separate confidence tags. The Luther/Erasmus schooling connection (§1, §3 B3) is contested in scholarship — R. R. Post's 1968 study rebutted Hyma's earlier thesis, arguing the Brethren mostly ran student hostels rather than schools and that the Reformation link is weak; the census's own wording is already careful ("a household connected with the Brethren"), and "documented" overstates it. ### B4 — Audiences

The interior, devotional register this Era 6 batch otherwise lacks. Lollardy and the Hussite movement are both, at root, protest movements against ecclesiastical authority. This candidate is not a movement that never challenged church authority — it submitted to that authority under real pressure (a suspended preaching license, prelates' opposition) rather than breaking from it, a real distinction in kind from open dissent, but not an absence of friction (see §2 A1). Its appeal remains distinct: personal spiritual formation and interior practice over institutional conflict as the movement's own center of gravity, even though real conflict with authority did occur.

A single-region claim is too narrow; the movement's own core was the Low Countries, but its reach was not confined there.
