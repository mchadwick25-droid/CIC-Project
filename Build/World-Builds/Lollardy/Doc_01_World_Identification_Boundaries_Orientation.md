## 1. Formation World Identity

**World Name:** Lollardy.

**Short Description:** An English vernacular reform movement, c. 1380–1520, that grew out of the Oxford theologian John Wyclif's attack on clerical wealth, papal and ecclesiastical authority, and the doctrine of transubstantiation, and his insistence that Scripture be available in English — but which, within a decade of its own origin, became something larger and more durable than its founder: a lay and artisan movement of household Bible-reading and mutual instruction, documented across a century and a half chiefly through the hostile trial records of the bishops who prosecuted it.

**Core Identity:** Lollardy is a movement rooted in one man's Oxford theology but not, by its own later period, organized or sustained by him or by any comparable clerical-academic authority. The census's own floor finding states this plainly: "the movement outlasted Wycliffe by well over a century, and what the trial records preserve are ordinary believers' own scripture-centred convictions rather than an Oxford theologian's system" (`Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §2, A3, quoting the census `floorNote`). The vendored corpus corroborates this at the level of primary evidence: the hostile trial record directly names, and lets speak, a tailor (John Badby), a layman the record itself calls "learned" — Walter Brute, who describes himself as "sinner, lay-man, husbandman, and a Christian ... of the Britons" (`foxe_acts-and-monuments-v3_cattley-townsend1837.txt`, lines 11162–63; the bishop's own process calls him "a lay person, learned, of our diocese," line 10722) — and household networks of readers and hearers reaching into the 1520s (Foxe's account of "the good martyrs of Amersham," martyred "in the beginning of king Henry VIII" under Bishop Longland of Lincoln — `foxe_acts-and-monuments-v3_cattley-townsend1837.txt`, line 32323 ff.) — **Confidence: Documented** for the vendored attestation; **Widely Accepted** for the broader scholarly characterization of Lollardy as predominantly a lay/artisan phenomenon by its later period.

**Distinctive Contribution — engaging the Hussite relationship directly, not deferring it.** `Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §3 B3 discloses, honestly and without resolving it, that this world sits in a real, direct textual-dependency relationship with a separate, later-batch world — Step 0's own language (§3 B3, B5) is explicit that the Hussite and Bohemian Brethren candidate (Atlas V.6, drafted 2026-09-25) belongs to a different, later batch, not this document's own 2026-09-15 batch, so it is not a batch-mate: the Hussite and Bohemian Brethren world's own Step 0 records that Jan Hus's *De Ecclesia* draws substantially on Wyclif's own writing, quoting Schaff's translator introduction that "Huss appropriated paragraph after paragraph from his predecessor and transferred them often with little verbal change to his own pages." This document does not minimize that dependency or attempt to resolve the cross-world corpus-map question it raises (§7 item 7 below carries that question forward, undecided, as Step 0 requires). What this document does argue is that direct textual dependency of one figure on another's writing is not the same claim as two formation worlds being ecologically redundant. Three features keep Lollardy its own distinct formation ecology despite the dependency:

- **A different, England-specific persecution regime, not merely a different regional flavor of the same repression.** Bohemia's Hussite crisis produced open war (the Hussite Wars, from 1420) and, eventually, a negotiated toleration (the Compacts of Basel/Prague) for a plural ecclesial settlement. England's own regime is structurally different: a *statutory* fusion of heresy and capital punishment (the 1401 *De heretico comburendo*, making burning for heresy the law of the land — `Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §1), reinforced by Archbishop Arundel's 1409 Constitutions, which forbade both unlicensed translation of Scripture into English and unlicensed preaching generally (ibid.), and then — after Sir John Oldcastle's failed 1414 rising — a further fusion with the law of *treason* specifically, so that Lollardy is prosecuted simultaneously as heresy against the church and sedition against the crown in a way Hussite Bohemia's own trajectory (toward civil war and eventual negotiated plurality) does not parallel. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted.**
- **The vernacular Bible project's own manuscript-survival scale, held open rather than compared.** The Wycliffite Bible — the complete translation project vendored here in the Forshall & Madden (1850) edition — survives in roughly 250 manuscripts, more than any other Middle English text (`Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §3 B2, citing the census). This document does not claim nothing comparable exists in the Hussite world's own corpus: the Hussite world's own Step 0 records Czech-vernacular preaching from 1402 and a vernacular hymnbook by 1501, so a vernacular-access tradition of some kind is on record there too, and this document has not checked whether a comparably scaled manuscript tradition also stands behind it. What is documented, and narrower than a cross-world comparison, is the scale of the Wycliffite Bible's own English manuscript survival on its own terms, and that Hus's own vendored textual dependency runs specifically through a Latin ecclesiological treatise (*De Ecclesia*), not through the vernacular-Scripture project this document is describing. — **Confidence: Documented** for the manuscript-survival claim as already established in Step 0; **held open** for any comparative claim about the Hussite world's own corpus, which this document does not have in hand to check.
- **A social composition the record itself, not inference, establishes as different in kind.** The Twelve Conclusions of the Lollards (1395), vendored here in their Latin trial-record form inside *Fasciculi Zizaniorum*, are addressed to "the full parliament of the realm of England" in a collective, unnamed voice — "*nos procuratores Dei*," "we, God's proctors" (`fasciculi-zizaniorum_shirley1858.txt`, heading at line 28381 ff.; corollary at line 28603) — not circulated as an academic disputation. The heading names no individual author, and whether the Conclusions' own authors were lay or clerical is not itself settled by the vendored text; any public posting, and any link to the "Lollard knights," rests on chronicle tradition outside this document's own vendored corpus. The trial records of Thorpe (1407), Badby (1410), and the Amersham household networks (1500s–1520s) are, by contrast, the movement's own primary surviving evidentiary mode — a household and artisan ecology visible because it was prosecuted, not because it left behind a clerical or princely archive of its own. This is a different social shape from the Hussite movement's own university-and-noble-led trajectory into open war. — **Confidence: Documented** that the Conclusions were addressed to Parliament in this collective form, per the vendored heading itself and nothing more (§2 Beginning point, below); **Contested/held open** whether their own authorship was lay, clerical, or mixed, and whether this comparative claim about the Hussite world's own social composition holds, since this document does not have that world's own Doc_01 in hand to check against — both named as limits on this comparison, not resolved here.

**Living Tradition Status.** **PENDING.** Per Constitution Article 29, Living Tradition Status confirmation is a project-lead act, not something this document can perform on its own behalf. Unlike the Coptic Orthodox Church's direct institutional descent from Alexandrian Christianity, or the papacy's and Eastern Orthodoxy's own traceable continuity from Imperial and Juridical Christianity's two primacy-claiming strands, Lollardy left no comparable surviving ecclesial body of its own — its household networks were suppressed, not institutionally transformed. The only continuity claim on record is the census's own, carefully unresolved one: a "Contested influence line to the English Reformation" (`relationsSummary`, quoted at `Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §2, A5). Whether that contested line to the (much later, and not-yet-built) English Reformation world is itself the kind of contact with a "living tradition" Article 29 contemplates — as opposed to a contact with another *historical* formation world whose own living-tradition question is separately its own to answer — is not obvious, and is flagged here as its own open question for the project lead, not assumed either way. **Status: PENDING project-lead determination, including of whether Article 29 squarely applies to this world at all.**

## 2. Historical Boundaries

**Time Horizon:** c. 1380–1520 CE, carried forward exactly as `Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §1 and the Source Readiness Dossier both already fix it (Atlas V.5: "c. 1380–1520, England"). This document does not reopen that window; it argues why it is a defensible one.

**Beginning point (c. 1380), defended.** The defensible beginning is not Wyclif's own earlier Oxford career as an academic critic of ecclesiastical wealth and papal authority (active from the 1360s, sharpened in works like *De Civili Dominio* from the mid-1370s) — that phase belongs to the larger world Lollardy emerged from, not to Lollardy itself (§5 below). The ecological rupture that actually opens this world falls in a tight cluster, c. 1379–1382: Wyclif's own turn to the specific, sacrament-centered doctrine that defines Lollard dissent going forward — denial of transubstantiation, argued in his own *De Eucharistia* and *Confessio* (traditionally dated 1379–1381) — the beginning of the vernacular Bible translation project his circle undertook (the Earlier Version, conventionally dated from the early-to-mid 1380s), and the Blackfriars ("Earthquake") Council of May 1382, at which Archbishop Courtenay's council condemned twenty-four conclusions drawn from Wyclif's teaching and moved the controversy out of Oxford's own academic confines into a matter of parliamentary and national concern. This last event is dated once, not twice, in this world's own vendored corpus, and that one dating is Arnold's own editorial apparatus, not the trial record. Arnold's headnote to "The Seven Werkys of Mercy Bodyly" introduces "the interesting double tract which follows" — that tract and its companion, "Pe Seven Werkys of Mercy Gostly" (the "bodily" and "spiritual" works of mercy, one continuous chaptering in the manuscript) — as "composed, it would seem, not long after the holding of the council convened at London by Archbishop Courtenay in May, 1382. (See p. 175.)" (`wyclif_select-english-works-v3_arnold1871.txt`, lines 10737–43). That cross-reference, "(See p. 175.)", points to the footnote at the tract's own page 175 (page marker, line 11149), attached to the "Bodyly" half's Cap. VI discussion of the friars' denial that tithes are pure alms, before the "Gostly" half begins (line 11291). The footnote is set in two columns; read as printed, the two columns are "This allusion to the earthquake / which occurred during the sitting of / Archbishop Courtenay's synod in" and "1382, on May 19 {*Fasc. Zizaniomm*, / p. 272), fixes so far the date of this / treatise" (ibid., lines 11189–91). Reconstructed as continuous prose — a normalized reading, not a vendored quotation — this reads: "This allusion to the earthquake which occurred during the sitting of Archbishop Courtenay's synod in 1382, on May 19 (*Fasc. Zizaniorum*, p. 272), fixes so far the date of this treatise." So Arnold gives one dating of one double tract, grounded in that tract's own allusion to the May 1382 earthquake and council; the headnote's own dating relies on, and does not independently duplicate, the footnote. Arnold's note does not say the council condemned anything; what it supports is a dating of the double tract to the months around the May 1382 council and earthquake. The condemnation of the twenty-four conclusions at that council stands on its own as **Widely Accepted**, on the strength of the council's own well-attested historical record — not as something this editorial note corroborates. Before this cluster, what existed was an Oxford academic's escalating critique of ecclesiastical wealth and authority; after it, a doctrinally distinct, publicly condemned, and increasingly lay-transmitted movement with its own defining artifact (the vernacular Bible) underway. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted** for the cluster of dates; **Dominant Modern Reconstruction** for treating this cluster, rather than a single event, as the defensible ecological beginning.

**Ending point (c. 1520), defended.** Step 0 itself states that "the movement survived underground as scattered household networks into the 1520s" (`Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §1). This document's own vendored corpus reaches into that period, though only as a forward reference rather than a trial record of its own: Foxe, arguing against Cope elsewhere in the text, mentions in passing that "the good martyrs of Amersham" suffered death "in the beginning of king Henry VIII" (whose reign began in 1509), naming Longland as "then bishop of Lincoln" (`foxe_acts-and-monuments-v3_cattley-townsend1837.txt`, lines 32322–29) — Longland held that see only from 1521, which dates this attestation to 1521–22, not earlier. Foxe himself defers the full account to a later volume not vendored here, vendored as "as  Avhen  b\"*?  '<>" / "we  come  to  that  time,  by  the  grace  of  Christ,  shall  hereafter  more  amply  and  notoriously  appear" [normalized: "as when we come to that time, by the grace of Christ, shall hereafter more amply and notoriously appear"] (ibid., lines 32331–33). Ending the window at c. 1520, close to but just before this vendored attestation's own 1521–22 date, keeps this world upstream of the census's own named, unresolved contest: "Contested influence line to the English Reformation" (`relationsSummary`, `Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §2, A5). This document, like the census before it, does not decide, here or anywhere in this document, whether or how far Lollard household networks fed directly into the early English Reformation; it ends the world's own window at the point where that question becomes live, rather than assuming an answer by where the window is drawn. — **Confidence: Documented** for the vendored sentence itself, as a forward reference rather than a trial record; **Contested** for any claim about what, if anything, connects it forward to the Reformation proper.

**Geographic Centers:** Oxford, London, East Anglia, and the Chilterns.
- **Oxford:** the movement's own point of origin — Wyclif's academic base, and the site of his condemnation and the expulsion of his Oxford circle (Nicholas Hereford, Philip Repingdon, John Aston) following the 1382 Blackfriars Council. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted.**
- **London:** the seat of Parliament, to which the Twelve Conclusions were addressed in 1395 (`fasciculi-zizaniorum_shirley1858.txt`, heading at lines 28383–85, vendored as "PLENO PARLIAMENTO RKCJNI '" / "AnCLLE" [normalized: *pleno parliamento regni Anglie*], "the full parliament of the realm of England" — the heading itself does not name London specifically), and the seat of the 1401 parliament at which Sautre was tried, per Foxe's own account of "a parliament holden at Westminster" (`foxe_acts-and-monuments-v3_cattley-townsend1837.txt`, line 18217 ff.). The 1414 statute, following Oldcastle's rising, was enacted at a parliament held at Leicester, not London. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted** that Parliament sat at Westminster/London for the 1395 and 1401 proceedings; **Documented** only that the Twelve Conclusions were addressed to "the full parliament," per the vendored heading itself.
- **East Anglia:** the site of the Norwich diocesan heresy trials of 1428–31. Step 0 §3 B1 corrects the census's own prior sourcing tags directly: Tanner's *Heresy Trials in the Diocese of Norwich, 1428-31* was one of only two Lollardy sources ever carrying the census's own "Verified" tag, but the volume itself is genuinely inaccessible to this build: the archive.org item is an "Access-restricted-item" (lending-library only, not downloadable), it has not been vendored, and its content has not been independently checked (`Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §3 B1, §4.1; `Build/worlds/_cross-world/dossiers/lollardy_Source_Readiness_Dossier.md`). What is independently on record is the trials' existence and date range, not the volume's own content, and no claim about the trials' own social composition or manner of prosecution rests on it anywhere in this document. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted** for the trials' existence and date range; the volume's own content is not independently checked here and no confidence rating is assigned to it.
- **The Chilterns:** the site of the Amersham household networks Foxe records as suffering "in the beginning of king Henry VIII" for possessing and reading vernacular Scripture (`foxe_acts-and-monuments-v3_cattley-townsend1837.txt`, line 32323 ff.) — this world's own latest directly-vendored geographic attestation. — **Confidence: Documented**, per the vendored citation.

**Cultural Environment:** A late-medieval English Church operating in Latin liturgy and Latin/scholastic academic argument at Oxford, against a lay world of growing, though still limited, vernacular literacy. Household reading circles — families and neighbors gathering to hear vernacular Scripture read aloud, sometimes led by literate laity, sometimes by a single trained reader for an otherwise unlettered group — are this world's own most distinctive site of formation, though neither of this document's two vendored attestations of the practice is a trial record. Foxe, reporting the clergy's own complaint to the king (not his own narration), writes that "the heretics and lollards of Wick-[garbled marginal-note bleed-through and OCR noise]-opinion, were suffered to preach abroad so boldly, to gather conventicles unto them, to keep schools in men's houses" — the vendored scan's own text is genuinely illegible at this point (a marginal note has bled into the line, followed by OCR noise), and "Wick-liffs" (modern: "Wickliff's") is this document's own reconstructed reading of the garbled word, not a clean quotation (`foxe_acts-and-monuments-v3_cattley-townsend1837.txt`, lines 28226–28); and, in the Amersham forward reference (§ Ending point, above), the "good martyrs of Amersham" who "had and read" certain books of Scripture are named alongside "the others who but only heard them read" and who "did bear faggots" for it, with children in some cases "compelled to set faggots unto their fathers" (ibid., lines 32323–29) — a passage that, once read past its single sentence naming Amersham, does describe collective reading and hearing, not only an individual practice, even though it remains a single, forward-deferred, un-cross-checked polemical aside rather than a trial record. Thorpe's own Examination attests individual preaching and priesthood, not household reading circles specifically, and is not cited for that claim here. Trade and craft networks recur in the record as a social substrate for the movement's transmission (Badby is recorded as a tailor by trade — `foxe_acts-and-monuments-v3_cattley-townsend1837.txt`, line 19229 ff.). The Twelve Conclusions' own ninth conclusion, on confession, has lords and ladies ("*domini et domi[n]ae*," rendered in the vendored OCR as "domiui et domiure," `fasciculi-zizaniorum_shirley1858.txt`, line 28735) attest that they dare not tell their confessors the truth for fear of them — evidence of penitents generally resisting confession, not, on its own, evidence of women's participation in Lollardy specifically. — **Confidence: Documented** for the named individuals and their trades and for both vendored passages' own wording; **Widely Accepted** for household reading circles as a recurring practice, resting on two independent but hostile, secondary-hand attestations rather than a trial record of the practice itself; **Inferential-Thin** for the interior experience of ordinary household participants, who are visible only through the shaping lens of prosecutorial and polemical record (Article 20 concern, carried forward at §3 and §7 below).

**Historical Pressures:** The 1401 statute *De heretico comburendo*, making burning for heresy the law of the land (`Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §1); Archbishop Arundel's 1409 Constitutions, forbidding unlicensed translation of Scripture into English and unlicensed preaching generally (ibid.); and, after 1414, the fusion of heresy with treason following Oldcastle's rising, which made the movement's own survival a matter of political as well as ecclesiastical danger. Foxe's own text paraphrases the constitution's prohibition rather than quoting it directly, in his polemic against Cope: "the constitution provincial of Thomas Arundel above-mentioned, where it was decreed, that the text of holy Scripture should not be had, or read in the vulgar tongue, from the time of Master John WicklifF for cvcr after, unless the said translation be approved first by the ordinary, or by provincial council, under pain and punishment of heresy" (`foxe_acts-and-monuments-v3_cattley-townsend1837.txt`, lines 32299–304; the vendored OCR itself renders "ever" as "cvcr," reproduced here rather than silently corrected). — **Confidence: Documented** that this is Foxe's own paraphrase of the constitution's content, independently verified against the vendored text; **Widely Accepted** for the statutes' dates and general effect.

**Historical Catalysts** (dated):
- **1377** — Pope Gregory XI's bulls condemning propositions drawn from Wyclif's own writing on dominion and church authority. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted.**
- **1382 (May)** — The Blackfriars ("Earthquake") Council, under Archbishop Courtenay, condemns twenty-four conclusions drawn from Wyclif's teaching and expels his Oxford adherents. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted**, on the council's own well-attested historical record. Arnold's vendored editorial apparatus dates a nearby double tract by allusion to the same council and earthquake (see beginning-point discussion above); that note dates the tract, not the condemnation, and is not this entry's own evidence.
- **1384** — Wyclif's death, at Lutterworth. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted.**
- **1391–93** — The trial of Walter Brute, whom the bishop of Hereford's own process calls "a lay person, learned, of our diocese," and who describes himself as "sinner, lay-man, husbandman, and a Christian ... of the Britons"; required by the bishop to answer in Latin, Brute writes that he "was required that I should write an answer in Latin to all those matters" (`foxe_acts-and-monuments-v3_cattley-townsend1837.txt`, lines 10690–91, 10722, 11162–66). Foxe also records "a Letter of the King against Walter Brute" dated 1392 (index at line 327). — **Confidence: Documented.**

He is nonetheless the first Lollard burned as a heretic in this world's own window. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted** for the sequence (writ before statute); the Foxe account itself carries an old-style regnal date ("1400," itself an OCR-garbled marginal reading, `a.d.hoo`, for what modern reckoning gives as 1401) for the trial proceedings that immediately precede the execution (line 18218 ff.), a discrepancy of calendar convention and OCR rather than of fact, noted here rather than silently reconciled.

- **1410** — John Badby, a tailor, burned as a heretic for denying transubstantiation (Foxe's own heading dates the proceeding "1409," an old-style-calendar discrepancy of the same kind noted above for Sautre). — **Confidence: Documented** for the trial and its content; **Widely Accepted** for the conventional modern date of the execution itself.
- **1414** — Sir John Oldcastle's failed revolt, after which Lollardy is prosecuted as treason as well as heresy. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted.**
- **1428–31** — The Norwich diocesan heresy trials. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted**, for the trials' existence and date range only; Tanner's own volume is genuinely inaccessible to this build and its content is not independently checked (§ Geographic Centers, above).
- **c. 1521–22** — The Amersham (Chiltern) household-network prosecutions, Foxe's own forward reference to "the good martyrs of Amersham" under Bishop Longland of Lincoln, who held that see only from 1521; this world's own latest directly-vendored attestation, though as a passing mention rather than a trial record (`foxe_acts-and-monuments-v3_cattley-townsend1837.txt`, lines 32322–29; see the Ending point discussion above). — **Confidence: Documented** for the vendored sentence itself.

## 3. Distinct World Criteria

Applying the Framework's own four questions directly:

- **What recurring formation ecology exists?** One recurring ecology, evidenced across the full 1380–1520 window: direct engagement with Scripture in English — read alone, heard read aloud in a household or company, or personally argued from — as the ground of religious conviction, set against clerical and papal mediation of that authority. This recurs in two registers, both grounding the same conviction rather than two different ones (§4 below tests, and rejects, reading this as a genuine Article 21 strand split): a household and lay-network register of reading, hearing, and mutual instruction (the "schools in men's houses" Foxe's own text reports the clergy complaining of, and the Amersham readers and hearers — § Cultural Environment, above), and a learned, personally-argued register in which one named individual, under direct examination or in a sustained written exhibit, defends that same conviction over their own name (Brute, Thorpe, Badby, Sautre). The ecology is documented almost entirely through the paradox of hostile process: the same bishops' courts and polemical historians who prosecuted or opposed this world's own participants are also the reason their words, and something of their own convictions, survive at all — which is why some instances of the learned register left a sustained composition and others did not, a fact about what the trial process happened to produce and preserve, not a second ecology in its own right.
- **What recurring gravities exist (preliminary, subject to Doc_04)?** Provisionally, and named here only as candidates for Step 4 testing, not as confirmed findings: scriptural sufficiency and lay access to Scripture in the vernacular; a reinterpreted eucharistic theology (Conclusion IV holds that the sacrament of the altar is "habitually" (*habitualiter*) Christ's body rather than transformed by "a feigned miracle," reporting — not quoting — what it calls "the Gospel doctor's" own *Trialogus* on this point — `fasciculi-zizaniorum_shirley1858.txt`, lines 28485–28523); and anticlerical suspicion of clerical wealth, temporal office, and sacramental gatekeeping (confession, indulgences, and the "power of the keys" are all three named together within Conclusion IX of the same text). These are named as candidates only; Doc_04 performs the six-test assessment Doc_01 does not.
- **What recurring patterns of worship, formation, interpretation, belonging, and authority exist?** Vernacular Scripture access is this world's own single most recurring and best-evidenced pattern, running from the translation project itself through the trial records' own repeated emphasis on what the accused had read, owned, or heard read aloud. Rejection of transubstantiation recurs as a specific, repeatedly prosecuted doctrinal marker (Sautre, Badby, and the Twelve Conclusions' own Conclusion IV all name it directly). Anticlericalism recurs across register — the Twelve Conclusions' own opening conclusions against church endowments in "temporalities" and against a priesthood "feigned with power higher than the angels'," vendored as "iictum potestate angehs altiori" [normalized: *fictum potestate angelis altiori*], a power not warranted by the New Testament (`fasciculi-zizaniorum_shirley1858.txt`, lines 28391 ff., 28415–21, phrase at 28419) — and, in a more diffuse form, across the trial records' own repeated emphasis on lay suspicion of priestly authority over confession, penance, and the dead. Rejection of pilgrimage and image veneration also recurs, at least in the Twelve Conclusions' own articulation (Conclusion VIII: pilgrimages and offerings to "dumb images of wood or stone" are "near akin to idolatry," and an image of the Trinity specifically "most abominable" — ibid., lines 28674 ff.), though this document does not assume every Lollard defendant across the full window held this specific position with equal emphasis; that proportionality question belongs to Doc_04/Doc_05. — **Confidence: Documented** for each cited textual instance; **Contested/held open** for how uniformly any one of these held across the movement's full social and geographic range.
- **What distinguishes this world from adjacent worlds?** From the Hussite and Bohemian Brethren world specifically: see §1 above (Distinctive Contribution) and §6 below. From this project's other confirmed and built worlds — none of which (per a direct check of `worlds/`: `alx`, `cappadocian`, `desert`, `don`, `gallic`, `grkap`, `hal`, `ijc`, `latap`, `lpc`, `pahc`, `rzg`, `syr`) is a vernacular-reform, trial-record-anchored English movement of this shape — there is no overlap risk requiring vigilance. The nearest built comparison in this project's current portfolio is Lutheran Wittenberg (`witt`), a batch-mate whose own Doc_01 grounds its distinctiveness in confessional/catechetical doctrine and a territorial, princely-sponsored magisterial Reformation (`witt_Doc_01_World_Identification_Boundaries_Orientation.md` §1). ## 4. Strand Determination (Article 21)

Per Constitution Article 21, strand is "a finding, never a presupposed universal schema," accountable to evidence and "never assigned to satisfy an architectural preference for plurality." The census's own floor finding — "the movement outlasted Wycliffe by well over a century" — is a temporal-development finding (Article 15, Historical Development and Instability), not by itself a strand finding, and this document does not treat it as one. Simply noting that Lollardy's later, more socially diffuse lay/artisan form came *after* Wyclif's own Oxford phase would be exactly the sequential-development error Article 21 warns against; it would show only that the movement developed over time, which every formation world does. Strand determination instead asks whether the evidence shows a genuinely *simultaneous* divergence in authority structure or ecological orientation, not a mere succession of one phase by another.

**Finding: strand-singular. The vendored evidence supports one recurring formation ecology, not a genuine Article 21 split.** Article 21 defines strand as "a meaningfully distinct pattern of formation emphasis, practice, authority structure, or ecological orientation within a single world, not merely a variation in detail," and states that "a world without established strands is treated as strand-singular throughout construction." Tested against that bar, on the actual evidence rather than on which kind of record happened to survive, Lollardy does not clear it.

A two-strand split — an "individually-argued" strand (Brute, Purvey, Thorpe) against a "communal-access" strand (the Twelve Conclusions, Badby, Norwich, Amersham), grounded in "how authority is grounded and defended" — does not hold against the vendored record, checked pillar by pillar:

- **Badby's own examination is individual in exactly the way Brute's and Thorpe's are.** Badby, a tailor, was examined in person before the bishop of Worcester and, later, before Archbishop Arundel and a bench of other bishops and lords. He answered for his own individually held conviction — vendored as "he  woidd  never  believe  otherwise  than  before  he  had  said,  taught,  and  answered" [normalized: "he would never believe otherwise..."] — and was "admonished and requested... oftentimes" to recant and refused, personally, to the end (`foxe_acts-and-monuments-v3_cattley-townsend1837.txt`, lines 19267–19332). The only thing distinguishing his record from Brute's or Thorpe's is that no sustained written composition of his own survives; Foxe preserves his examination's *content*, just not in his own hand. That is a fact about the record's survival, not about how Badby's own authority was grounded: a register or genre-of-record difference, not a divergent authority structure — and Badby is the very figure a "communal-access" strand would need to anchor.
- **The Twelve Conclusions' own defining trait — "shared, direct access to Scripture in English" — is not what the Conclusions themselves argue.** They argue from reason, from the New Testament's own silence on clerical wealth and sacerdotal claims, and from a collective, unnamed "*nos procuratores Dei*" voice (`fasciculi-zizaniorum_shirley1858.txt`, line 28603) — but, as §5 below states directly, none of the twelve conclusions addresses vernacular Scripture access at all. A document's authorship being collective and anonymous is not evidence that its authority claim rests on communal Scripture access; it is evidence only that its authors chose not to sign it.
- **Purvey's own vendored text is a recantation, not a personally-composed defense of his own authority.** The document is headed, vendored as 'InX"IPIT  CONFESSIO  ET  REVOCATIO  EJUSDEM  JoHANNIS / PURVEY  SACERDOTIS  CORAM  DOMIXO  ARCIIIEPISCOPO / CanTUARIENSI,  ET  SUO  PROVIXCIALI  CONCILIO  DE / SUIS  ERRORIBUS  ET  H.F,RESIBUS,  LONDONIIS.' [normalized: *Incipit Confessio et Revocatio Ejusdem Johannis Purvey Sacerdotis coram Domino Archiepiscopo Cantuariensi, et suo Provinciali Concilio, de suis erroribus et haeresibus, Londoniis*] — "Here begins the confession and revocation of the same John Purvey, priest, before the lord Archbishop of Canterbury and his provincial council, concerning his own errors and heresies, at London" (`fasciculi-zizaniorum_shirley1858.txt`, lines 30603–11). Purvey names himself, vendored as "Ego  Johanne.s  Purvey,-  presbjter  indignus" [normalized: *Ego Johannes Purvey, presbyter indignus*], "nuper  suspectas,  infamatus,  accusatus  et  manifeste  convictus" [normalized: *suspectus*, agreeing with the other masculine adjectives] — "lately suspected, defamed, accused, and manifestly convicted" — and submits himself, in his own words, "correctioni, arbitrio, et informationi... reverendi patris" — "to the correction, judgment, and instruction... of the reverend father" (ibid., lines 30616–27). Foxe separately lists him among those who "did shrink; many did revolt and renounce, for danger of the law," and states plainly that he "recanted at Paul's cross" (`foxe_acts-and-monuments-v3_cattley-townsend1837.txt`, lines 20315–17). Nothing in the vendored text shows Purvey composed the recantation formula himself, and nothing in it defends his own authority; it abjures. Separately, "a priest who led the Wycliffite Bible's own revision through the 1390s" is a traditional attribution modern scholarship disputes and this document does not carry forward without a **Contested** tag. What the recantation *does* show — an individual personally processed, by name, before ecclesiastical authority — is the same register as Brute, Thorpe, and Badby, even though what he did under that process was submit rather than defend.
- **Norwich supplies no checked content at all.** Tanner's volume is genuinely inaccessible (§2 above); nothing about *how* Norwich prosecuted its defendants — collectively, individually, or otherwise — is independently on record here. It cannot anchor any characterization of a "communal" register.
- **Amersham, read past its own single sentence, does show a real collective pattern — but a thin, single-sourced, forward-deferred one.** Foxe's aside names readers ("the good martyrs of Amersham... having and reading certain books of Scripture"), hearers ("the others who but only heard them read"), and implicated family members ("the children were compelled to set faggots unto their fathers") together (`foxe_acts-and-monuments-v3_cattley-townsend1837.txt`, lines 32323–29). That is genuine content, not nothing — but it is one polemical aside, not cross-checked against any other source, explicitly deferred by Foxe himself to a later, unvendored volume (normalized: "as when we come to that time... shall hereafter more amply and notoriously appear," ibid., lines 32331–33; vendored OCR given in full at § Ending point, above). It documents a household reading-and-hearing practice; it does not, on its own, establish a distinct *authority structure* set against the individually-examined figures' own structure — the Amersham readers are themselves engaging Scripture directly, exactly as Brute, Thorpe, and Badby each did.

**What the evidence does show, recurring across every named figure and group in this window, is one thing: direct engagement with Scripture in English — read, heard, or personally argued — as the ground of conviction, set against clerical and papal mediation of that authority.** Brute writes his own defense from Scripture and reason; Thorpe defends "the office of priesthood (though we are vnworthie thereto)" — his own account uses "we," not "I," at this specific point — by "authoritie of his owne lawe" and the witness of "great doctors and saints" (`foxe_acts-and-monuments-v3_cattley-townsend1837.txt`, lines 21340–44); Badby refuses to abandon his own reading of what the sacrament is; the Twelve Conclusions argue anticlericalism from Scripture and reason in a collective voice; and the households at Amersham read and hear the same Scripture together. None of this rests on a documented, recurring difference in how authority itself is grounded between any subset of these figures — only on a documented difference in register: some engaged Scripture and defended their conviction alone, under personal examination or in a sustained composition; others engaged it together, in a household or company, visible to us only through a hostile aside or a general complaint about "conventicles" and "schools in men's houses" (`foxe_acts-and-monuments-v3_cattley-townsend1837.txt`, lines 28226–28). That register difference is real, and this document carries it forward as internal plurality under Article 16 — "differing local practices... that emerge from differing communal priorities, pressures, and conditions" — rather than dressing it up as an Article 21 strand the evidence does not support. — **Confidence: Documented** for each named figure's or group's own vendored self-description and mode of engagement; **Widely Accepted** for treating the register difference as plurality within one ecology rather than a strand split; **Contested** for Purvey's traditional role in the Later Version of the Wycliffite Bible.

**Governing consequence for later steps:** with no confirmed strand split, cross-strand gravity testing (Article 21's own mechanism) does not apply; Doc_04's six-test assessment instead tests each candidate gravity against this single ecology directly, including whether it holds across both the individually-argued and household registers named above as internal plurality.

## 5. Preliminary Forces Identification

Per the Forces Framework's Step 1 requirement, this is orientation for Step 2's scope, not the complete forces analysis compiled at Step 8.

**What generated this world:** *External:* the late-medieval English Church's own accumulated temporal wealth and political entanglement — the very condition the Twelve Conclusions' own first article names directly, dating the church's own decline to "when the church of England began to endow itself in temporalities, after its great step-mother, the great Roman Church" (`fasciculi-zizaniorum_shirley1858.txt`, lines 28391 ff., paraphrased from the Latin) — together with the wider disruption of papal authority's own credibility during the Great Western Schism (from 1378), a background condition this document notes as Widely Accepted in general historiography without a specific vendored citation pinning Wyclif's own reaction to it. *Internal:* Wyclif's own prior academic conviction, already developed in his writing on civil and ecclesiastical dominion before this world's own window opens, that true lordship and authority depend on a state of grace rather than on office as such — a conviction that, applied to the sacrament of the altar and to papal claims alike, generates this world's own defining doctrinal departures. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted** for the Schism's background role; **Dominant Modern Reconstruction** for characterizing Wyclif's dominion theology as this world's own specific internal generative seed.

**What larger world was it embedded in:** the late-medieval English Church itself — its parochial and monastic structure, Oxford University as its own intellectual center, and the English crown's own uneasy, shifting patronage of and hostility toward reform, oscillating across the reigns of Richard II, Henry IV, and Henry V. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted.**

**What was it responding to:** clerical wealth (the Twelve Conclusions' own recurring target, from Conclusion I onward, Documented directly against the vendored text) and, per Arundel's own 1409 Constitutions and their restriction of unlicensed English Scripture (§2 above, Historical Pressures), the authorities' own reaction against lay vernacular Scripture access — the constraint the authorities imposed, which is what the vendored record directly attests, rather than a claim about lay believers' own prior access being absent, which none of the Twelve Conclusions addresses. — **Confidence: Documented** for clerical wealth as the Twelve Conclusions' own target; **Documented** for the authorities' restriction of vernacular Scripture as the reaction Arundel's Constitutions attest, not for any claim about the state of lay access itself before that restriction.

**What was it refusing:** transubstantiation, named directly in Conclusion IV — vendored as "onmes Jiommes. nisi smt pauci, m idolatnam" [normalized: *omnes homines, nisi sint pauci, in idolatriam*] within "Quod fictum miraculum sacramenti panis inducit..." — "that the feigned miracle of the sacrament of bread leads all people, except a few, into idolatry," reporting (not quoting) what it calls "the Gospel doctor's" own *Trialogus* as holding instead that the bread of the altar is "habitually" Christ's body (`fasciculi-zizaniorum_shirley1858.txt`, lines 28486–28490); indulgences, named directly in Conclusion IX's own corollary, condemning the pope's "feigned indulgence from penalty and guilt" — vendored as 'fictam indul-' / 'gentiain  a  pcena  et  a"''  culpa' [normalized: *fictam indulgentiam a poena et a culpa*] (ibid., lines 28780–81); and images and pilgrimage, named directly in Conclusion VIII, which calls offerings made to "dumb images of wood or stone" "near akin to idolatry" and singles out an image of the Trinity as "most abominable" (ibid., lines 28674 ff.) — all three confirmed in a single 1395 document rather than scattered across unrelated sources, whose own authorship — lay, clerical, or mixed — the vendored text does not itself settle (§1, §4 above). — **Confidence: Documented**, all three verified directly against the vendored Latin text, with the vendored OCR reproduced where it is garbled rather than silently cleaned up.

## 6. World Continuity & Distinction

**What this world inherits:** Wyclif's own Oxford theology of dominion-by-grace, his denial of transubstantiation, and his conviction that Scripture's own authority is supreme over ecclesiastical tradition — all already developed, in nascent form, before this world's own window opens, and taken up, transformed, and carried forward by a movement that in time exceeded him. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted.**

**What this world transmits forward:** Two things, held at different confidence levels and not conflated. First, and with direct, named textual evidence, outward rather than forward in time: Wyclif's own Latin ecclesiology feeds directly into Jan Hus's *De Ecclesia*, per the Hussite world's own Step 0 record (§1 above). This is transmission *outward*, to a contemporary, geographically separate movement — not this world's own later inheritance, and not something this document resolves as a matter of corpus-map assignment (§7 below carries that question forward). Second, and far more cautiously: the census's own `relationsSummary` names a "Contested influence line to the English Reformation" — a claim this document states in the census's own careful language rather than its own. This document does not assert, anywhere above, that Lollard household networks caused, seeded, or substantially shaped the English Reformation; it names that as a live, unresolved historiographical question, carried forward rather than answered. — **Confidence: Documented** for the Wyclif-to-Hus textual dependency (per the Hussite world's own already-cited record); **Contested**, explicitly, for the English-Reformation influence line.

**Relationship to the Hussite and Bohemian Brethren world:** transmission outward, not inheritance received. Lollardy does not derive from the Hussite movement; Wyclif's own writing predates and partly generates Hus's. This document does not import Hussite formation logic, vocabulary, or ecological findings into Lollardy's own construction, nor does it treat the shared textual root as license to do so — per Constitution Article 3's own governing distinction, a genuine historical transmission relationship between two worlds is not the same thing as "cross-world contamination" in the constitutional sense, which concerns a *builder* importing another world's own vocabulary or formation logic into a world it does not belong to. The transmission here is a fact about the fifteenth century, not a construction shortcut available to whoever builds either world.

**Living heir(s):** none mapped. Per Article 28/29 discipline (§1 above), this document names no living institutional heir for Lollardy — unlike Alexandria's Coptic Orthodox Church or Imperial and Juridical Christianity's papal and Orthodox continuations, no comparable surviving ecclesial body traces its own institutional descent to this world. The only continuity claim on record is the contested Reformation influence-line named above, itself unconfirmed and not self-confirmed here.
