## 1. Candidate identification

Atlas V.5, "Lollardy," c. 1380–1520, England. Grew from the teaching of the Oxford theologian John Wycliffe — who attacked church wealth and papal authority, denied transubstantiation, and argued scripture should be available in English — but, per the census's own already-cleared floorNote, quickly outgrew him: its adherents were mostly ordinary people, artisans and household readers, who owned and shared vernacular scripture and met to read it aloud. Burning for heresy was made law in England in 1401; Archbishop Arundel's 1409 constitutions forbade unlicensed English scripture translation; Sir John Oldcastle's failed 1414 rising made Lollardy treason as well as heresy. The movement survived underground as scattered household networks into the 1520s.

### B2 — Ecology

Strong on primary vernacular text (the Wycliffite Bible itself, ~250 surviving manuscripts, more than any other Middle English text). Arnold Vols. I–II are in fact the Sunday-Gospels/Proprium/Commune-Sanctorum/Ferial-Gospels/Sunday-Epistles preaching cycle — the same material Hudson & Gradon later edited separately as the collective *English Wycliffite Sermons*. Arnold's own preface sets aside a "spurious and doubtful writings" section for disputed items in Shirley's catalogue; separately, modern scholarship generally treats this cycle, and much of Matthew's collection, as Wycliffite (collective) rather than Wyclif's own personal writing — a substantial share of the already-vendored corpus is itself the movement's own collective voice, not Wyclif's own academic writing. The Twelve Conclusions' Latin text is now vendored inside *Fasciculi Zizaniorum*, closing that specific accessibility gap, though its content has not yet been assessed against this movement's own doctrine. The anonymous Bible tracts the census names as "real internal texts" remain unconfirmed for accessibility this pass, since the research brief focused on the sources named in §3 B1 specifically; that gap stands as stated in §4 below.
