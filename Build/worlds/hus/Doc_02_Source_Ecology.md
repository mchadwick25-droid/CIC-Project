## 1. Primary Sources

Eight works are vendored and assigned to this world (`cic/corpus-map/the-hussite-and-bohemian-brethren-movement.yaml`). About 6.1 million characters in all. Only two works, both by Hus, are `tradition` role, and together they are about 1.4 million characters, roughly a quarter of the shelf. Everything else is `context` role: five secondary or hostile works, and one later Unity text.

**Hus's own voice (tradition role), from inside the window:**

- *The Letters of John Hus*, trans. Workman & Pope (1904): 82 letters, 1408–15 (rows 1–6). Some are written in Czech. Workman says 66 of them come from the *Monumenta*, one of those spurious, nine were first printed by Höfler, and the rest by Pez, Erben and Palacký (row 6). The letters reach lay congregations in Prague and Pilsen, nobles and friends, and, from Constance, his last words.
- *De Ecclesia. The Church*, trans. Schaff (1915), Hus's treatise of 1413 (rows 7–9). Large parts follow Wyclif closely (§3).

**Hostile contemporary source (context role):**

- Piccolomini, *Historia Bohemica* (1592 Latin printing; rows 11–12). A Catholic writer, later Pope Pius II. The scan is only partly clean (§7).

**Secondary histories (context role), 1871–1920:** Gillett Vol. II (rows 13–15); Lützow's *Hussite Wars*, *Bohemia*, and *Life & Times of Master John Hus* (rows 16–21). These carry, in English, the texts of the Four Articles, the accounts of the Tábor assemblies, the Compactata, and the Unity's beginnings. All the movement's institutional history reaches this world through them.

**The Unity of the Brethren (context role, outside the window):** the *Ratio Disciplinae* (1632/33) with Seifferth's Introduction and Comenius's Notes (rows 22–24). It is the only Unity text on the shelf and it is 115 years after the window closes. Row 24 (the body of the *Ratio*) is Excluded as a Named Comparandum; rows 22 and 23 (the Introduction and the *Ratio*'s Preface) are Native and licensed only for what the Unity later said about its own origins.

**Vendored but not assigned to this world:** Foxe's *Acts and Monuments*, Vol. III (row 25). Its contents list shows about a hundred pages on Hus: his public notices before leaving for Constance; the testimonial of the papal inquisitor, Bishop Nicholas of Nazareth, that he found Hus "a faithful and a catholic man"; the petitions of the Bohemian nobles and of the University of Prague; Hus's answer to the 26 articles drawn from *De Ecclesia*; his appeal. The volume is assigned only to Lollardy, and its corpus-map note says it "was not checked for Hus/Bohemian content." Its Hus section has now been checked for existence and structure (row 25). It is a 16th-century Protestant martyrology (here in the 1837 Cattley–Townsend edition) that presents Latin documents in English: a second witness, and a partisan one. A request to assign it here is Open_Gaps request R1. Until assigned, the Registry licenses nothing from it.

**What this list does not contain.** No source in Czech. No Utraquist church record, no Taborite text, no Unity text from 1457 to 1517. No text of the Four Articles, the Compactata, or the Prague Articles in the original Latin or Czech (the English of the first comes from Gillett and Lützow). No hymnbook, no catechism, no confession. No document in a woman's voice, and none from a peasant, a townsperson or a Taborite in his or her own words. §6 and §7 state what follows from this.

## 2. Author Gravity Assessment

Five dimensions: Visibility, Representativeness, Influence, Limitations, Transmission History.

**Jan Hus (c. 1369–1415).**
- *Visibility:* Over-visible. He is the only tradition-role voice. Everything the world says in its own words, before the Unity, comes through one man.
- *Representativeness:* He speaks for the Bethlehem preaching movement and for the Prague reformers. He does not speak for the Taborites, who came after him, or for the Unity. Workman reports that Hus's own concern was not the cup: "Hitherto, Hus had taken little interest in the matter" (Letter XLI note, row 3). The chalice is the movement's defining demand, yet Hus is not its author.
- *Influence:* Central. The movement took its name, its martyr and its memory from him. The Unity's own Preface calls its founders his true offspring (row 23).
- *Limitations:* *De Ecclesia* follows Wyclif closely. Schaff: "Huss appropriated paragraph after paragraph from his predecessor." Workman, following Loserth, goes further: the works of Hus are "for the most part mere copies of" Wyclif's (row 10). Hus's originality is contested (§3). The *Letters* are the least derivative voice. Workman writes that "Everything Hus writes is the result of his own soul's experience" in quoting Bishop Creighton, and the letters were kept by his secretary, Peter of Mladoňovice, who is also the author of the eyewitness account of his death (row 30; not vendored).
- *Transmission History:* The *Letters* reach us through Workman & Pope (1904), who translated from Höfler, Erben, Palacký and the *Monumenta*, and they warn that the *Monumenta* text of at least one letter "has been hopelessly doctored" (Letter XVII note). *De Ecclesia* reaches us through Schaff's 1915 translation of the *Historia et Monumenta J. Hus* (Frankfurt, 1715, a reprint of the 1558 edition). Schaff compared the two printings paragraph by paragraph and found no verbal difference, but could not consult a manuscript (row 9). Neither book is vendored in Latin or Czech. Some letters were written in Czech and reach us only in English.

**Aeneas Sylvius Piccolomini (1405–1464, Pope Pius II).**
- *Visibility:* The only contemporary hostile voice on the shelf, and so the only outside witness to the movement's first generation.
- *Representativeness:* He speaks for a Catholic humanist's view, and by his own preface writes of events he says he partly saw and partly heard (the preface, row 11; the scan of that sentence is garbled). He was born in 1405; for 1415 and 1420 he cannot have been an eyewitness. His account of the burnings follows a letter of Poggio Bracciolini that he names in the text.
- *Influence:* Gillett quotes him at length on the constancy of Hus and Jerome at the stake (pp. 73–74, row 14): "They went to their punishment as to a feast."
- *Limitations:* Lützow, in the preface to *The Hussite Wars*, says Aeneas "often attacks his enemies in a distinctly unfair manner" and lays "great stress on the appearance of the Adamite fanatics." His tone is plain in the sect chapter's heading, *De perfida secta Hussitarum* (roughly, the treacherous sect of the Hussites).
- *Transmission History:* A 1592 printing, whose title page says it was reprinted from an old codex. The vendored file's header calls it a second witness by an older rule; the current rule is that scan quality decides. The scan has long s as f, interleaved marginal notes, and misreadings (row 11). The dedicatee is not legible.

**David S. Schaff (translator of *De Ecclesia*, 1915).** He is the best guide to the Wyclif dependence and a partisan of the "debt to Wyclif" view. His introduction adds an argument of his own, that Hus and Luther set a principle against the inquisition that later Reformers, including Calvin and Bullinger, failed to keep. That is a Protestant reading and not evidence about Hus.

**Herbert B. Workman and R. Martin Pope (the *Letters*, 1904).** Workman's title page names him as the author of *The Age of Wyclif* and *The Age of Hus*. His introduction is admiring of Hus the man and severe on Hus the writer. The editorial notes are the source for the Bethlehem appointment, the chalice and the Waldensians (rows 2–3).

**Count Francis Lützow (1909, 1914, 1920).** He writes English for an English-speaking readership, as a Bohemian; his preface argues that the movement "depended by no means entirely on foreign influences" and that the view of Hussitism as "merely artificially fostered Wycliffism" is "nonsense" (quoting Kybal; row 20). His account of the Unity leans on Goll (row 33), not on the Unity's own records. He cites Palacký, Goll and Kybal throughout and foregrounds the struggle between Slav and Teuton (he says Hussite wars "form part of the great struggle between Slav and Teuton"); that emphasis is his framework and must not be treated as the movement's own.

**Ezra Hall Gillett (Vol. II, 1871).** An American author (the volume was printed in Boston in 1871). His note says the substance of ch. XV first appeared in the *Presbyterian Quarterly Review* for June 1856. He writes with sympathy for Hus and for the Calixtines' moderation and with less sympathy for Tábor ("desperate and fanatical courage"). He is the source for the full text of the Four Articles in English and for the Tábor assemblies. His footnote names a chronicle by a "chancellor of New Prague," which is identified here as Lawrence of Březová's; the scan of that footnote is garbled, so the identification is Widely Accepted and not proved from the scan.

**Bishop B. Seifferth and Jan Amos Comenius (the *Ratio* volume).** Seifferth (1866) is a bishop of the Brethren's Church of his day (the Moravian Church, on the census's account; the title-page scan is garbled) and edits as an insider. His Introduction gives the 1457 founding and the 1467 Waldensian ordination as history. Comenius (1660) supplies the Notes, and their tone is that of the last bishop of an extinct church; Note [2] states that no single individual founded the Unity but many pious men acting together (the scan garbles the word *individual*). The 1633 Preface speaks for the Unity's institution. All three write about the window from outside it.

## 3. Secondary Scholarship Assessment

All secondary scholarship on the shelf was written between 1871 and 1920. None of it is modern specialist work (Kaminsky 1967, Fudge 2002 and 2010, and the *Cambridge History* chapters are named in the census and are not vendored: rows 31, 36, 37). Modern claims below rest on 19th-century and early 20th-century works and are marked accordingly.

**1. How original was Hus? Contested.** Schaff and Workman, following Loserth, stress that Hus took text from Wyclif. Lützow quotes Kybal ("The view that Hussitism is merely artificially fostered Wycliffism appears to me logically and historically as nonsense") and stresses earlier Bohemian reformers: Waldhauser, Milíč and Matthew of Janov. Schaff himself records that Loserth revised his earlier judgment after Flajšhans's edition of Hus's *Super IV. Sententiarum* (1905). The textual dependence of *De Ecclesia* on Wyclif is Widely Accepted. Whether Hussitism as a whole is "Wycliffism" is Contested. Both sides are carried.

**2. The 1467 ordination by Waldensians. Contested.** Seifferth's Introduction and the *Ratio*'s Preface say the Brethren sent a deputation to a Waldensian community in Austria, which consecrated three of their ministers as bishops. Lützow says a Brother Michael "on insufficient evidence is said to have been ordained by a Waldensian bishop," and adds that "evidence is rather against the influence of the Waldenses on the foundation of the 'Unity,'" calling it "one of the most controverted points in Bohemian history." The *Ratio*'s Preface says the Waldensian leader, Bishop Stephen, was burned at Vienna before a proposed union could be completed. The census's story of the ordination by lot rests on the Brethren's *Acta*, which are not vendored, and the census marks it "from reference summaries; primary text not yet read" (row 39). Nothing in the vendored texts mentions a lot.

**3. Were the Brethren descended from the Taborites? Contested.** Lützow: "The connection of the Taborites with the Bohemian Brethren was long a disputed point in Bohemian history; all recent Bohemian historians, however, maintain its existence." He adds that Goll reports the Brethren "did not wish to be considered as continuators of the Taborites." Carried at full strength.

**4. The Adamites. Dominant Modern Reconstruction.** Lützow (1914) says the Adamites "had no connection whatever with Hussitism," that the first mention in Bohemia is a 1409 letter to the archbishop, and that the chronicler Březová, "almost our sole authority," is not impartial. This rests on Nedoma's article of 1891 and is unchecked against later work.

**5. The origin of the lay chalice. Contested.** Workman: two Waldensians from Dresden, Peter and Nicholas, persuaded Jakoubek in the summer of 1414 (row 3). Piccolomini: Jakoubek found communion in both kinds praised in old codices, chiefly Dionysius and Cyprian (row 11). Not necessarily exclusive.

**6. Hus and the chalice.** Documented in the vendored edition, not disputed there: Hus "had taken little interest" until late and came to it "decisively" only under Jakoubek's influence; the Bethlehem itself, under priest Hawlik, at first protested the innovation.

## 4. Formation Narrative Sources

- **Hus's own letters** are the best narrative of one life's formation: a peasant's son, ordained in 1400 (by Workman's account, from Hus's own statement, for "the comfortable life led by the clergy"), then a preacher who tells his disciple Martin to "flee the society of women" and to leave off "fine clothes" and chess (Letter XXXV; Workman's contents). Tier 1 in the Framework's four-tier story scheme (Documented to Widely Accepted at the narrative level) where the letters themselves speak; Tier 3 where Workman fills in.
- **The Constance narrative** reaches us in three tiers of proximity. Hus's own last letters (Tier 1); Workman's and Gillett's accounts (secondary, sympathetic); Piccolomini's tribute (hostile, and drawn from Poggio, so second-hand). The eyewitness, Peter of Mladoňovice, is not vendored (row 30). The census's Tier 1 story "The Sixth of July at Constance" rests on him and on Richental, and its own verification field says the primary text was not read (rows 30, 40).
- **The Unity's origin stories** exist here as the Unity's later self-account: the *Ratio*'s Preface (1633) and Comenius's Note [2] (1660). By the Framework's scheme these are Tier 3 (attributed tradition): they show what the Unity believed formation produced, not what happened in 1457. The census's Kunvald lot story is Tier 2 by the census's own rating, rests on sources not vendored, and is not licensed (row 39).
- **Tábor's assemblies** (1419) are described by Gillett from a Utraquist chronicler and by Piccolomini from the other side (about thirty thousand people at Bechyně, in his text). Tier 3: the Utraquist chronicler was hostile to Tábor. Their distinctive detail (men, women and children in separate groups; "brother and sister"; no instruments) is a Utraquist observer's report.
- **The martyrs of 1412** who were carried to the Bethlehem to the hymn *Isti sunt sancti*, with women dipping their handkerchiefs in the blood (Workman), are reported by Workman as a formation scene; the tier waits on his source, which the passage does not name.

## 5. Material Culture and Daily Life Sources

- **Vendored:** none. There is no archaeology, no town record, no manuscript description.
- **Named in the vendored texts:** the Bethlehem Chapel, "a vast building destroyed by the Jesuits in 1786" (Workman); the map of the Tábor assemblies (Gillett); the Unity's household order, as the *Ratio* of 1632/33 codified it (row 24: excluded).
- **Social background** is drawn from Lützow and Gillett at secondary level: the German–Czech divide in Prague and the university; the utraquist nobles' confederacy under Vladislav II; peasants' rights under the 1485 peace ("a certain amount of religious liberty was also granted to the peasants whose faith differed from that of the lords on whose estates they lived"; Lützow, *Bohemia*, row 18).
- **Surrounding religious environment:** a Catholic majority in many towns and on many estates; the Waldensians; Jews in Prague. Jews appear in the vendored evidence only as victims (Lützow: "a large number of Germans and Jews were massacred"). No Jewish voice is on the shelf.
- **The gap is stated, not filled.** The material conditions of an Utraquist parish, a Tábor household, or a Unity congregation at Kunvald cannot be reconstructed from what is vendored.

## 6. Source Asymmetries and Missing Voices

Under Article 20 the primary duty is to name whose voices the sources structurally omit, and why.

**Overrepresented:** Hus; the Prague university and clerical elite; the Council of Constance's own procedure; the Utraquist nobility, through the Four Articles and the Compactata; English-language 19th-century historians.

**Structurally missing, and why:**

- **Women.** Women appear: the queen (Lützow), the nuns of Letter II, "women without number" who rallied to Hus (Workman), the women who dipped handkerchiefs in the martyrs' blood, and, at Tábor, the women's group in the assemblies. None speaks in her own words. Where a woman is addressed by Hus, it is as an object of caution (Letter XXXV: "flee the society of women"). The record omits them because the surviving sources are written by clergy and university men about clergy and university concerns. This is a structural absence, not a finding that women's role was small.
- **Ordinary lay people, peasants and townspeople.** The Tábor assemblies, the parish communions, the Prague disturbances before the 1485 peace are seen only through observers. Hus's Czech-language letters to "the faithful Bohemians" address them but do not record their answers.
- **The Taborites.** The whole strand is seen through Utraquist chroniclers, a hostile Italian bishop, and 19th-century historians. Its own articles are known only as summarised by Gillett. This is the largest asymmetry inside the world.
- **The early Unity.** No Unity text from 1457 to 1517 is vendored. Everything on the shelf about it is later or secondary.
- **Czech speakers.** The movement's own language is not on the shelf at all. Every Czech-language letter, sermon and hymn reaches us in English, at one remove. The Czech-language originals count as primary evidence under the project's ruling, and none is vendored (Open_Gaps requests R2 to R7).
- **Jews and Germans in Bohemia.** They appear as victims of Hussite violence in the vendored evidence (1419; the years before the 1485 peace). Their account of the period is absent.

**Evidential visibility is not ecological visibility.** The world's most formative material is likely the parish preaching, the Bethlehem congregation, the household, and the Unity's small communities. The shelf's evidence is the treatise, the trial and the war. Reconstruction that reaches past the evidence is marked Inferential/Thin. A "bounded reconstruction" of any marginalised voice needs a specific textual trace and is not attempted here.

## 7. Required Disclosure

**1. The Unity's own in-window voice is absent (Step 0 §4 item 2, binding).** No confession, catechism, letter, discipline ordinance or hymnbook by the Unity from 1457 to 1517 is on the shelf. Seifferth's Introduction names two documents in the window, the 1504 Confession to King Vladislav and the 1508 letters to Dr. Augustine, and says they were first printed in a folio volume of 1535 (the title, *Fasciculus rerum expetendarum et fugiendarum*, is legible in the scan at line 8268 of the file; the other mention is garbled). Requests are in `Open_Gaps_Tracking.md`. Any claim about the Unity before 1517 rests on secondary or later voices and is tagged accordingly.

**2. The *Ratio* is 1632/33 practice, not 1457–1517 practice (row 24).** The household order, the catechesis, the office of "matrons," the ordination forms and the hymn practice it records are the Unity of the Thirty Years' War era. They belong to VI.24. A voice for this world must not draw on them as though they described the 15th century.

**3. The Latin Piccolomini is only partly usable as a primary source (row 11).** The current rule is that a clean original can be primary and a garbled scan stays a second witness. This scan has systematic long-s errors, which the quote gate can normalise, but it also has interleaved marginal notes and real misreadings that no normalisation removes. Short clean phrases can be quoted. Long passages need the page image. The corpus-map note says the scan "is clean, not flagged as garbled"; that is not what reading it shows. Open_Gaps item 3 raises this.

**4. The English of the Four Articles is a 19th-century translation.** Gillett and Lützow give them in English. Both say they are quoting; neither gives the Latin or Czech, and Lützow notes that a more extensive version exists "containing further statements and many biblical quotations." The text a voice may quote is not yet the original.

**5. The 1662 *Appeale* pamphlet** was checked and closed for unusable OCR, not for absence (row 38). A future pass could revisit it by re-OCR. The vendored *Letters* already carry Hus's appeal language (row 4).

**6. Foxe (row 25)** is on the shelf and not yet assigned here (request R1).

**7. Living tradition.** The Seifferth volume is edited by a bishop of the Moravian Church, and its Notes come from the last bishop of the old Unity. Whatever this world says of the Unity is mediated through a living tradition's own account of itself (Doc_01 §1).
