## 1. Primary Sources

Twenty-three files are vendored and assigned to this world (`cic/corpus-map/the-hussite-and-bohemian-brethren-movement.yaml`: 24 entries, because Brown's *Fasciculus* carries two). They come to 35,341,097 characters (36,047,134 bytes). The count was made two ways, with Python `len()` and with `wc -m` under `LC_ALL=C.UTF-8`, and both agree (Registry, "The corpus, counted two ways"). Three files are assigned whole and concern this world only in part, so the working figure is 22,880,482 characters. The tradition role holds 12,852,753 of them (56 per cent). Of those, 8,919,310 (39 per cent of the working corpus) are in eight files that the corpus map treats as quotable once each wording is checked against the page image. The other 3,933,443 are second-witness or attributed. Hus's two works in English together are only 6 per cent of the working corpus.

Every file has a witness status (Registry, "Original-language rows and witness status"). A *first witness* is a legible original that can be quoted once the page image is checked. A *second witness* is a scan whose wording is too unreliable to quote. No page image was available for this document, so every quotation below is matched to the OCR text only.

**First witness: Hus and one lay contemporary (tradition role).**

- *The Letters of John Hus*, trans. Workman & Pope (1904): 82 letters, 1408–15, in English (rows 1–6 and 42). Some were written in Czech. Workman says 66 of them come from the *Monumenta*, one of those spurious, nine were first printed by Höfler, and the rest by Pez, Erben and Palacký (row 6).
- The same letters in Latin and Czech: Palacký, *Documenta Mag. Joannis Hus* (1869; rows 55–57), and Novotný's critical *Korespondence a dokumenty* (1920; rows 58–59). Palacký's volume also holds the Latin of Peter of Mladoňovice's eyewitness *Relatio* of the trial and burning (row 56).
- *De Ecclesia. The Church*, trans. Schaff (1915), Hus's treatise of 1413, in English (rows 7–9). Large parts follow Wyclif closely (§3). The Latin original is not on the shelf.
- Hus's Czech works, ed. Erben (1865–68; rows 60–64): the exposition of the creed, the Czech *Postilla* and shorter works, among them the *Dcerka* written for a woman reader.
- Petr Chelčický, *Síť víry* (*The Net of Faith*), in Czech (rows 65–66).

**Second witness or attributed (tradition role).**

- The *Tractatus responsivus*, a Latin tract that its editor attributes to Hus from tone and content and not from a manuscript ascription (row 67).
- Lawrence of Březová, *Historia Hussitica*, ed. Goll (1893; rows 68–69): an Utraquist chronicler, hostile to Tábor. His chronicle prints the Four Articles with proofs, twelve articles of the Taborites and a list of the Taborite priests' articles.
- The Brethren's Latin confession to King Vladislav and their *Excusatio*, in Brown's 1690 print of the *Fasciculus rerum expetendarum* (rows 70–71).

**Hostile contemporary source (context role).** Piccolomini, *Historia Bohemica*, in a 1592 printing and a 1524 printing (rows 11–12 and 72). A Catholic writer, later Pope Pius II. Whether either scan can be quoted is not yet ruled (§7).

**The wars' own documents (context role).** Palacký, *Urkundliche Beiträge zur Geschichte des Hussitenkrieges*, vols. 1 and 2 (1873; rows 74–77). They include the papal legate's Latin reply to the Four Articles, which quotes them (row 74), and documents on the making of the Compactata (row 76). For the Taborite documents that were read, Palacký prints only a German summary and a pointer to the *Archiv český*.

**Secondary histories (context role), 1837–1920.** Gillett Vol. II (rows 13–15); Lützow's *Hussite Wars*, *Bohemia*, and *Life & Times of Master John Hus* (rows 16–21); the Hus section of Foxe's *Acts and Monuments*, vol. III (row 78); and the Hus and Taborite sections of Van Braght's *Martyrs Mirror* (row 79). Lützow and Gillett carry, in English, the texts of the Four Articles, the accounts of the Tábor assemblies, the Compactata and the Unity's beginnings. Foxe and Van Braght are at second or third hand and are partisan.

**The Unity of the Brethren (context role, almost wholly outside the window).** The *Ratio Disciplinae* (1632/33) with Seifferth's Introduction and Comenius's Notes (rows 22–24 and 43). It is 115 years after the window closes. Row 24 (the body of the *Ratio*) is Excluded as a Named Comparandum. Rows 22 and 23 (the Introduction and the *Ratio*'s Preface) are Native and licensed only for what the Unity later said about its own origins. Row 43 holds two short quotations from the Brethren's 1508 document. Comenius's *Historia Fratrum Bohemorum* (1702) is the Unity's later account of its own origins, on the shelf as a second witness (row 73).

**Other Hus material on the shelf.** Some vendored works assigned to other worlds also hold Hus or Hussite material. They are not evidence for this world, and no Registry row licenses anything from them. They are recorded as candidates in `Open_Gaps_Tracking.md` (section E and entry 27). Placing any of them here needs the project lead's authorisation.

**What the shelf still lacks.** No Latin original of *De Ecclesia*. No Utraquist church record. No Taborite text in the Taborites' own voice: their articles reach us through Březová's hostile chronicle, and their documents of the wars, where they were read, as Palacký's German summaries. No clean text of the Four Articles as the Praguers issued them: they are quoted in a legate's reply (row 74) and in Březová's garbled scan (row 68). No Unity text from 1457 to 1517 at first-witness quality, and no Unity hymnbook, catechism or discipline ordinance from the window. No document in a woman's voice, and none from a peasant, a townsperson or a Taborite in his or her own words. §6 and §7 state what follows from this.

## 2. Author Gravity Assessment

Five dimensions: Visibility, Representativeness, Influence, Limitations, Transmission History.

**Jan Hus (c. 1369–1415).**
- *Visibility:* Over-visible. He is the main tradition-role voice, and in Latin and Czech as well as English. Chelčický is the only other tradition-role author at first witness (rows 65–66). Everything the world says in its own words, before the Unity, still comes mainly through one man.
- *Representativeness:* He speaks for the Bethlehem preaching movement and for the Prague reformers. He does not speak for the Taborites, who came after him, or for the Unity. On the cup, Workman reports that Hus came to it late: "Hitherto, Hus had taken little interest in the matter", and he "soon committed himself decisively to the opinions of Jakoubek" (note before Letter XLIII, row 3). From Constance, on 21 June 1415, Hus told Hawlik not to oppose "the sacrament of the Lord's cup" (Letter LXXI, row 42). The chalice is the movement's defining demand, and Hus defended it at the end. But Jakoubek, not Hus, began it in Prague. The Latin of that letter is on the shelf (Palacký no. 80 and Novotný no. 141; rows 55 and 58), and in it Hus says he wrote arguments for the cup at Constance. Novotný also prints a letter (no. 95), which he dates to November 1414, urging a priest to promote communion in both kinds. Its date, its addressee and, on Palacký's judgment, its authorship are uncertain (row 58). Whether Hus took little interest before his imprisonment is therefore Contested (§3 item 6).
- *Influence:* Central. The movement took its name, its martyr and its memory from him. The Unity's own Preface calls its founders his true offspring (row 23).
- *Limitations:* *De Ecclesia* follows Wyclif closely. Schaff: "Huss appropriated paragraph after paragraph from his predecessor." Workman, following Loserth, goes further: the works of Hus are "for the most part mere copies of" Wyclif's (row 10). Hus's originality is contested (§3). The *Letters* are the least derivative voice. Workman quotes Bishop Creighton's judgment: "Everything Hus writes is the result of his own soul's experience". The letters were kept by his secretary, Peter of Mladoňovice, who is also the author of the eyewitness account of his death (row 56, in Latin).
- *Transmission History:* The *Letters* reach us in English through Workman & Pope (1904), who translated from Höfler, Erben, Palacký and the *Monumenta*. They warn that the *Monumenta* text of at least one letter "has been hopelessly doctored" (note to Letter XX). The Latin and Czech letters are also on the shelf, in Palacký's *Documenta* (1869) and Novotný's critical edition (1920). Both name the manuscripts they used, among them the so-called Mladoňovic manuscript (rows 55, 58). For the letter to Hawlik the Latin agrees with Workman's English (Letter LXXI), except that "of the bread" is Workman's rendering (row 55). Erben's edition of 1868 called two of the cup letters spurious, and Palacký and Novotný print them from the manuscripts (row 63). A Czech text of a letter can be a later translation of the Latin, which Novotný marks (nos. 95* and 139*; row 58). *De Ecclesia* reaches us through Schaff's 1915 translation of the *Historia et Monumenta J. Hus*. Schaff used the Frankfurt edition of 1715, a reprint of an earlier edition of 1558, and he says the earlier one was printed at Frankfurt (row 48). Schaff compared the two printings paragraph by paragraph and found no verbal difference, but could not consult a manuscript (row 9). The Latin *De Ecclesia* is not on the shelf.

**Aeneas Sylvius Piccolomini (1405–1464, Pope Pius II).**
- *Visibility:* The only contemporary hostile voice on the shelf, and so the only outside witness to the movement's first generation.
- *Representativeness:* He speaks for a Catholic humanist's view. His preface is headed "AD ALPHONSVM REGEM ARAGONVM", so it is addressed to King Alfonso of Aragon (row 11). It says that the matters he reports were partly seen and partly heard. The Latin is legible with OCR noise (about line 421 of the file). He was born in 1405, so for 1415 and 1420 he cannot have been an eyewitness. His account of the burnings follows a letter of Poggio Bracciolini that he names in the text.
- *Influence:* Gillett quotes him at length on the constancy of Hus and Jerome at the stake (pp. 73–74, row 14): "They went to their punishment as to a feast."
- *Limitations:* Lützow, in the preface to *The Hussite Wars*, says Aeneas "often attacks his enemies in a distinctly unfair manner" and lays "great stress on the appearance of the Adamite fanatics". His tone is plain in the sect chapter's heading, *De perfida secta Hussitarum* (roughly, the treacherous sect of the Hussites).
- *Transmission History:* A 1592 printing, whose title page says it was reprinted from an old codex. The vendored file's header calls it a second witness by an older rule. The current rule is that scan quality decides (§7). The scan has long s as f, interleaved marginal notes, repeated page runs and misreadings (row 11). A second printing, Cologne 1524, is also on the shelf and misreads many letters (row 72).

**David S. Schaff (translator of *De Ecclesia*, 1915).** He is the best guide to the Wyclif dependence and a partisan of the "debt to Wyclif" view. His introduction adds an argument of his own: that Hus and Luther set a principle against the inquisition that later Reformers, including Calvin and Bullinger, failed to keep. That is a Protestant reading and not evidence about Hus.

**Herbert B. Workman and R. Martin Pope (the *Letters*, 1904).** Workman's title page names him as the author of *The Age of Wyclif* and *The Age of Hus*. His introduction is admiring of Hus the man and severe on Hus the writer. The editorial notes are the source for the Bethlehem appointment, the chalice and the Waldensians (rows 2–3).

**Count Francis Lützow (1909, 1914, 1920).** He writes English for an English-speaking readership, as a Bohemian. His preface argues that the movement "depended by no means entirely on foreign influences" and that the view of Hussitism as "merely artificially fostered Wycliffism" is "nonsense" (quoting Kybal; row 20). His account of the Unity leans on Goll (row 33), not on the Unity's own records. He cites Palacký, Goll and Kybal throughout. He also foregrounds the struggle between Slav and Teuton. He credits Droysen with showing that the Hussite wars "form part of the great struggle between Slav and Teuton" (*Hussite Wars*, row 17). That emphasis is his framework and must not be treated as the movement's own.

**Ezra Hall Gillett (Vol. II, 1871).** An American author (the volume was printed in Boston in 1871). His note says the substance of ch. XV first appeared in the *Presbyterian Quarterly Review* for June 1856. He writes with sympathy for Hus and for the Calixtines' moderation and with less sympathy for Tábor ("desperate and fanatical courage"). He is the source for the full text of the Four Articles in English and for the Tábor assemblies. He calls his account of the assemblies "the Calixtine narrative". His footnote names a *Diarium*, whose author, it says, was chancellor of New Prague. The two columns of that footnote are interleaved in the scan, so the wording is garbled. This document's identification of that author with Lawrence of Březová is its own inference, tagged Inferential/Thin, and it is not proved from the scan.

**Palacký, Novotný and Erben (editors of the Latin and Czech originals).** Palacký (*Documenta*, 1869; *Urkundliche Beiträge*, 1873) prints Hus's letters and the documents of the trial from manuscripts, with Latin prefaces and notes of his own. In the *Beiträge* he gives a German summary and a pointer to the *Archiv český* where the text is printed there. Novotný (1920) gives a critical text with an apparatus of manuscripts and Czech notes, and marks a letter as uncertain where Palacký did. Erben (1865–68) edited Hus's Czech works from the oldest known sources, and his notes in vol. 3 judge two cup letters spurious (row 63). Each editor's prefaces and notes are his own and are not Hus's.

**Lawrence of Březová (chronicler, to 1422).**
- *Visibility:* The main source on Tábor's first years, on the Four Articles with their proofs, and on the Adamites. Lützow calls him "almost our sole authority" for the Adamites.
- *Representativeness:* An Utraquist, close to the Prague university. Goll's note reports a manuscript title that calls him chancellor of the New Town of Prague (the scan is garbled; row 68).
- *Influence:* What the Taborite programme looks like on the shelf is what he chose to copy.
- *Limitations:* Lützow says his "hatred of the Taborites was even intenser than his hatred of the adherents of the Church of Rome". He copies the Taborite priests' articles into a chronicle that is hostile to them.
- *Transmission History:* Goll's edition of 1893 prints the Latin with a contemporary Czech version and variants. The scan is a second witness. The Latin is heavily misread, and the Czech reads better (row 68).

**Petr Chelčický.**
- *Visibility:* The only tradition-role author besides Hus at first witness.
- *Representativeness:* A layman who writes for himself. On Lützow's account, through Goll, his teaching lies behind the Unity's early refusal of war. That link is Lützow's (row 18), and the *Net* does not show it.
- *Influence:* The Unity's intellectual origin, on the census's and Lützow's account.
- *Limitations:* His chapters on whether killing is ever lawful were located and not read through (row 65).
- *Transmission History:* The text is the 1521 printing, edited in 1912. The 1521 Preface is in the third person and is not his (row 65).

**The Brethren of the Unity (the Latin confession and the *Excusatio*).**
- *Visibility:* The only texts in the Unity's own name from inside the window that are on the shelf.
- *Representativeness:* An institutional address to the king, not a private voice.
- *Limitations:* The print's headings call the writers *Fratres Waldenses*, a label that Comenius says the Brethren never accepted. The print does not settle whether its text is that of 1504 or of 1508. The scan is a second witness (row 70).
- *Transmission History:* The Brethren's Latin reached Gratius's collection of 1535 and then Brown's reprint of 1690. A Catholic compiler chose to print it.

**Foxe and Van Braght.** Foxe (an Elizabethan martyrology, here in an 1837 edition) and Van Braght (a Dutch martyrology of 1660, here in an 1886 English edition) write about Hus and the Taborites long after them, from partisan positions and through intermediate sources (rows 78–79). Van Braght counts the Taborites among the Waldenses and adds Hus to a non-swearing lineage. Those claims belong to the Anabaptist reading and not to Hus.

**Bishop B. Seifferth and Jan Amos Comenius (the *Ratio* volume).** Seifferth (1866) is a bishop of the Brethren's Church of his day (the Moravian Church, on the census's account; the title-page scan is garbled). He edits as an insider. His Introduction gives the 1457 founding and the 1467 Waldensian ordination as history. Comenius (1660) supplies the Notes, and their tone is that of the last bishop of an extinct church. Note [2] states that no single individual founded the Unity but many pious men acting together (the scan garbles the word *individual*). The 1633 Preface speaks for the Unity's institution. All three write about the window from outside it. Comenius's *Historia Fratrum Bohemorum*, printed at Halle in 1702 with a preface by Buddeus, is also by the last bishop of the old Unity. It is a second witness with heavy misreadings (row 73).

## 3. Secondary Scholarship Assessment

All secondary scholarship on the shelf was written between 1837 and 1920. None of it is modern specialist work. Kaminsky (1967), Fudge (1998, 2002 and 2010), Šmahel (2002), Brock (1957) and the *Cambridge History* chapters are named in the census or in the standard bibliographies and are not vendored (rows 31, 36, 37, 52–54). Modern claims below rest on 19th-century and early 20th-century works and are marked accordingly.

**1. How original was Hus? Contested.** Schaff and Workman, following Loserth (row 44), stress that Hus took text from Wyclif. Lützow quotes Kybal ("The view that Hussitism is merely artificially fostered Wycliffism appears to me logically and historically as nonsense"; rows 20, 46) and stresses earlier Bohemian reformers: Waldhauser, Milíč and Matthew of Janov. Schaff himself records that Loserth revised his earlier judgment after Flajšhans's edition of Hus's *Super IV. Sententiarum* (1905; row 47). The textual dependence of *De Ecclesia* on Wyclif is Widely Accepted. Whether Hussitism as a whole is "Wycliffism" is Contested. Both sides are carried. The *Tractatus responsivus*, which its editor attributes to Hus from tone and content, also takes "a good deal" from Wyclif's *De potestate pape*, in its editor's words (row 67). The attribution is Thomson's argument and not a manuscript ascription, so the tract is not counted as evidence on either side.

**2. The 1467 ordination by Waldensians. Contested.** Seifferth's Introduction and the *Ratio*'s Preface say the Brethren sent a deputation to a Waldensian community in Austria, which consecrated three of their ministers as bishops. Lützow says a Brother Michael "on insufficient evidence is said to have been ordained by a Waldensian bishop". He gives no year for it. He adds that "evidence is rather against the influence of the Waldenses on the foundation of the 'Unity,'" and calls it "one of the most controverted points in Bohemian history". The *Ratio*'s Preface says the Waldensian leader, Bishop Stephen, was burned at Vienna before a proposed union could be completed. Comenius's history of 1702 (row 73, second witness) tells the story at more length. It has nine men chosen by vote, twelve sealed slips (nine blank and three inscribed) given out among them, and three men ordained by the Waldensians' Bishop Stephen. It adds that the Brethren never denied having received the power of ordaining, and with it an outward succession, from the Waldensians, though at times they kept silent about it (the scan garbles the last words). The census's story of the ordination by lot rests on the Brethren's *Acta*, which are not vendored, and the census marks it "from reference summaries; primary text not yet read" (row 39). Whether the census's story and Comenius's drawing of slips are the same is not established. In the vendored texts only Comenius, a partisan voice writing about 190 years later, describes a lot.

**3. Were the Brethren descended from the Taborites? Contested.** Lützow: "The connection of the Taborites with the Bohemian Brethren was long a disputed point in Bohemian history; all recent Bohemian historians, however, maintain its existence." He adds that Goll reports the Brethren "did not wish to be considered as continuators of the Taborites", and that they protested against this theory "with a degree of energy which was not justified by the facts of the case". Carried at full strength.

**4. The Adamites. Contested.** Lützow (1914) says the Adamites "had no connection whatever with Hussitism". He says the first mention in Bohemia is a 1409 letter to the archbishop, and that the chronicler Březová, "almost our sole authority", is not impartial. This rests on Nedoma's article of 1891 (row 45) and is unchecked against later work. Against it stands Březová's own account, as Lützow reports it. On that account, the moderate Taborite party expelled more than 200 people from Tábor itself, and these people went on to the nudism that Piccolomini and Březová describe. The Adamites' link to Tábor is therefore disputed inside the vendored evidence. Březová's own text is on the shelf (row 68). His Latin says that a group of both sexes, cast out from the brethren's community at Tábor, settled on an island and fell into errors under a peasant who called himself Moses (the scan garbles the key words), and his Czech describes their nude dances and their shameful death in 1421 (the year is read from a noisy line). That supports the expulsion from Tábor, at second-witness level. Lützow's figure of more than 200 was not found in these passages. No modern specialist work is vendored, and this pass did not check one. Both readings are carried.

**5. The origin of the lay chalice. Contested.** Workman: two Waldensians from Dresden, Peter and Nicholas, persuaded Jakoubek in the summer of 1414 (row 3). Piccolomini: Jakoubek found communion in both kinds praised in old codices, chiefly Dionysius and Cyprian (row 11). Not necessarily exclusive. Goll's Czech note in the Březová volume (row 68) records that the Breslau manuscript of the chronicle makes Peter of Dresden, not Jakoubek, the first impulse to the cup, and asks whether that passage was added later, as Böhringer and Palacký held. The note is noisy and was read in outline.

**6. Hus and the chalice. Documented that he defended it from prison; Contested that he came to it late.** Workman's note says that "Hitherto, Hus had taken little interest in the matter" and that he "soon committed himself decisively to the opinions of Jakoubek". The note also says that the Bethlehem itself, under priest Hawlik, at first protested the innovation. Hus's own letter to Hawlik of 21 June 1415 asks him not to oppose the cup and to "prepare to suffer for the eating of the bread and the communion of the cup" (Letter LXXI, row 42). The Latin is on the shelf: "Noli resistere sacramento calicis domini" and "praepara te ad passionem in manducatione et communione calicis" (Palacký no. 80, row 55), and "Motiva pro communione calicis aestimo dabunt tibi, quae scripsi in Constantia" says he wrote arguments for the cup. Novotný prints the letter as no. 141 from six manuscripts, including the so-called Mladoňovic manuscript (row 58). A second letter, to his friends, protests that the council condemns as error the laity's drinking from the Lord's cup (row 55). Erben (1868) judged both letters spurious. He gave three reasons: the Czech translation is dated 1414, when Hus was still in Bohemia; Hus mentions the matter in no other letter of those weeks; and Mladoňovic would have included them (row 63). The Latin manuscripts and the critical edition go against him, so the defence from prison rests on the letter's own text. That Hus took little interest earlier is Workman's judgment of 1904. Against it stands Novotný's no. 95, a letter urging a priest to promote "communionem utriusque speciei" (Latin, row 58), which he dates to November 1414. No manuscript of it survives, and its text rests on the printed *Opera*. Its date, its addressee and, on Palacký's judgment, its authorship are uncertain. Novotný himself reads it as Hus's, written at Constance "když Hus přijímání pod obojí byl již schválil", and says Hus in prison spoke of the cup "zdrželivě", with reserve, on a question where he had earlier been more decided. Both readings are carried.

## 4. Formation Narrative Sources

- **Hus's own letters** are the best narrative of one life's formation: a peasant's son, ordained in 1400 (by Workman's account, from Hus's own statement, for "the comfortable life led by the clergy"), then a preacher who tells his disciple Martin to "flee the society of women" and to leave off "fine clothes" and chess (Letter XXXV; Workman's contents). Tier 1 in the Framework's four-tier story scheme (Documented to Widely Accepted at the narrative level) where the letters themselves speak. Tier 3 where Workman fills in. The Latin and Czech originals are on the shelf (rows 55–59). Hus's Czech *Dcerka*, written for a woman who had vowed virginity, and his Czech exposition of the creed show him teaching in the vernacular (rows 60 and 63).
- **The Constance narrative** reaches us in three tiers of proximity. Hus's own last letters are Tier 1. The eyewitness, Peter of Mladoňovice, is on the shelf in Latin (row 56), and his Part V, on the condemnation and the burning, is the eyewitness account there. Workman's and Gillett's accounts are secondary and sympathetic. Piccolomini's tribute is hostile, and drawn from Poggio, so second-hand. Workman's narrative and the Latin agree on Hus's answer at the stake and on his hymn, except that Workman's third clause, "Who was conceived of the Virgin Mary", reads *natus*, "born", in the Latin (row 56). The census's Tier 1 story "The Sixth of July at Constance" rests on Mladoňovic and on Richental. Its own verification field says the primary text was not read. Richental is not vendored (row 40).
- **The Unity's origin stories** exist here as the Unity's later self-account: the *Ratio*'s Preface (1633), Comenius's Note [2] (1660) and his *Historia* (printed 1702). By the Framework's scheme these are Tier 3 (attributed tradition). They show what the Unity believed formation produced, not what happened in 1457. The census's Kunvald lot story is Tier 2 by the census's own rating, rests on sources not vendored, and is not licensed (row 39). Comenius's *Historia* (row 73, second witness) tells of a drawing of twelve sealed slips in 1467. It is the nearest text on the shelf to the census's story, and whether they are the same story is not established.
- **Tábor's assemblies** (1419) are described by Gillett from what he calls "the Calixtine narrative" and by Piccolomini from the other side (about thirty thousand people at Bechyně, in his text). Tier 3, since the Utraquist chronicler was hostile to Tábor. Their distinctive detail (men, women and children in separate groups; "brother and sister"; no instruments) is a Utraquist observer's report. Březová's chronicle (row 68) is the likeliest source of that narrative (§2), and his own account of the assemblies was not read in this pass.
- **The martyrs of 1412** who were carried to the Bethlehem to the hymn *Isti sunt sancti*, with women dipping their handkerchiefs in the blood (Workman), are reported by Workman as a formation scene. The tier waits on his source, which the passage does not name.

## 5. Material Culture and Daily Life Sources

- **Vendored:** none. There is no archaeology, no town record, no manuscript description.
- **Named in the vendored texts:** the Bethlehem Chapel, "a vast building destroyed by the Jesuits in 1786" (Workman); the map of the Tábor assemblies (Gillett); the Unity's household order, as the *Ratio* of 1632/33 codified it (row 24: excluded).
- **Social background** is drawn from Lützow and Gillett at secondary level. It covers the German–Czech divide in Prague and the university, and the utraquist nobles' confederacy under Vladislav II. It also covers peasants' rights under the 1485 peace: "a certain amount of religious liberty was also granted to the peasants whose faith differed from that of the lords on whose estates they lived" (Lützow, *Bohemia*, row 18).
- **Surrounding religious environment:** a Catholic majority in many towns and on many estates; the Waldensians; Jews in Prague. Within the window, the vendored passages show Jews as victims of mob violence (1422; before the 1485 peace). Lützow's phrase is "a large number of Germans and Jews were massacred". Before the window, Lützow notes that "Many Jews flocked to Conrad's sermons" (*Life & Times*, on Waldhauser). No Jewish voice is on the shelf.
- **The gap is stated, not filled.** The material conditions of an Utraquist parish, a Tábor household, or a Unity congregation at Kunvald cannot be reconstructed from what is vendored.

## 6. Source Asymmetries and Missing Voices

Under Article 20 the primary duty is to name whose voices the sources structurally omit, and why.

**Overrepresented:** Hus; the Prague university and clerical elite; the Council of Constance's own procedure; the Utraquist nobility, through the Four Articles and the Compactata; English-language 19th-century historians; Latin-writing Utraquist and Catholic chroniclers and legates (Březová, Piccolomini, Ferdinand of Lucca).

**Structurally missing, and why:**

- **Women.** Women appear: the queen (Lützow), the nuns of Letter II, "women without number" who rallied to Hus (Workman), the women who dipped handkerchiefs in the martyrs' blood, and, at Tábor, the women's group in the assemblies. None speaks in her own words. Hus writes to nuns in Letter II, on virginity, and sends them a song. He also writes in Czech to a woman who had vowed virginity, in the *Dcerka* (row 63). In Letter XXXV he warns his disciple Martin to "flee the society of women". The record omits women because the surviving sources are written by clergy and university men about clergy and university concerns. This is a structural absence, not a finding that women's role was small.
- **Ordinary lay people, peasants and townspeople.** The Tábor assemblies, the parish communions, the Prague disturbances before the 1485 peace are seen only through observers. Hus's Czech-language letters to "the faithful Bohemians" address them but do not record their answers. Chelčický writes for lay people from a lay position (rows 65–66), but he is one man.
- **The Taborites.** The strand is seen through Utraquist chroniclers, a hostile Italian bishop, a papal legate and 19th-century historians. Its articles are on the shelf in three forms. Březová's chronicle gives the twelve articles that the Taborite priests and captains presented to Prague in August, and a list of the Taborite priests' articles, as a hostile chronicler copied them (row 68, second witness). Palacký's *Urkundliche Beiträge* lists Taborite documents of 1422, 1426 and 1428 as German summaries only, with pointers to the *Archiv český* (row 74). Gillett summarises the articles in English (row 13). No Taborite text in the Taborites' own voice, at first witness, is on the shelf. This is the largest asymmetry inside the world. It is not total, since the articles reach us at second hand.
- **The early Unity.** Nothing from the Unity's own hand for 1457 to 1517 is on the shelf at first witness. The Brethren's Latin confession to King Vladislav and their *Excusatio* are on the shelf as a second witness (row 70), with two short quotations from the 1508 document (row 43). Everything else on the shelf about the Unity is later or secondary.
- **Czech speakers.** The movement's own language is on the shelf: Hus's Czech works and letters (rows 58–64), Chelčický (rows 65–66) and the Czech version of Březová's chronicle (row 68). The Czech-language originals count as primary evidence under the project's ruling, once each wording is checked against the page image. What the shelf lacks in Czech is any other voice: no parish sermon, no hymn and no Unity text before 1517. Hus's Czech *Postilla* is the largest body of his preaching and was not read (row 62).
- **Jews and Germans in Bohemia.** In the vendored evidence for the window they appear as victims of mob violence (1422; the years before the 1485 peace). Their own account of the period is absent.

**Forces lens (Forces Framework Step 2).** The forces are those named in Doc_01 §6. Externally they are the papal schism and conciliar authority, the crusades, Rome's refusal after 1436 to confirm an Utraquist archbishop, and royal pressure on the Unity. Internally they are the Utraquist–Taborite split and the Unity's withdrawal.

*Which sources speak to external forces.*
- The council and the schism: Hus's own letters from Constance (rows 1, 4, 42, and in Latin rows 55 and 58); Mladoňovic's *Relatio* (row 56); Gillett's chapters on the trial (row 14); Schaff's introduction (row 9); Workman's account of the Council of Pisa (row 2). Piccolomini gives the Catholic view (row 11).
- The crusades and the imperial diets: Lützow, *Hussite Wars* (rows 16–17); the papal legate's reply to the Four Articles (row 74).
- Basel, the Compactata and Rome's refusal after 1436: Lützow, *Bohemia* (row 18); the conclusion of the Compactata at Iglau, in Palacký's documents (row 76).
- Royal pressure on the Unity: the enactments of 1508, in Lützow (row 18) and in Seifferth's Introduction, which mentions "the edict of Wladislaw against the Brethren" (row 22).

*What the silences show.* The papal and conciliar side speaks in its own words in part. A papal legate's Latin reply to the Four Articles is on the shelf (row 74), and Palacký's *Documenta* holds documents of the council's process (row 57, not read beyond the *Relatio*). Bulls and crusade sermons were not looked for in the volumes read. Most of what pressed on the movement still comes to us through Hus's replies, through the historians and through hostile Catholic writers, so the pressure is visible while its own voice is thin. No source on the shelf has economic conditions, plague or famine as its subject. Whether the historians treat them beyond passing mentions was not read in this pass. No source shows how these forces pressed on a parish, a household or a Tábor camp.

*Survivorship patterns.*
- Hus's letters survive because friends collected them. Workman reports that the *Monumenta* text of at least one letter "has been hopelessly doctored", so what survives has been edited by later hands (note to Letter XX, row 1). The collection of Peter of Mladoňovic is among the sources of the Latin letters (rows 55 and 58).
- The Taborite side lost its field army at Lipany (1434), and Tábor was taken by an Utraquist commander in 1452 (Lützow). It survives through Utraquist and Catholic writers and their modern historians (see the Taborites, above). The record of it is largely the victors' record, and the force that ended it was inside the movement. Its articles survive in the chronicle of its Utraquist enemy (row 68).
- The Unity's record survives through an exile church. The *Ratio* is of 1632/33. The Notes are largely Comenius's, of 1660. The editor, Seifferth, is a bishop of the Moravian Church of 1866. The 1508 letters survive as short quotations in that volume. The Brethren's Latin texts survive in a Catholic compilation of 1535 and its reprint of 1690 (row 70). That chain suggests that persecution, from the enactments of 1508 to the later suppression (census VI.24), most shaped what remains of the Unity. Confidence: Inferential/Thin. It is an inference from the chain of transmission.
- Rome's opposition is well attested, but mostly through its targets (Hus's replies) and through later historians. Piccolomini is the only Roman-side witness on the shelf. Its prominence in the sources is partly an effect of what survives. It is not a finding that Rome was the most threatening force.

**Evidential visibility is not ecological visibility.** The world's most formative material is likely the parish preaching, the Bethlehem congregation, the household, and the Unity's small communities. The shelf's evidence is the treatise, the trial and the war. Reconstruction that reaches past the evidence is marked Inferential/Thin. A "bounded reconstruction" of any marginalised voice needs a specific textual trace and is not attempted here.

## 7. Required Disclosure

**1. The Unity's own in-window voice is almost absent (Step 0 §4 item 2, binding).** No first-witness text by the Unity from 1457 to 1517 is on the shelf. The Brethren's Latin confession to King Vladislav and their *Excusatio* against two letters of Dr. Augustine are on the shelf as a second witness (row 70). The print is of 1690, its long s is read as f, and its letters are garbled. It does not say whether its text is that of 1504 or of 1508, and its headings call the writers *Fratres Waldenses*. Its outline is legible: the Trinity, Christ's death, burial, rising and ascension, the church, and the Eucharist in both kinds. The Seifferth volume quotes two short passages from the Brethren's 1508 document (row 43). One says that "we willingly conform" to customs "not contrary to the word of God". The other says: "We are not ashamed of our priests because they labour according to their ability with their own hands". The Latin of the second is in the *Excusatio* (row 70), whose heading says it answers two letters of Dr. Augustine to the king. Seifferth calls the document letters "to" Dr. Augustine, and the two descriptions are not reconciled. The Latin of the first quotation was not located. Seifferth's Introduction also names the 1504 Confession to King Vladislav. It says these documents seem to have been first printed in a folio of 1535. The scan of that note is garbled at line 306, and the hedge is legible only as a misreading of "seem". The title, *Fasciculus rerum expetendarum et fugiendarum*, is legible at line 8267 only with one letter misread. The fragments reach us at two removes or more: the 1535 printing, then the 1866 English of Seifferth. Brown's print of 1690 reprints the same 1535 collection, so it is the same chain and not a second one. The Notes are largely Comenius's of 1660, and this pass did not establish who chose or translated the passage. Any claim about the Unity before 1517 rests on these texts or on secondary or later voices and is tagged accordingly. Still missing: Gregory the Patriarch's letters, the discipline ordinances, a catechism and the 1501 hymnbook. Requests are in `Open_Gaps_Tracking.md`.

**2. The *Ratio* is 1632/33 practice, not 1457–1517 practice (row 24).** The household order, the catechesis, the office of "matrons," the ordination forms and the hymn practice it records are the Unity of the Thirty Years' War era. They belong to VI.24. A voice for this world must not draw on them as though they described the 15th century.

**3. The Latin Piccolomini is on the shelf in two scans, and their quotability is not yet ruled (rows 11–12 and 72).** The 1592 scan prints long s as f throughout ("Hufit" for the Latin *Hussit-*). It interleaves marginal notes with the text and misreads letters. It also repeats runs of pages: lines 3889 and 4513, 4056 and 4664, and 4138 and 4742 carry the same text. The sect chapter is interleaved with the chapter on Žižka (lines 4300–4345). The quote gate normalises only the character ſ, and this file contains none, because the scan prints f. `cic/texts/REGISTRY.yaml` records no letterform apparatus for the file. The 1524 printing keeps the long s as ſ and has no interleaved marginal notes, but it misreads many letters within words (row 72). The two printings agree in substance on the passages compared, which were Poggio as the source of the burning narrative and the martyr's honours given to Hus and Jerome. Neither is clean. Under the Library's ruling of 25 September 2026 (point 5), a garbled scan stays a second witness until a clean witness of the same work is vendored. Whether the two printings together meet that rule is for the Library to decide (Open_Gaps entry 3). Until then, every Piccolomini passage is treated as a second-witness reading, and nothing from it is licensed for quotation. The corpus-map note says the 1592 scan "is clean, not flagged as garbled under the Library's own OCR-quality rule". Reading the file does not bear that out.

**4. The Four Articles: the Latin is on the shelf, but not in a clean edition of the Articles as issued.** Gillett and Lützow give them in English. Both say they are quoting. Neither gives the Latin or Czech, and Lützow notes that a more extensive version exists "containing further statements and many biblical quotations." The Latin is on the shelf in two places. A papal legate's reply of July 1420 quotes the Praguers' four points "de verbo ad verbum" in legible Latin, in the order cup, preaching, clergy, sins (row 74). Březová prints the articles with proofs, in Latin and in Czech, in the Praguers' letter to the army, in the order preaching, cup, clergy, sins, in a garbled scan (row 68). The two witnesses differ in order, and their wording was not collated. A hostile source's quotation is not the text as the Praguers issued it, and a garbled scan is a second witness. So a voice may not yet quote the Articles in Latin or Czech (Open_Gaps request R6).

**5. The 1662 *Appeale* pamphlet** was checked and closed for unusable OCR, not for absence (row 38). A future pass could revisit it by re-OCR. The vendored *Letters* already carry Hus's appeal language (row 4).

**6. Vendored works assigned only to other worlds** that hold Hus or Hussite material are not evidence here (§1). They are candidates in `Open_Gaps_Tracking.md` (section E and entry 27). Foxe's vol. III and Van Braght are assigned to this world as context (rows 78 and 79).

**7. Living tradition.** The Seifferth volume is edited by a bishop of the Moravian Church, and its Notes come from the last bishop of the old Unity. Whatever this world says of the Unity is mediated through a living tradition's own account of itself (Doc_01 §1).

**8. Witness status and the page image.** The new volumes are original-language primary evidence under the Library's ruling of 25 September 2026, and they are quotable only once each wording is checked against the page image. No page image was available for this document. Every quotation in this document and in the Registry is matched to the OCR text of the vendored file, misreads included. The second witnesses (Březová, the Brethren's Latin items, Comenius, both Piccolomini printings and the *Tractatus responsivus*) support what a passage says in outline and are not quoted.

## 9. Search record, field-bibliography sweep, and saturation

**Search record (STARLITE headings).** *Sampling strategy:* the 396 text files in `cic/texts/` (358 `.txt` and 38 `.xml`) were searched by full text. The directory holds 403 entries, and the other seven are metadata files, the index database and the intake folder. `REGISTRY.yaml` lists the same 396 files. The search terms were "Hus" and "Huss" as whole words; "Bohemian Brethren", "Unity of the Brethren", "Unitas Fratrum" and "Bohemian Confession"; and Hussite, Utraquist, Calixtine and Taborite. The corpus map, the texts registry, the cross-world queues and dossiers, and the census entries V.5, V.6 and VI.24 were read. *Type:* source discovery for a construction step. *Approaches:* full-text search; corpus-map and registry read; census `sources` and `documentedStories` fields; snowball from bibliographic footnotes and bibliographies in the vendored apparatus (Schaff, Workman, Lützow, Gillett, Seifferth); the Library's acquisition against those leads (Open_Gaps entry 26); whole-line searches of the new Czech and Latin volumes for the cup, the Four Articles, the Taborite articles and the Adamites. *Range of years:* 1402–1517 for subject; later witnesses noted. *Limits:* no external network instrument was used. English, Latin, Czech and German were searched, and the Latin and Czech files are OCR-noisy, so a search can miss a misread word. The counts were made twice, with Python regular expressions on whitespace-normalised text and with `grep` line by line. They agree except where a phrase runs across a line break (Registry, "The shelf search"). *Inclusion and exclusions:* a file is included as a Registry row if the corpus map assigns it to this world. A vendored work assigned only to another world is recorded as a candidate in Open_Gaps section E. *Terms used:* as above, plus Czech and Latin names (*Jednota*, *Unitas*, *Compactata*, *utraquism*, *Taborit*). *Electronic sources:* the local corpus only. All searches were run on 30 September 2026.

**Field-bibliography sweep: not run.** No external bibliographic instrument was consulted. Instruments not reached are named in the Registry's saturation statement. The saturation statement there is not closed, and the Step 2 completion standard's saturation requirement is not met until a sweep has been run and dispositioned.
