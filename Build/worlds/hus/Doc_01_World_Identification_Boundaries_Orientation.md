## 1. Formation World Identity

**World Name:** The Hussite and Bohemian Brethren Movement.

**Short Description:** A Czech-language reform movement that began at Prague's Bethlehem Chapel and became a national church. Jan Hus was appointed preacher at the Bethlehem Chapel in March 1402. He was burned at the Council of Constance on 6 July 1415. His death turned a university and preaching movement into a national one. Its practical demand was communion in both kinds, bread and wine, for lay people (called *utraquism*, from the Latin *sub utraque specie*, or *Calixtine*, from *calix*, "cup"). Hussite armies resisted repeated crusades. In 1436 Bohemia won the Compactata, a legal recognition of its church practice. Two bodies outlasted the wars. One was the Utraquist church, which held most parishes. The other, from 1457, was the Unity of the Brethren, a smaller, stricter body that at an early stage took the name *Unitas Fratrum*. Between them in time stood the field communities of Tábor (1419–52). The window is c. 1402–1517.

**Core Identity:** A world in which Christian life is lived in one's own language. The Scriptures are the test of every church order, and the cup is given to the laity. The world is held together by the memory of a preacher who died for refusing to say what he did not believe. Inside that shared memory, three ways of living it out compete. The first is the national church that must govern a kingdom. The second is the field communities of Tábor, which wanted the whole order remade at once. The third is the Brethren, who withdrew to a disciplined community and, on Lützow's account, began by refusing the sword.

**Distinctive Contribution:** No other world in the portfolio reconstructs a non-Roman national church that won legal recognition before Luther. Lollardy (V.5) stayed underground. Devotio Moderna stayed inside existing church structures. This world fought, negotiated, and held parishes and a university for a century. Its second half, the Unity, also produced a distinctive community discipline and, by the census's account, an early printed vernacular hymnbook (1501).

**Living Tradition Status:** Triggered, not confirmed. The census records this world as `living: true`. It traces the Unity's line to the Moravian Church, which it places at Herrnhut in Saxony from 1722. Article 29 covers a world whose reconstruction "touches a tradition that continues into the present". It makes confirmation a freeze-eligibility gate (Constitution Article 29). Two things need naming at confirmation. The first is which present-day churches are the descendants. The census names the Moravian Church only, and confirmation should check whether others in the Czech lands claim the Hussite or Brethren heritage. The second is how far a reconstruction of 1402–1517 may speak for them. Under the one-world ruling (§5), the Unity's claim to a living tradition falls inside this world. One vendored source is itself mediated through a living tradition. Bishop B. Seifferth edited and translated the *Ratio Disciplinae* volume (1866), and its title page names him a bishop of the Brethren's Church. Doc_02 §2 records that mediation. Confirmation itself is open and belongs to the project lead (§9).

## 2. Historical Boundaries

**Approximate Time Horizon:** c. 1402–1517.

- **Beginning (1402).** Hus's appointment as preacher at the Chapel of the Holy Innocents of Bethlehem, Prague. The vendored *Letters* say: "Two years later (March 14, 1402) he was appointed preacher at the Chapel of the Holy Innocents of Bethlehem" (Workman, Part I introduction). Lützow, in a separate work, gives the year: Hus "obtained in 1402 the important appointment of preacher at the Bethlehem chapel" (*Life and Times*). This document dates the start by the appointment. It does not say that his preaching began in 1402. Workman's note to Letter XVIII says that, by Hus's own statement, "the first year of his preaching was 1401". The note adds that "Hus is here reckoning, therefore, from his appointment to priest's orders in 1400". The "c." covers the difference. Confidence: the appointment is Documented, since Workman gives the day and Lützow independently gives the year. Hus's 1401 is Documented as his own statement. Workman's explanation of it is his reading.
- **The chapel.** Two wealthy laymen founded and endowed the chapel on 24 May 1391. They set a condition: its rector must "preach every Sunday and festival exclusively in the Czech" language (Workman). Hus took over a pulpit that already had a Czech-language mission. This is why the world begins at the chapel and not at his birth or his university career.
- **Ending (1517).** 1517 is the census's window close. It is not a rupture. The Utraquist church and the Unity continue for another century. They are the subject of the separate census entry `czech-churches-last-century` (VI.24, 1517–c. 1627, "Utraquists and Unitas"), to which this entry `continuesAs`. The *Ratio Disciplinae* (1632/33) belongs to that entry's timeline, not this one. Doc_08's Ending cell must record this honestly: no internal fracture closes the world in 1517 (§6).

**Developments internal to the window:** Hus preaches (1402–14). The university's charter changes (1409). Hus is condemned and burned (1415). The Four Articles of Prague follow (1420). The crusades and the field wars come next. The Utraquists and Taborites split, and the Taborite field army is defeated at Lipany (1434). The Compactata are accepted (5 July 1436, Lützow). Tábor is captured (1452). The Unity is founded (1457). The Peace of Kutná Hora follows (1485). At the end of the fifteenth century the Unity divides into a "Great" and a "Small" party. King Vladislav's enactments of 1508 persecute the Brethren.

**Developments that indicate transition:** the accession of the Habsburg line and the Reformation in Germany, both after 1517. The Reformation's early contact with Bohemians is recorded in Luther's *Address to the Christian Nobility* (1520), a later witness outside the window (§7).

**Geographic Centers:**

- **Prague.** The Bethlehem Chapel. The university, where the 1409 charter change gave "the Czech element … three votes, and the foreign nations reduced from three to one" (Schaff's introduction). The city councils of the Old and New Town, which split over the Taborite articles in 1420 (Gillett, ch. XV).
- **Southern Bohemia.** Tábor, the field community of 1419–52. Its 1419 assemblies gathered, by one account, more than forty thousand people (Gillett, ch. XV). Gillett cites a *Diarium Belli Hussitici* by a chancellor of New Prague. Identifying that author with Lawrence of Březová is this document's own inference, and it is tagged Inferential/Thin. Gillett's footnote is garbled in the scan. Lützow adds that Tábor was taken by an Utraquist commander in 1452.
- **North-east Bohemia and Moravia.** Kunvald near Žamberk, the Unity's first settlement (Lützow, *Bohemia*). Seifferth's introduction says that the Brethren retired in 1457 to "the Barony of lititz" (the scan's spelling), in the north-east of Bohemia. This document does not test whether the two name the same place. The Unity's own late account says that about two hundred congregations stood in Bohemia and Moravia by about 1500. That account is the *Ratio*'s own Preface of 1633, printed in the Seifferth volume. Confidence: Inferential/Thin. The figure rests on one partisan witness writing more than a century later.
- **Constance.** The place where the world's central event happened, outside the kingdom. The council's proceedings are recorded in Gillett, Vol. II, ch. I–II, and in the Letters from Constance (XXXVII–LXXXII).
- **Kutná Hora.** The site of the 1485 peace.
- **Basel.** The council that concluded the Compactata.

**Cultural Environment:** Bohemia is a kingdom inside the Holy Roman Empire. Its kings are also, at times, Emperors (Wenceslas IV and Sigismund; later King Vladislav II, in Lützow's numbering). Czech and German speakers share Prague. Latin is the language of church and university. Hus's *Letters* are mixed, and some are written in Czech. Workman calls it "characteristic of Hus's intense nationalism that it should have been written in Czech" of his December 1411 appeal to the Supreme Court of Bohemia (Letter XI). Queen Sophia attended Hus's sermons at the Bethlehem Chapel, according to Lützow's *Hussite Wars*. The wider church is split. Workman describes the cardinals' Council of Pisa of 1409 as an attempt to end "the great schism" (Letters, Part I). Schaff's introduction gives the schism's dates as 1377–1415 in one place and 1378–1417 in another. The discrepancy is carried, not resolved.

**Historical Pressures:**

- The papal schism and the crisis of conciliar authority, which gave Constance its authority to judge Hus (Schaff, Introduction).
- German–Czech tension inside the university and the city. It ended with the 1409 charter and the departure of the German masters, whom Piccolomini says went to Leipzig. His chapter on the Hussites' sect is headed *De perfida secta Hussitarum* in the printed book, and the scan misreads part of the heading. The quotability of this scan is not yet ruled (Open_Gaps entry 3).
- The condemnation of Wyclif's books in Prague. Schaff notes that in 1410 the archbishop seized two hundred of them and burned them.
- Crusades against Bohemia. The number varies between accounts. Lützow numbers the invasions of 1421 and 1427 as the second and third crusades. A footnote says that "modern writers generally" do so, and it doubts that the expedition of 1422 was a crusade at all. On that numbering the first is the invasion of 1420. A further campaign under Cardinal Cesarini failed in 1431. The census counts five. Confidence: Documented that repeated crusades were called against Bohemia between 1420 and 1431. Contested for their number.
- After 1436, the persistent refusal of Rome to confirm an Utraquist archbishop. Lützow (*Bohemia*) says the Bohemians "declined to accept the Compacts in the modified form suggested by the Council till Rokycan had been recognized as Archbishop of Prague". The Council's representatives "still refused to ratify the election of Rokycan." The Utraquist priests did not settle the question of ordination. In Lützow's words, "the moderate Utraquists always endeavoured to obtain Catholic ordination for their priests" (*Hussite Wars*).
- Political and social violence. On 30 July 1419 the burgomaster and several councillors of the New Town were thrown from the town-hall windows (Lützow, *Bohemia*). In 1422 the priest John of Želivo was executed. A Prague crowd then plundered houses, attacked the Jews, and robbed or destroyed their possessions (Lützow, *Hussite Wars*, quoting a contemporary chronicler). Under King Vladislav II, before the 1485 peace, a Prague crowd stormed three town halls, and "a large number of Germans and Jews were massacred" (Lützow, *Bohemia*).

**Historical Catalysts:** the 1391 foundation of the Bethlehem Chapel; Hus's appointment in March 1402; the 1409 university charter; the condemnation of Wyclif's writings (1410). Then Hus leaves Prague and writes *De Ecclesia* (1413). He travels to Constance in autumn 1414 (Letter XXXIV). Jakoubek gives the lay chalice in Prague in the summer of 1414. On 15 June 1415 the Council of Constance declares the withholding of the cup lawful (Gillett, ch. III). Hus writes Letter LXXI on the cup on 21 June 1415 and is burned on 6 July. Later come the 1419 Tábor assemblies, the 1419 Prague defenestration, the Four Articles of 1420 and the Compactata of 1436. The Unity is founded in 1457, and the Peace of Kutná Hora follows in 1485.

## 3. Distinct World Criteria

- **What recurring formation ecology exists?** A vernacular preaching ecology (Bethlehem Chapel, Czech-language letters to lay congregations). The chalice as a lay practice. A fixed programme, the Four Articles, that a whole kingdom could hold to. From 1457 there is a second ecology: a community discipline of elders, ministers, household order and hymnody. It is known in detail only from the *Ratio Disciplinae* of 1632/33, which is outside the window (Doc_02 §7).
- **What recurring gravities exist (preliminary, subject to Doc_04)?** Named here as candidates for testing, not as findings. Each carries an Author Gravity risk because the evidence is concentrated:
  1. Scripture, or "the law of Christ," as the standard by which every church order is judged. The first Article of Prague asks that "the Word of God shall in the kingdom of Bohemia be freely and without impediment proclaimed and preached" (Lützow, *Hussite Wars*, ch. on the Prague articles). The Unity's late self-description names "the leading principle of Huss, that the law of Christ is sufficient for the government of the church militant" (the *Ratio*'s own Preface of 1633, in the Seifferth volume). Risk: this reaches us largely through Hus's own texts, which borrow heavily from Wyclif (§7).
  2. The cup for the laity. Risk: in Prague the cup was Jakoubek's cause before it was Hus's. Workman writes that, in 1414, "Hitherto, Hus had taken little interest in the matter". Workman adds that he "soon committed himself decisively to the opinions of Jakoubek" (note before Letter XLIII). From prison at Constance, on 21 June 1415, Hus wrote to Hawlik, the preacher at the Bethlehem: "do not oppose the sacrament of the Lord's cup, which was instituted of Christ" (Letter LXXI). He also wrote: "prepare to suffer for the eating of the bread and the communion of the cup". So Hus defended the cup at the end, but he did not begin it, and it must not be attributed to him as its author. Its Waldensian or antiquarian origins are disputed (§7).
  3. Czech language and national belonging. Risk: modern nationalist historiography, including Lützow's own, foregrounds it.
  4. Fidelity to truth under pressure (Hus's refusal to recant). Risk: our access is largely through Hus's friends and later Protestant historians.
  5. The church as the whole body of the predestinate, with Christ as its only head (*De Ecclesia*). Risk: the doctrinal core is Wyclif's (§7).
  6. As a tension: the sword against non-resistance. Taborite and Hussite field armies stand against Chelčický's teaching and the Unity's refusal of violence (§5). The tension also runs inside the Unity. On Lützow's account, the "Small" party held to non-resistance and soon became extinct, and the "Great" party "accommodated its teaching to a certain extent to temporal ideas" (§4). Risk: our only account of the split is Lützow's, and he relies on Goll, who is not vendored.
- **What recurring patterns of worship, formation, interpretation, belonging, and authority exist?** Worship: communion in both kinds; Czech preaching; the "Isti sunt sancti" hymn sung when the three martyrs of 1412 were carried to Bethlehem (Workman). Formation: catechesis and hymns in Unity households, evidenced only by the later *Ratio* (Doc_02 §7). Interpretation: appeal to Scripture and the primitive church. Belonging: Gillett describes the Tábor assemblies from what he calls "the Calixtine narrative". "The rich and the poor sat down together, and priest and layman were undistinguished by garb." They called one another "brother and sister". Authority: contested throughout. The claimants are the council, the crown, the university, the elected bishop and the Unity's elders.
- **What distinguishes this world from adjacent worlds?** See §7.

## 4. World Separation Criteria

The Framework asks: when does one world become another? The six sub-questions are run below against the movement's own three parts. The evidence cuts both ways, and this section records both.

- **Has a new gravity emerged?** Yes, in the Unity's early years. Its refusal of war follows Chelčický's teaching. Lützow calls his most original doctrine the belief in "the absolute and unconditional sinfulness of bloodshed" (*Bohemia*). Set against the Hussite armies' record, this is a distinct pattern. It did not stay constant. Lützow reports that about the end of the fifteenth century the Brethren divided into a "Great" and a "Small" party. The "Small" party, "which soon became extinct, maintained in its entirety the teaching of Chelcicky, which included doctrines such as non-resistance to evil-doers". The "Great" party "reconciled itself with the world, and by partly abandoning its earliest principles secured the future existence of the 'Unity.'" Lützow hedges the point ("As far as we can judge"). The ground for separation is strong for c. 1457 to c. 1500 and weaker for the last years of the window. Confidence: Inferential/Thin, since it rests on one secondary account.
- **Has a major gravity disappeared?** Not clearly, but the evidence is uneven. The Unity itself claims Hus's principle that "the law of Christ is sufficient for the government of the church militant" (the *Ratio*'s Preface, 1633). The cup shows in the Unity only in the *Ratio* of 1632/33, where deacons may distribute "not only the cup, but the bread" at large communions. That is later practice. No vendored Unity source speaks of the Czech language. So the continuity of the cup and of Czech-language Scripture is not shown for 1457–1517.
- **Has formation changed substantially?** Yes for the Unity, but the evidence is late. Its household discipline is known in detail only from 1632/33. Confidence: Inferential/Thin for 1457–1517, because our detailed evidence is from 1632/33.
- **Has worship changed substantially?** Modestly, on the evidence we have. The census says the Unity printed a vernacular hymnbook by 1501, and none is vendored. The Tábor assemblies allowed "no sound of musical instruments" (Gillett, from the Calixtine narrative).
- **Has authority changed substantially?** Yes. The Unity has its own ministers and, by its own account, its own bishops (§5). The Utraquists never resolved the question of ordination.
- **Has interpretation changed substantially?** Partly. Chelčický reads the Gospel as forbidding oaths and killing. He also "believed himself in accord with Wycliffe in maintaining the Real Presence" while opposing the Taborites' eucharistic teaching (Lützow, *Bohemia*).

**Finding.** The separation case is real. The Unity differs from the Utraquists in formation and authority, and in its early years on the sword. Two grounds for keeping one world come from the evidence. First, the Unity claims descent from Hus. Its Preface of 1633 was issued in the name of the Unity's Seniors and Ministers. On p. 95 of the English text it says that the founders "were in truth the genubie offspring of the holy martyr Huss" (the scan misprints "genuine"). Second, the link between Tábor and the Unity is disputed, but "all recent Bohemian historians" maintain it on Lützow's account (§5). One further ground is portfolio-level, not ecological. The census holds V.6 and VI.24 as two entries, joined by `continuesAs`. V.6 names the Bohemian Brethren in its title, and VI.24 is titled "Utraquists and Unitas". That describes how the portfolio is arranged. It is not evidence about the movement itself. The project lead has ruled that this is one world with three strands (§5, §9). Whether the Unity should instead be a separate world was put to the project lead as a portfolio-level decision, and the ruling settles it.

## 5. Strand Determination

Constitution Article 21 defines a strand as "a meaningfully distinct pattern of formation emphasis, practice, authority structure, or ecological orientation within a single world, not merely a variation in detail." A strand is a finding that must answer to evidence.

**Ruling.** The project lead ruled on 30 September 2026 that the Hussites are one world with three strands, the Utraquist, the Taborite and the Unity, for the window c. 1402–1517. The ruling is recorded in `Build/Ministry/Operations/Audits/hus_Round1_Revision_2026-09-30.md`. This section sets out the evidence for each strand and the two alternatives the ruling did not choose. It does so at full strength, so that the ruling can be tested against Doc_04.

**Finding: three strands.** The strands are the Utraquist (Calixtine) national church, the Taborite field communities, and the Unity of the Brethren. They share Hus's memory, the chalice, Czech-language Scripture and the appeal to "the law of Christ." Those shared features are claimed for the strands by the sources named below. They are not all shown for each strand. The strands differ on Article 21's dimensions of practice, authority structure, formation emphasis and ecological orientation.

| | Utraquist | Taborite | Unity of the Brethren |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Practice** | Communion in both kinds within the inherited liturgy. Gillett: some Calixtines were "scarcely differing, except on the single point of the cup, from the Roman Catholic church." | Open-air assemblies, no musical instruments, priests and laity "undistinguished by garb," images and vestments rejected (Gillett, ch. XV). | Own community discipline, hymns, catechesis, known in detail only from 1632/33. Withdrawal from the Calixtines (the *Ratio*'s Preface, English text). |
| **Authority structure** | The crown and the Utraquist nobles' confederacy (Lützow, *Bohemia*). Priests seek Catholic ordination (Lützow). No confirmed archbishop. | Elected bishop, Nicholas of Pelhřimov (Lützow: "openly seceding from the Roman Church"). **Evidence and confidence, strand by strand.**

- *Utraquist.* The best-evidenced strand. The Four Articles are given in full in Gillett (ch. XV, pp. 441–44) and in summary in Lützow. Confidence: Documented that the strand existed as a distinct church body. Contested on whether it had a stable authority structure (§2).
- *Taborite.* Distinct in practice and authority on the vendored evidence. But it is known almost entirely from hostile, partisan or secondary sources. Piccolomini is hostile. Lützow says of the chronicler Lawrence of Březová, on whom the Adamite story depends, that his "hatred of the Taborites was even intenser than his hatred of the adherents of the Church of Rome". Gillett's account of the assemblies is, in his own words, "the Calixtine narrative". Confidence: Widely Accepted that it was a distinct wing. Inferential/Thin for its doctrine beyond what these sources report.
- *Pikarts and Adamites.* Piccolomini and Březová describe the Adamites as a sect of nudists. Lützow says that they "had no connection whatever with Hussitism" and that Piccolomini "often attacks his enemies in a distinctly unfair manner" (*Hussite Wars*, preface and ch. on Tábor). Lützow's view rests on an article by Nedoma printed in 1891. Later work is unchecked, and no modern specialist work is vendored. Against it stands Březová's own account, as Lützow reports it. On that account the Adamites were more than 200 people whom the "more faithful" Taborite party expelled from Tábor itself. Confidence: Contested. Both readings are carried.
- *Unity.* Its detailed voice is the *Ratio Disciplinae* (1632/33), which is outside the window. In-window evidence is reported by Seifferth's introduction. It names a 1504 Confession to King Vladislav and two letters of 1508 to Dr. Augustine (Doc_02 §7). It and the Notes also quote short passages of the 1508 letters. Confidence: Widely Accepted that the Unity formed in 1457 and separated from the Calixtines. **Contested** on the Waldensian ordination of 1467. Seifferth accepts it. Lützow's note says "evidence is rather against the influence of the Waldenses on the foundation of the 'Unity,'" and calls it "one of the most controverted points in Bohemian history". Lützow's phrase "on insufficient evidence" is about Michael, curate of Žamberk, who is "said to have been ordained by a Waldensian bishop". Lützow gives no year for it, so it is not a comment on Seifferth's synod of 1467.

**The strand count: the two alternatives and what each costs.**

- *Two strands, with Tábor as a phase of the Utraquist strand.* The case for it is real. This document's own §1 says that two bodies outlasted the wars. The census's continuation entry names two ("Utraquists and Unitas"). Tábor is a bounded phase: 1419–52 covers 33 of the window's 115 years. Article 21 speaks of a pattern "within a single world", and it does not say whether a time-bounded phase can be a strand. The Taborite column also rests on hostile or partisan witnesses, as the bullet above shows. The cost is this. The Utraquist–Taborite split is the world's clearest internal fracture (§6). The Taborites also had an elected bishop, while the Utraquist priests sought Catholic ordination. Folding Tábor into the Utraquist strand would hide both, and it would erase the movement's radical pole.
- *Two worlds, with the Unity separate.* The case for it is also real. The Unity claims a distinct identity. Its Preface says that "the Calixtines had forestalled the denomination of Hussites", and that the Brethren seceded from them in 1457. The Unity's formation and authority differ most from the rest of the movement (§4). It is also the strand with the living tradition. The cost is this. It would split what the census keeps together in V.6. It would also leave a Unity world with no complete in-window text on the shelf, only two short quotations from the 1508 letters (Doc_02 §7). And the census's VI.24 already carries the Unity forward from 1517, so the Unity's own world would sit between two entries that name it.
- *One world, three strands (the ruling).* Its cost is that it puts three ecologies under one Representative question. Its Taborite strand also has no voice of its own on the shelf. Its gain is that it keeps the sword and non-resistance inside one world. It also matches the census's grouping, which is a portfolio-level reason and not an ecological one.

**Links between strands.** The link between Tábor and the Unity is disputed. Lützow: "The connection of the Taborites with the Bohemian Brethren was long a disputed point in Bohemian history; all recent Bohemian historians, however, maintain its existence." Goll reports that the Brethren "did not wish to be considered as continuators of the Taborites". Lützow adds that they protested against this theory "with a degree of energy which was not justified by the facts of the case". The Unity's own Preface of 1633 speaks warmly of Tábor. It says that the city of Tabor alone "held out for many years, defending with the sword their purity of doctrine and their constancy in the faith". This is **Contested** and is carried at full strength. The Preface is the Unity's own later voice on the link, and it stands beside Goll's report.

**What holds across strands.** Hus's memory. Piccolomini, a hostile source, reports that among the Bohemians the burned men earned "Martyrum honores", in his chapter on the Hussites' sect (the quotability of this scan is unruled; Open_Gaps entry 3). Also the cup, Scripture as norm and the Czech language, as far as the evidence in §4 shows them. These are the candidates for cross-strand testing at Doc_04. The strand finding governs strand attribution in every later document.

**What this document does not decide.** Whether the Taborite strand can carry a Representative or a gravity of its own is not decided here. No Taborite voice is vendored. That question belongs to Doc_02 (source asymmetry) and Doc_04. Nor does this document decide anything about Representative identity, which belongs to the project lead.

## 6. Preliminary Forces Identification

Constitution Article 22 requires that a world be "situated against the forces that shaped it across both external and internal dimensions," and that where "no internal fracture is attested … that finding is recorded honestly." The sketch below is Layer 1 (Historical Event) only. The complete analysis is Doc_08.

**What generated this world:** two connected triggers. First, a Czech-language preaching pulpit founded by laymen in 1391 and filled by Hus in 1402. Second, the reception of Wyclif's writings in Prague, which gave Hus and others a ready theology of the church. The chalice, raised by Jakoubek in 1414, supplied the practical demand.

**What larger world it was embedded in:** the late-medieval Latin church, in crisis over the papal schism. The Kingdom of Bohemia, inside the Empire under the Luxembourg kings. The German–Czech tension in the university and the city. And, in the census's framing, the memory of Bohemian reformers before Hus. Lützow names Conrad Waldhauser, Milíč and Matthew of Janov as forerunners. He quotes Kybal: "The view that Hussitism is merely artificially fostered Wycliffism appears to me logically and historically as nonsense" (*Life and Times*, preface). This is set against Schaff's view that Hus "appropriated paragraph after paragraph" from Wyclif (§7).

**What it was responding to:** the burning of Hus and the breach of his safe-conduct. Luther later called the safe-conduct broken, in his 1520 address. It was also responding to the Council's decree of 15 June 1415 against the cup, and to the crusades called against Bohemia.

**What it was refusing:** the rule that lay people receive only the bread, and the clergy's secular wealth (Article III of Prague). It refused the claim that a council or pope decides questions of the faith against Scripture, which was Hus's own stance at Constance. For the Unity in its early years, it also refused war and the world's order as such.

## 7. World Continuity & Distinction

**What this world inherits:**

- **Wyclif's writings.** The link is direct textual borrowing, not merely a shared mood. Schaff's translator's introduction to the vendored *De Ecclesia* says: "Huss appropriated paragraph after paragraph from his predecessor and transferred them often with little verbal change to his own pages," drawing on Wyclif's *De Ecclesia* and *De potestate Papae*. It adds: "Never … did a man owe more to mortal teacher than Huss did to John Wyclif." Wyclif's own *Tractatus de Ecclesia* (Loserth, 1886) is vendored under Lollardy (`wyclif_de-ecclesia-lat_loserth1886.txt`). The Wyclif link also reached Hus's letters. In Letter VI, Hus writes to the English Wycliffite Richard Wyche. He says he told a large assembly, "numbering, I suppose, nearly ten thousand", about Wyche's letter as he preached. The people were so moved that "they begged me to translate it into our mother tongue". Workman's contents line for that letter says Hus "read it in the Bethlehem". Confidence: Widely Accepted for the textual dependence of *De Ecclesia* (Loserth prints the parallel passages, per Schaff). **Contested** as a general account of the movement. Lützow, quoting Kybal, rejects the view that Hussitism is "merely artificially fostered Wycliffism" and stresses Bohemian forerunners. Schaff records that Loserth revised his own earlier judgment of Hus's originality after Flajšhans's work (Introduction). Both views are carried. **Wyclif died in 1384; the relationship runs through his texts, not through contact between the two men.** This finding is binding on Lollardy's own Doc_01 (§9).
- **Bohemian forerunners.** Conrad Waldhauser, Milíč of Kroměříž and Matthew of Janov, on Lützow's account.
- **The Waldensians.** Contested (§5). Workman also reports that "two Waldensians from Dresden, Peter and Nicholas" persuaded Jakoubek in 1414 to give the cup to the laity. Piccolomini says that Jakoubek found communion in both kinds praised in old codices, chiefly Dionysius and Cyprian. The two accounts of the chalice's origin differ and are both carried. Confidence: Contested.
- **The Latin church's law and sacraments.** Hus quotes the canon law and the Fathers heavily (Schaff, Introduction), and his creed is the Church's (§8).

**What this world transmits:**

- To **Luther and Lutheran Wittenberg (VI.1).** Luther writes in 1520: "we must honestly confess the truth … that John Huss and Jerome of Prague were burnt at Constance in violation of the Papal, Christian, and Imperial oath and safe conduct" (Wace–Buchheim, *First Principles of the Reformation*; the Jacobs–Spaeth *Works*, vol. 2, has the same passage in a different translation). The vendored Bacon–Allen volume prints the Luther hymn *Jesus Christus unser Heiland* as "Improved" from the Communion Hymn of John Huss, *Jesus Christus, noster Salus*. The stanzas are Luther's revision and must not be given to Hus. The Luther files belong to other worlds. They are cited here only as later witnesses to reception, and nothing from them is licensed to this world. The census also gives Luther's remark "we are all Hussites without knowing it". It is not found by a quick search in the vendored Luther files and is carried as the census's own claim, not checked here.
- To **the Czech churches' last century (VI.24)**, and through the Unity to the Moravian Church (census `legacy` field, not vendored).

**Continuity and change.** The Framework asks three further questions. *What shared material functions differently here than in adjacent ecologies?* Wyclif's ecclesiology reached Hus as text and served a preaching pulpit and then a kingdom's church programme. In England, the same body of ideas stayed underground as Lollardy. *Where does continuity mask change?* The name "Hussite" covers the Calixtines, the Taborites and, in the Unity's Preface, a name the Unity says the Calixtines had "forestalled" and so declined. Hus's memory stays constant while the cup and the sword mean different things in different strands. On the cup, Hus himself came late to it. *Where does discontinuity mask continuity?* The Unity's secession of 1457 reads as a break with the Calixtines. Yet its Preface claims Hus's own principle, and it praises Tábor. Tábor's fall (1452) reads as an ending. Yet "all recent Bohemian historians" maintain the Tábor–Unity link, on Lützow's account, while Goll reports that the Brethren rejected it (§5).

**Distinctness from adjacent worlds:**

- **Lollardy (V.5).** Direct textual borrowing runs from Wyclif to Hus (above). Lollardy stayed an underground lay network and never won legal standing. The two are correctly separate worlds, and their divergence is carried in both Doc_01s.
- **Devotio Moderna (V.7).** An interior devotional movement inside the existing church. No figure or text overlaps.
- **The Anabaptist Movements (VI.3).** Later, and no overlap identified in the window.
- **Lutheran Wittenberg (VI.1) and the Reformed cities (`rzg`).** After the window. They cite or share the Hussite memory but hold different church orders.
- **`czech-churches-last-century` (VI.24).** The direct continuation. The boundary is 1517. Its later documents (the 1575 Bohemian Confession, the Kralice Bible, Comenius) are not this world's.

## 8. The Article 4 check (Step 0 §4 item 1, binding on Doc_01)

Step 0's floor check was a targeted search across two works. It is extended here across the full vendored corpus and the movement's other strands. The five commitments are quoted below from Constitution V2.3, Article 4 ("On the Scope of 'Movement'"), in the same words as the Step 0 header. Article 4 also says: "A movement need not have recited the Creed, formally adopted it, or existed at a date when doing so was possible." So the test is plain sense. It asks whether a commitment is affirmed, not whether the Nicene words appear.

**Hus, in his own voice, commitment by commitment.**

1. "One God, the Father, the Almighty, maker of heaven and earth, of all that is, seen and unseen."
   - *Shown.* *De Ecclesia*, ch. VIII (p. 70): faith "concerns the past, namely such things as that God created the world, that Christ was incarnate". Ch. IV (p. 34) has Hus reasoning that "his Father is almighty". Letter XXI: "God the Father, Son, and Holy Ghost, eternally one God."
   - *Not shown in his words.* The phrases "maker of heaven and earth" and "of all that is, seen and unseen". A full-text search finds neither.
2. "Jesus Christ as the only Son of God, eternally begotten of the Father, God from God, Light from Light, true God from true God, begotten not made, of one Being with the Father."
   - *Shown.* *De Ecclesia* p. 18 (ch. III): "Christ is the individual, the true God and man." Ch. IX (p. 84): Peter "confessed Christ to be very God and very man." Letter XXXIX: "the Lord Jesus, very God, the son of the chaste Virgin Mary." Letter XXXIV: "Who is God eternal and true man, born of the Virgin Mary."
   - *Not shown in his words.* "Only Son", "eternally begotten", "begotten not made", "Light from Light" and "of one Being with the Father". "Begotten" occurs once in the *Letters*, of a son born to a woman. "One substance" occurs once in *De Ecclesia*, and it is Gregory speaking of the church.
   - *Trial evidence (secondary).* At Constance Hus "repeated the article from the Athanasian creed upon the Trinity", according to Gillett (Vol. II, pp. 53–54).
3. "Jesus Christ as truly human — incarnate of the Holy Spirit and the Virgin Mary, 'became truly human.'"
   - *Shown.* Letter XXXIV: "God eternal and true man, born of the Virgin Mary." Letter XXXIX: "the son of the chaste Virgin Mary." *De Ecclesia* p. 70: "Christ was incarnate". Workman's narrative of the burning quotes the words "Who was conceived of the Virgin Mary" as Hus reached the third clause of his prayer (secondary).
   - *Not shown in his words.* "Incarnate of the Holy Spirit". No passage in the two works says so.
4. "Christ's death under Pontius Pilate, burial, bodily resurrection on the third day, ascension, and his return in glory to judge the living and the dead."
   - *Shown.* Death under Pilate: Letter XX, "He came, the All-powerful, to suffer under Pilate's power". Burial and rising: Letter XVII, the high priests and Pilate "gave Him to death and laid Him in the tomb; but He rose again". The third day: Letter LXXIII, "He knew He would rise again on the third day". Burial for three days: Letter XXVIII, "the three days He was in the tomb". Ascension: *De Ecclesia* ch. IV (p. 28), "who ascended again into heaven". Session and return: *De Ecclesia* p. 70, "Christ sits at the right hand of the Father … Christ will come again in judgment."
   - *Not shown in his words.* "Bodily" resurrection, and "in glory to judge the living and the dead" as Hus's own wording. In *De Ecclesia* he quotes 2 Timothy for Christ "who shall judge the living and the dead", but that is a scripture quotation and not a statement of his own.
5. "The Holy Spirit as Lord and giver of life, worshiped and glorified together with the Father and the Son."
   - *Shown.* Letter XXI: "God the Father, Son, and Holy Ghost, eternally one God." *De Ecclesia* p. 34: "Christ and his Father are one with the Holy Spirit — who is Christ's gift."
   - *Not shown in his words.* "Lord and giver of life" and "worshiped and glorified". The phrase "Lord and Giver of Life" appears in the Preface to the *Letters*, but it is the editors' words, not Hus's.

**Corroboration from the trial.** Workman reports that Hus was charged at Constance with having said that "he was the fourth person in the Trinity", and he notes the charge as a "monstrous article". Gillett reports that Hus answered "God forbid that such an imagination as that I should call myself a fourth person of the Trinity should have been thought of by me". Gillett adds that he "repeated the article from the Athanasian creed upon the Trinity", declaring his firm belief in it (Gillett, Vol. II, pp. 53–54).

**The other strands.**

- *Taborites.* The vendored evidence is hostile or secondary. Their recorded departures concern the eucharist and images, not the creed. Martin Húska ("Loquis") was burned for his eucharistic views (Lützow). Gillett says that the Taborite articles "pertained rather to moral conduct and rules of life, than to points of faith." Nothing vendored shows a Taborite denial of any of the five commitments. Confidence: Inferential/Thin, since no Taborite voice is vendored.
- *Pikarts and Adamites.* Piccolomini and Březová describe the Adamites as a sect of nudists. Lützow says they were not Hussites at all, and their standing is Contested (§5). Nothing in the vendored corpus lets this world's floor be judged by them.
- *Unity.* In-window evidence for the five commitments is not vendored. The two short quotations from the 1508 letters (§5) do not touch them. The 1632/33 *Ratio* has its young members recite "the Apostles' creed" together at their profession of faith and has absolution declared in the name of the Trinity. That is later practice and is outside the window.

**Result.** Nothing in Hus's own voice denies any of the five commitments, and nothing in the secondary and hostile reports on the Taborites does so either. Clause by clause, the two works and the trial evidence show these things. Commitment 4 is largely shown, including the third day and the ascension. Commitments 1, 2, 3 and 5 are shown in substance, but not in the Nicene wording. The clauses set out as not shown in commitments 1 to 5 are silent, not denied. Hus's works are a theological treatise and a set of letters, and they were not written to recite a creed. The floor holds on the evidence for Hus's own voice. It is not a completed check of the Unity in its own window, because no Unity voice from 1457–1517 is vendored beyond two short quotations.
