## 1. Formation World Identity

**World Name:** The Hussite and Bohemian Brethren Movement.

**Short Description:** A Czech-language reform movement that began at Prague's Bethlehem Chapel and became a national church. Jan Hus was appointed preacher at the Bethlehem Chapel in March 1402. He was burned at the Council of Constance on 6 July 1415. His death turned a university and preaching movement into a national one. Its practical demand was communion in both kinds, bread and wine, for lay people (called *utraquism*, from the Latin *sub utraque specie*, or *Calixtine*, from *calix*, "cup"). Hussite armies resisted repeated crusades. In 1436 Bohemia won the Compactata, a legal recognition of its church practice. Two bodies then developed: the Utraquist church, which held most parishes, and from 1457 the Unity of the Brethren, a smaller, stricter body that later took the name *Unitas Fratrum*. The window is c. 1402–1517.

**Core Identity:** A world in which Christian life is lived in one's own language, with the Scriptures as the test of every church order, and with the cup given to the laity. The world is held together by the memory of a preacher who died for refusing to say what he did not believe. Inside that shared memory, three ways of living it out compete: the national church that must govern a kingdom, the field communities of Tábor that wanted the whole order remade at once, and the Brethren who withdrew to a disciplined community and refused the sword.

**Distinctive Contribution:** No other world in the portfolio reconstructs a non-Roman national church that won legal recognition before Luther. Lollardy (V.5) stayed underground. Devotio Moderna stayed inside existing church structures. This world fought, negotiated, and held parishes and a university for a century. Its second half, the Unity, also produced a distinctive community discipline and, by the census's account, an early printed vernacular hymnbook (1501).

**Living Tradition Status:** Triggered, not confirmed. The census records this world as `living: true`: the Unity's line runs to the Moravian Church, which the census places at Herrnhut in Saxony from 1722. Article 29 covers a world whose reconstruction "touches a tradition that continues into the present" and makes confirmation a freeze-eligibility gate (Constitution Article 29). Two present-day facts need naming at confirmation: which present-day churches are the descendants (the census names the Moravian Church only, and confirmation should check whether others in the Czech lands claim the Hussite or Brethren heritage), and how far a historical reconstruction of 1402–1517 may speak for them. One vendored source is itself mediated through a living tradition: the *Ratio Disciplinae* volume was edited and translated by Bishop B. Seifferth (1866), whose title page names him a bishop of the Brethren's Church. That mediation is recorded in Doc_02 §2. Confirmation itself is open and belongs to the project lead (§9).

## 2. Historical Boundaries

**Approximate Time Horizon:** c. 1402–1517.

- **Beginning (1402).** Hus's appointment as preacher at the Chapel of the Holy Innocents of Bethlehem, Prague. The vendored *Letters* say: "Two years later (March 14, 1402) he was appointed preacher at the Chapel of the Holy Innocents of Bethlehem" (Workman, Part I introduction). This document dates the start by the appointment and does not say that his preaching began in 1402. Workman's own note reports that by Hus's statement "the first year of his preaching was 1401" (Letter XVII note), and that "He was elected to the Bethlehem March 14, 1402." The "c." covers the difference. Confidence: the appointment date is Documented in the vendored edition; the exact start of his preaching is Contested between Hus's own reckoning and the appointment date.
- **The chapel.** The chapel was founded and endowed on 24 May 1391 by two wealthy laymen, on the condition that its rector "preach every Sunday and festival exclusively in the Czech" language (Workman). Hus took over a pulpit that already had a Czech-language mission. This is why the world begins at the chapel and not at his birth or his university career.
- **Ending (1517).** 1517 is the census's window close. It is not a rupture. The Utraquist church and the Unity continue for another century and are the subject of the separate census entry `czech-churches-last-century` (VI.24, 1517–c. 1627, "Utraquists and Unitas"), to which this entry `continuesAs`. The *Ratio Disciplinae* (1632/33) belongs to that entry's timeline, not this one. Doc_08's Ending cell must record this honestly: no internal fracture closes the world in 1517 (§6).

**Developments internal to the window:** Hus's preaching career (1402–14); the 1409 change to the university's charter; his condemnation and burning (1415); the Four Articles of Prague (1420); the crusades and the field wars; the Utraquist–Taborite split and the defeat of the Taborite field army at Lipany (1434); the Compactata (5 July 1436, Lützow); the capture of Tábor (1452); the founding of the Unity (1457); the Peace of Kutná Hora (1485); the Unity's continuing growth and its persecution under King Vladislav's enactments of 1508.

**Developments that indicate transition:** the accession of the Habsburg line and the Reformation in Germany, both after 1517. The Reformation's early contact with Bohemians is recorded in Luther's *Address to the Christian Nobility* (1520, `luther_works-v2-selected_jacobs-spaeth1916.txt`), which is a later witness and outside the window (§7).

**Geographic Centers:**

- **Prague.** The Bethlehem Chapel; the university (Hus's own lectures; the 1409 charter change gave "the Czech element … three votes, and the foreign nations reduced from three to one," Schaff's introduction); the city councils of the Old and New Town that split over the Taborite articles in 1420 (Gillett, ch. XV).
- **Southern Bohemia.** Tábor, the field community of 1419–52. Its 1419 assemblies gathered, by one account, more than forty thousand people (Gillett, ch. XV, citing a *Diarium Belli Hussitici* by a chancellor of New Prague, which is identified here as Lawrence of Březová's chronicle; Gillett's footnote is garbled in the scan); Lützow adds that it was taken by an Utraquist commander in 1452.
- **North-east Bohemia and Moravia.** Kunvald near Žamberk, the Unity's first settlement (Lützow, *Bohemia*). Seifferth's introduction gives the "Barony of Lititz" for the same move. By the Unity's own late account, about two hundred congregations stood in Bohemia and Moravia by about 1500 (the *Ratio*'s own Preface of 1633, printed in the Seifferth volume). Confidence: Inferential/Thin. The figure rests on one partisan witness writing more than a century later.
- **Constance.** The place where the world's central event happened, outside the kingdom. The council's proceedings are recorded in Gillett, Vol. II, ch. I–II, and the Letters from Constance (XXXIX–LXXXII).
- **Kutná Hora.** The site of the 1485 peace.
- **Basel.** The council that concluded the Compactata.

**Cultural Environment:** Bohemia is a kingdom inside the Holy Roman Empire, ruled by kings who are also, at times, Emperors (Wenceslas IV and Sigismund; later King Vladislav II, Lützow's numbering). Czech and German speakers share Prague. Latin is the language of church and university. Hus's *Letters* are mixed: some are written in Czech. Workman calls it "characteristic of Hus's intense nationalism that it should have been written in Czech" of his December 1411 appeal to the Supreme Court of Bohemia (Letter XI). Queen Sophia attended Hus's sermons at the Bethlehem Chapel, according to Lützow's *Hussite Wars*. The wider church is split: Workman describes the cardinals' Council of Pisa of 1409 as an attempt to end "the great schism" (Letters, Part I). Schaff's introduction gives the schism's dates as 1377–1415 in one place and 1378–1417 in another; the discrepancy is carried, not resolved.

**Historical Pressures:**

- The papal schism and the crisis of conciliar authority, which gave Constance its authority to judge Hus (Schaff, Introduction).
- German–Czech tension inside the university and the city, ending with the 1409 charter and the departure of the German masters, whom Piccolomini says went to Leipzig (*Historia Bohemica*, the chapter on the Hussites' sect, headed *De perfida secta Hussitarum* in the printed book; the scan misreads part of the heading).
- The condemnation of Wyclif's books in Prague (Schaff notes that in 1410 the archbishop seized two hundred of them and burned them).
- Crusades against Bohemia. Lützow counts the invasion of 1421 as the second and 1427 as the third crusade; the first came in 1420 and Cardinal Cesarini's failed campaign of 1431 is a fourth. Confidence: Widely Accepted for the count, as Lützow himself says "modern writers generally" number them this way.
- After 1436, the persistent refusal of Rome to confirm an Utraquist archbishop. Lützow: the Bohemians "declined to accept the Compacts in the modified form suggested by the Council till Rokycan had been recognized as Archbishop of Prague" and the Council's representatives "still refused to ratify the election of Rokycan." The Utraquist priests did not settle the question of ordination: "the moderate Utraquists always endeavoured to obtain Catholic ordination for their priests" (Lützow, *Hussite Wars*).
- Political and social violence: on 30 July 1419 Prague councillors were thrown from the town-hall windows (Lützow, *Bohemia*); under King Vladislav II, before the 1485 peace, a Prague crowd stormed three town halls, and "a large number of Germans and Jews were massacred" (Lützow, *Bohemia*).

**Historical Catalysts:** the 1391 foundation of the Bethlehem Chapel; Hus's appointment in March 1402; the 1409 university charter; the condemnation of Wyclif's writings (1410); Hus's departure from Prague and his writing of *De Ecclesia* (1413); his journey to Constance in autumn 1414 (Letter XXXIV) and his burning on 6 July 1415; Jakoubek's lay chalice in Prague in the summer of 1414; the Council of Constance's decree of 15 June 1415 declaring the withholding of the cup lawful (Gillett, ch. III); the 1419 Tábor assemblies; the 1419 Prague defenestration; the Four Articles of 1420; the Compactata of 1436; the founding of the Unity in 1457; the Peace of Kutná Hora in 1485.

## 3. Distinct World Criteria

- **What recurring formation ecology exists?** A vernacular preaching ecology (Bethlehem Chapel, Czech-language letters to lay congregations), the chalice as a lay practice, and a fixed programme (the Four Articles) that a whole kingdom could hold to. From 1457 a second ecology: a community discipline of elders, ministers, household order and hymnody, known in detail only from the *Ratio Disciplinae* of 1632/33 (outside the window, §7 of Doc_02).
- **What recurring gravities exist (preliminary, subject to Doc_04)?** Named here as candidates for testing, not as findings. Each carries an Author Gravity risk because the evidence is concentrated:
  1. Scripture, or "the law of Christ," as the standard by which every church order is judged. The first Article of Prague asks that "the Word of God shall in the kingdom of Bohemia be freely and without impediment proclaimed and preached" (Lützow, *Hussite Wars*, ch. on the Prague articles). The Unity's late self-description names "the leading principle of Huss, that the law of Christ is sufficient for the government of the church militant" (the *Ratio*'s own Preface of 1633, in the Seifferth volume). Risk: this reaches us largely through Hus's own texts, which borrow heavily from Wyclif (§7).
  2. The cup for the laity. Risk: it was not Hus's own concern (§8), so it must not be attributed to him, and its Waldensian or antiquarian origins are disputed (§7).
  3. Czech language and national belonging. Risk: modern nationalist historiography, including Lützow's own, foregrounds it.
  4. Fidelity to truth under pressure (Hus's refusal to recant). Risk: our access is largely through Hus's friends and later Protestant historians.
  5. The church as the whole body of the predestinate, with Christ as its only head (*De Ecclesia*). Risk: the doctrinal core is Wyclif's (§7).
  6. As a tension: the sword against non-resistance. Taborite and Hussite field armies against Chelčický and the Unity's refusal of violence (§5).
- **What recurring patterns of worship, formation, interpretation, belonging, and authority exist?** Worship: communion in both kinds; Czech preaching; the "Isti sunt sancti" hymn sung when the three martyrs of 1412 were carried to Bethlehem (Workman). Formation: catechesis and hymns in Unity households, evidenced only by the later *Ratio* (§7 of Doc_02). Interpretation: appeal to Scripture and the primitive church. Belonging: the peasant and the noble, the Czech speaker and the priest, all "brother and sister" in the Tábor assemblies (Gillett). Authority: contested throughout: the council, the crown, the university, the elected bishop, the Unity's elders.
- **What distinguishes this world from adjacent worlds?** See §7.

## 4. World Separation Criteria

The Framework asks: when does one world become another? The six sub-questions are run below against the movement's own three parts. The evidence cuts both ways, and this section records both.

- **Has a new gravity emerged?** Yes, in the Unity. Its refusal of war, following Chelčický's teaching that "the absolute and unconditional sinfulness of bloodshed" (Lützow, *Bohemia*), is a distinct pattern against the Hussite armies' own record.
- **Has a major gravity disappeared?** Not clearly. The cup, Scripture and the Czech language all continue in the Unity (the *Ratio*'s Preface).
- **Has formation changed substantially?** Yes for the Unity's household discipline, and this is the strongest ground for separation. Confidence: Dominant Modern Reconstruction, because our detailed evidence is from 1632/33.
- **Has worship changed substantially?** Modestly. The Unity keeps the cup and adds its hymnody; the Tábor assemblies had no musical instruments at all (Gillett).
- **Has authority changed substantially?** Yes. The Unity has its own ministers and, by its own account, its own bishops (§5), where the Utraquists never resolved the question of ordination.
- **Has interpretation changed substantially?** Partly. Chelčický reads the Gospel as forbidding oaths and killing, and "believed himself in accord with Wycliffe in maintaining the Real Presence" while opposing the Taborites' eucharistic teaching (Lützow, *Bohemia*).

**Finding.** The separation case is real. The reasons for keeping one world are these: the Unity itself claims descent from Hus (the *Ratio*'s Preface (1633, in the name of the Unity's Seniors and Ministers), English text p. 95, calls its founders "the holy martyr Huss"'s true offspring; the scan misprints the adjective); the census already treats V.6 and VI.24 as one movement with parts (VI.24 is titled "Utraquists and Unitas"); and the world's central shared feature, the cup and the Czech-language Scripture, runs through all parts. Recommended finding: **one world with strands** (§5). Whether the Unity should instead be a separate world is a portfolio-level decision that belongs to the project lead (§9, question 1).

## 5. Strand Determination

Constitution Article 21 defines a strand as "a meaningfully distinct pattern of formation emphasis, practice, authority structure, or ecological orientation within a single world, not merely a variation in detail." A strand is a finding that must answer to evidence.

**Finding: three strands.** The strands are the Utraquist (Calixtine) national church, the Taborite field communities, and the Unity of the Brethren. They share Hus's memory, the chalice, Czech-language Scripture and the appeal to "the law of Christ." They differ on all four of Article 21's dimensions.

| | Utraquist | Taborite | Unity of the Brethren |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Practice** | Communion in both kinds within the inherited liturgy. Gillett: some Calixtines were "scarcely differing, except on the single point of the cup, from the Roman Catholic church." | Open-air assemblies, no musical instruments, priests and laity "undistinguished by garb," images and vestments rejected (Gillett, ch. XV). | Own community discipline, hymns, catechesis; withdrawal from "the Calixtines" (the *Ratio*'s Preface, English text). |
| **Authority structure** | Crown, Estates, university, a consistory; priests seek Catholic ordination (Lützow); no confirmed archbishop. | Elected bishop, Nicholas of Pelhřimov (Lützow: "openly seceding from the Roman Church"); field captains. | Elders, ministers, synods; ordination reportedly from a Waldensian bishop in 1467 (Seifferth), a claim Lützow calls "on insufficient evidence." |
| **Formation emphasis** | Preaching and the four-point programme; a whole population's religion. | Chiliastic expectation, "widely spread at Tabor" from 1420 (Lützow); moral reform by force. | Personal discipline and pacifism from Chelčický; growth by membership. |
| **Ecological orientation** | Engaged with crown, Rome and the Compactata. | Revolutionary and military until defeat at Lipany (1434) and the capture of Tábor (1452). | Withdrawn, then, by Lützow's account, divided into a "Great" and a "Small" party at the end of the fifteenth century. |

**Evidence and confidence, strand by strand.**

- *Utraquist.* The best-evidenced strand. The Four Articles are given in full in Gillett (ch. XV, pp. 441–44) and in summary in Lützow. Confidence: Documented that the strand existed as a distinct church body; Contested on whether it had a stable authority structure (§2).
- *Taborite.* Distinct in practice and authority on the vendored evidence, but known almost entirely from hostile or secondary sources: Piccolomini (hostile), Gillett and Lützow (secondary), with Gillett drawing on the chronicle just named. Confidence: Widely Accepted that it was a distinct wing; Inferential/Thin for its doctrine beyond what these sources report. Piccolomini's stress on the Adamites is disputed by Lützow, who says the Adamites had "no connection whatever with Hussitism" and that Piccolomini "often attacks his enemies in a distinctly unfair manner" (Lützow, *Hussite Wars*, preface and ch. on Tábor). Confidence: Dominant Modern Reconstruction, since Lützow's view leans on an article by Nedoma printed in 1891 and later work is unchecked.
- *Unity.* Its detailed voice is the *Ratio Disciplinae* (1632/33), which is outside the window. In-window evidence is reported by Seifferth's introduction, which names a 1504 Confession to King Vladislav and two letters of 1508 to Dr. Augustine (§2 of Doc_02). Confidence: Widely Accepted that the Unity formed in 1457 and separated from the Calixtines; **Contested** on the Waldensian ordination of 1467 (Seifferth accepts it; Lützow's note says "evidence is rather against the influence of the Waldenses on the foundation of the 'Unity,'" and calls it "one of the most controverted points in Bohemian history").

**Links between strands.** The link between Tábor and the Unity is disputed. Lützow: "The connection of the Taborites with the Bohemian Brethren was long a disputed point in Bohemian history; all recent Bohemian historians, however, maintain its existence," while Goll reports that the Brethren "did not wish to be considered as continuators of the Taborites." This is **Contested** and is carried at full strength.

**What holds across strands.** Hus's memory (Piccolomini, a hostile source, reports that among the Bohemians the burned men earned "Martyrum honores," in his chapter on the Hussites' sect); the cup; Scripture as norm; the Czech language. These are the candidates for cross-strand testing at Doc_04. The strand finding governs strand attribution in every later document.

**What this document does not decide.** Whether the Taborite strand can carry a Representative or a gravity of its own, given that no Taborite voice is vendored, is a question for Doc_02 (source asymmetry) and Doc_04. It is not decided here. Nor does this document decide anything about Representative identity, which belongs to the project lead.

## 6. Preliminary Forces Identification

Constitution Article 22 requires that a world be "situated against the forces that shaped it across both external and internal dimensions," and that where "no internal fracture is attested … that finding is recorded honestly." The sketch below is Layer 1 (Historical Event) only. The complete analysis is Doc_08.

**What generated this world:** two connected triggers. First, a Czech-language preaching pulpit founded by laymen in 1391 and filled by Hus in 1402. Second, the reception of Wyclif's writings in Prague, which gave Hus and others a ready theology of the church. The chalice, raised by Jakoubek in 1414, supplied the practical demand.

**What larger world it was embedded in:** the late-medieval Latin church in crisis over the papal schism; the Kingdom of Bohemia inside the Empire under the Luxembourg kings; the German–Czech tension in the university and the city; and, in the census's framing, the memory of Bohemian reformers before Hus. Lützow names Conrad Waldhauser, Milíč and Matthew of Janov as forerunners and quotes Kybal: "The view that Hussitism is merely artificially fostered Wycliffism appears to me logically and historically as nonsense" (*Life and Times*, preface). This is set against Schaff's view that Hus "appropriated paragraph after paragraph" from Wyclif (§7).

**What it was responding to:** the burning of Hus and the breach of his safe-conduct (Luther later called the safe-conduct broken in his 1520 address); the Council's decree of 15 June 1415 against the cup; and the crusades called against Bohemia.

**What it was refusing:** the rule that lay people receive only the bread; the clergy's secular wealth (Article III of Prague); the claim that a council or pope decides questions of the faith against Scripture (Hus's own stance at Constance); and, for the Unity, war and the world's order as such.

|---|---|---|

## 7. World Continuity & Distinction

**What this world inherits:**

- **Wyclif's writings.** The link is direct textual borrowing, not merely a shared mood. Schaff's translator's introduction to the vendored *De Ecclesia* says: "Huss appropriated paragraph after paragraph from his predecessor and transferred them often with little verbal change to his own pages," drawing on Wyclif's *De Ecclesia* and *De potestate Papae*, and "Never … did a man owe more to mortal teacher than Huss did to John Wyclif." Wyclif's own *Tractatus de Ecclesia* (Loserth, 1886) is vendored under Lollardy (`wyclif_de-ecclesia-lat_loserth1886.txt`). Letter VI, to the English Wycliffite Richard Wyche, was read out in the Bethlehem Chapel. Confidence: Widely Accepted for the textual dependence of *De Ecclesia* (Loserth prints the parallel passages, per Schaff). **Contested** as a general account of the movement: Lützow, quoting Kybal, rejects the view that Hussitism is "merely artificially fostered Wycliffism" and stresses Bohemian forerunners; Schaff records that Loserth revised his own earlier judgment of Hus's originality after Flajšhans's work (Introduction). Both views are carried. **Wyclif died in 1384; the relationship runs through his texts, not through contact between the two men.** This finding is binding on Lollardy's own Doc_01 (§9).
- **Bohemian forerunners.** Conrad Waldhauser, Milíč of Kroměříž and Matthew of Janov, on Lützow's account.
- **The Waldensians.** Contested (§5). Workman also reports that "two Waldensians from Dresden, Peter and Nicholas" persuaded Jakoubek in 1414 to give the cup to the laity, while Piccolomini says Jakoubek found communion in both kinds praised in old codices, chiefly Dionysius and Cyprian. The two accounts of the chalice's origin differ and are both carried. Confidence: Contested.
- **The Latin church's law and sacraments.** Hus quotes the canon law and the Fathers heavily (Schaff, Introduction), and his creed is the Church's (§8).

**What this world transmits:**

- To **Luther and Lutheran Wittenberg (VI.1).** Luther writes in 1520: "we must honestly confess the truth … that John Huss and Jerome of Prague were burnt at Constance in violation of the Papal, Christian, and Imperial oath and safe conduct" (Wace–Buchheim, *First Principles of the Reformation*; the Jacobs–Spaeth *Works*, vol. 2, has the same passage in a different translation). The Luther hymn *Jesus Christus unser Heiland* is printed in the vendored Bacon–Allen volume as "Improved" from the Communion Hymn of John Huss, *Jesus Christus, noster Salus*. The census also gives Luther's remark "we are all Hussites without knowing it," which is not found by a quick search in the vendored Luther files and is carried as the census's own claim, not checked here.
- To **the Czech churches' last century (VI.24)**, and through the Unity to the Moravian Church (census `legacy` field, not vendored).

**Distinctness from adjacent worlds:**

- **Lollardy (V.5).** Direct textual borrowing runs from Wyclif to Hus (above). Lollardy stayed an underground lay network and never won legal standing. The two are correctly separate worlds, and their divergence is carried in both Doc_01s.
- **Devotio Moderna (V.7).** An interior devotional movement inside the existing church. No figure or text overlaps.
- **The Anabaptist Movements (VI.3).** Later, and no overlap identified. The Moravian setting later shelters Hutterites, after this window.
- **Lutheran Wittenberg (VI.1) and the Reformed cities (`rzg`).** After the window; they cite or share the Hussite memory but hold different church orders.
- **`czech-churches-last-century` (VI.24).** The direct continuation. The boundary is 1517. Its later documents (the 1575 Bohemian Confession, the Kralice Bible, Comenius) are not this world's.

## 8. The Article 4 check (Step 0 §4 item 1, binding on Doc_01)

Step 0's floor check was a targeted search across two works. It is extended here across the full vendored corpus and the movement's other strands. Article 4's five commitments are quoted in the Step 0 header.

**Hus, in his own voice.**

1. *One God, the Father almighty.* De Ecclesia, ch. VII (p. 70): faith "concerns the past, namely such things as that God created the world, that Christ was incarnate." Ch. IV (p. 34) has Hus reasoning that "his Father is almighty."
2. *Christ as true God.* De Ecclesia p. 18: "Christ is the individual, the true God and man." Letter XXXIX: "the Lord Jesus, very God, the son of the chaste Virgin Mary." The technical Nicene terms (begotten, of one Being) do not appear in either work on a full-text search; the substance does.
3. *Truly human, born of the Virgin Mary.* Letter XXXIV: "our Lord Jesus Christ … God eternal and true man, born of the Virgin Mary."
4. *Death under Pilate, burial, resurrection, ascension, return.* Letter XX: "He came, the Allpowerful, to suffer under Pilate's power." Letter XVII: the high priests and Pilate "gave Him to death and laid Him in the tomb; but He rose again." De Ecclesia p. 70: "Christ sits at the right hand of the Father … Christ will come again in judgment."
5. *The Holy Spirit.* Letter XXI: "God the Father, Son, and Holy Ghost, eternally one God." De Ecclesia p. 34: "Christ and his Father are one with the Holy Spirit — who is Christ's gift." The formula "Lord and giver of life, worshiped and glorified" does not appear in either work; Hus's evidence for commitment 5 is partial.

**Corroboration from the trial.** Hus was charged at Constance with having said that he was "the fourth person in the Trinity." Gillett reports that Hus answered "God forbid that such an imagination as that I should call myself a fourth person of the Trinity should have been thought of by me" and "repeated the article from the Athanasian creed upon the Trinity," declaring his firm belief in it (Gillett, Vol. II, pp. 53–54). Workman notes the charge as a "monstrous article." The papal inquisitor's own testimonial, printed in Foxe, said he found Hus "a faithful and a catholic man" (not yet assigned to this world; §9).

**The other strands.**

- *Taborites.* The vendored evidence is hostile or secondary. Their recorded departures concern the eucharist and images, not the creed: Martin Húska ("Loquis") was burned for his eucharistic views (Lützow), and Gillett says the Taborite articles "pertained rather to moral conduct and rules of life, than to points of faith." Nothing vendored shows a Taborite denial of any of the five commitments. Confidence: Inferential/Thin, since no Taborite voice is vendored.
- *Pikarts and Adamites.* Piccolomini and Březová describe the Adamites as a sect of nudists; Lützow says they were not Hussites at all. Nothing in the vendored corpus lets this world's floor be judged by them.
- *Unity.* In-window evidence for the five commitments is not vendored. The 1632/33 *Ratio* has its young members recite "the Apostles' creed" together at their profession of faith and has absolution declared in the name of the Trinity. That is later practice and is outside the window.

**Result.** No creedal question is found in Hus's own voice, and none in the secondary and hostile reports on the Taborites. The floor holds on the evidence.
