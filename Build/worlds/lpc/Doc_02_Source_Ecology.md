## 1. Primary Sources

This world's evidentiary situation is close to the inverse of the sibling Donatism build's own: rather than a narrow, hostile-side concentration, this world holds the largest and most direct primary-source base of any confirmed world in the portfolio to date, **among what is currently vendored, on its own primary-source (`role: tradition`) count** (94 `tradition`-role census entries as of 2026-09-29, 92 distinct titles among them counting each once; still the largest of any atlas file in `cic/corpus-map/` on every count attempted: 104 raw entries against the next-largest's 69, 94 `tradition`-role entries against 61) — both anchor figures' own surviving corpora, in their own words, not filtered through an opponent's quotation. The corpus map's own raw entry count is 104, not 94: ten of the 104 are `role: context` (seven of those ten modern, 1901–1921, secondary scholarship, §3 below; the other three are the 411 *Gesta*, Prosper's chronicle and the Latin Codex Theodosianus), which does not belong inside a "primary-source base" figure at all. The 94 `tradition` entries also contain two exact-title duplicates (the Enchiridion, the Scillitan Martyrs, each counted once from an English source and once from a new Latin/Greek one). Many other `tradition` entries are original-language second witnesses for works also held in English, catalogued under a differently worded title (for example the Latin of *City of God*, the *Confessions*, the *Letters*, the *Retractationes* and Cyprian's *Opera*). The count of these second-witness entries is not stated here, because this document keeps no list of them. They are original-language second-witness material, consistent with the original claim's own spirit — but the English evidentiary base is **not** unchanged: Possidius's *Vita Augustini* (row 192) is a genuinely bilingual edition whose complete English translation is new primary-narrative content this world did not otherwise have any of (§1's own Formation-narrative paragraph and §2 below). That qualifier is not decorative: this world's own modern pastoral corpus is materially larger than what the vendored 19th-century NPNF Series I volumes alone can contain — Johannes Divjak's **1975** discovery added 29 previously unknown Augustine letters (CSEL 88, 1981) and François Dolbeau's Mainz find, identified **1990** and published **1996**, added 26 previously unknown sermons, both disproportionately *pastoral*, the ordinary work of a bishop, which is this world's own named subject (neither vendored; Registry rows 49–50). Peter Brown's own 2000 revised edition of his standard biography (row 30) exists in significant part because of these two discoveries — this specific claim about that edition is licensed at rows 49–50; row 30's own Licensed-For excludes "vivid, specific claims" of exactly this kind — and a Registry that cited that edition without naming what made it necessary was under-resourced at exactly the point Doc_03 onward draws from; rows 11 and 19 (the general-correspondence and sermon bodies) are qualified accordingly below. Four of the 94 `tradition` entries come from Petschenig's CSEL 51–53 (*De baptismo*, *Contra epistulam Parmeniani*, *Contra Cresconium*, *De unico baptismo*; Registry row 214). Thirteen further files vendored on 2026-09-29 (Registry rows 215–227) are not yet in the corpus map, so the figures above exclude them. The following groups the corpus map's works thematically; full row-by-row classification (Type, Confidence, Boundary Status, Licensed-For) lives in `Source_Registry.md`.

**Cyprian of Carthage (bishop 248/249–258).** The *Epistles* (82 letters, Registry row 1) are this world's central documentary corpus for its Cyprian phase: the Decian persecution and the lapsed crisis, the Felicissimus schism, the Novatianist rival consecration at Rome, and the rebaptism controversy with Pope Stephen. This corpus's own account of Cyprian's election ("your suffrage and God's judgment," against a rival faction's "ancient venom," Epistle XXXIX — **not Epistle XL**, which is a different letter and carries neither phrase) and his uniform position as *object*, never solicitor, of state power — a corpus-wide negative check across every occurrence of "proconsul," "magistrate," "emperor," and "imperial" in the vendored volume, re-run independently across three separate Doc_01 review rounds (Doc_01 §7) — are both directly re-verified against source. The acts of the *Seventh Council of Carthage* (256 per modern scholarship — the vendored corpus in fact carries this council under three dates, this world's own Registry rows 4 and 42 recording all three — on rebaptism; Registry row 4) carry Cyprian's own egalitarian, non-coercive conciliar-authority theory in his own words as presiding bishop — "neither does any of us set himself up as a bishop of bishops... every bishop, according to the allowance of his liberty and power, has his own proper right of judgment" — directly quoted and re-verified at source. *On the Unity of the Church* (Registry row 3) is the classic treatise on schism and the one episcopate, written amid the Novatianist and Felicissimus crises; it does not itself carry the conciliar-authority quotation, which belongs to the 256 preface alone. A cluster of pastoral treatises (Registry row 5) — *On the Mortality*, *On the Lord's Prayer*, *On Works and Alms*, and others — carries the ordinary congregational content: catechesis, almsgiving, and, in *De Mortalitate*'s case, lived pastoral response to the plague of the 250s.

**Augustine of Hippo (bishop 395/396–430).** The vendored corpus is the largest in the portfolio for any single figure: the *Confessions*; the vendored volume's own 168 letters, split by the corpus map's own staging file into a 17-letter Jerome cluster (deliberately double-placed with the Hieronymian world, since the exchange has voice on both sides), an 11-letter Donatist cluster (`role: context`, assigned to `donatism`) and a 2-letter Pelagian cluster (`role: context`, assigned to `pelagianism`) — neither assigned to `latin-pastoral-congregational-christianity` at all; Registry row 43 handles the one letter from the Donatist cluster this document draws on directly, on its own terms as a source Native to this world rather than as evidence of Donatism's own side, per row 43's own reasoning — and a 138-letter general-correspondence body (Registry rows 9–11); the central anti-Donatist works, *On Baptism, Against the Donatists* and *The Correction of the Donatists* (Letter 185), both directly and repeatedly re-verified at source across Doc_01's nine review rounds (Registry rows 12–13); ~97 sermons and ~695,000 words of Psalm exposition, preached and dictated, most delivered to his own congregations at Hippo and Carthage; 124 Tractates on John; the catechetical works (*On the Catechising of the Uninstructed*, *On Christian Doctrine*, *The Enchiridion*); the anti-Manichaean corpus and a thirteen-work anti-Pelagian corpus, each occasioned by a live controversy Doc_01 §6 names as a recurring gravity candidate (resisting a purity- or sufficiency-test that would exclude the compromised); and *City of God*. ## 2. Author Gravity Assessment

Per the Construction Framework's five-dimension author-gravity discipline (Constitution Article 26), both anchor figures are assessed across Visibility, Representativeness, Influence, Limitations, and Transmission History.

**Cyprian of Carthage.**
- *Visibility:* High but narrow in duration — a ten-year episcopate (248/249–258), concentrated almost entirely on two acute crises (the Decian persecution and its lapsed-reconciliation aftermath, and the rebaptism dispute with Stephen), against which his own ordinary, non-crisis pastoral routine is comparatively under-visible in the surviving letters, which are disproportionately occasional (written to address a specific crisis) rather than routine.
- *Representativeness:* Represents an African metropolitan bishop's own voice specifically, at the head of a large, organized see capable of convening councils of dozens of bishops on short notice (Doc_01 §6) — not a small or marginal congregation, and not, on the rebaptism question, the eventual position of the wider church (Rome's own view prevailed on that specific point, per Doc_01 §5's own account of Augustine's later argument that Cyprian's specific ruling "was contrary to that which was afterwards brought to light... by the authority of a plenary Council"). Nor is Cyprian's own voice free-standing: at least four of his own minor treatises (Registry row 5) directly rework, follow, or depend on Tertullian — a real, non-Native influence-source Doc_01 §8 item 5 binds this document to name here rather than only at §1's own Named Comparandum treatment of Tertullian (row 29), per that item's own instruction not to "let it pass silently a second time."
- *Influence:* Foundational for this world's own ecclesiology of the one episcopate and, on the point Doc_01 §8 item 10 holds open rather than settled, real and substantial but *contested* on conciliar authority specifically: Doc_01 §5 records Augustine "arguing *with* Cyprian, disputing his ruling while claiming his communion" on the rebaptism question — a stronger continuity datum than deference would have been, since a bishop argues this way only with his own tradition's own father — but Doc_01 §8 item 10 itself, not yet closed, names Cyprian's own egalitarian, non-coercive conciliar-authority theory and Augustine's own hierarchical, correctable one as "real, substantial differences" not "fully settled" to touch this world's own recurring gravities. This document's own account states the dispute rather than compressing it into unqualified continuity. Also foundational for the Latin theological vocabulary itself, at one remove: Cyprian works directly within the idiom Tertullian is credited with forging (Doc_01 §7), so Cyprian's own influence on this world is itself partly the transmission of an influence he received.
- *Limitations:* The corpus embeds letters by others (Cornelius, the Roman clergy, Firmilian of Caesarea, the confessors) under Cyprian's own name, per the corpus map's own note on CCEL's whole-body attribution — a transmission fact this Registry (row 1) discloses rather than treats as though every letter in the body is demonstrably Cyprian's own composition. The corpus is also occasional rather than systematic: it documents what crises provoked correspondence, not Cyprian's ordinary, undocumented weekly pastoral routine.
- *Transmission History:* Preserved through the Latin Western manuscript tradition as a canonical Father of the church on both sides of the later rebaptism-doctrine dispute his own letters record — his conciliar authority theory was cited by Augustine against the Donatists (this world's own boundary work, Doc_01 §7), and the Donatists themselves appealed to Cyprian's own authority and to the 256 council's own judgments for their rebaptism doctrine — grounded on this world's own vendored primary corpus rather than on a corpus-map annotation or an unverified cross-world citation (Registry row 13, Confidence A). Augustine's own *On Baptism, Against the Donatists* states this directly, in his own continuous words, at Book III ch. 2 §2: what the Donatists bring forward is "from the Council and the epistles of Cyprian, to the effect that Christ's baptism may not be given by the hands of heretics." The surrounding volume corroborates the same claim through its own editorial apparatus rather than through further first-person Augustine text — the NPNF preface's own quotation of Augustine's *Retractations* II.18 ("who strive to defend themselves by the authority of the most blessed bishop and martyr Cyprian") — the work Registry rows 67 (Mutzenbecher's CCSL 57, in copyright) and 78 (Knöll's CSEL 36, public domain — now actually vendored, row 209, closing the gap §2's own Augustine entry below names) both now carry, and the vendored edition's own editorial summaries of Books II, VI and VII (they are the edition's third-person *argumenta*, not Augustine's text, the same editorial-voice distinction this document already draws for row 42's Zonaras quotation and for Letter CCXI at §6 below). This is Augustine's own testimony about the Donatist appeal, not an independent Donatist-authored source making it in their own words — a distinction worth stating plainly rather than blurring. The underlying claim rests on Book III ch. 2 §2. A figure preserved and argued from by parties who reached opposite later conclusions is not a transmission-selection risk in the way a one-sided hostile-quotation corpus is, but it does mean his own words are read by later readers already embedded in disputes he did not himself anticipate.

**Augustine of Hippo.**
- *Visibility:* Overwhelmingly high and unusually well-documented across his own thirty-five-year episcopate (395/396–430) — the largest surviving body of evidence for any figure in this project's own portfolio, spanning sermons, letters, treatises, and a first-person spiritual autobiography (the *Confessions*) with no real analogue elsewhere in this world's own corpus.
- *Representativeness:* Represents an African bishop writing from a position of increasing institutional confidence relative to Donatism specifically (post-405 Edict of Unity, post-411 Conference) — his own later anti-Donatist and anti-Pelagian works are not written from a contested, undecided position the way Cyprian's mid-crisis letters often are.
- *Influence:* Immense and lasting, both for how this world's own theology of grace, original sin, and the two cities becomes foundational for the entire subsequent Western tradition (Doc_01 §7), and — the specific risk this dimension exists to flag — for how disproportionately his own voice, rather than any other North African bishop's, has come to stand for "what the ordinary Latin pastoral bishop of this period was like." This is a claim about *episcopal* voice specifically, not a claim that no other individual is named or heard from in this world's own corpus at all — §6 below documents real, if narrower, non-episcopal and lay first-person material (Pontius, Epistles XX–XXI, several of Augustine's own women correspondents), and this dimension's own risk is precisely that a reader could mistake Augustine's episcopal dominance for the whole of this world's own voice, which it is not.
- *Limitations:* The great majority of ordinary-believer and ordinary-congregant experience of this world survives only as it is refracted through one or the other bishop's own preaching and correspondence, not in the believer's own words — a real, load-bearing asymmetry this document names directly at §6 below, alongside the narrower, real exceptions §6 also documents.
- *Transmission History:* Preserved through the Catholic institutional tradition's own manuscript transmission, as the doctrinally and institutionally victorious party's literature on every dispute he engaged (Donatism, Manichaeism, Pelagianism) — a transmission advantage this world shares with, and inherits in part from, the same institutional continuity the sibling Donatism build's own Author Gravity work names as working systematically against its own preservation. More specifically: the vendored NPNF Series I volumes are 19th-century English translations (Series editor Philip Schaff), a further translating hand between Augustine's own Latin and this document's own citations, distinct from and later than the Latin manuscript transmission itself. **Substantially closed:** a specific base Latin edition is now vendored and directly checkable for the Confessions (CSEL 33, row 197, a modern critical edition), the general and Jerome-cluster correspondence (CSEL 34/1–2, 44, 57 Pars IV, rows 195–196, 193, modern critical editions), City of God (CSEL 40, rows 198–199, a modern critical edition), the Retractationes (CSEL 36, row 209, a modern critical edition), the Expositions on the Psalms (Migne PL 36–37, row 201, the 1861 Maurist printing, **not** a modern critical edition), and On Christian Doctrine and the Enchiridion (the Tauchnitz/Maurist text, row 200, likewise **not** a modern critical edition). **Not yet closed, named rather than assumed innocuous — row 16 (*On Christian Doctrine*) is closed, by row 200, not open:** no base Latin edition of any kind is vendored for the Sermons (row 19), the anti-Manichaean corpus (row 22), the anti-Pelagian corpus (row 23), the catechetical/creedal works and *De Trinitate*-and-related treatises (rows 15, 18, 25), the Tractates on John and related exegetical works (row 21), or — the most consequential of these, since it is the row this document's own Cyprian entry above quotes Augustine's continuous words from — **the anti-Donatist corpus (rows 13–14, row 13 at Confidence A)**. The gap remains real for all of these specifically, narrower than before but not eliminated.

**Pontius the Deacon**, treated separately as a distinct formation-narrative voice rather than folded into the Cyprian entry above, since his own perspective (an eyewitness deacon writing after his bishop's martyrdom) is not the same as Cyprian's own first-person letters.
- *Visibility:* Visible only through the one work attributed to him, *The Life and Passion of Cyprian* — no other writing by Pontius survives in this world's own corpus.
- *Representativeness:* Represents a close subordinate's own retrospective, hagiographically-shaped account of his bishop, not a peer bishop's or an ordinary congregant's perspective. He is nonetheless this world's own clearest non-episcopal, extended first-person clerical voice (a deacon, not a bishop) — a real, if narrow, exception to the two-bishops-dominate-everything pattern named throughout this section, and named again at §6 below where the Article 20 discharge depends on it.
- *Influence:* Substantial for how Cyprian's own conversion and election are known at all — the acclamation account this document and Doc_01 both draw on (§1 above) has no surviving parallel in Cyprian's own letters.
- *Limitations:* Written after Cyprian's own martyrdom, by a deacon with every institutional and personal reason to idealize his bishop; the genre-level Author Gravity risk this fact creates is assessed directly at §4 below.
- *Transmission History:* The ANF05 volume's own introductory notice states its base Latin text for the Cyprianic corpus is Migne's, with the Oxford edition's own letter-numbering appended in notes for reference (its translator for the Cyprian material is the Rev. ## 4. Formation Narrative Sources

Per Construction Framework Part II, hagiographic and narrative sources are assessed separately from theological treatises and institutional records, since they carry formation content differently than argument does.

**Pontius the Deacon, *The Life and Passion of Cyprian, Bishop and Martyr*.** *Authorship and date:* attributed to Pontius, Cyprian's own deacon; composed after Cyprian's martyrdom (258), within living memory of the events it narrates. *Proximity:* a first-generation, eyewitness-adjacent account — Pontius accompanied Cyprian into exile at Curubis, per the work's own narrative — not a later legendary elaboration. *Genre conventions:* early Christian biography with hagiographic shaping already present (the martyr's steadfastness, the manner of death as the completion of a formed life), though earlier and less developed in convention than the fuller passio form that follows it. *Formation ecology revealed:* how a Carthaginian congregation and its clergy understood their bishop's own path into office (the acclamation account, §1 and §2 above) and his conduct under persecution — a close subordinate's own testimony to what formation under this world's founding crisis looked like from inside the bishop's own household. *Author Gravity risk:* real and named directly — written by a deacon with every personal and institutional reason to idealize his own bishop, after that bishop's own martyrdom had already begun to be venerated (the *Life* itself does not state that Cyprian ordained Pontius, only that Pontius was one of his "household companions" chosen to share his exile — checked directly, not asserted); this document does not treat its account of Cyprian's own interior motivations as a neutral historical record on that basis, while treating its narration of external, checkable events (the exile to Curubis, the manner and circumstances of the martyrdom) as more reliable than its characterization of Cyprian's own inner disposition.

**Possidius, *Sancti Augustini Vita* (*Life of Augustine*).** *Authorship and date:* attributed to Possidius, bishop of Calama and Augustine's own friend of nearly forty years; composed after Augustine's own death (430), drawing on decades of first-hand acquaintance. *Proximity:* a bishop's own retrospective, written from decades of friendship that began as a younger monastic disciple's relationship to Augustine (§2 above qualifies the peer/subordinate contrast precisely) and matured into shared episcopal office by 397 — a materially different vantage than Pontius's own, unvarying deacon's-eye account of Cyprian, and this world's own only formation-narrative source written by someone who came to hold episcopal office himself. *Genre conventions:* Christian biography in the same broad tradition as Pontius's *Life*, composed with that earlier work's own example in view; the extent to which Possidius's own account is independently structured, as against modeled on Pontius's, is not assessed. *Formation ecology revealed:* a fellow bishop's own account of Augustine's formation into and conduct of the episcopal office, a source Doc_01 §5 now cites directly (*Vita* ch. VIII: the Megalius identification and the popular acclamation at Augustine's consecration), and which the build thread has read in full (`Review-Artifacts/Possidius_Full_Read_2026-09-16.md`; §1 and §2 above). *Author Gravity risk:* real and named directly, on the same footing as Pontius's — written by a close friend after the subject's own death, with every personal reason to idealize a man he had known for decades; this document does not treat Possidius's own account of Augustine's interior life as a neutral historical record on that basis, the same discipline already applied to Pontius's account of Cyprian, though this document has not yet independently tested that discipline against Possidius's own specific narrative choices.

**Cyprian's own letters as formation-narrative-adjacent material.** Several of Cyprian's own letters on the lapsed and on Felicissimus's schism (Registry row 1) function partly as narrative — first-person accounts of a community's crisis and its resolution — rather than purely as doctrinal argument, though they remain classified as primary correspondence rather than as a separate narrative genre, consistent with how the corpus map itself treats them.

## 6. Source Asymmetries and Missing Voices

This world's own evidentiary asymmetry is not, primarily, a hostile-source problem in the way the sibling Donatism build's own is — both anchor voices speak in their own words, as bishops of the tradition this world's own construction centers on, not as an opponent's quotation. The asymmetry here is narrower and different in kind: **two named, elite, male, clerical voices carry nearly this entire world's own surviving record across a 184-year span.**

- **Elite/literate/male/episcopal skew (Article 20).** The great majority of this world's own surviving record is authored by, or organized around, one of two bishops — but not all of it. **The non-episcopal clergyman is Pontius**, a deacon, whose extended first-person account (Registry row 7) is the whole subject of §1, §2, and §4 above. **Two lay believers' own letters survive in their own words:** Epistles XX and XXI, "Celerinus to Lucian" and "Lucian Replies to Celerinus" (Registry row 1) — two confessors, writing to each other in the first person about the reconciliation of Celerinus's lapsed sisters at Rome, neither yet ordained at the time of writing. **Named women appear, in real narrative weight, inside Registry row 11's own licensed body:** Letter CXXVI, to Albina (a.d. 411) — Augustine's own first-person account of the riot in his basilica at Hippo when the congregation demanded Pinianus's ordination, written to answer Albina's own charge that Pinianus had been coerced under threat of death; and Letter CCXI, to the Nuns of Hippo (a.d. 423) — addressing the revolt in the women's monastery his own sister had led as prioress; **the vendored volume's own editorial framing**, not Augustine's text itself, characterizes the letter as embodying the rule of the Augustinian orders ("of historical value, as embodying the rules of nunneries belonging to the Augustinian orders"), a textual relationship between this letter and the *Praeceptum* that is a live question in the scholarship and is attributed to the editorial hand here rather than absorbed as Augustine's own statement, per the discipline Doc_01 established for this exact shape of claim. Both independently re-verified against source (Registry row 11). What remains true: no source anywhere in this world's own vendored corpus is authored by an ordinary lay believer *writing about ordinary congregational life as such*, rather than about a specific crisis (the lapsed reconciliation, a riot, a monastic revolt) that drew a bishop's own attention and thereby survival.
