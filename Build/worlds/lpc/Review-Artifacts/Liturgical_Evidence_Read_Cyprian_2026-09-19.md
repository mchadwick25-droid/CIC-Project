## 2. Findings by domain

### Domain 1 — Baptism as administered

**Finding 1.1 — A fixed baptismal interrogation existed and is partially quoted.**
Cyprian, arguing that a heretical minister cannot validly baptize, quotes the interrogation itself:
> "For when we say, "Dost thou believe in eternal life and remission of sins through the holy Church?" we mean that remission of sins is not granted except in the Church"
— Epistle LXIX §2 (Oxford ed. Ep. lxx; CSEL 3.2, Hartel p. 767–768: *"nam cum dicimus: credis in uitam aeternam et remissionem peccatorum per sanctam ecclesiam"*).
**Statement**, not presupposition: Cyprian directly quotes the question. **Confidence: Widely Accepted** (the wording is stated, not inferred, and the Latin and English match exactly).

**Finding 1.2 — Post-baptismal anointing (chrism) is named as necessary, and is tied to oil consecrated on the altar for the Eucharist.**
> "It is also necessary that he should be anointed who is baptized; so that, having received the chrism, that is, the anointing, he may be anointed of God, and have in him the grace of Christ. Further, it is the Eucharist whence the baptized are anointed with the oil sanctified on the altar."
— Epistle LXIX §2 (CSEL 3.2, Hartel p. 768: *"ungi quoque necesse est eum qui baptizatus est, ut accepto chrismate id est unctione esse unctus Dei et habere in se gratiam Christi possit."* The following sentence in the vendored Hartel print reads *"porro autem eucharistia estis unde baptizati unguntur oleum in altari sanctificatum"* — quoted exactly as the file has it, "estis" and all; the word does not parse as ordinary Latin prose there, and an ANF footnote to this same passage separately records that "an authorized reading here is, 'But further, the Eucharist and the oil, whence the baptized are anointed, are sanctified on the altar,'" indicating a live manuscript-variant question at this exact clause. This document does not silently resolve "estis" to "est" and presents both the garbled transcription and the variant question rather than a cleaned-up composite.).
**Statement.** **Confidence: Widely Accepted** for the practice of post-baptismal anointing itself; **Inferential-Thin** for exactly how the oil's sanctification and the Eucharistic offering were procedurally joined (Cyprian names the connection but does not describe a rite order). Note: the word "Exomologesis" that an ANF footnote glosses onto a *different* passage (Domain 3) shows this translator supplies underlying technical terms in footnotes; here no such footnote is attached, so "chrism"/"the anointing" is Cyprian's own English rendering, not translator apparatus.

**Finding 1.3 — Baptismal renunciation of the devil is presupposed, not described as a formula.**
Reproaching those who apostatized under torture, Cyprian writes:
> "Could the servant of God stand there, and speak and renounce Christ, when he had already renounced the devil and the world?"
— *On the Lapsed* §8.
**Presupposition, not statement**: Cyprian does not quote a renunciation formula; the sentence only makes rhetorical sense if renouncing "the devil and the world" was something every baptized person had already done. **Confidence: Dominant Modern Reconstruction** (the baptismal renunciation is well attested for this period across sources; this passage corroborates it for Carthage specifically but does not supply its wording).

**Finding 1.4 — Infant baptism is decreed as of right, and the timing debate presupposes an existing practice of baptizing within days of birth.**
> "we all thought very differently in our council. For in this course which you thought was to be taken, no one agreed; but we all rather judge that the mercy and grace of God is not to be refused to any one born of man."
— Epistle LVIII §2 (council of 66 bishops; Oxford ed. Ep. lxiv), responding to Fidus's proposal that infants be baptized only on the eighth day per the analogy to circumcision. The letter's own occasion — a dispute over whether to baptize "within the second or third day after their birth" (§2) — is a **statement** that early infant baptism, not merely infant baptism eventually, was already the debated norm; the letter decrees against delay, not for the practice's introduction (its own Argument line says as much: Cyprian "is Not Establishing Any New Decree"). **Confidence: Widely Accepted.**

The same letter also states, of the newborn about to be baptized:
> "Nor ought any of us to shudder at that which God hath condescended to make. For although the infant is still fresh from its birth, yet it is not such that any one should shudder at kissing it in giving grace and in making peace"
— Epistle LVIII §4. This is a **statement** about a kiss given to the infant "in giving grace" — plausibly connected to the baptismal/reconciling kiss tradition attested elsewhere in Latin Christianity — but Cyprian does not name it a liturgical *osculum pacis* rite here, and this is the only kiss-language found in the corpus searched (see the "yielded nothing" note above). **Confidence: Inferential-Thin** on whether this is *the* kiss of peace rite specifically, rather than an ordinary affectionate gesture Cyprian is defending against squeamishness.

**Finding 1.5 — Clinical (sickbed) baptism by sprinkling/affusion is defended as fully valid, against those who denied it, revealing both the practice and its contested status.**
> "You have asked also, dearest son, what I thought of those who obtain God's grace in sickness and weakness, whether they are to be accounted legitimate Christians, for that they are not to be washed, but sprinkled, with the saving water."
And:
> "nor ought it to trouble any one that sick people seem to be sprinkled or affused, when they obtain the Lord's grace"
— Epistle LXXV §12 (To Magnus). Cyprian goes on to report, and reject, a nickname given to such converts:
> "moreover, in respect of some calling those who have obtained the peace of Christ by the saving water and by legitimate faith, not Christians, but Clinics, I do not find whence they take up this name"
— Epistle LXXV §13. **Statement**, both of the practice (sprinkling for the gravely ill) and of a live dispute over its sufficiency — Cyprian's own side of the argument, not a neutral report; the ANF editorial note flags that "Clinics" were in fact "treated by canonical law as less fit for" clerical advancement even after Cyprian's defense, i.e., his own argument did not fully settle the question. **Confidence: Widely Accepted** for the practice; **Contested** for its full equivalence to immersion baptism, which is precisely what this letter is arguing and therefore cannot be reported as settled.

**Finding 1.6 — Deathbed/emergency baptism-adjacent administration could be performed by a deacon in the priest's absence, for the parallel case of reconciliation (see Domain 3, Finding 3.2) — no equally explicit statement was found that a deacon could baptize a dying catechumen, only that a deacon could "supply a lawful hand to the absent priest"** in administering baptism generally, per an ANF editorial note at Epistle LXIX §2 (marked `[[NOTE-START]]...[[NOTE-END]]`, hence **not quoted as Cyprian's own words** — reported here as apparatus, not as a Cyprian statement, precisely to avoid the paraphrase-as-quotation and note-as-voice defects this task warns against).

**Finding 1.7 — Imposition of hands after baptism, to confer and "seal" the Holy Spirit, is stated directly by Cyprian himself as the Church's own ordinary completion of baptism, on the model of Acts 8 (Peter and John at Samaria) — and is argued at length as clearly distinct from the water-rite itself in the separate dispute over converts from heresy.**
On the ordinary case, Cyprian to Jubaianus:
> "so that they who are baptized in the Church are brought to the prelates of the Church, and by our prayers and by the imposition of hands obtain the Holy Spirit, and are perfected with the Lord's seal"
— Epistle LXXII §9 (To Jubaianus, Concerning the Baptism of Heretics; Oxford ed. Ep. lxxiii; CSEL 3.2, Hartel p. 785, Epistula LXXIII c.9, quoted exactly as the vendored file has it: *"quod nunc quoque apud nos geritur, ut qui in ecclesia baptizantur praepositis ecclesiae offerantur et per nostram orationem ac manus inpositionem sspiritunx sanctum consequantur et signaculo dominico consummentur"* — the string `sspiritunx` is plainly an OCR/print artifact standing for *spiritum* ("obtain the Holy Spirit"), not a real Latin word; it is left exactly as the file prints it rather than silently corrected, on the same principle applied throughout this correction pass). **Statement**, in Cyprian's own voice, of an ordinary post-baptismal rite: prayer and imposition of hands by "the prelates of the Church," received by every candidate, that gives the Spirit and "the Lord's seal" (*signaculum dominicum*) — the same act Finding 1.2's chrism/anointing may be a second name for, or a companion rite to; this document does not resolve which, and flags the two findings' possible overlap rather than asserting they are one and the same act. **Confidence: Widely Accepted.**

On the separate, contested case of converts from heresy/schism, Cyprian to Magnus:
> "If they have, why are hands imposed on those who are baptized among them when they come to us, that they may receive the Holy Ghost, since He must surely have been received there, where if He was He could be given?"
— Epistle LXXV §11 (To Magnus, restating the argument also made in Ep. LXXIV/Firmilian). **Statement** that imposition of hands upon converts from heresy, distinct from the water-baptism the Church requires them to undergo again, was standard practice on Cyprian's own side of the dispute. **Confidence: Widely Accepted** that the rite existed and was disputed in its sufficiency for this second, contested population specifically; **Contested**, by definition, on whether hand-imposition alone (Cyprian's opponents, including the spurious anonymous *De Rebaptismate*) or hand-imposition only after re-baptism (Cyprian) was efficacious for converts from heresy — this is the very axis Doc_03 already logs under "heresy — validity of baptism."

**Finding 1.8 — The false-prophetess episode (Firmilian's letter) is presuppositional gold: an imitation only works if the imitated form was fixed and recognizable.**
> "also to baptize many, making use of the usual and lawful words of interrogation, that nothing might seem to be different from the ecclesiastical rule"
— Epistle LXXIV §11 (CSEL 3.2, Hartel p. 318, Ep. LXXV c.10: *"baptizaret quoque multos usitatas et legitima uerba interrogationis usurpans, ut nihil discrepare ab ecclesiastica regula uideretur"*), and:
> "Can it be believed that either remission of sins was given, or the regeneration of the saving laver duly completed, when all things, although after the image of truth, yet were done by a demon?"
— same section. **Presupposition, explicitly and doubly so**: Firmilian's entire point — that this baptism was invalid *despite* looking identical to a real one — only works if there existed a fixed, recognizable set of baptismal words ("the symbol of the Trinity," "the legitimate and ecclesiastical interrogatory," both named in §11) that a deceiver could successfully copy. This is the strongest single piece of evidence in the whole read that a stable baptismal liturgical form existed in mid-third-century Cappadocia/Carthage circles, precisely because the letter is not describing the form to teach it — it is assuming the reader already recognizes it well enough to be shocked that a demon reproduced it. **Confidence: Dominant Modern Reconstruction** (this is inference from presupposition, clearly labeled as such, not a direct liturgical description).

### Domain 2 — The Eucharist

**Finding 2.1 — The mixed cup: what is on the table, argued at exhaustive length.**
> "the cup which is offered in remembrance of Him should be offered mingled with wine"
— Epistle LXII §2 (To Cæcilius). And, summarizing the whole letter's argument:
> "Thus the cup of the Lord is not indeed water alone, nor wine alone, unless each be mingled with the other"
— Epistle LXII §13. **Statement**, argued as a point of "the tradition of the Lord" against those using water alone ("Hydroparastatae," per an ANF footnote — apparatus, not quoted as Cyprian's word). **Confidence: Widely Accepted.**

**Finding 2.2 — The Eucharist's own timing is stated directly, and is itself evidence the practice had already shifted from the Last Supper's own evening setting — a rare case in this corpus of Cyprian stating a presupposition explicitly rather than leaving it implicit.**
> "But we celebrate the resurrection of the Lord in the morning."
— Epistle LXII §16. Cyprian poses and answers his own rhetorical question about why the Church does not celebrate "after supper" as Christ did, concluding the morning timing is deliberate (resurrection-symbolism) even though it departs from the Last Supper's own evening hour. **Statement** of current practice ("we celebrate... in the morning"); the *reason* given for it is Cyprian's own theological argument, not a report of anyone else's explanation, so the justification itself should be read as argued, not attested as consensus. **Confidence: Widely Accepted** for the practice (morning celebration); the specific resurrection-rationale is Cyprian's own apologetic, not independently corroborated in the read.

**Finding 2.3 — Who administers the cup, and self-communication by hand, both stated directly in a first-person eyewitness account.**
> "When, however, the solemnities were finished, and the deacon began to offer the cup to those present, and when, as the rest received it, its turn approached, the little child... turned away its face, compressed its mouth with resisting lips, and refused the cup."
— *On the Lapsed* §25 (CSEL Hartel p. 255: *"calicem diaconus offerre praesentibus coepit"*). And, of an adult recipient:
> "he could not eat nor handle the holy of the Lord, but found in his hands... that he had a cinder"
— *On the Lapsed* §26 (CSEL Hartel p. 256: *"cinerem ferre se apertis manibus inuenit"* — "found that he was carrying ash in his open hands"). **Statement**, given explicitly as Cyprian's own eyewitness testimony ("Learn what occurred when I myself was present and a witness," §25). This establishes: the deacon administers the cup to communicants in order; the consecrated bread is received into the recipient's own open hands, not placed directly in the mouth. **Confidence: Widely Accepted** for both the deacon's role at the cup and hand-communication of the bread — these are stated, not inferred, and independently corroborated by the matching Latin.

**Finding 2.4 — Home reservation of the Eucharist for the absent/sick is presupposed by an anecdote about a woman's private container.**
> "another woman, when she tried with unworthy hands to open her box, in which was the holy (body) of the Lord, was deterred by fire rising from it"
— *On the Lapsed* §26 (CSEL Hartel p. 256: *"quaedam arcam suam in quo Domini sanctum fuit manibus inmundis temptasset aperire"*). **Presupposition**, not an explicit statement of the practice's purpose: Cyprian never says outright "the faithful keep the reserved sacrament at home for later use," but the story only makes sense if a private *arca* ("box/ark") containing "the Lord's holy thing" (*Domini sanctum*) at a lay person's own house was an unremarkable, pre-existing fact the audience would recognize without explanation. **Confidence: Dominant Modern Reconstruction** — the practice of home reservation for the sick/absent is well attested for this period across other sources (Justin, Tertullian); this passage corroborates it for Carthage but, again, presupposes rather than describes it.

**Finding 2.5 — Exclusion from the table is enacted physically: unworthy communicants "intrude" bodily, and the altar itself is a marker of legitimacy.**
> "Returning from the altars of the devil, they draw near to the holy place of the Lord, with hands filthy and reeking with smell... they intrude on the body of the Lord"
— *On the Lapsed* §15. And, on schism specifically:
> "he dares to set up another altar, to make another prayer with unauthorized words, to profane the truth of the Lord's offering by false sacrifices"
— *On the Unity of the Church* §17 (numbering per the ANF's own running section markers in that treatise). **Statement**, in both cases, though the second is a polemical description of the schismatic's own act rather than a rubric; together they presuppose (rather than state outright) that legitimate Eucharistic offering in this world is tied to one bishop, one altar, and a fixed form of prayer from which "unauthorized words" visibly depart. **Confidence: Widely Accepted** for one-altar/one-bishop practice; **Inferential-Thin** for the claim that a fixed prayer-text (rather than merely a fixed office/minister) is what "unauthorized words" departs from — Cyprian never quotes the authorized words to contrast them.

**Finding 2.6 — The offering made for the dead: named directly, tied to an annual liturgical calendar of martyr commemorations.**
> "We always offer sacrifices for them, as you remember, as often as we celebrate the passions and days of the martyrs in the annual commemoration."
— Epistle XXXIII §3 (To the Clergy and People, About the Ordination of Celerinus as Reader). **Statement.** **Confidence: Widely Accepted.** (An ANF editorial note glosses this as "Memorial thanksgivings," arguing it shows the absence of purgatorial ideas since martyrs "were allowed by all to go at once to bliss" — this interpretive gloss is apparatus, not Cyprian's own claim, and is not relied on here.)

**Finding 2.7 — Reader as a liturgical office reading the Gospel from a raised platform, adjacent to but distinct from the Eucharist itself.**
> "what else behoved to be done except that he should be placed on the pulpit... that he should read the precepts and Gospel of the Lord"
— Epistle XXXIII §4. **Statement.** Domain note: this documents the liturgy of the word (reading) as a distinct, named ministry (*lector*) within the same assembly the Eucharist belongs to, though the letter does not describe how the two parts of the service related in sequence. **Confidence: Widely Accepted** for the office; **Not Attested** for the internal order of readings relative to the offering, which this letter simply does not address.

### Domain 3 — Penance and reconciliation

**Finding 3.1 — The stages are named directly: penance for a set time, public confession (*exomologesis*), then imposition of the bishop's and clergy's hand, which alone confers the right of communion.**
> "For although in smaller sins sinners may do penance for a set time, and according to the rules of discipline come to public confession, and by imposition of the hand of the bishop and clergy receive the right of communion"
— Epistle IX §2 (Oxford ed. Ep. xvi). An ANF footnote attached to "public confession" reads simply: `"Exomologesis."` — identifying, as translator's apparatus (not Cyprian's own English word, and clearly marked as a footnote in the source, not quoted here as if it were his), the Latin/Greek technical term behind the phrase. The Hartel Latin confirms the term is in Cyprian's own text, not only the translator's gloss: *"et secundum disciplinae ordinem ad exomologesin ueniant, et per manus inpositionem episcopi et cleri ius communicationis accipiant"* (CSEL 3.2, Hartel p. 521, Epistula XVI). **Statement**, and precisely locatable in both languages. **Confidence: Widely Accepted.**

The same letter continues, describing what the premature reconciliations it condemns skip:
> "while the penitence is not yet performed, confession is not yet made, the hands of the bishop and clergy are not yet laid upon them, the eucharist is given to them"
— Epistle IX §2. This confirms, by negative example, the same three-part sequence (penitence → confession → imposition of hands) as the thing being bypassed. **Statement. Confidence: Widely Accepted.**

**Finding 3.2 — Deathbed reconciliation could be administered by a presbyter, or, in extremity, a deacon — an explicit statement of who may perform the rite and under what urgency.**
> "that they who have received certificates from the martyrs... should, without waiting for my presence, before any presbyter who might be present, or if a presbyter should not be found and death begins to be imminent, before even a deacon, be able to make confession of their sin, that, with the imposition of hands upon them for repentance, they should come to the Lord with the peace which the martyrs have desired"
— Epistle XII §1 (Oxford ed. Ep. xviii). **Statement**, directly naming the minister hierarchy for this rite: bishop normally, presbyter in his absence, deacon only if death is imminent and no presbyter is available. **Confidence: Widely Accepted** — this is one of the most procedurally explicit passages in the entire corpus read.

**Finding 3.3 — The confessors' certificate (what modern scholarship calls the *libellus pacis*) altered the process's own timing, without itself being the reconciling rite.**
> "they who have received a certificate from the martyrs, and can be assisted by their help with the Lord in respect of their sins, if they begin to be oppressed with any sickness or risk; when they have made confession, and have received the imposition of hands on them by you in acknowledgment of their penitence, should be remitted to the Lord with the peace promised to them by the martyrs"
— Epistle XIII §2 (Oxford ed. Ep. xix). **Statement.** This shows the certificate (English rendering; the underlying Latin word is *libellus*, confirmed in Hartel — e.g. *"libello a martyribus accepto"*, CSEL 3.2 p. 522) functioned as a claim on accelerated admission *contingent on sickness*, not as a substitute for confession and the imposition of hands, both of which the letter still requires. **Confidence: Widely Accepted** for the mechanism; **Contested**, within Cyprian's own corpus (Ep. LI/Antonianus, below), for how uniformly this was actually honored across individual confessors and bishops.

**Finding 3.4 — Reconciliation, in the ordinary (non-emergency) case, is a public, congregational act requiring the bishop, clergy, and "the people who stand fast" together — not a private clerical transaction.**
> "the chief officers meeting together with the clergy in the presence also of the people who stand fast, to whom themselves, moreover, honour is to be shown for their faith and fear, we may be able to order all things with the religiousness of a common consultation"
— Epistle XIII §2. **Presupposition rendered nearly explicit**: Cyprian does not call this "the reconciliation rite" outright, but states it as the necessary form any ordinary case must take, in contrast to the emergency exception just described. **Confidence: Widely Accepted.**

**Finding 3.5 — Tiered treatment by category of lapse, and the deathbed exception stated as council policy, with its own rationale given explicitly.**
> "it was decided, dearest brother, the case of each individual having been examined into, that the receivers of certificates should in the meantime be admitted, that those who had sacrificed should be assisted at death, because there is no confession in the place of the departed"
— Epistle LI §17 (To Antonianus; Oxford ed. Ep. lv; the Latin phrase for "the place of the departed" is glossed by an ANF footnote as *"Apud inferos"* — apparatus identifying, not supplying, the Latin, so not itself quoted as Cyprian's English). **Statement**, and doctrinally load-bearing: readmission at the point of death is not sentiment but a stated theological rule — no repentance is possible after death, so mercy must be extended before it, whatever a person's category of lapse. **Confidence: Widely Accepted.**

**Finding 3.6 — Catechumens near death receive the same pastoral urgency as the lapsed, presupposing that some death-bed provision (most plausibly emergency baptism, though not stated as such here) existed for the still-unbaptized.**
> "To the hearers also, if there are any overtaken by danger, and placed near to death, let your vigilance not be wanting; let not the mercy of the Lord be denied to those that are imploring the divine favour."
— Epistle XII §2, with the ANF footnote confirming "hearers" translates *audientibus, scil. catechumens* (apparatus, correctly used here only to identify the referent, not quoted as Cyprian's own word choice). **Presupposition**, not statement: Cyprian does not say what is actually done for a dying catechumen, only that something merciful must be. Read alongside Epistle II §3 (Roman clergy to Carthage, quoted in the corpus though not Cyprian's own hand): "Moreover, catechumens when seized with sickness ought not to be deceived… but help is to be afforded them" (glossed by an editorial note as concerning "the implied promise of their preparation for baptism" — again apparatus, not quoted as the letter's own wording, offered only to show the same concern recorded independently by a second, non-Cyprian voice). **Confidence: Inferential-Thin** — this is the weakest-grounded finding in this domain; the corpus never states plainly that catechumens were baptized on their deathbed, only that they were not to be abandoned, and the specific remedy is never named.

### Domain 4 — The catechumenate

**Finding 4.1 — Catechumens are a named, distinct order within the community, with their own pastoral oversight requirement.**
Epistle XII's own title states this plainly: "To the Clergy, Concerning the Lapsed and Catechumens, that They Should Not Be Left Without Superintendence" (div3 `iv.iv.xii`). **Statement**, at the level of the letter's own occasion (the clergy had been neglecting catechumens as well as the lapsed), though — as Finding 3.6 shows — the letter does not describe what catechetical instruction itself consisted of. **Confidence: Widely Accepted** for the office's existence; **Not Attested** for its content or duration.

**Finding 4.2 — *De Dominica Oratione* is catechetical in form and stated purpose, teaching the Lord's Prayer as the church's own set prayer, but does not describe a baptismal-preparation curriculum or state that this text was used with catechumens specifically.**
> "Himself also gave a form of praying—Himself advised and instructed us what we should pray for. He who made us to live, taught us also to pray"
— *On the Lord's Prayer* §2. And:
> "Let us therefore, brethren beloved, pray as God our Teacher has taught us."
— *On the Lord's Prayer* §3. **Statement** that this is instruction in *how* to pray, addressed to "brethren beloved" generally (the treatise's own opening address) — **not** addressed explicitly to catechumens, and not framed anywhere in the text read as a pre-baptismal document. Doc_02 §9's own characterization of this treatise as key to the catechumenate is therefore a reasonable inference from its didactic form and content (it expounds the creed-adjacent set prayer clause by clause, in exactly the manner catechetical instruction elsewhere in this period takes), but this read did not find Cyprian himself stating the treatise's use in catechesis, or its place before or after baptism, anywhere in the text. **Confidence: Inferential-Thin** for the specific catechumenal-use claim; **Widely Accepted** for the observation that its form is instructional/expository rather than homiletic-occasional.

**Finding 4.3 — No description of pre-baptismal instructional content (creed, moral teaching, duration) was found stated directly anywhere in the corpus read.** This is reported as absence, tested as follows: full-text search of the Cyprian range of the English corpus for `catechumen` returned eight hits (Epistle II §3; Pontius's Vita §1, §6; Epistle XII's own title and §2; Epistle LXXIV/Firmilian §21; a Testimonies heading, "That the catechumen ought now no longer to sin"; and one hit inside the 19th-century editor's own "Elucidations" apparatus, which is **not Cyprian's voice** and was excluded from any finding above on that ground alone). None of these eight instances describes *what* was taught, for how long, or in what setting; they establish only that catechumens existed as a recognized order requiring pastoral care. **Confidence: Not Attested** — this is a genuine gap in the vendored corpus, not an inference this document is declining to draw.

## 3. Proposed lexicon-term candidates

Per Doc_06 §5 item 3's own instruction, these are **proposed only** — nothing is added to any lexicon by this document.

**Candidate A — *manus impositio* (episcopi et cleri) / "imposition of hands," as the name for the reconciling rite itself.**
- Latin: *"per manus inpositionem episcopi et cleri ius communicationis accipiant"* — Hartel, CSEL 3.2, Epistula XVI, p. 521.
- English (Cyprian's own main text, ANF): "by imposition of the hand of the bishop and clergy receive the right of communion" — Epistle IX §2 (Oxford ed. Ep. xvi).
- Denotes: the specific ministerial act — the bishop's (or, in the ordinary case, the bishop's and clergy's) laying-on of hand upon a penitent — that formally completes penance and restores the "right of communion" (*ius communicationis*). Distinguishable from the certificate (*libellus*) that could accelerate the timetable (Finding 3.3) but did not substitute for this act, and from the confession stage (*exomologesis*, Candidate B) that precedes it.
- Why Doc_03's method could not have found it: Doc_03's own discovery pass (per its Notes, quoted above in this document's own preparation) swept for high-frequency crisis nouns ("the lapsed," "confessor," "reconciliation," "penitence") and explicitly declined to propose *libellus*/*libelli* as a headword because that word-family never occurs in the English corpus. It did not surface the specific ministerial-act phrase "imposition of hands," which sits inside the *definition* of reconciliation rather than being a noun naming the crisis or its subjects — a different kind of term than the ones that discovery pass was scoped to catch.
- Confidence: **Widely Accepted** (the phrase is stated plainly, in both languages, at a precisely locatable spot).

**Candidate B — *exomologesis* (Greek loanword in Latin), for the public-confession stage of penance.**
- Latin: *"et secundum disciplinae ordinem ad exomologesin ueniant"* — same locus as Candidate A, Hartel CSEL 3.2, Epistula XVI, p. 521. Independently attested elsewhere in Hartel's Cyprian corpus, e.g. *"ne in ecclesia exomologesin crimisnum facerent"* (CSEL 3.2, p. 683, Epistula LVIIII/59, c.13) — quoted exactly as the vendored file has it; the file's own text reads "crimisnum," which is plainly an OCR/print artifact for "criminum" ("of crimes/sins"), not a real word, and is given here unaltered rather than silently corrected.
- English: rendered by the ANF translator as "public confession," with a footnote supplying the underlying term, `"Exomologesis."` (apparatus, correctly distinguished above from Cyprian's own English wording).
- Denotes: the ritualized, public avowal of sin — a formal disciplinary stage, not a private admission — that must occur before the imposition of hands (Candidate A) can restore communion.
- Why Doc_03's method could not have found it: the term is rendered in English translation by an ordinary-sounding phrase ("public confession") rather than transliterated, so a frequency sweep of the English text for a distinctive headword would not surface it; it only becomes visible by cross-checking the Latin at the specific locus, which is exactly this task's own method and not Doc_03's.
- Confidence: **Widely Accepted** for the term's existence and locus; **Technical Concept** in Doc_03's own tag vocabulary, since a modern reader's sense of "confession" (private, auricular) is materially different from this stage's public, disciplinary character.

**Candidate C — *chrisma* / *unctio*, for the post-baptismal anointing.**
- Latin: *"ut accepto chrismate id est unctione esse unctus Dei et habere in se gratiam Christi possit"* — Hartel, CSEL 3.2, p. 768, Epistula LXX (Ep. 70), c. 2.
- English: "having received the chrism, that is, the anointing, he may be anointed of God, and have in him the grace of Christ" — Epistle LXIX §2 (Oxford ed. Ep. lxx).
- Denotes: the oil-anointing administered immediately after water-baptism, using oil consecrated at the altar in connection with the Eucharistic offering — a distinct sacramental element from the water-rite itself, and named as *necessary* ("it is also necessary that he should be anointed"), not optional.
- Why Doc_03's method could not have found it: Doc_03's own candidate list treats "baptism" as a single high-frequency headword (687 occurrences are reported, but that count is drawn from Augustine's *On Baptism, Against the Donatists*, not from the Cyprian corpus read here) and does not distinguish the water-rite from the distinct anointing that follows it; a frequency sweep for "baptism" alone would never isolate this second, separately-named rite-element, which occurs only once, in one letter, under its own word.
- Confidence: **Widely Accepted** for the practice's existence in this letter; **Inferential-Thin** for how widespread or uniform the practice was across the province, since this is its only occurrence in the corpus read.

**Candidate D — *interrogatio* (baptismi), for the fixed baptismal questioning itself as a named, technical rite-element.**
- Latin: *"Sed et ipsa interrogatio quae fit in baptismo testis est ueritatis"* — Hartel, CSEL 3.2, p. 767, Epistula LXX (Ep. 70), c.2; and, independently, *"usitatas et legitima uerba interrogationis"* / *"interrogatio legitima et ecclesiastica"* — Hartel, CSEL 3.2, p. 318, Epistula LXXV (Firmilian; ANF Epistle LXXIV), c.10–11.
- English: "the very interrogation which is put in baptism is a witness of the truth" (Ep. LXIX §2); "the usual and lawful words of interrogation" / "the legitimate and ecclesiastical interrogatory" (Ep. LXXIV §11).
- Denotes: the fixed, recognized set of questions put to the candidate at the water-rite itself — distinct from the "symbol of the Trinity" invoked in naming, and independently attested at two different loci by two different correspondents (Cyprian and Firmilian), which is stronger cross-attestation than any other single rite-term found in this read.
- Why Doc_03's method could not have found it: this is a term describing the *form* of a moment within baptism, not a noun for a status, a crisis, or a party to a dispute — outside the category of vocabulary Doc_03's own discovery pass was scoped to (person/status nouns and named formulas of institutional authority). It is also cross-attested (Finding 1.8) precisely through a presuppositional argument (the false-prophetess episode), the reading method this document's own brief was built around and Doc_03's frequency-sweep method was not.
- Confidence: **Widely Accepted** for the term and its cross-attestation; **Not Attested** for the interrogation's actual wording, which nowhere in the corpus read is quoted in full.

## 4. What could not be established

- **The wording of the baptismal renunciation formula.** Its existence is well presupposed (Finding 1.3); its actual words are nowhere quoted in the corpus read.
- **The wording of the baptismal interrogation in full**, beyond the single clause Cyprian quotes (Finding 1.1) and the second-hand characterization in Firmilian's letter (Finding 1.8). Whether the interrogation was Trinitarian in three questions (as later, better-attested sources describe) or some other form is **not attested** here.
- **Whether immersion (as opposed to affusion) was the normal mode for the non-gravely-ill.** Cyprian defends affusion for the sick as fully equivalent (Finding 1.5), which itself presupposes immersion was the ordinary mode being compared against, but no passage in the corpus read states plainly "the well are immersed."
- **What was taught to catechumens, and for how long** (Finding 4.3) — a genuine, tested absence, not an inferred one.
- **Whether *De Dominica Oratione* was in fact used as a catechumenal text**, as opposed to general instruction to the already-baptized (Finding 4.2) — Doc_02 §9's characterization is a reasonable reading of the treatise's form, but this read did not find Cyprian himself stating that use.
- **The kiss of peace as a named liturgical act** — searched for and not found (see §1, "yielded nothing"); Finding 1.4's infant-kissing passage is the nearest adjacent material and is explicitly not the same claim.
- **A fixed Eucharistic consecration formula** — presupposed by Firmilian's account of the false prophetess ("the sacrament of the accustomed utterance," Finding 1.8's surrounding context) but never quoted anywhere in the corpus read.
- **Precisely how widespread the chrism/anointing practice (Candidate C) was** — attested at exactly one locus in the corpus read; this document does not generalize a single letter's practice to "the world" without more corroboration than was found here.
