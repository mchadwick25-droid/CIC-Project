      From about 246 to 430, this was the ordinary church of two cities in
      Roman North Africa, Carthage and Hippo. Each congregation had one
      bishop, given a particular people and answerable for them. When a
      persecution in 250 made many of them give way, their bishop did not
      write them off. He set a road back, examined and walked in the open.
      Cyprian of Carthage held that road, and he was put to death in 258. A
      century and a half later, Augustine of Hippo argued that Cyprian had
      been wrong about baptism, but never called him an outsider. This
      church argued hard about the font, about councils and about grace, but
      always inside a bond it would not break. Both bishops speak at length
      in their own words. Almost everyone else is missing: the ordinary
      believer, the women, and the people who gave way.

      This is the ordinary church of Latin-speaking Roman North Africa, from
      about 246 to 430, and it lived in two cities. Carthage was the chief
      see of its province and the largest Latin Christian city outside Rome.
      Hippo Regius was a port further along the coast, in the church
      province of Numidia. The name used here is a modern label, because
      this church had no special name for itself. It saw itself simply as
      the church, doing the ordinary work of caring for the people in front
      of it.
    grounded_in: [lpc.core.latin-pastoral-congregational-christianity, lpc.ambient.two-cities-scale]
  - text: >-
      At its center stood one plain fact: a bishop was given a particular
      people, in one place, and he answered for them. He preached to them
      week after week, taught those preparing for baptism, washed them at
      the font and fed them at the table. When they failed, it was his own
      wound before it was a case to judge. Cyprian put it in one image: the
      shepherd is wounded most deeply when his flock is wounded. The tie ran
      both ways. The people could call a man to the office against his will,
      and they could crowd before his seat and refuse to go home.

      Cyprian was a trained public speaker who turned to the church in about
      246. Within two or three years the people of Carthage made him their
      bishop by acclaim. He was still newly baptized, and five presbyters,
      the clergy just below a bishop, opposed him. He later named what had
      put him there: the people's voice, joined to God's judgment. A century
      and a half later, Augustine's path ran the same way. In 391 the
      congregation at Hippo seized him and had him made a presbyter while he
      wept.

  - text: >-
      In 250 came the first persecution enforced across the whole empire by
      plan. It did not mainly demand that Christians give up their faith,
      but a recorded act of sacrifice to the gods, proved by a certificate.
      Some Christians sacrificed, and others paid to have the sacrifice
      recorded without making it. Both groups were called the lapsed. They
      were the church's own people, and now they asked to come back. To shut
      them out for good would say that one hour under pressure decides a
      life. To let them straight back would say the hour meant nothing.

      So the church kept a road back. A failed member's name was set down,
      and what had truly changed in the person was watched and weighed. At
      the end, the same people who saw the fall received the person home, in
      the open. A rival claim pressed against this road. Confessors were
      Christians who had been questioned, had not denied Christ, and had
      lived. Some of them wrote certificates of peace for the lapsed, and
      Cyprian complained that thousands went out every day. He held that the
      church's peace was not any one sufferer's to give, and that tension
      was never fully settled.
