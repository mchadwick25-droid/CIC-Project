## Story Text

*This is the death of Cyprian as two early texts tell it. One is by his deacon, Pontius, who wrote it in the form of a saint's life. The other is the* Acta Proconsularia*, an early Christian compilation built around the records of his hearings. Its compiler is unnamed. Where both texts agree, the story says so plainly. Where only one of them speaks, the story says which.*

**The night.** Cyprian was held for one night in an officer's house. Pontius says that he and the other friends "were as usual in his company." The people stayed outside. Pontius writes that they, "in anxiety that nothing should be done throughout the night without their knowledge, kept watch before the officer's door." The *Acta* says the same in fewer words: the whole people of the brothers came together there, and all of them stayed in the street before the door.

**The morning.** A great crowd went with him. Pontius says he went out "walled in on all sides by the ranks of a mingled multitude." The *Acta* says a large crowd came together at the place called Sexti in the morning, and that after the sentence a large crowd followed him out.

**The sentence.** The proconsul read it from a tablet. Pontius reports that it called Cyprian "a standard-bearer of the sect, and an enemy of the gods, and one who was to be an example to his people; and that with his blood discipline would begin to be established." The *Acta* gives the sentence in nearly the same words: he was the author and standard-bearer of the worst crimes, he would be an example to those he had joined to himself, and discipline would be made firm by his blood. On the sentence the two texts are not independent witnesses. Only the *Acta* gives Cyprian's answer: *Deo gratias*, "Thanks be to God."

**The cry.** Then the crowd called out. The *Acta* gives their words: *Et nos cum ipso decollemur*, "Let us too be beheaded with him." Pontius does not quote the cry. He says that the people's "general wish" was "that the entire congregation should suffer at once in the fellowship of a like glory," and he calls them blessed because they, "in outspoken words, suffered with such a bishop as theirs."

**The field.** Here only the *Acta* speaks. He was led out to the field of Sextus. He took off his heavy cloak, knelt on the ground and prayed. He gave his outer robe to his deacons and stood in his linen tunic. He waited for the executioner, and when the man came he told his people to give him twenty-five gold pieces. The brothers laid linen cloths and hand-cloths before him.

**The eyes.** Both texts say he covered his own eyes. They differ on the cloths. Pontius says that, "having with his own hands bound his eyes," he tried to hurry the executioner. The *Acta* says Cyprian covered his eyes with his own hand, and that when he could not tie the hand-cloths himself, the presbyter Julianus and the subdeacon Julianus tied them for him. This story does not choose between the two. It records that the *Acta* names the two men who tied the cloths and Pontius does not.

**What only Pontius says.** Pontius adds four things that the *Acta* does not mention, and they are his. The place was "level, so that it affords a noble spectacle, with its trees thickly planted on all sides." People who could not see over the crowd "had climbed up into the branches of the trees," and Pontius says this was "that there might not even be wanting to him (what happened in the case of Zacchæus), that he was gazed upon from the trees." The Zacchaeus comparison is Pontius's own remark. He then writes of the executioner, who "with difficulty clasped the blade in his failing right hand with trembling fingers, until the mature hour of glorification strengthened the hand of the centurion with power granted from above." The Latin of that sentence can be read two ways, and one scholar takes Cyprian, and not the executioner, as the man whose fingers hardly trembled as he guided the sword. The *Acta* calls the executioner a *spiculator* each time, and it says nothing of any strengthening. That reading of the moment is Pontius's.

**The burial.** Here only the *Acta* speaks. His body was laid close by, because the pagans were curious. In the night it was lifted and carried with candles and torches to the grounds of Macrobius Candidianus on the Mappalian road, near the fishponds, "with great prayer and triumph."

## Formation Ecology Connection

**Gravities: G1 (Pastoral Office as Territorial Flock-Keeping), G8 (Confessor-Authority).** Doc_08's Force 3A-1 and the whole external column press through the bond between one bishop and one congregation. This account is that bond at its end. The detail that carries it is the crowd, which both texts attest. The people kept watch at the door all night. They followed him out. They called out to die with him. On the *Acta*'s account his own clergy tied the cloth over his eyes.

**What it shows that other evidence does not show as directly.** Cyprian's letters argue that the bishop is answerable for the flock. What no letter can show is what the community did at the end of the completed life, and that is what these two texts record. The ideal they share is plain. It is readiness in the bishop, and a people who stay.

**Why it belongs in the encounter.** Per CF V7.4, the formation ideal communicated is part of the evidence, and here the events themselves are also attested by two texts. The story gives a participant both, and shows where the texts differ.

## Tier Justification

**Tier 1, on the core that Pontius and the *Acta* both attest.** Pontius meets Tier 1's author test. He is a named deacon with an identifiable place in the community, he is within the horizon, and he was with Cyprian on the last night (§15). `lpcstory001` and `lpcstory002` assign him Tier 1 on that basis. The *Acta* is a second text. It is an early Christian compilation built around the hearing records, in one edition (Hartel's, printed also by Gebhardt and Knopf). Those three printings are one text and not three witnesses, and the compiler is unnamed, so the *Acta* corroborates a named author and does not carry Tier 1's author test alone. Confidence is Widely Accepted and not Documented for that reason. The *Acta* contains no miracle, and its form is mixed: it reads partly as a record, with consular dates, places and question-and-answer under fixed formulas, and partly as Christian narrative (*"beatum Cyprianum"*, H 41463; a closing doxology, H 41625–41626). `Source_Registry.md` row 41 calls it "the one strictly documentary (as opposed to hagiographic) record", and the provenance column of that row marks the phrase as builder prior knowledge. The vendored text supports neither half of the phrase as worded. Fixing the row belongs to Doc_02's owner.

**What each text gives, checked detail by detail against the Latin.** The *Acta* is quoted from Knopf's printing, whose Latin is cleanest, and agrees word for word with Gebhardt's and with the OCR of Hartel's own volume at each passage used.

- **Attested by both.** The night watch at the door (Pontius §15, H 41249–41250; *Acta* 2: *"illuc uniuersus populus fratrum conuenit"* K 4877–4879, *"omnes in uico ante ianuam hospitii principis manserant"* K 4881). The crowd (Pontius §16, H 41269; *Acta*: *"multa turba eum prosecuta est"* K 4915–4917). The sentence (Pontius §17, H 41315–41317; *Acta* 4: *"eris ipse documento his, quos scelere tuo tecum adgregasti: sanguine tuo sancietur disciplina"* K 4907–4909). The people's part in his death (Pontius §18, *"publicata uoce compassus est"* H 41360–41361; *Acta* 5: *"Et nos cum ipso decollemur"* K 4914–4915). Harnack (1913, line 1748) ties Pontius's phrase to this cry. Cyprian's own hand over his eyes.
- **The *Acta* alone.** *"Cyprianus episcopus dixit: Deo gratias"* (K 4910–4911). *"genu in terra flexit et in orationem se domino prostrauit"* (K 4918–4919). The twenty-five gold pieces (K 4921–4922). The burial: *"inde per noctem sublatum cum cereis et scolacibus"* (K 4950).
- **Pontius alone.** The trees (*"personae fauentes in ramos arborum repserant"*, H 41351), the Zacchaeus comparison (H 41352–41353), the hurried executioner (*"moram carnificis urgere temptabat"*, H 41353–41354), the trembling fingers (H 41354–41355), the hand strengthened *"concesso desuper uigore"* (H 41356–41357) and the rank of centurion. Harnack (line 1743) asks of the rank: *"war der Henker ein Centurio?"* and glosses the phrases as Johannine (*Joh.* 17,1; 19,11). The *Acta* calls the man *spiculator*.
- **In tension.** Pontius: *"sed iam ligatis per manus suas oculis"* (H 41353). The *Acta*: *"postea uero beatus Cyprianus manu sua oculos sibi texit. qui cum lacinias manuales ligare sibi non potuisset, Iulianus presbyter et Iulianus subdiaconus ei ligauerunt"* (K 4924–4927; G 6639–6642; H 41592–41594, where the OCR prints the name as *lulianus*). The story gives both and decides neither.

**The sentence about the trembling fingers has a disputed subject.** Pontius's Latin is *"labente dextera gladium uix trementibus digitis circuibat"* (H 41354–41355; the OCR prints *gladiuui*). ANF makes the executioner the subject. Harnack's German makes Cyprian the subject, with his fingers *"kaum daß seine Finger dabei zitterten"* (lines 5234–5238). The story therefore gives the sentence as Pontius's, in ANF's wording, and says the reading is disputed.

**The ground Pontius describes is uncertain in the manuscripts.** ANF's "level" follows the *aequalis* reading. Hartel prints his own conjecture *conuallis*, a valley (H apparatus, line 41344). The story quotes ANF and attaches it to Pontius.

**The convention-shaped details are marked as Pontius's own.** The Zacchaeus comparison is an authorial aside, and the strengthened hand is a providential intervention at the climax. Pontius's account of Cyprian's death is shaped by the genre Delehaye catalogues, and `Source_Registry.md` rows 99, 159 and 212 license that scholarship. Those details carry Contested confidence, or Inferential/Thin where the convention shapes them, and the story does not rest on them. Harnack calls the tree-climbing and the Zacchaeus allusion *"kleine unschuldige Hervorhebungen"* (lines 2574–2576), small innocent emphases, and that reading should be heard at its strength. What the story rests on is the core both texts attest.

**How the two texts relate, from the vendored scholarship only.** Harnack (1913) says Pontius cannot yet have had the *Acta* in the compiled form we read, though that compilation is also very old. He reports Monceaux's view that Pontius knew only the report of the first hearing and the wording of the second sentence. Knopf's bibliography lists studies from 1913 to 1919 that argue over the sources of Cyprian's death (Reitzenstein, Corssen, Franchi de' Cavalieri). Their contents are not vendored, and this build cannot say what they argue. Pontius points to the *Acta* himself at §11: *"what God's priest replied to the interrogation of the proconsul, there are Acts which relate."*

**The general Construction Framework question stays open.** CF V7.4's Tier 3 definition has a genus clause and a hagiographic-convention clause, and the framework does not say which governs when they conflict (Doc_09 §8 item 7). This story does not need the answer, because it no longer rests on the convention-shaped details.

## Usage Guidance

The Representative may tell this as **the death of Cyprian as two early texts give it**, with Pontius named as the author and the *Acta* named as an early Christian compilation, its compiler unnamed, that corroborates him. The Representative says which details both texts attest (the night watch, the crowd, the sentence, the people's part, his own hand over his eyes), which only the *Acta* gives (*Deo gratias*, the kneeling, the gold pieces, the burial by torchlight), and which only Pontius gives (the trees, the Zacchaeus comparison, the trembling fingers, the strengthened hand). **The Representative does not offer Pontius's providential detail as fact.** It reports it as his reading.

**Additional guidance.** If a participant asks "did that really happen?", the honest answer has three parts and the Representative should give all three. Cyprian was executed under Valerian and Gallienus, on the eighteenth day before the Kalends of October (14 September) in 258; the *Acta* gives the day and the emperors, and the year comes from Bardenhewer's literary history. Two early texts agree on the core of how it happened. And they disagree on one detail, who tied the cloths over his eyes. If a participant asks that question, the Representative says the texts differ and names both. If a participant asks for the trial itself, the Representative says the *Acta* is a Christian compilation built around the hearing records, and not a court transcript.

## Absent Story Note

**What cannot be told is what any one person in the crowd made of it.** No spectator left an account of his own, and nothing survives from the executioner or the soldiers. What can be told is what the crowd said as a body, and the *Acta* records it: *Et nos cum ipso decollemur*. Pontius says the people kept watch at the door and suffered with their bishop "in outspoken words."

**Pontius points the reader to the Acts himself, at §11.** That is outside the §§15–19 span this chunk's Source field declares, and it describes the banishment hearing of 257 and not the capital trial of 258: "what God's priest replied to the interrogation of the proconsul, there are Acts which relate." §11 opens "Banishment followed these actions." The *Acta* does open with the 257 hearing before the proconsul Paternus, which ends in exile to Curubis, and then gives the 258 hearing, so the pointer is sound.
