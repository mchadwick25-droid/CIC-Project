## Quick Meaning

For us, the flock is not a metaphor for the church in general but the particular people a particular man is answerable for — and answerable to — by name, week after week, in one place.

---

## World Meaning

A bishop here is not an officer of a wider institution who happens to be posted somewhere. He is given a people. They are in front of him on the day he preaches, they are the ones he baptizes and buries, and when they fail it is not an administrative problem he manages but a wound he carries: *it is the shepherd that is chiefly wounded in the wound of his flock.*

The charge runs in both directions, and that is the part most easily missed. He is answerable **for** them — placed on high, in the place of a shepherd, to keep watch, and if found neglectful he will hear what was said to those placed in charge before him. He is also answerable **to** them, because they are very often the ones who put him there, and they are capable of standing in front of his seat and refusing to disperse until he hears them.

Nothing else about the office makes sense without this. The penitential road back exists because a shepherd does not write off a sheep. The weekly preaching exists because these particular people, and not an audience in general, have to be fed. The questions about who may baptize and who may be received are urgent because they are questions about *these* people's standing, not abstractions. A man who holds this office and has no particular people has not got the office.

---

## Ecological Function

The flock anchors nearly everything else in this lexicon. Penitential discipline and reconciliation are what a shepherd does with a sheep that has failed. Preaching and catechesis are the office's own ordinary work. Communion is the standing of those inside the charge. The one episcopate is what many such charges make together, and the conciliar formulas are arguments about how the holders of those charges relate. A participant who understands this term understands why this world's authority is exercised in the register of care and answerability rather than jurisdiction and decree.

## Distortion Risk

**Modern Hearing:**
A modern participant is likely to hear "flock" as soft, sentimental, and slightly patronizing — a pious way of describing churchgoers, implying passivity on their part and gentle benevolence on the pastor's. They will also tend to hear "bishop" as a regional administrator several removes from ordinary congregational life.

**World Hearing:**
The word carries obligation and exposure, not sentiment. The flock is a charge that can be lost, and its failure is reckoned to the one who holds it. The people in it are not passive: they elect, they demand, they riot, and they are capable of forcing a bishop's hand or refusing to accept his word. And the bishop is not remote — he is the man who preaches to these people on Sunday and knows which of them did not come.

## Key Sources

Cyprian's own words carry the image throughout: *De Lapsis* 4 (*"it is the shepherd that is chiefly wounded in the wound of his flock"*); the watch-keeping passage (*"it devolves upon us who appear to be placed on high, in the place of a shepherd... to keep watch over the flock; if we be found neglectful, it will be said to us, as it was said to our predecessors also"*); and the relief provision for *"those who have stood with unshaken faith and have not forsaken Christ's flock."* Registry rows 1, 2, 3, 5. Augustine's Sermons carry the cognate fold image (row 19). Doc_03's sweep of Cyprian's own vendored corpus returns 113 stem occurrences of flock/shepherd/pastor, 109 counting the pastoral nouns alone — see Doc_05 §4 for the counting rule, which Doc_03 did not state.

**Author Gravity note:** the dense verbal evidence is Cyprian's; Augustine's use of the image is real but far less frequent. The term is cross-phase on both bishops' conduct, not on comparable word-counts in each.

**Editorial-apparatus note:** the vendored ANF text prints an editor's bracketed remark — *"[This exercise of jurisdiction, vice episcopi, is to be noted.]"* — inside the watch-keeping sentence. It is the 19th-century American editor's, not Cyprian's, and no part of this entry rests on it.
