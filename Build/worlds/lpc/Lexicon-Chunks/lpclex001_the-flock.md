## Quick Meaning

For us, the flock is not a metaphor for the church in general but the particular people one bishop is given, in one place. He answers for them, and they can hold him to it.

---

## World Meaning

A bishop here is not an officer of a wider institution who happens to be posted somewhere. He is given a people. They are in front of him on the day he preaches, they are the ones he baptizes, and when they fail it is not an administrative problem he manages but a wound he carries: *it is the shepherd that is chiefly wounded in the wound of his flock.*

The charge runs in both directions, and that is the part most easily missed. He is answerable **for** them — a portion of the flock is assigned to each pastor, to rule and govern, and he must give account of his doing to the Lord. He is also answerable **to** them, because they are very often the ones who put him there, and they are capable of standing in front of his seat and refusing to disperse until he hears them.

Nothing else about the office makes sense without this. The penitential road back exists because a shepherd does not write off a sheep. The weekly preaching exists because these particular people, and not an audience in general, have to be fed. The questions about who may baptize and who may be received are urgent because they are questions about *these* people's standing, not abstractions. A man who holds this office and has no particular people has not got the office.

---

## Ecological Function

The flock anchors nearly everything else in this lexicon. Penitential discipline and reconciliation are what a shepherd does with a sheep that has failed. Preaching and catechesis are the office's own ordinary work. Communion is the standing of those inside the charge. The one episcopate is what many such charges make together, and the conciliar formulas are arguments about how the holders of those charges relate. A participant who understands this term understands why this world's authority is exercised in the register of care and answerability rather than jurisdiction and decree.

## Distortion Risk

**Modern Hearing:**
A modern participant is likely to hear "flock" as soft, sentimental, and slightly patronizing — a pious way of describing churchgoers, implying passivity on their part and gentle benevolence on the pastor's. They will also tend to hear "bishop" as a regional administrator several removes from ordinary congregational life.

**World Hearing:**
The word carries obligation and exposure, not sentiment. The flock is a charge that can be lost, and its failure is reckoned to the one who holds it. The people in it are not passive: they elect, they demand, they riot, and they are capable of forcing a bishop's hand or refusing to accept his word. And the bishop is not remote — he is the man who preaches to these people on Sunday and knows which of them did not come.

## Key Sources

Cyprian's own words carry the image throughout: *De Lapsis* 4 (*"it is the shepherd that is chiefly wounded in the wound of his flock"*); Epistle LIV §14, to Cornelius (*"a portion of the flock has been assigned to each individual pastor, which he is to rule and govern, having to give account of his doing to the Lord"*); and Epistle V, the relief provision for *"those who have stood with unshaken faith and have not forsaken Christ's flock."* Registry rows 1, 2, 3, 5. Augustine's Sermons carry the cognate fold image (row 19). A whole-word count (case-insensitive, notes excluded) over the Cyprian volume of the vendored edition returns 102 occurrences of flock, shepherd and pastor, letters written to Cyprian included. Doc_03's 113 is a substring count; Doc_05 §4 gives the rule.

**Author Gravity note:** the dense verbal evidence is Cyprian's; Augustine's use of the image is real but far less frequent. The term is cross-phase on both bishops' conduct, not on comparable word-counts in each.

**Editorial-apparatus note:** the vendored ANF text prints an editor's bracketed remark — *"[This exercise of jurisdiction, vice episcopi, is to be noted.]"* — inside Epistle II, in a sentence about keeping watch over the flock. That letter is from the Roman clergy to the Carthaginian clergy, not from Cyprian, and this entry does not use it. In Epistle LIV §14 an endnote of the same edition, *"[Note this decree, 'by all of us,' and what follows.]"*, sits earlier in the sentence quoted above. It is the 19th-century American editor's, not Cyprian's, and no part of this entry rests on it.
