## Quick Meaning

For us, grace names the insistence that no one's own effort is ever sufficient — that whatever good a person manages was given to them before they managed it.

---

## World Meaning

Reported as the world's own self-understanding — not assessed for historical accuracy; confidence calibration applies to the historical-event layer only.

The claim is simple to state and hard to sit with: you did not start it. Whatever you have made of yourself, something was given first, and if you subtract the gift there is nothing left that would have moved. Not a boost to an effort already under way — the beginning of the effort.

It is argued against a real opponent who is not a fool and is not obviously wrong. The other position is decent and attractive: God commanded it, therefore it can be done; a person who tries can obey. To refuse that is to say something uncomfortable about how little of a life is self-made, and it is refused anyway, at enormous length, again and again, because the alternative makes the whole of pastoral work a matter of exhorting people to try harder.

And it lands in the congregation, not only in the argument. The person listening has failed at something and is being told that the failure is not the last word and also that the recovery will not be their own achievement. That is the shape of it: no one is beyond help and no one helps themselves.

## Ecological Function

Grace is the one gravity in this world that is formation *content* rather than formation medium — a sustained catechetical and polemical project rather than a practice. It is propagated through preaching and catechesis, and it stands in a structural resemblance to penitential discipline, addressing the compromised believer in a different register. A participant who understands this term understands the second phase's central theological labour. It is also **freestanding**: nothing else in this ecology depends on it resolving one way or the other.

## Distortion Risk

**Modern Hearing:**
A modern participant is likely to hear grace as a warm general benevolence, or as "grace under pressure," or as a denominational shibboleth in a Protestant–Catholic argument they may already have opinions about — and to hear the anti-Pelagian position as harsh determinism.

**World Hearing:**
A specific and contested claim about the priority of divine action over human capacity, hammered out against a named opponent over roughly a decade, with direct consequences for how a congregation is taught, how failure is handled, and what a pastor can reasonably ask of people.

## CT Contest Type

**Relationship to present-day traditions**, and secondarily **Meaning**.

The live contest is not principally about what Augustine wrote — the corpus is enormous and directly vendored — but about how that historical position stands to the way living traditions use the word now. The Reformation-era and post-Reformation appropriations of Augustine on grace, the Catholic–Protestant disagreements that ran through them, and the modern scholarly reassessment of Pelagius himself (whether the position Augustine attacks is the position Pelagius actually held, and how far the surviving picture is shaped by his opponents' framing) all sit on top of this term. A participant arriving with the word *grace* is very often arriving from inside one of those later arguments.

Secondarily, a **Meaning** contest: the relationship between grace, free will and predestination in Augustine's own later writing is itself read differently by serious readers, and this entry does not adjudicate it.

**What this entry therefore does not do:** it does not present the anti-Pelagian position as the settled meaning of the word across Christian history, and it does not characterize Pelagius. Doc_02 §6's routing note places the characterization of this world's opponents with Article 23 and this world's eventual Representative, not with the lexicon.
