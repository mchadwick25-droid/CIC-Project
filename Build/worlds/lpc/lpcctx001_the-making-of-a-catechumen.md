## Primary Content

Before anyone comes to the water, they come to you first as one who does not yet believe — and you mark them as such before you teach them anything at all. You sign them with the cross, on the brow. You give them salt to taste. It is not yet the gift; it is the claim laid on them, the mark that says: this one has begun to be sought.

Then the teaching begins, and it follows one order, never the other way. First you teach what to believe — the creed, spoken over them, phrase by phrase, until it is theirs to hold. Only once that is fixed do you teach them what to ask for — the Lord's own prayer, the shape of asking rightly. You have said it to people directly: the order established for your own care requires that they learn first what to believe, and only after that, what to ask. Believing comes before asking, because a person who does not yet know whom they address cannot yet ask rightly.

And near the end, you ask the creed back. You gave it to them once, whole; now they give it back to you, whole, from memory, in front of the gathered church that will receive them. This is not a formality performed because custom requires it. It is the last proof, before the water, that what was placed in them took hold — that they can say, in their own voice, what they are about to be washed into.

You do not think of this as instruction alone. It is the shaping of a mouth that will one day speak the creed back at its own children in turn. The salt on the tongue, the cross on the brow, the order that will not be reversed, the handing-over and the giving-back — this is how a stranger to the faith becomes someone your church can answer for, before the water ever touches them.
