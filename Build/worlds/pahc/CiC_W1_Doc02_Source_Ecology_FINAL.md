## 1. Primary Voices — Author Gravity Assessment

Six primary voices anchor this world's evidentiary base. Each is assessed on all five required dimensions: Visibility, Representativeness, Influence, Limitations, Transmission History.

### 1.1 The Didache (anonymous, composite; Syria, likely, though provenance is itself unresolved)

- **Visibility:** Nearly invisible for eighteen centuries — the text was effectively lost, known only through indirect echoes (Apostolic Constitutions Book VII reworks it) until 1873. Its modern visibility is therefore a 19th-century event, not a continuous transmission the way Ignatius's letters were continuously read and cited by later fathers.
- **Representativeness:** Uncertain. It reads as a church-order manual for a specific, plausibly Syrian community, not a universally-circulated document — there is no evidence any other community in this world's own network (Rome, Asia Minor) knew or used it. It should not be treated as representative of the whole network's practice, only of one (likely Syrian) community's own.
- **Influence:** Retrospectively enormous once rediscovered (it reshaped 20th-century scholarly understanding of earliest church order and liturgy), but zero influence on how any other ancient community in this world understood itself, since it was not in circulation among them.
- **Limitations:** Composite, layered document (Two Ways catechism plus church-order material); the text cannot securely date any specific instruction to a specific year, only to a broad final-form window.
- **Transmission History — the single most important calibration fact about this source.** Roughly 90% of the text depends on one manuscript: Codex Hierosolymitanus (the "Bryennios manuscript," Panaghiou Taphou 54), copied by a scribe named Leon and dated by its own colophon to 11 June 1056 CE. It sat in Constantinople (not Jerusalem, despite the name) until 1887, and was found there by Philotheos Bryennios in 1873; his editio princeps appeared in 1883. Partial, independent corroboration exists but is thinner than often implied: a Coptic (Middle Egyptian/Fayyumic, not Sahidic) parchment fragment covering only Did. 10:3b–12:2a; a fragmentary Oxyrhynchus papyrus (P.Oxy. 1782, late 4th century) covering two small discontinuous scraps; a disputed Georgian collation from a since-destroyed manuscript; and the Latin "Doctrina Apostolorum" (two manuscripts), an independent witness to the underlying "Two Ways" source rather than to the Didache itself. **This is a genuine single-point-of-failure transmission chain for the great majority of the text.** *[Confidence on the manuscript facts themselves: Documented. Confidence on the text's own representativeness and dating: Contested/Inferential-Thin.]*

### 1.2 1 Clement (traditionally Clement of Rome; anonymous in its own text)

- **Visibility:** High and continuous — cited as scripture-adjacent by several ancient authorities (included in Codex Alexandrinus alongside the New Testament) and never lost.
- **Representativeness:** Strong for Rome specifically — this is Rome writing with real, exercised corrective authority to Corinth, the best-attested single act of translocal authority in this world's early decades. Not representative of Antioch or Asia Minor, which have no equivalent Roman-style document this early.
- **Influence:** Substantial and traceable — its own account of church order (interchangeable episkopos/presbyteros for a plural governing college, chs. 42, 44) is the single strongest textual anchor for this document's Strand B finding in Doc_01.
- **Limitations:** Its date is more contested than textbook treatments suggest (see Transmission/dating note below); its rhetorical purpose (settling a specific Corinthian leadership dispute) may shape how it presents church order, possibly overstating settled hierarchy for persuasive effect.
- **Transmission History:** Codex Alexandrinus (5th century) preserves the letter but loses a leaf, breaking off at 1 Clement 57:7 through the end of ch. 63; Codex Hierosolymitanus (the same 1056 CE manuscript as the Didache) supplies the complete text, including the missing prayer material at the end. A Syriac copy (Cambridge Add. 1700, 1169/1170 CE) and two Akhmimic Coptic witnesses (not Sahidic, and not British Library, correcting a common error) also survive. An ancient Latin translation does exist (Namur, Ms. Sem. 37, 11th-century copy, first published 1894), plausibly reflecting a translation as early as the 2nd century. **Risk calibration:** thicker manuscript base than the Didache — Alexandrinus and Hierosolymitanus cross-check most of the text independently; only the chs. 57–63 lacuna in Alexandrinus depends solely on the later manuscript. *[Confidence on manuscript facts: Documented. Confidence on the traditional c. 96 CE/Domitianic date: Contested — see Doc_01 Section 10 and the expanded note in Section 10 below.]*

### 1.3 Ignatius of Antioch's letters (middle recension, 7 letters)

- **Visibility:** High, continuous, and unusually well-attested in the history of scholarship itself (a genuine centuries-long critical detective story — see Transmission below).
- **Representativeness:** This is the most contested representativeness question in the whole source set, carried forward directly from Doc_01's Strand Determination (Section 6): does Ignatius describe an already-secured monepiscopal reality in Antioch/Asia Minor, or is he the most aggressive advocate for a position not yet won even among his own addressees (Trevett, Maier, Brent)? Doc_01 already treats this as unresolved and provisional; this document does not re-litigate it, only carries the caveat forward.
- **Influence:** Enormous on later church history (earliest sustained argument for monepiscopacy) but, per the representativeness caveat above, his own influence *within his own lifetime* on the communities he wrote to is less certain than his later canonical status implies.
- **Limitations:** Authenticity/dating three-way split (see Section 10) directly limits how much historical weight any single claim drawn from these letters can bear.
- **Transmission History:** Three recensions exist — long (4th-century expansion, 6 spurious additional letters, the only version known in the West before the 1640s), middle (7 letters, now consensus-authentic), and short (3-letter Syriac abridgment). The middle recension's authentication is a genuine, documented critical-scholarship narrative: James Ussher, using two rediscovered Latin manuscripts (Caiensis, Cambridge Gonville & Caius 395, and the now-lost Montacutianus), published his edition in 1644/1645; Isaac Vossius confirmed this with the Greek Codex Mediceo-Laurentianus (11th century, Florence) in 1646; Jean Daillé attacked even the middle recension as spurious in 1666; John Pearson refuted him in 1672, settling the question for two centuries; William Cureton's 1843–45 discovery of Nitrian Syriac manuscripts reopened the debate by championing the shorter 3-letter Syriac text as most original, decisively refuted by Theodor Zahn (1873) and J.B. Lightfoot (1885/1889), whose *Apostolic Fathers, Part II* cemented the current consensus (Lightfoot himself started as a Curetonian sympathizer before reversing). **This is worth naming explicitly: the "settled" status of the middle recension is itself the product of a two-century critical dispute, not an unbroken assumption.** *[Confidence on the recension/manuscript history: Documented. Confidence on dating/provenance of the letters themselves: Contested — see Section 10.]*

### 1.4 Polycarp of Smyrna's Letter to the Philippians

- **Visibility:** Continuous but textually incomplete (see Transmission below).
- **Representativeness:** Important cross-check on Strand A (Doc_01, Section 6) — Polycarp identifies himself only with "the presbyters," not a bishop's title, in his own letter, despite Ignatius separately addressing him as Smyrna's bishop.
- **Influence:** Moderate in its own era (forwarded alongside Ignatius's letters at the Philippians' own request, per its own ch. 13); retrospectively significant as cross-generational evidence linking the Ignatian and later-2nd-century church (Irenaeus knew Polycarp personally).
- **Limitations:** The unity-vs-two-letter-splice debate (Harrison 1936 vs. Hartog/Holmes's unity argument, with Berding's middle position) affects how confidently any single passage can be dated within Polycarp's own lifetime.
- **Transmission History:** All Greek manuscripts (fifteen -- six in family alpha, nine in family beta, counted directly from the University of Helsinki's HULCE critical edition manuscripts list, Myllykoski, 2022-24. This is a full physical-witness count including several manuscripts that are direct copies of other witnesses already on the list (the HULCE stemma itself shows o<v, f<o, p<f, e<f); Paul Hartog's 2013 Oxford critical edition, using a stemmatically-filtered count of independently significant witnesses rather than all physical copies, reports "eight" Greek manuscripts -- both figures are correct under their own respective counting conventions, and this document uses the fuller HULCE count while flagging the convention difference so a reader cross-checking Hartog's edition does not encounter an unexplained apparent contradiction) derive from one and the same Renaissance-era archetype and break off at 9.2, mid-sentence, fused directly to Barnabas 5.7 due to a lost quire. Chapters roughly 10–14 survive only via a Latin translation (oldest copy 9th century). Eusebius quotes chapters 9 and 13 in Greek (*Historia Ecclesiastica* III.36.13–15) — since ch. 9 falls within the Greek-manuscript-attested zone, this cross-checks manuscript fidelity there, but ch. 13 falls inside the lacuna, making Eusebius the sole check on the Latin translator's accuracy for that section. **Risk calibration:** roughly a third of the letter (chs. 10–14) has no independent Greek check beyond a single ancient translation and one patristic quotation. *[Confidence on manuscript facts: Documented. Confidence on unity/dating: Contested.]*

### 1.5 The Shepherd of Hermas (Hermas, Rome)

- **Visibility:** High in antiquity — cited as scripture by Irenaeus, Clement of Alexandria, and Origen; included in Codex Sinaiticus; only later excluded by Athanasius. Its canonical status was genuinely fluid, not settled from the start.
- **Representativeness:** Strong for Rome specifically, reinforcing 1 Clement's plural-presbyter picture (*Vision* 2.4.3 explicitly addresses plural "presbyters who preside over the Church").
- **Influence:** Substantial in its own era given its near-canonical status for over a century; a valuable window into ordinary Roman penitential and ethical concerns rather than doctrinal argument.
- **Limitations:** Composite, multi-stage composition likely spanning decades; no single date can be assigned to the whole work.
- **Transmission History:** No Greek manuscript preserves the whole text — this is an explicitly eclectic, patchwork reconstruction, arguably the thinnest base of the six primary voices alongside the Didache. Codex Sinaiticus (4th century) preserves only Vision 1.1 through Mandate 4.3.6; Codex Athous (Mount Athos, 14th–15th century per Holmes, though Kirsopp Lake's earlier 8th–9th century proposal is now a superseded minority view) is the fullest Greek copy, running to Similitude 9.30.3 before also breaking off — losing the end of Similitude 9 and all of Similitude 10. Its own transmission has a documented complication: the forger Constantine Simonides stole three leaves from Athos and made a transcript in 1855, later sold to and published by Leipzig scholars in 1856 — a separate episode from Simonides's later, rejected claim to have written Codex Sinaiticus itself. A 3rd-century Michigan papyrus (P.Mich. 129) covers Similitudes 2–9; Bodmer Papyrus 38 (Greek) covers Visions 1–3. Two distinct ancient Latin versions exist (the complete "Vulgata" and the partial "Palatina"); Sahidic and Akhmimic Coptic fragments survive; the Ethiopic version's completeness is itself disputed among specialists. **The ending of Similitudes 9–10 rests on Latin alone, with no Greek check at all.** *[Confidence on manuscript facts: Documented, with the Simonides episode itself a documented forgery-adjacent complication worth flagging for any reviewer checking Athos-derived readings. Confidence on composition dating: Contested.]*

### 1.6 Justin Martyr

- **Visibility:** High, continuous, well-attested — the best-anchored dating in this entire source set (First Apology c. 153–157 CE, per Minns and Parvis's Oxford edition, 2009; Dialogue with Trypho before his death c. 165 CE).
- **Representativeness:** His First Apology 65–67 is the fullest early description of Sunday worship (readings, homily, common prayer, kiss of peace, eucharist, distribution to the absent by deacons, collection for the needy) — but current scholarship (Paul Bradshaw's diversity thesis) treats this as one Roman data point, not a network-wide template; more traditional surveys (Ferguson) use it as if it were a common-core description. This document follows Bradshaw's caution: Justin's own description should not be silently generalized to Antioch or Asia Minor.
- **Influence:** Substantial — the earliest sustained Christian philosophical apologetic, and (per Doc_01 Section 2.2) itself one marker of this world's own closing transition into a new theological-institutional mode.
- **Limitations:** Writing for an outside (imperial) audience, in a genre (apologetic) designed to present Christian practice as maximally respectable to Roman sensibilities — his description of worship may be shaped by that persuasive purpose, not a neutral ethnographic account.
- **Transmission History:** Textually thinner than his high visibility might suggest — the corpus rests essentially on a single 1364 CE manuscript (Parisinus graecus 450) for most of the First and Second Apologies, partially cross-checked against a 4th-century papyrus scrap. *[Confidence on dating: Widely Accepted, the strongest anchor in this set. Confidence on representativeness of the liturgical description: Contested.]*

### 1.7 Excluded canonical material: the Pastoral Epistles (1–2 Timothy, Titus) — added at Step 9 Validation & Testing (2026-07-08)

Not treated as Native evidence for this world. This is stated here explicitly, for reasons specific to this world's own evidentiary method, rather than left as an unstated project-level default excluding canonical New Testament material generally — the gap the Part VI Differentiation Testing pass surfaced (`pahc_Validation_Testing.md`, Finding 8).

**Two independent grounds, either sufficient on its own.**

*Authorial-voice category mismatch.* Every one of this world's six primary voices writes in a real, contemporary, self-identified voice: Ignatius as a condemned prisoner writing under his own name and known biography; Polycarp identifying himself in his own letter; 1 Clement anonymous but institutionally self-locating (Rome writing to Corinth in its own moment); the Didache's compiler(s) anonymous but not claiming to be someone else; Hermas narrating his own visions in first person; Justin writing an apologetic addressed, in his own name, to a named emperor. The Pastoral Epistles are different in kind: they are addressed as if written by Paul, an apostolic-generation figure whose own death (c. 64–68 CE, per Doc_01 §2.1's own periodization) falls before this world's own 70 CE floor. If genuinely Pauline, they document the prior, apostolic-generation world, not this one, and fall outside this world's own temporal scope by definition. If, as the critical majority holds, they are later pseudonymous compositions written in a departed apostle's name specifically to carry his authority forward, they belong to a documented literary strategy — pseudonymous apostolic voice — that none of this world's own six primary voices employs. This world's authority crisis (Doc_01 §7; G01) is defined by figures actively, visibly arguing in their own name for their own claimed authority (Ignatius, 1 Clement's own author); a text that resolves the same crisis by borrowing a departed apostle's own voice is answering this world's central question by a different, and evidentially separate, method than this world's own attested voices use.

*Canon-formation trajectory versus occasional correspondence.* This world's own six primary voices are, without exception, occasional texts — addressed to a specific community's specific, dated situation (1 Clement to Corinth's leadership dispute; Ignatius's seven letters to specific churches encountered on a specific journey; Polycarp to Philippi at a specific request) — never written with an eye to eventual inclusion in a closed, authoritative canon, because (per Doc_01 §1's own Distinct World Criteria) this world's own defining feature is that scripture was read alongside living apostolic testimony, not yet a closed canon. The Pastoral Epistles, whatever their composition date, are transmitted and read as Pauline scripture from very early in their own reception history — their canonical status, unlike every one of this world's own primary voices' initially uncertain and contested reception (Sections 1.1–1.5 above), was never seriously in question once in circulation. They belong to the closed-canon world this world's own defining criterion says had not yet arrived, not to this world's own still-open, still-argued evidentiary situation.

**What this does not claim.** This is not a claim that the Pastoral Epistles are late, inauthentic, or historically worthless — their own dating question (commonly placed anywhere from genuine Pauline authorship in the 60s CE to pseudonymous composition as late as c. 100–140 CE) is itself contested and not resolved by this document. It is a narrower, world-specific claim: whichever dating is correct, the Pastorals' own genre — apostolic-voice pseudepigrapha oriented toward eventual scriptural status, or genuine apostolic-generation correspondence predating this world's own floor — places them outside this world's own six-primary-voice evidentiary base by this world's own stated criteria, not by an unexamined project-level default. Household-management-as-office-qualification content from 1 Timothy 3 continues to appear in Doc_01 §4 only as secondary-scholarship background illustrating a broader Greco-Roman household-code convention (per Balch, Osiek/MacDonald), never as Native primary evidence for this world's own practice. *[Confidence: the authorial-voice and canon-trajectory distinctions themselves are Documented — a direct reading of how these texts present themselves and were received; the specific composition-dating question for the Pastorals is Contested and not adjudicated here.]*

### 1.8 Aggregate Author Gravity accounting: Ignatius — added at Step 9 Validation & Testing (2026-07-08)

Section 1.3 above already flags Ignatius's own representativeness as the single most contested question in this world's whole source set, and later documents disclose Ignatius-dependency piecemeal — once per gravity (Doc_04, G01/G04/G05), once per lexicon term (Doc_06's episkopos, presbyterion, the tuned-strings image), once per story (Doc_09's Stories 001, 011, partially 012). No document sums this dependency in one place. This section closes that gap — the Author Dominance finding surfaced at Part VI Validation & Testing (`pahc_Validation_Testing.md`, Section 9).

**Summed across the build:** of the seven candidate gravities Doc_04 tested, three depend substantially or entirely on Ignatius as their only Asia Minor evidentiary voice — G01's Strand A (monarchical-office) content, G04 (Martyrdom as Formation-Shaping Meaning-Response) in its entirety, and G05 (Boundary-Drawing Against Contemporary Rivals) in its entirety (Doc_04's own Open Item 1: "G01's Strand A-specific content, G04, and G05 — all three rest on Ignatius as their only Asia Minor evidentiary voice"). Of Doc_09's thirteen catalogued stories, two rest on Ignatius alone (Stories 001, 011) and a third (012) partially. Nearly all of the emotionally vivid, individually-quotable content surfacing across Doc_05, Doc_07, Doc_09, and the World Profile — martyrdom-readiness ("food for wild beasts"), the "one eucharist" instruction, the tuned-strings image of office, the sustained anti-docetic argument — is Ignatian; Strand B's own comparably vivid content is comparatively thin (Doc_07 §2A names this directly).

**A compounding risk not previously connected to this accounting:** a serious minority scholarly position (Hübner/Lechner, Section 1.3 above; Doc_01 §10; Doc_08 §7) holds the Ignatian corpus may be 160–180 CE Roman pseudepigraphy rather than an authentic, contemporaneous Antiochene witness at all. If correct, this would not merely weaken Ignatius's representativeness of Antioch/Asia Minor c. 107–115 (already disclosed everywhere Ignatius is cited) — it would mean a meaningful share of this world's own felt texture rests on a source whose very status as evidence for this world's period and regions is itself live-contested, not only its representativeness within that period assuming its traditional dating is correct. This section names, for the first time in one place, how much of this world's own vivid character that single Contested dependency actually carries.

**What this does not mean.** This is not a claim that Ignatius should be excluded, downweighted formally, or treated as less reliable than this world's construction has treated him throughout — Doc_04's own classification discipline already prevented Ignatius-dependent content from reaching Primary status anywhere it mattered (G01 and G03 were both downgraded from initial Primary candidacy specifically to guard against this). It is a transparency finding: a downstream reader or reviewer should be able to see, in one place, how concentrated this world's most vivid and quotable material actually is, rather than discovering it only by independently summing scattered per-document disclosures. *[Confidence: the per-gravity, per-story, and per-term dependency facts summed above are each independently Documented in their own source document; the aggregate characterization ("nearly all... vivid content is Ignatian") is this document's own synthesis of already-disclosed facts, not a new independent finding.]*

## 2. Secondary Voices — Secondary Scholarship Assessment

Organized by sub-topic, with each scholar's actual center of expertise verified rather than assumed from name-recognition alone, per the `cic-source-registry` skill's explicit discipline.

**General/synthetic treatments:** Raymond E. Brown, *The Churches the Apostles Left Behind* (1984) — Brown was primarily a Johannine specialist; this book extends his Johannine-community method outward, touching Ignatius/Clement territory at the margins rather than as his own core focus. Bart D. Ehrman's Loeb *Apostolic Fathers* (2003) is a direct, core-relevant critical edition; his *Lost Christianities* (2003) is adjacent but oriented toward later 2nd–4th century heterodoxy. Michael W. Holmes's *Apostolic Fathers* (3rd ed. 2007) is the standard one-volume critical text/translation and a genuine, narrow specialization. Larry Hurtado's *Lord Jesus Christ* (2003) and *At the Origins of Christian Worship* (1999) explicitly cover this window (c. 30–170 CE) and remain standard, actively-debated references.

**Roman Christianity:** Peter Lampe, *From Paul to Valentinus* (Eng. trans. 2003) — unchallenged as the reference monograph on Roman house-church topography; no rival synthesis has emerged.

**Ignatius/Antioch/Asia Minor:** Allen Brent (genuine specialist, Second Sophistic reading), Christine Trevett (genuine Ignatius/Montanism specialist), Harry Maier (genuine, patronage/household sociology reading), Paul Foster (genuine, sustained Apostolic Fathers/Ignatius strand). **Flag:** Timothy D. Barnes is not primarily an Ignatius scholar — his career and reputation rest overwhelmingly on 4th-century material (*Constantine and Eusebius*, *Athanasius and Constantius*); his Ignatius contribution is a single 2008 article. Cite him as a supporting voice for the redating position, not as an Ignatius specialist in his own right. Reinhard Hübner and Thomas Lechner are confirmed as the core "Munich school" pseudepigraphy/redating camp.

**Household/social structure:** Wayne Meeks's *The First Urban Christians* is foundational methodologically, but his own direct textual coverage is strictly Pauline/1st-century — later scholars explicitly extended his method into the 2nd century, rather than Meeks himself having done so. Roger Gehring is a genuine oikos-structure specialist. Carolyn Osiek is the strongest match in this group — her Hermeneia commentary on the Shepherd of Hermas (1999) is the only full-length English commentary on that text. David Balch is a genuine household-codes specialist. Margaret MacDonald's expertise spans Pauline through deutero-Pauline material, less centered specifically on this world's own core texts.

**Didache scholarship:** Kurt Niederwimmer's Hermeneia commentary (1998) remains a major standard reference (a fixed historical contribution, not live ongoing scholarship, since Niederwimmer died in 2015). **Flag:** Aaron Milavec's 2003/2004 commentaries remain valid as scholarship, but his current professional identity has shifted to gender studies — citing him as an active Didache scholar today would be outdated. Clayton Jefford is the most consistently active current Didache/Apostolic Fathers specialist.

**Misattribution risks flagged for this world, per the skill's explicit requirement to verify rather than assume:**
- **G.W.H. Lampe (Cambridge, d. 1980) vs. Peter Lampe** — highest-priority flag. G.W.H. Lampe compiled *A Patristic Greek Lexicon*, a philological reference spanning the 2nd–8th centuries — a different person, era, and genre from Peter Lampe's historical-sociological monograph on Roman house churches. The near-identical surname creates a real citation-confusion risk that should be checked every time "Lampe" is cited in this world's future documents.
- **Andrew Louth** — specializes in Byzantine/later patristic mystical theology (5th–8th century); genuinely different period, easily mis-dropped into a 2nd-century bibliography.
- **Frances Young** — *From Nicaea to Chalcedon* centers on 4th–5th century material despite "early church" branding.
- **J.N.D. Kelly** — *Early Christian Doctrines* nominally spans 1st–5th century, but its center of gravity is Nicene/post-Nicene doctrine; usable here only with pinpoint early-chapter citations, never as a general authority.
- **Henry Chadwick** — moderate risk; his Justin/Clement/Origen work is genuinely relevant, but his flagship monographs (Augustine, Priscillian, Boethius) are 4th–6th century.

## 3. Institutional Evidence

What exists: 1 Clement's extended Levitical-priestly analogy for church order (chs. 40–44); Ignatius's explicit threefold-office program (bishop, presbyters, deacons) urged onto multiple Asia Minor churches; the Didache's own instructions for transitioning from itinerant apostles/prophets/teachers toward locally-appointed bishops and deacons (Did. 15:1) — read by scholars as evidence of an office structure still actively forming, not yet standardized. *[Documented that these texts contain this content; Widely Accepted that Did. 15:1 marks a transitional moment away from itinerant ministry, per the scholarly consensus Doc_02's own secondary-voice assessment (Section 2) draws on.]*

What does not exist: no institutional records, membership rolls, council minutes, or administrative documents of any kind survive from this world in this window. Everything institutional we know is reconstructed from occasional, persuasive, or polemical correspondence — never from the institution's own routine self-documentation. This is a structural limitation on everything claimed in Doc_01's Strand Determination and should be read as such. *[Documented that no institutional self-documentation survives; Inferential-Thin as to why — the legal-risk explanation offered in Section 9 below is this document's own inference, not a directly-attested cause.]*

## 4. Liturgical Evidence

Justin Martyr's *First Apology* 65–67 (c. 153–157 CE, Rome) is the fullest early description: Sunday assembly, readings from "the memoirs of the apostles or the writings of the prophets," a president's homily, common prayer, the kiss of peace, bread/wine/water offered with thanksgiving, distribution including to the absent via deacons, and a voluntary collection for orphans, widows, the sick, prisoners, and travelers. *[Widely Accepted as an accurate description of Roman practice; Contested as a network-wide template — see Bradshaw's diversity thesis above.]*

The Didache (chs. 7–10, 14) gives an independent, textually earlier-feeling liturgical layer — read here with the Section 1.1 representativeness caveat kept in view throughout, not only as a trailing bracket, since the hedge should not be separable from the claim it qualifies: baptism in running water preferred, pouring as an explicit fallback; a cup-before-bread eucharistic order with no institution narrative at all (strikingly different from later, Pauline-institution-narrative-based liturgies); Wednesday/Friday fasting explicitly distinguished from "the hypocrites'" (Pharisaic) Monday/Thursday practice; and ch. 14's instruction to confess transgressions before breaking bread on the Lord's Day "that your sacrifice may be pure." Andrew McGowan reads this as a genuinely primitive liturgical stratum predating institution-narrative eucharists, a reading this document notes but does not adopt as settled; Bradshaw counsels more caution given the Didache's own uncertain, possibly localized (Syrian) provenance and Contested/Inferential-Thin representativeness (Section 1.1 above), and this document follows Bradshaw's caution here as it does throughout. *[Contested, both on dating and on how far this can be generalized beyond its likely single community of origin — McGowan's reading should not be taken as carrying more confidence than Section 1.1's own representativeness tag licenses.]*

1 Clement 40–44 argues from Levitical/priestly analogy for proper order and "acceptable" sacrifice-language around worship, but Allen Brent and Paul Bradshaw both caution this is a rhetorical, analogical argument deployed to settle a governance dispute (the deposed Corinthian presbyters), not a neutral liturgical description in its own right. *[Contested as direct liturgical evidence; Widely Accepted as evidence of how Rome argued for legitimate order.]*

Pliny's Letter 10.96 (c. 111–113 CE, Bithynia-Pontus) supplies the only outside, non-Christian liturgical description in this world: a pre-dawn meeting, a hymn "to Christ as to a god" sung antiphonally, a moral oath, and a later reassembly for an ordinary shared meal. Whether this "meal" is the eucharist proper, an agape/love-feast, or a strategic split of the two to evade Trajan's ban on political dining-clubs (*hetaeriae*) remains a live, unresolved scholarly question. The information itself came from two enslaved women, called *ministrae*, under torture — a method ancient jurists themselves distrusted, and a serious limitation on how much weight this single source can bear. *[Documented that Pliny wrote this; Contested on what the "meal" specifically was; the torture-derived nature of the testimony is a standing limitation, not merely a footnote.]*

Ignatius's own letters supply eucharistic language: *Philadelphians* 4's "one eucharist" instruction ("Take heed, then, to have but one Eucharist... one cup... one altar; as there is one bishop"), and, separately, *Smyrnaeans* 8's own requirement that a valid eucharist be administered only by or with the bishop's authorization. **Corrected this revision** (a misattribution in the prior text had reversed these two citations, crediting "one eucharist" to *Smyrnaeans* 8; this was independently caught during a downstream cold review and verified against two translation traditions — Doc_03's own lexicon-candidate work had already made this same correction independently, without this document itself having been updated to match). Per Section 1.3 above, how much either clause reflects settled practice versus an aspirational program is exactly the open question Doc_01's Strand Determination already carries forward.

## 5. Material Evidence

This section requires unusual honesty about how thin the evidentiary category actually is. Current scholarship converges — this is close to mainstream consensus among specialists in early Christian archaeology, not a fringe minimalist position — that for Rome, Antioch, and Asia Minor in this specific 70–200 CE window, the record is essentially archaeologically invisible.

No securely-dated, unambiguously Christian architecture, funerary material, or epigraphy exists before the very last years of the 2nd century, and even the "boundary" cases cluster at or just after 200 CE:

- **Architecture:** L. Michael White's periodization treats specially-renovated Christian meeting space (*domus ecclesiae*) as a third-century-and-later phenomenon; Kristina Sessa's more recent work argues every site conventionally called *domus ecclesiae* except Dura-Europos actually postdates Constantine. Dura-Europos itself (Syria) is dated c. 232–256 CE — three-plus decades after this world's own closing boundary — and must not be used as evidence for this world's own period. Claimed early Roman *tituli* (San Clemente, Santi Giovanni e Paolo, Santa Prisca) as 1st/2nd-century house-churches are now rejected or unsupported by archaeologists. No candidate site exists at all for Antioch or Ephesus/Smyrna in this window.
- **Funerary evidence:** No catacomb galleries are securely Christian before the late 2nd century at the earliest; modern scholarship has walked back earlier (De Rossi/Wilpert) claims of 1st-century Christian catacomb material. Éric Rebillard's work argues burial in this period was organized by family, not ecclesial identity, with Christian, Jewish, and pagan remains physically intermingled without distinguishing markers well into the 3rd century.
- **Epigraphy:** The best candidate, the Abercius inscription (Hierapolis, Phrygia), is dated c. 192–216 CE — right at or just after this world's own boundary — and is written in coded, non-explicit language. A 19th-century historiographic dispute existed over whether it is even Christian (Cybele/Attis-priest epitaph theories from Ficker and Dieterich); current mainstream scholarship treats those alternative readings as largely refuted and leans toward accepting the Christian identification as fairly secure, though the coded language means no unambiguous, explicit Christian inscription is securely dated before 200 CE regardless of the Abercius question specifically.
- **Papyrology/numismatics:** No documentary (non-literary) papyrus mentioning Christians survives from before 200 CE. The Alexamenos graffito (anti-Christian mockery) is itself only "c. 200 CE," borderline at best.

**Disposition:** this document treats the material-evidence category for World #1 as thin-to-absent by design of the evidence itself, not by any failure of research. Doc_02's own confidence vocabulary should mark any material-culture claim for this world as, at best, Inferential-Thin, and future documents should resist the temptation to borrow richer 3rd-century material evidence (Dura-Europos above all) backward into this world's own reconstruction.

## 6. Ordinary Participant Evidence and Outside Witnesses

No source in this category is written by an ordinary participant in their own voice. All are either elite outside observers or later elite Christian leaders describing others. Four outside/hostile witnesses supply the only non-Christian-authored evidence for this world:

- **Pliny the Younger, *Letters* 10.96–97** (c. 111–113 CE) — see Section 4 above for content; as evidence of lived practice, it is filtered through elite Roman *superstitio* prejudice and, for its most granular detail, through torture-extracted testimony from two enslaved women. *[Documented as a text; Contested/limited as a reliable window into internal Christian experience.]*
- **Tacitus, *Annals* 15.44** (Great Fire, 64 CE) — describes Nero scapegoating Christians, names "Christus" executed under Pilate, and describes brutal executions for "hatred of the human race." The passage's basic authenticity is not seriously disputed among specialists (fringe interpolation claims exist but have little scholarly uptake); a separate, genuinely live, non-fringe debate (Brent Shaw vs. Christopher Jones) concerns whether a discrete, fire-linked persecution of Christians *as a named group* actually occurred, distinct from the passage's textual authenticity. *[Documented as authentic text; Contested on the specific historical event it describes.]*
- **Suetonius** — *Life of Claudius* 25.4's "Chrestus" reference (c. 49 CE) is majority-read as a garbled "Christus" reflecting messianic disputes within Roman synagogues (Dunn, Brown/Meier, Smallwood), but this is genuinely disputed among classicists (Barbara Levick; Heikki Solin's documentation of 126 Romans actually named Chrestus, a common name among slaves/freedmen). *Life of Nero* 16.2 notes Christians punished among a list of public-order measures, with no explicit fire connection stated — the link to 64 CE is inferred from context, not stated in the text. *[Contested on the Chrestus identification; Inferential-Thin on the Nero 16.2/fire connection specifically.]*
- **Lucian of Samosata, *The Passing of Peregrinus*** (c. 165 CE) — a satire, not a neutral account, but one that independent scholarship (Jones, Bremmer) reads as preserving genuine communal practices beneath its mockery: widows and orphans waiting at the prison, officials bribed, visitors traveling from across Asia at communal expense, "sacred books" read aloud. The specific move of reading Tertullian's later *Apology* 39 (on the communal fund) as corroboration of Lucian's account is this document's own synthesis, not a citation to a specific scholar who makes that exact triangulation — both texts independently attest mutual-aid practice, but the document has not verified that Jones or Bremmer themselves draw this particular cross-text link, and this should not be read as more sourced than it is. *[High confidence on mutual-aid/prisoner-care as a real underlying practice, attested independently in both texts; the specific characterization of "Peregrinus" himself is inseparable from Lucian's explicit satirical intent; the Lucian-Tertullian connection specifically is this document's own synthesis.]*

## 7. Source Asymmetry and Missing Voices

**Women.** Named exceptions (Phoebe, Prisca, Junia, Lydia, and this world's own Pliny-attested *ministrae*) are real but should not stand in for the invisible ordinary majority — Osiek, MacDonald, and Tulloch's own work explicitly flags this gap. Pre-4th-century female ministry was not yet a sex-distinguished formal office: no 1st–2nd-century Asia Minor inscription names a woman deacon (the epigraphic record for that specific title only begins in the 4th–6th centuries), and the "deaconess" references sometimes attributed to Ignatius at Antioch actually come from the spurious Pseudo-Ignatian long recension, not his authentic letters — a distinction this document flags explicitly so it is not repeated as an error in later documents. Ordinary women's participation is, per Clark, Kraemer, and Brooten, structurally unrecoverable except through inference, archaeology, or hostile outsider polemic (i.e., Pliny).

**Enslaved people.** Household codes (Colossians 3, Ephesians 6, 1 Peter 2) address enslaved members directly, proving their real presence in this world's congregations, but are authored by free leaders instructing enslaved people toward obedience, never contesting owners' control over their bodies (Jennifer Glancy's framing, itself contested on method by J. Albert Harrill, who argues for a more literary and less experiential reading — a genuine, unresolved scholarly disagreement worth naming rather than picking a side on prematurely). Kyle Harper's demographic estimates (roughly 10–20% of the imperial population enslaved) imply substantial enslaved membership in these urban congregations, with no surviving voice of their own.

**Non-literate, non-elite members generally.** Literacy estimates for the wider Greco-Roman world run 10–20% (Harris); applied to Christian congregations c. 100 CE, Keith Hopkins estimated only roughly 2% were fluent enough to have produced texts like these, with female literacy statistically negligible. Every surviving text in this world's source base — Ignatius, Polycarp, Justin, 1 Clement's own author, the Didache's compiler(s) — represents a narrow literate leadership stratum. No non-elite authorial voice survives from this world at all.
