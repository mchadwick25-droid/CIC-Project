## 1. Human Ecology — the person this world's evidence can actually show us

### 1.1 Formation Ecology — practices, habits, disciplines, formative relationships

What formed a person in this world was rarely presented to them as "formation" — it was the ordinary texture of belonging to an *ekklesia*. For a member of a community under a single *episkopos*, flanked by presbyters and deacons, the formative rhythm was one of gathering, obedience, and being folded into a single body under a single president. For a member of a community governed instead by a council of presbyters, the formative rhythm ran differently: no single figure's voice carried that same weight, and the discipline of the community was worked out collegially rather than through one office. A person in either kind of community also had to learn how to weigh a visiting teacher against a known standard — whether that teacher's words matched what the community itself already held — since the alternative, an itinerant figure arriving with no such standard to be tested against, was a live and recent memory, not a settled danger already dealt with.

Across communities of either kind, the eucharistic gathering was the single most formative recurring practice — the occasion a member of any of these communities returned to, again and again, to be re-formed into the body they belonged to. That the form of this gathering differed from community to community was invisible to the person living inside any one of them: for that person, their own eucharist *was* "the" eucharist.

A member of the Roman assemblies knew a further formative rhythm alongside these: a penitential one. Visions and instruction reaching that community dwelt at length on whether wrongdoing committed after baptism could still be forgiven, and a member formed inside that community carried the discipline of self-examination those visions pressed on, not only the discipline of gathering and obedience.

*Grounding note:* the *episkopos*-centered rhythm above reflects G01's Strand A content (Doc_04, Supporting — resting overwhelmingly on Ignatius's own testimony, an Author Gravity risk Doc_04 flagged and acted on via reclassification, though the underlying single-voice dependency itself remains unresolved); the presbyterial-council rhythm reflects G01's Strand B content (1 Clement, addressing a *presbyterion*). The eucharistic-formation claim draws on G07 (Primary, cross-strand: Didache 9–10, Ignatius's *Smyrnaeans* 8, Justin's *First Apology* 65–67), and Doc_04's own Cross-Check on G07 is explicit that the diversity among these three forms is real, not merely a matter of emphasis. The penitential rhythm draws on Hermas's *Shepherd*, part of this world's Strand B (Rome) evidentiary base per Doc_02.

### 1.2 Emotional and Affective Reconstruction — hopes, fears, emotional weighting

A member of this world's communities lived with a standing, unpredictable exposure rather than a daily siege: the knowledge that a name could be given, an accusation made, and a choice demanded, without warning and without any way to know in advance whether it would happen to them. This was not constant terror — most days held no such demand — but it was also not a remote or theoretical possibility; it was near enough that a member of these communities organized some real part of their emotional life around it.

For those formed most directly by a leader awaiting his own arrest, this same exposure could be turned toward something closer to eager anticipation than dread — a longing to be consumed rather than spared, framed as the truest possible imitation of the one this community followed. This was a real, felt pattern for the specific people who held it and for the communities most shaped by their example — it was not necessarily the pattern of every ordinary member facing the same exposure without that same leader's example immediately before them.

For a member of the Roman assemblies, the sharper fear ran elsewhere: not toward the possibility of arrest, but toward the possibility that one's own worst failure, committed after baptism, might already have used up whatever mercy remained. That fear, and the corresponding hope that mercy might still be extended once more, occupied a Roman member's emotional life in a way a member formed under Ignatius's own urgent warnings would not have recognized.

Hope, in every one of these communities, ran through the same channel: a letter arriving from another *ekklesia* was never merely information. It was proof that the *ekklesia* a member belonged to was larger than the room they sat in — and that proof was itself formative, producing a felt sense of belonging to something larger than the local assembly.

*Grounding note (Forces integration, per Framework Step 5 — this lens cannot be written without it):* the exposure described above draws on G03 (State Pressure/Legal Precarity, Supporting, cross-strand), rendered here at Doc_04's own Persistence-test calibration — intermittent and unpredictable, not continuous. Pliny's letter (Registry P07) is the evidentiary anchor for the mechanism itself. The eager-anticipation pattern draws on G04 (Martyrdom as Formation-Shaping Meaning-Response, Supporting, Strand A only — Ignatius's own language in *Romans* 4, and the community producing the Martyrdom of Polycarp); per Doc_04's own Cross-Check, this document does not extend that intensity to "the ordinary, non-elite member" without evidence, since Doc_02's source-asymmetry constraint (elite, literate, mostly male voices dominating the surviving record) applies with particular force to affective reconstruction. The Roman penitential fear draws on Hermas. Hope-via-correspondence draws on G02 (Primary), whose own Formation-test result is quoted directly in the final sentence above.

## 2. Community Ecology — how these scattered assemblies held together

### 2.1 Meaning Transmission — teaching, memory, storytelling, apprenticeship

Meaning in this world moved overwhelmingly by letter, and a member of one *ekklesia* rarely encountered the wider movement's teaching directly — they encountered it as a specific writer had already shaped it, in response to a specific, local argument. A letter teaching obedience to established elders arrived because elders somewhere had actually been deposed; a letter urging unity under a single bishop arrived from a man on his way to his own execution, anxious about a rival teaching he could feel circulating nearby. A member of one community rarely heard teaching offered on its own, apart from whatever trouble had made it necessary to say.

That teaching traveled this way, rather than through settled institutions of record, was not simply this world's preference: a durable, seizable written record was also a durable, seizable piece of evidence, in an environment where a name and an accusation were sometimes all it took. A member of this world's communities transmitted meaning the way people transmit meaning when writing things down carries its own risk, not only when the technology for doing so is simply absent.

Apprenticeship into the faith was not yet handled by anything like a formal catechetical office. What is closest to a fixed instructional text for those preparing for baptism was a short, older teaching about two ways — a way of life and a way of death — taught before the water itself.

*Grounding note:* this section draws on G02 (Primary; 1 Clement's Corinthian intervention and Ignatius's letters as the load-bearing examples), G01's Strand-specific content, and G05 (Ignatius's docetic anxiety). The institutional-silence-as-forces-effect claim draws directly on Doc_02 Section 9's "what silences reveal" finding and Doc_04's own G03 Dependency-test line, both of which read the absence of institutional record-keeping as plausibly explained by legal exposure rather than by simple non-survival — genuine forces integration into this dimension, addressing round-1 review's finding that this sub-dimension had none. The apprenticeship material draws on the Didache's own Two Ways material (chs. 1–6), taught before baptism per Didache 7; per Doc_03's citation discipline, the Two Ways material is Didache content, distinct from the separate fact of how the manuscript containing it survived to reach modern readers.

### 2.2 Memory Structures

Two different ways of remembering ran through this world. One kind of memory ran through named, individual figures and their own preserved words — a letter-writer's voice, carried forward and read aloud after his death, and a martyrdom account that already, within this world's own lifetime, began marking the day of a leader's death as a day set apart. The other kind of memory ran through corporate continuity rather than any one person's voice — an argument that ministry itself had passed down in an unbroken, if loosely conceived, line, and a body of visionary instruction that generated its own authority through revelation rather than through any named chain of individual witnesses.

*Grounding note:* the first pattern draws on Ignatius's own corpus and the Martyrdom of Polycarp (G04, Strand A only); the second draws on 1 Clement and Hermas (Strand B). **Carried forward from Doc_04, not resolved here:** whether a third Asia Minor profile exists beyond Ignatius's own direct addressees remains genuinely open; this section's memory-structure account is built without it, and should be revisited rather than assumed settled if later work develops that material.

## 3. Boundary Ecology

*(Elevated to a standalone lens this revision, per Section 0's reconciliation — the Framework's own "additional ecology dimensions specific to the world's character" allowance, applied because boundary-drawing is one of Doc_01's own Distinct World Criteria for this world, and because the Forces Framework names Boundary Ecology as a peer lens to Human and Community Ecology rather than a dimension subordinate to either.)*

### 3.1 Boundary Structures

Boundary-drawing in this world was live, anxious, and unfinished, not a settled defense already won. For a member of a community shaped most directly by anti-docetic concern, a rival teaching that denied the reality of the body's suffering felt near and threatening enough to argue against at length, letter after letter, city after city. Refusing a separate eucharist from a rival teacher and holding fast to the one eucharist under the community's own bishop were, for that member, the same act — belonging and boundary-marking were not experienced as two different decisions.

A second boundary ran alongside the doctrinal one, harder and more public: the boundary the state itself drew around a public identity that had, in living memory, become a chargeable offense on its own. A member of this world's communities, in either strand, learned where the edges of a safe public identity lay — a lesson pressed home by a governor's own letter describing exactly what evidence he required and exactly what a simple denial would spare a person.

The two boundaries were not felt with equal intensity everywhere. The doctrinal anxiety belonged most sharply to the communities closest to the rival teaching itself; the state's harder boundary pressed on communities in both regions, though intermittently and unpredictably rather than as a constant siege.

*Grounding note (Forces integration, per Framework Step 5 and Step 7):* the doctrinal boundary draws on G05 (Boundary-Drawing, Tensional, Strand A only — Ignatius's anti-docetic argument, evidenced by exactly one voice; Doc_01's own disclosure that Marcionite, Valentinian/Gnostic, and Montanist movements were contemporary, geographically overlapping rivals). This document does not extend that anxiety's intensity to Rome, where Doc_02/Doc_04 find no comparable content. The state boundary draws on G03 (Supporting, cross-strand — Pliny's letter, Registry P07, as the anchor; Suetonius's "Chrestus" identification remains Contested per Doc_02 and is not resolved here).

## 4. Worship Ecology

### 4.1 Worship Integration Analysis

Worship did the heaviest lifting of any single practice in this world, without being the only thing that mattered. The form that gathering took differed from community to community, though no member living inside any one of them could have known that — each held their own gathering as simply *the* eucharist, not one of several variants.

A community gathered and gave thanks — for the life and knowledge made known to them, in words spoken over the cup and the broken loaf before the meal itself began. For a member of a community under a single bishop, attending that bishop's own eucharist and refusing any other was inseparably an act of worship and an act of belonging. For a member of the Roman assemblies, worship had already begun generating its own explanation of itself — an account of why the bread and cup, once this thanksgiving was spoken over them, were no longer regarded as common food at all, tied to the deepest claims this community made about its own founder's flesh. And in at least one community's memory, worship had begun to organize time itself: a day, once a year, set aside as the day of a leader's own death, spoken of as a birthday rather than an ending.

*Grounding note:* this section draws on G07 (Primary; Didache 9–10's thanksgiving-prayer sequence and cup-before-bread order, Registry-traceable within Doc_02 Section 4; Ignatius's *Philadelphians* 4 for the "one eucharist" instruction, per Doc_03's corrected citation, distinct from *Smyrnaeans* 8's separate bishop-validated-eucharist material; Justin's *First Apology* 65–67 for the fuller explanatory form, held per Doc_02's own caution against reading Justin's greater explanatory detail as evidence of greater representativeness). The "one eucharist" instruction's dual function as both an authority claim (G01) and a boundary claim (G05) is Doc_04's own Dependency-test finding, confirmed in its final review round. The liturgical-time claim draws on the Martyrdom of Polycarp 18's *dies natalis* language, which Doc_04's own G04 entry treats as this world beginning to hand off to a later period, not as something this world's own practice had yet fully organized around.

### 4.2 Baptismal Practice

Entry into this world began at the water, preceded by instruction in the older two-ways moral teaching distinguishing the way of life from the way of death (see 2.1). For the community behind the Didache, the water itself allowed real latitude — running water where it could be had, but standing water, or water poured three times over the head where immersion was not possible — performed under a threefold naming of Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, with the candidate and baptizer expected to fast beforehand. For a member of the Roman assemblies a generation or two later, the same basic shape held with its own elaboration: prayer and fasting beforehand, water invoked under a fuller triadic naming — "God the Father and Lord of the universe... our Saviour Jesus Christ... the Holy Spirit" — and the rite itself carried a name of its own, illumination, marking not only cleansing but a kind of new sight. For both communities, what followed immediately after was the same in substance: admission, for the first time, to the community's own table. A member who had not yet gone under the water could not yet eat at it.

For a member of the Antioch/Asia Minor strand, what survives is thinner and differently shaped — not a description of the water itself, but a claim about who could authorize it. Baptism there was not a household matter or a wandering teacher's own prerogative; like the eucharist, it belonged to the bishop, or to whomever the bishop entrusted it. A member formed under that instruction carried, alongside whatever the water itself meant to them personally, the further sense that belonging to the rite and belonging to the bishop's own oversight were the same act.

*Grounding note:* Didache 7–8 (water-type flexibility, threefold pouring as a permitted alternative to immersion, triadic naming, pre-baptismal fasting) remains this world's only detailed procedural account and is not superseded by what follows. Justin's *First Apology* 61 (prayer and fasting beforehand, washing under an expanded triadic invocation, "illumination" as the rite's own name) and 65–66 (baptism as an explicit precondition for eucharistic admission) supply a second, independent, comparably detailed procedural account — Strand B, Rome, c. 153–157 CE. Text quoted from the Roberts-Donaldson (ANF) translation; this project's own established citation for Justin's dating (Minns and Parvis, Oxford 2009) was not directly accessible for this specific passage's wording, so the English phrasing above should be read as accurate in substance and structure but not necessarily verbatim against a modern critical edition — flagged honestly rather than presented as a precise quotation from Doc_01's own cited edition. Justin and the Didache are broadly compatible (same core sequence: pre-baptismal fasting, triadic water-naming, baptism-before-eucharist) but not verbally identical, and are held here as two independently-arrived-at accounts, not harmonized into one composite rite, per this world's established diversity-first discipline (Doc_02 §4).

Ignatius's *Smyrnaeans* 8:2 ("it is not lawful without the bishop either to baptize or to celebrate a love-feast") is the only Strand A datum located after a full search of all seven authentic letters. It is genuinely thin: it confirms baptism was practiced, valued, and gatekept by episcopal authority in Antioch/Asia Minor, and nothing more — it supplies no water-method, formula, or sequence detail comparable to Didache or Justin. Two other Ignatian passages reference baptism but concern *Christ's own* baptism by John as an anti-docetic, creedal proof-point (*Ephesians* 18:2, *Smyrnaeans* 1:1), not community practice, and are not used as procedural evidence here. *Polycarp* 6:2's "let your baptism endure as your arms" is a stock military metaphor presupposing the rite, contributing no descriptive content. *Romans* 7's "living water" was checked and excluded — it is martyrdom-longing imagery, not baptismal language, and citing it here would have been a sourcing error. This document does not claim Ignatius corroborates Justin's or the Didache's *procedure* — only that Strand A independently attests the practice existed and mattered enough to be placed under the same authority as the eucharist. This is a genuine, if modest, improvement on baptism's prior single-source (Didache-only) evidentiary base — not a full cross-strand procedural reconstruction — and should be characterized in downstream documents (Doc_06, Doc_09) with that same calibration.

## 5. Organizational Ecology

### 5.1 Authority Structures

This world's members lived inside a live, unresolved question, not a settled office, and two different regions answered it with two different confident practices. In one, obedience to a single bishop, flanked by presbyters and deacons, was experienced as the very shape of unity — an image its own strongest advocate reached for was a lyre's strings, each different, tuned together into one sound. In the other, a council of presbyters was experienced as the ordinary, sufficient shape of oversight, with no felt lack created by the absence of any single presiding figure.

*Grounding note:* draws on G01 (Supporting, Contested specific content, cross-strand in the sense that some form of local office existed in both regions, though the *specific* monarchical-versus-collegial content remains region-specific and Contested per Doc_04). This document does not resolve which experience was "more correct" or more original — Doc_04 found this exact question is the Contested content that keeps G01 from Primary, not something resolved by treating one answer as more advanced than the other. **The lyre image is Ignatius's own (*Ephesians* 4: "your justly renowned presbytery... is fitted as exactly to the bishop as the strings are to the harp") — verified directly against the primary text rather than assumed from paraphrase. Consistent with Open Item 2's treatment of the oikos citations (Section 10), this citation should likewise be formally logged as a new Source Registry consideration before Doc_06 or Doc_07 rely on it as established, since it is not currently a Doc_02 Registry row.**

### 5.2 Formation Logic

Office in this world was argued for, not assumed. A letter argues at length for why deposing legitimately appointed elders was wrong; another argues at length for why a bishop must be obeyed as one obeys God; a community's own instructional text gives directions for electing overseers and assistants precisely because the practice of relying on traveling, unaffiliated teachers was still a live and recent alternative, not a danger already settled. A world that has to argue this hard for an arrangement has not yet secured it, and this world held that felt precariousness rather than the calm of an already-settled institution.

*Grounding note:* draws on 1 Clement, Ignatius, and the Didache's own transition language (15:1, per Doc_02 Section 3).

## 6. Ministry Ecology

### 6.1 Interpretive Ecology

A member of this world did not experience being called by this movement's name as a matter of private belief alone — carrying that name in public was, at once, a legal exposure and a claim about the truth, held together as a single thing rather than two. Interpreting what had been handed down ran through at least three genuinely different postures existing side by side: community instruction handed down as a fixed teaching text, an individual leader's own claimed continuity with apostolic teaching, and instruction received through vision — none of these subordinated to the others within this world's own lifetime.

*Grounding note:* draws on the outside witnesses (Pliny, Tacitus, Suetonius, Lucian) for the legal/theological dual character of the name itself, per Doc_02's Attention Presence/Absence analysis and Doc_04's G03; the three interpretive postures draw on the Didache, Ignatius/Polycarp, and Hermas respectively.

### 6.2 Representative Theological Patterns

Two communities in this world worked out a living theology under two different pressures at once. One insisted, against a rival teaching, that its founder had suffered truly and in the flesh, not merely in appearance. The other wrestled with moral rigor and with whether mercy remained available at all after a real failure committed once the water of baptism had already been received. Neither community had settled the question so much as answered its own live pressure as that pressure was actually felt.

*Grounding note:* the first strand draws on G05 (Tensional, Strand A only — this document does not describe this composite pattern itself as "cross-strand," since G05 remains Strand-bound; the pattern synthesizes two strand-specific phenomena rather than implying G05 achieved cross-strand status). The second draws on Hermas (Strand B).

### 6.3 Power, Influence, and Historical Dynamics

A member of this world carried the weight of a generation that could no longer simply point to someone who had walked with the founder himself — the people who could say "I was there" were dying, and what replaced their direct witness was argument: a letter insisting its own writer stood in unbroken continuity with what had been handed down, a bishop insisting his own office carried that same weight forward. This was not a settled inheritance quietly received; it was something actively, urgently claimed, precisely because the alternative — the last living link simply running out — was close enough to be felt.

Not every voice in this world carried equal weight, even among its own members. Whoever spoke loudest and was heard furthest was, overwhelmingly, someone literate, well-placed, free — a bishop on his way to Rome, a prominent Roman writer addressing Corinth, a man given visions to record. A member of the household such a figure addressed, an enslaved member, a woman named only as somebody's wife rather than as a widow or a patron in her own right — these members of the very same communities had no comparable way to make their own voice carry — what they themselves actually thought and felt went with them, unspoken by anyone but themselves.

*Grounding note:* the eyewitness-generation-loss material draws on Doc_01 Section 7, which names this loss as a primary force behind G01's own organizing work. The unequal-voice material draws on Doc_04 Section 1's source-asymmetry constraint (elite, literate, mostly male voices dominating the surviving record) — a standing limitation this document has treated throughout (Sections 1.2, 3.1, and here) as bearing on every claim above about how "a member of this world" experienced anything. A further, historiographical point belongs to Doc_07's integrated analysis rather than to this inhabited reconstruction: the same correspondence network (G02, Primary) this world built to survive its own scattered, exposed condition is the structural mechanism that allowed its own texts, and therefore its own self-understanding, to survive into the modern evidentiary base at all — this document notes the connection but defers its full treatment to Doc_07, since assessing a world's impact on the historical record is an analytical judgment made from outside the world, not part of its own inhabited experience.
