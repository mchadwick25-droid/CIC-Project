## Story Text

In 393, a deacon named Maximian was elected as a rival primate at Cebarsussi, in direct defiance of Primian, the sitting Donatist primate of Carthage. It was, in miniature, the same act this world's own founders had once taken against Caecilian: a rival consecration, answering a grievance with a new bishop rather than submission to the one already in place.

The mainstream response was neither quiet nor measured. A much larger council — three hundred and ten bishops — met at Bagai in 394 and condemned the Maximianists in language that did not soften what it meant: the decree, preserved in Augustine's own quotation of it, describes the schismatics as shipwrecked men "dashed by the waves of truth upon the sharp rocks," their bodies covering the shore, so thoroughly that "they fail to find so much as burial."

And then, having said this, the council did something its own decree's language did nothing to prepare a listener for. When Felicianus of Musti and Prætextatus of Assuris — two of the condemned Maximianist bishops — were brought back into the fold, it was done without rebaptism and without reordination. A general named Optatus Gildonianus enforced the reconciliation with military force. The very men whose ordination the council had just described in the imagery of shipwreck and unburied death were received back into full office, exactly as they had been ordained under the schism the council had just condemned in the harshest terms it had at hand.

Augustine, no friend to any Donatist argument, seized on the contradiction directly and made it the center of his own case: if reordination could be waived here, on the mainstream Donatist party's own authority, then the absolute logic of G1 and G2 — that invalidly-ordained clergy must always be rebaptized and reordained, without exception — did not hold even for the party that preached it most fiercely.

## Tier Justification

This is Tier 1 material of high confidence: the council's own decree is quoted directly (not merely summarized) in a datable primary source, the participants are named (Maximian, Primian, Felicianus of Musti, Prætextatus of Assuris, Optatus Gildonianus), and the sequence of events — the 393 rival consecration, the 394 condemnation, the subsequent reception without rebaptism or reordination — is corroborated across two separate works by the same author (On Baptism and Answer to the Letters of Petilian), which is a stronger evidentiary base than a single citation.

The one caveat worth stating plainly: this account survives entirely through Augustine, who quotes the Bagai decree specifically to build his own central argument against Donatist rebaptism logic — his selection and framing of the quotation serve that adversarial purpose. This does not cast doubt on the decree's own wording, which Augustine is quoting rather than paraphrasing, but it does mean the story arrives already embedded in an argument this world's own tradition would frame differently, and that framing difference belongs in Usage Guidance rather than being treated as neutral narration.

## Usage Guidance

The Representative may draw on this story as documented history with strong confidence. Augustine's own adversarial purpose may be named factually, as a plain fact about what happened rather than as an account of how the words reached us or what they can or cannot be checked against: "Augustine, arguing against this world's own rebaptism logic, quotes the council's own decree directly to make his case — but the decree's own words, and the reconciliation that followed it, are this world's own record and this world's own history." The council's harsh language, the reconciliation without rebaptism, and the rival's own use of the contradiction may all be presented factually, without characterizing what the record does or does not preserve, corroborate, or permit saying beyond that.

Additional guidance specific to this story: a participant may ask whether this reconciliation exposes the movement's own rebaptism doctrine as inconsistent or insincere. The Representative should not resolve this question for the participant one way or the other; it may be honestly presented as a real tension this world's own institutional history contains — pragmatic reconciliation choosing continuity of office over the absolute application of a principle the movement otherwise held without exception — without adopting Augustine's own conclusion that this proves the whole doctrine hollow, and without pretending the tension does not exist.
