## Story Text

Every year, on the twelfth of March — "the fourth of the Ides of March," in the sermon's own reckoning — this community gathers to remember what was done at Carthage in the name of unity.

The account it tells is not soft. Imperial agents — Leontius, a count, and Ursatius, a duke — came under orders to enforce a single church, with the bishop Caecilian and the tribune Marcellinus standing behind them. Soldiers seized a basilica. What had been a house of prayer became, in the sermon's own bitter phrase, a place of feasting and license. A boy, a catechumen not yet baptized, lay dying inside it and begged those around him: "Help me — a catechumen." Whether he received what he asked is not the point the sermon lingers on; that he asked it, in that place, at that hour, is.

A bishop named Honoratus of Sicilibba felt a tribune's sword graze his throat and lived. Others did not. The sermon says they were killed inside the basilica itself — not by the sword, but by clubs, while they knelt at prayer with their eyes closed, trusting the ground they stood on. Every age, every sex, the account insists. They were buried where they fell, within the building's own walls, because there was nowhere else and no one to stop it. A bishop arriving from Advocata to see what was happening was killed before he could so much as drink water offered to him — the sermon calls this, with open irony, the "hospitality" Carthage gave him: he paid for it with his own blood.

This is what is read aloud, every twelfth of March, to a congregation that was not there and cannot verify every particular — and reads it anyway, because the day itself is how the community has chosen to keep faith with what it believes happened to its own.

## Formation Ecology Connection

This story is this world's clearest surviving evidence of **G3 — Martyr-Cult Identity** (Doc_04 §3.3) functioning as a *practiced*, not merely asserted, formation mechanism: the annual commemoration ("in solemni et anniversaria commemoratione," per the text's own words) is a liturgical act, repeated yearly, that keeps a specific persecution narrative structurally present in the community's own worship calendar. It also connects to **Force 1A-1 / 2A-2** territory (Doc_08 §3) as a formation echo of the founding *traditor* dispute and the escalating persecution pattern — the "unity" operation described here belongs to the same imperial-and-episcopal coercion logic Doc_08's own Cell 1A and 2A forces trace across this world's whole history.

It shows something Doc_02 §4 and Doc_05 already argue in the abstract more vividly than either document can on its own: that this world's formation ecology treated martyrdom not as a past fact to be cited but as an anniversary to be *kept* — a formation practice with a date on the calendar, not only a doctrine in a treatise.

## Tier Justification

This story does not fit Tier 1 cleanly: Tier 1 requires a named author with an identifiable social location, and this sermon's own authorship is genuinely contested between this project's two vendored scholarly treatments — Mabillon's editorial heading dates it to 340 without naming an author, while Monceaux's dedicated chapter (Source_Registry.md row 40) proposes a considerably earlier composition (c. 317-320) and floats, without asserting as settled, the possibility that Donatus the Great himself delivered it. A story cannot be tiered as "named author, identifiable social location, datable with reasonable confidence" when this world's own best available scholarship disagrees by two decades on the date and does not agree on the author at all.

It also does not fit Tier 2's own definition precisely: Tier 2 requires transmission in *collected* form with an identifiable collection history (a sayings-collection, an apophthegmata-style anthology). This is a single sermon, not a collection, even though it was clearly read and re-read liturgically.

Tier 3 is the correct fit: this is material "attributed to specific figures or moments but resting on collected tradition rather than direct documentation," and — more specifically — it is exactly the kind of formation-ideal-bearing commemorative material Tier 3's own definition anticipates when it says "the community is showing what it believed formation produced, even if the specific details are shaped by genre." The annual commemoration is itself the strongest evidence of Tier 3's own logic in action: whatever the sermon's precise authorship and date, the fact of its liturgical repetition is independently attested by the sermon's own text, and that repetition — not the verifiability of every detail inside it — is the formation ecology evidence this story actually offers.

Source-level caveat: this sermon survives only in raw, uncorrected Latin OCR (the vendored file's own header flags this), and no established published English translation was consulted for this chunk. The two short phrases rendered in quotation marks above ("Help me — a catechumen," the "hospitality" given the visiting bishop) were checked directly against the underlying Latin ("*Subvenite... catechumeno mihi*"; "*hospitalitatis*") and are close, literal renderings of short, simple phrases — not a claim of certified translation. The longer narrative passages are told in indirect, reported form for this reason, not quoted as verbatim English.

## Usage Guidance

The Representative may offer this story as the tradition's own account of what a formed life confronting persecution looked like, with the attributed/commemorative framing made explicit: "This is the community's own sermon, read every year on the anniversary of these events, though who first delivered it and exactly when remain genuinely disputed among the scholars who study it." The specific claimed details — the tribune's grazed throat, the exact manner of the killings, the visiting bishop's death — should not be offered as verified historical fact; they should be offered as what the tradition itself chose to remember and repeat, which is a different and no less real kind of evidence.

Additional guidance specific to this story: because this text is genuinely contested as to date and authorship, the Representative should be prepared, if asked directly, to name that contest rather than pick a side the evidence does not settle. The Representative should not attribute this sermon to Donatus the Great as settled fact — Monceaux's proposal is a scholarly hypothesis, not this world's own confirmed record.
