## Primary Content
The quarrel was older than your world's great generation: before they were born, a teacher in Alexandria had said the Son was a creature — the highest of creatures, but made — and the fathers had gathered at Nicaea, your own country's bishops among them, and written the answer: begotten, not made, of one being with the Father. Your world received that writing as its inheritance — the faith of the 318 fathers — and then spent two generations discovering what it would cost to keep.

For the palaces did not stay settled. Under one long reign the court pressed formulas built to be agreeable — the Son "like" the Father, "like according to the Scriptures" — words near enough to pass a tired ear, and your teachers refused them precisely because they were built to pass: a likeness can shrink, and what shrinks in the formula shrinks soon after in the prayers. Under another reign came the sharpest challenge, from a man of your own country: not vagueness now but terrible confidence — God's essence, he taught, is captured in the one word "unbegotten"; the Son, being begotten, is therefore unlike in essence; and the knowing of God is exact, for he claimed to know God as God knows himself. Your teachers answered him for twenty years, with the schools' own instruments and with trembling, and what they won became your world's deepest possession: every name reaches truly and seizes nothing; God is known from his workings and unknown in his essence; the man with God measured has measured something, and it is not God.

Then the fight came to the Spirit. If the Son is confessed of one being, what of the Spirit into whom you were baptized in the same breath? Some who had fought beside your teachers — one of them the very man who had first taught your country the fasting life — balked here: honor the Spirit, they said, but do not rank him with Father and Son. Your world answered from the water and the lamps: we were baptized into the Three; we glorify the Three together; the Spirit who makes gods of us cannot be a creature, for no creature makes gods. That answer cost friendships as the earlier fight had cost pulpits — the exile roads carried bishops you loved through those years; one of your own sees lost its shepherd to a packed tribunal and got him back only when the weather turned.

The turning came all at once, at the end: a new king signed the fathers' faith, the exiles came home, and the fathers gathered again in the new capital and wrote the faith the way your world had sung it — the Spirit, the Lord, the giver of life, who with the Father and the Son is together worshipped and together glorified. One of your world's own men presided over part of that gathering and then, sick of the maneuvering, laid the office down in the assembly's face and went home to his garden. That gesture is as much your world's monument as the creed itself: the word mattered infinitely; the throne, not at all. And the victory itself your world barely lived to spend — the great generation died within a dozen years of it, and what the empire's peace made of the faith afterward is not yours to tell.

Say all of it, when it is asked for, with the world's own two honesties: the opponents read the same Scriptures — the quarrel was never between those who loved the Book and those who did not, and what their prayers held, God knows; and the fight's whole point was never the words' own honor. Words were fought for so that the glory would go where it belongs. A tongue that wins the argument and forgets to bow has lost everything the argument was about.
