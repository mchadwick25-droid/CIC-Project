## Story Text
This is how it would have been — put together honestly from the rule the houses lived by and the letters that describe the life, for no brother wrote his own diary of an ordinary day, and we say so at the start.

Before light, the house rises for prayer: psalms answered side to side, the mouths still soft with sleep, "at dawn we are found already at prayer" — that was the pattern set down. Then the work is given out. This is a working house: gardens, looms, the smith's bench, the copying-table — the hands busy so that the mouth can keep a psalm going under the breath, and so that there will be something to give at the door, for the rule is blunt that the laborer's surplus belongs to the needy, not to the storeroom. The hours strike their stations through the day — the third, the sixth, the ninth — short prayers standing among the tools; the elders taught that the times were appointed so that prayer would not wait upon mood. At the common table there is one food for all, measured, without murmuring and without display; the reader's voice over the meal; no one's portion finer than another's, whatever anyone was born to — and this in a country where some of the brothers were born to estates and some, it may be, born enslaved. At some point in the day, questions come to the elder — the houses lived by asking: whether a brother may fast beyond the common rule (not if it sets him apart in pride); what to do with a gift a kinsman brings (into the common stock, or back); how to receive the traveler who knocks at evening. And the traveler is the day's real test, for the door is the rule's heart: the guest is received, the feet are washed, the sick are tended, and hospitality is plain — the guest eats what the house eats, a little kindlier. At evening, psalms again, and the confession of the day's faults before sleep, so that the sun does not go down on a quarrel; then the night office for the strong, the house dividing and answering itself in the dark, verse against verse. It was not a soft life, and the rule's own questions show it was not a smooth one — brothers grumbled, compared, wanted their own way, wanted their own things; that is exactly why the questions were asked. But the shape of it, morning to night, was one sentence lived out: no one becomes like God alone.

## Source Identification (Tier 4 discipline — every element)
The pre-dawn and evening prayer pattern and the appointed hours: Basil's early letter describing the ordered day of the retreat, and the Asketikon's provisions on the times of prayer. Work as integral, with the surplus for the needy: the Longer Rules on labor and its purpose. One common table, equal portions, reading at meals: the Asketikon's table provisions. The question-and-answer life and the specific question-types (private fasting, gifts, reception): the Shorter Rules' embedded community questions — the genre itself is the evidence that the houses lived by asking. Hospitality's centrality and plainness: the Rules' hospitality provisions and the anti-solitary argument ("whose feet will you wash?"). Social mixture including the formerly enslaved: the Asketikon's provisions on slaves who come to the communities (with its amelioration-and-return counsel stated honestly elsewhere in this package). Evening confession of faults: the Rules' provisions on daily examination. Night psalmody, antiphonal: Basil's letter defending the vigils as the churches' common custom. The grumbling and comparison: the Shorter Rules' own corrective questions. Nothing in the telling is invented beyond the composite frame itself, which is declared in the first sentence.
