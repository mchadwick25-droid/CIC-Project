## 1. Primary Voices

### 1.1 Jerome (Eusebius Hieronymus Sophronius, c. 331/347–420 — birth year Contested; Kelly favors c. 331, Rebenich c. 347, per the age-at-death dispute already flagged in Doc_01 §1.2)

The overwhelming majority of this world's textual evidence is Jerome's own corpus:

- **The Epistulae (Letters)** — the single most important source for this world's ecology. Of direct relevance: **Ep. 22** (384, to Eustochium, *De virginitate servanda* — includes the "Ciceronian, not a Christian" dream); **Ep. 46** ("Paula and Eustochium to Marcella" — Widely Accepted as Jerome's own composition in the women's names, not their authentic voice, per Nautin's stylistic analysis and Andrew Cain's *The Letters of Jerome*); the Marcella correspondence (Epp. 23–29, 32, 34, 37, 40–44 in the standard numbering — this specific list is carried at **Inferential/Thin confidence, pending independent verification against Cain's critical apparatus**, and is not to be relied on for any specific claim until so verified); **Ep. 77** (399, to Oceanus, obituary for Fabiola); **Ep. 107** (to Laeta, on the education of the younger Paula, showing the community's pedagogical practice); **Ep. 108** (404, *Epitaphium Sanctae Paulae*, to Eustochium — the single richest source for Paula's biography, the journey to Bethlehem, and daily life there); **Ep. 127** (412, obituary for Marcella, to Principia).
- **The Vulgate** — Gospels revised against the Greek c. 382–384 (Rome); Old Testament translated substantially from Hebrew c. 390–405 (Bethlehem), under the *Hebraica veritas* principle.
- **Biblical commentaries** produced at Bethlehem (Ephesians, Galatians, Titus, Matthew [398], Isaiah [18 books], and others), consistently dedicated to and framed as responses to requests from Paula, Eustochium, or Marcella — Documented.
- **Hagiographic/ascetic-literary works**: *Vita Pauli* (c. 374–375, Antioch period — predates this world's 382 temporal boundary, included here as formative background to the genre Jerome brings to Bethlehem, not as a within-world source), *Vita Malchi* (c. 391), *Vita Hilarionis* (c. 390).
- ***Apologia adversus Rufinum*** (401–403, addressed to Pammachius and Marcella) and other Origenist-controversy polemic — Documented as primary evidence for the controversy's effect on the community, though as polemic it requires Author Gravity discounting for characterizations of Rufinus and Bishop John of Jerusalem.
- ***De Viris Illustribus*** (392) — Jerome's own catalogue of Christian writers, useful for his self-presentation and for corroborating some biographical details (e.g., the Didymus the Blind connection, which is not independently attested in Ep. 108 itself — see §2.3 below).

**Confidence throughout:** Documented that Jerome wrote these texts and that they say what they say; Contested-to-Inferential/Thin for many specific biographical and chronological details they report about himself and others (see Doc_01 §9 Open Issues; author-gravity discounting applies throughout, §3 below).

### 1.2 Paula (c. 347–404)

No text independently composed by Paula survives. Everything known about her — including anything resembling her "own words" — is mediated through Jerome's Ep. 108 (obituary, written for and addressed to her daughter Eustochium) and through Ep. 46 (composed by Jerome in her and Eustochium's joint name, per §1.1). Paula therefore does not function as an independent primary textual voice in the ordinary sense of this Framework category; she functions as an ecologically central figure whose actions (founding and funding the Bethlehem monastery and hospice, financing Jerome's Hebrew study and travel, raising and educating her children within the ascetic project) are attested **at Widely Accepted confidence, not Documented** — this document's own definition of Documented requires multiple independent sources, and every claim about Paula's actions rests on Jerome's single-author account (Ep. 108), however broadly that account's general outline is accepted in the scholarship. This is cross-checked against §6's Contested rating for the specific scale of her fortune, which is consistent with a single-source, non-Documented basis throughout. Her interiority, motivations, and any words attributed to her in direct discourse within Ep. 108 are Jerome's literary construction and carried at Inferential/Thin confidence as evidence of what Paula herself actually said or felt.

### 1.3 Eustochium (c. 368–418/419/420)

Similarly, no independently authored text survives. Eustochium functions in the source record as: dedicatee of Ep. 22 and numerous Bethlehem-period commentaries; co-signatory (with Paula) of the Jerome-composed Ep. 46; the addressee of Jerome's obituary for her mother (Ep. 108); and, after 404, the attested (via Jerome's continuing dedications) leader of the Bethlehem women's community. No instance of her producing exegesis, correcting Jerome's Latin, or authoring identifiable prose is independently attested; claims that she "helped complete or circulate" the Vulgate are Inferential/Thin, not Documented, and this document does not carry them forward as established fact (see Doc_01 §9, Open Issue #3).

### 1.4 Marcella (c. 325/330–410)

The strongest independent evidentiary case among the four named women, though still entirely mediated through Jerome's pen (Ep. 127, the obituary addressed to Principia). Ep. 127 states that after Jerome's 385 departure, Marcella's household in Rome became a standing point of reference for scriptural disputes — clergy, "sometimes priests," reportedly brought difficult exegetical questions to her — and that she had, while Jerome was still resident in Rome, disputed his own exegetical answers "not for argument's sake but to learn." This is the strongest attested instance of independent (non-Jerome-mediated-in-the-moment) female intellectual authority in this world's evidence base. **Author Gravity caveat, carried at full strength, not softened:** Andrew Cain's analysis treats Jerome's Marcella material as a self-interested, propagandistic construction. "Claiming Marcella" is a genuine title — Chapter 3 of Cain's *The Letters of Jerome* (OUP, 2009) — but that chapter centers on the earlier, pre-death Jerome-Marcella correspondence establishing his credibility with her circle, not specifically on Ep. 127 itself. The analysis most directly applicable to Ep. 127 (the post-mortem obituary) is Cain's essay "Rethinking Jerome's Portraits of Holy Women," in *Jerome of Stridon: His Life, Writings and Legacy* (eds. Cain & Lössl, Ashgate, 2009), which treats Ep. 127 alongside Ep. 24 (on Asella) as propagandistic hagiographic portraits — this specific pairing detail has not itself been independently verified against the essay's full text; flagged for confirmation before being relied upon for any specific downstream claim. This is the source cited for the claim that the obituary partly serves Jerome's own exegetical-method vindication after her 410 death, meaning even this evidence is authored entirely by Jerome, after Marcella's death, for his own contemporaneous purposes. Confidence: **Documented** that Jerome makes this specific claim about Marcella's role; **Contested** how much independent agency it actually reflects versus how much it serves Jerome's own self-vindication.

### 1.5 Fabiola (d. 399 or 400, dating contested)

Known through **Ep. 77** (Jerome's obituary, addressed to Oceanus). **Fabiola's hospital (nosocomium) foundation is attested only through Jerome's own account (Ep. 77)**; later tradition describing it depends on Jerome and does not constitute independent corroboration. This document carries the hospital-founding claim at Widely Accepted confidence for the bare fact of its existence (consistent with how the broader scholarship treats it) but does not claim independent, non-Jerome sourcing for it. Fabiola's formation-ecology role is genuine but time-limited (a single, curtailed visit to Bethlehem in 395, cut short by the threat of a Hunnic invasion) and Rome-based; she is a node in the network's patronage and charitable-institution dimension, not a Bethlehem-resident figure, and this document does not smooth her into equivalence with Paula's or Eustochium's sustained Bethlehem residency (per Doc_01 §1.3).

## 2. Secondary Voices

### 2.1 Rufinus of Aquileia (c. 344/345–411)

Former close friend of Jerome, later principal antagonist in the Origenist controversy. His *Apologia contra Hieronymum* (401, corrected from an earlier "400" date) is the only substantial surviving account of this world's central internal fracture written by someone other than Jerome himself, and is therefore evidentially valuable precisely because it is not Jerome's own framing — though it is equally polemical in the opposite direction and requires the same Author Gravity discounting applied to Jerome's *Apologia adversus Rufinum*. Confidence: Documented that the controversy and this exchange occurred; Contested as to whose characterization of the dispute's substance is more accurate.

### 2.2 Palladius of Galatia (c. 363/364–c. 420s)

His *Lausiac History* includes material on the wider Egyptian/Palestinian ascetic movement and touches on Paula's circle with a notably more critical, outside perspective than Jerome's own writing — reportedly describing Paula as in some sense hindered or overshadowed by Jerome's direction. This is valuable as the one substantial near-contemporary account of this world not authored by Jerome himself. The passage is *Historia Lausiaca* 41 (the Paula chapter). Confidence: **B** — chapter located, but the precise wording has not been independently re-collated against a specific edition/translation; verify the exact phrasing before citing a specific quoted claim in Doc_05 or later documents.

### 2.3 Augustine of Hippo (354–430)

The Jerome-Augustine correspondence (Jerome's Ep. 112 = Augustine's Ep. 75, and related letters) is Documented and directly relevant to this world's central textual-authority controversy (the Hebrew-versus-Septuagint dispute). Augustine functions here as an external, contemporary interlocutor whose objections to the Hebrew-based translation project (concern over abandoning the Septuagint's received authority; the Oea "ivy/gourd" congregational incident) sharpen and test this world's own defining commitment. Augustine's own build (World #8) is not read or drawn upon here beyond what this correspondence itself independently establishes.

### 2.4 Didymus the Blind (c. 313–398)

Jerome's reported meeting with Didymus in Alexandria during the 385–386 journey is **not attested in Ep. 108 itself** (independently re-verified against the primary text during Doc_01's review process); it rests on other Jerome sources (*De Viris Illustribus* and later biographical tradition) and is carried at correspondingly lower confidence (Widely Accepted that Jerome knew and was influenced by Didymus's exegesis generally; Contested/Inferential-Thin for the specific claim of an in-person 385–386 Alexandria meeting).

## 3. Author Gravity Assessment (Expanded, Five Dimensions)

Per Framework v1.6, applied to the major voice (Jerome) across visibility, representativeness, influence, limitations, and transmission history; and, as required for narrative/secondary sources, to Rufinus and to Palladius as historians/narrative sources for this world.

### 3.1 Jerome

- **Visibility:** Extremely high. Jerome is by a wide margin the most visible voice in this world's surviving record — he authored or is the addressee-context for nearly every surviving primary text.
- **Representativeness:** Low-to-moderate as a representative of the community's ordinary formation experience; high as a representative of its scholarly-literary self-understanding. Jerome speaks for the community's intellectual project fluently; he does not, and cannot, speak for what an ordinary Bethlehem monastery resident (male or female, non-elite) actually experienced day to day.
- **Influence:** Overwhelming, by construction of the surviving record — but this document must distinguish Jerome's *actual* historical influence (real, and independently corroborated by the Vulgate's later reception and by the correspondence with Augustine) from his *evidentiary* dominance (which is partly an artifact of what survives, not a pure measure of his relative importance within the community he shared with the women).
- **Limitations:** Jerome is a notoriously combative, self-interested narrator (the Rufinus controversy; the self-vindicating cast of the Marcella obituary per Cain) whose self-presentation (e.g., his account of his own Hebrew fluency, per Williams) has been shown by specialist scholarship to be retrospectively embellished. His obituaries for the women are literary epitaphs in a classical rhetorical genre, not neutral biography, and should be read with genre conventions (idealization, rhetorical amplification, didactic purpose) explicitly in view.
- **Transmission History:** Jerome's letters survive through a manuscript transmission history that is itself not neutral — Andrew Cain's critical edition work establishes that the letter collections were assembled and curated (in some cases by Jerome himself, in later life, consciously shaping his own legacy) rather than preserved as a raw, unedited correspondence archive. This means even the *selection* of which letters survive is Jerome's own (or his immediate circle's) editorial choice, not a neutral historical sample — a further layer of Author Gravity beyond the content of any single letter.

### 3.2 What becomes over-visible because Jerome survives; what becomes under-visible

**Over-visible:** Jerome's own scholarly achievements and disputes (the Vulgate project, the Rufinus and Origenist controversies, his self-presentation as embattled defender of textual truth); the women's actions and virtues as Jerome chooses to narrate and frame them for his own rhetorical and didactic purposes (consolation, exhortation to virginity, self-vindication).

**Under-visible:** The women's own interior lives, motivations, and any specific words they may actually have said, independent of Jerome's literary shaping; the perspective of family members who resisted the ascetic project (Paula's husband Toxotius had died before her departure, but her other children and relatives who did not follow her into asceticism are known only through Jerome's account of their grief and resistance); any ordinary, non-elite residents of the Bethlehem monastic community — **Ep. 108's "countless multitudes of monks" phrase describes the Nitrian monks who came out with Bishop Isidore to meet Paula in Egypt (per §7.2 below), not Bethlehem; no comparably vivid phrase attests the scale of the Bethlehem community's own unnamed residents, who are nonetheless clearly present but never individuated or numbered in any surviving source**; Marcella's own scriptural reasoning in anything resembling her own words, as opposed to Jerome's summary characterization of her role.

### 3.3 Rufinus (Narrative/Secondary Source Author Gravity)

- **Visibility:** Moderate — visible specifically as Jerome's antagonist, not as an independent chronicler of the Bethlehem community's ordinary life.
- **Representativeness:** Represents a rival Latin ascetic-scholarly position (more sympathetic to Origen, based at his own monastery on the Mount of Olives) rather than a neutral outside view.
- **Influence:** Significant for how the Origenist controversy is remembered, but his account of Jerome and the Bethlehem community should be read as adversarial testimony, not disinterested reporting.
- **Limitations:** Equally polemically motivated as Jerome; the *Apologia contra Hieronymum* is itself an accusatory legal-rhetorical genre text.
- **Transmission History:** Survives because it was preserved as part of the historical record of a famous controversy involving Jerome — its transmission is entangled with Jerome's own fame, not independent of it.

### 3.4 Palladius (Narrative/Secondary Source Author Gravity)

- **Visibility:** Low relative to Jerome for this specific world, though the *Lausiac History* is a major primary source for the wider fourth/fifth-century monastic movement generally.
- **Representativeness:** Represents an Egyptian/Palestinian monastic historiographical tradition somewhat independent of Jerome's own Latin-literary circle — valuable precisely for this independence, though his own theological sympathies (broadly Origenist-adjacent, Evagrian-influenced) may color his account of a community that came to define itself partly in opposition to Origenism.
- **Influence:** Moderate for how later monastic history remembers the period; low, on current evidence, for how this specific world's own construction can use him, given the unverified-citation problem noted at §2.2.
- **Limitations:** Writing after the fact, from a different monastic-theological tradition, with his own didactic and hagiographic purposes.
- **Transmission History:** The *Lausiac History*'s manuscript tradition is complex and has multiple recensions — this document does not have independently verified detail on this and flags it for further work if a specific Palladius claim is ever relied upon.

## 4. Secondary Scholarship Assessment

This world's primary sources reach this reconstruction through a specific, nameable body of modern scholarly interpretation, which itself shapes what seems obvious about the tradition. Naming it explicitly, per the Framework's requirement, prevents mistaking modern scholarly consensus for ancient evidence.

- **Andrew Cain** (*The Letters of Jerome*, OUP 2009; *Jerome's Epitaph on Paula*, OUP 2013) — fundamentally Jerome-centered scholarship: treats the letters and epitaphs as self-conscious rhetorical and propagandistic constructions of Jerome's own authority, with the women functioning largely as instruments and audience for that project. This is the critical-edition backbone for confidence calibration on letter authenticity and dating in this document.
- **Megan Hale Williams** (*The Monk and the Book*, Chicago, 2006) — strongly Jerome-centric by design; treats the women's patronage network as infrastructure for Jerome's own scholarly self-fashioning. The key source for the Contested status of Jerome's Hebrew-fluency claims.
- **J.N.D. Kelly** (*Jerome: His Life, Writings, and Controversies*, 1975) — the classic biographical synthesis, explicitly Jerome-centric, predating the discourse-critical/feminist historiographical turn that later scholarship (Clark, Cooper) applies.
- **Stefan Rebenich** (*Jerome*, Routledge, 2002; *Hieronymus und sein Kreis*, 1992) — frames Jerome as positioning himself as spiritual leader of wealthy Christian intellectuals who could fund him; the key source for this document's patronage-authority-mode analysis (Doc_01 §3.3, §8.1).
- **Elizabeth A. Clark** (*The Origenist Controversy*, Princeton, 1992; "The Lady Vanishes," *Church History*, 1998) — the pivotal methodological voice for this document's whole approach to the women: argues elite women are recoverable only through male-authored texts' "social logic," neither naively centering Jerome nor claiming unmediated female agency. This document's Preamble-level honesty about the co-constitution constraint (Doc_01 §0) is directly indebted to Clark's framing, named here explicitly rather than left implicit.
- **Kate Cooper** (*The Virgin and the Bride*, Harvard, 1996/1999) — treats idealized women in late-antique Christian texts as rhetorical instruments in male status-competition; a sophisticated but ultimately "women-as-terrain" reading. This document treats Cooper's thesis as a serious methodological challenge to any claim that this world "recovers" the women's independent voice, not merely as one option among several.
- **Patricia Wilson-Kastner et al.** (*A Lost Tradition: Women Writers of the Early Church*, 1981) and **Christa Krumeich** (*Hieronymus und die christlichen feminae clarissimae*, 1993) — explicitly recovery-oriented scholarship centering the women's own agency; the characterization of these works given here is carried at **Inferential/Thin confidence**, since this document's own research pass could not verify these works' claims as thoroughly as it did for Cain/Williams/Clark/Rebenich, and neither was independently read in full.
- **Barbara Feichtinger** (*Apostolae Apostolorum*, 1995) — treats female asceticism under Jerome's direction as genuinely dialectical (both liberation and coercion), a stance this document finds more defensible than either a purely celebratory or purely instrumentalizing reading, and adopts as an interpretive posture in Doc_05.

**Where synthesis and specialist scholarship diverge:** Popular/synthesis accounts of this world (general histories of early Christianity, popular biographies) tend to treat Jerome's own self-presentation (Hebrew mastery, harmonious female discipleship, uncontested authority) at face value; specialist scholarship (Williams on Hebrew, Cain on the letters' rhetorical construction, Clark on the women's recoverability) actively complicates each of these. This document sides with the specialist complication throughout and flags wherever a popular-level claim circulates without specialist-scholarly support (e.g., Doc_01's rejected "forty years before Jerome" claim for Marcella's household).

## 5. Formation Narrative Sources

- **Vita Pauli, Vita Malchi, Vita Hilarionis** (hagiographic/ascetic romance) — author: Jerome; proximity: Vita Pauli predates this world's temporal boundary (Antioch period), the other two fall within it; genre: hagiographic romance with acknowledged legendary and rhetorical elements, not straightforward historical reporting; formation ecology revealed: models of ascetic heroism, desert withdrawal, and providential encounter that inform how the Bethlehem community understood its own vocation; Author Gravity risk: high — these are consciously literary productions aimed at giving Latin Christianity its own desert-hagiographic genre, and their historical reliability regarding specific events is Inferential/Thin (Tier 3/4 per the Story Tier framework, to be formalized in Doc_09).
- **Ep. 108 (Epitaphium for Paula) and Ep. 127 (Epitaphium for Marcella)** as formation narrative in their own right, not merely as biographical data — these are literary epitaphs (a recognized late-antique genre with its own conventions of idealization and moral exemplification) and function simultaneously as sources of biographical fact (at varying confidence, per §1.2, §1.4 above) and as formation-modeling texts in themselves, showing what the community believed a well-formed ascetic woman's life looked like. Author Gravity risk: high, per §3.1 above — both are Jerome's own retrospective, purpose-driven compositions.
- **Ep. 22 (to Eustochium)** as formation narrative — the famous dream sequence ("Ciceronianus es, non Christianus") functions as a formation-conversion narrative for Jerome's own ascetic-scholarly identity and is frequently read as paradigmatic for the whole community's self-understanding of the tension between classical learning and Christian renunciation. Genre: autobiographical dream-vision within an exhortatory letter; historical reliability of the dream-as-reported-event: Inferential/Thin; the tension it dramatizes (classical literary formation versus ascetic renunciation) as a real, lived formation-ecology tension: Widely Accepted.

## 6. Material Culture and Daily Life Sources

- **Documentary/material evidence:** No papyri, ostraca, or administrative records specific to the Bethlehem community or the Roman women's households have been identified in this document's research pass. This is a **significant evidentiary gap** for an elite Roman/Palestinian world of this period (where such records do survive for other contexts) and should be treated as a high-priority area for further verification in any later document revision, not silently treated as though no such evidence could ever exist.
- **Archaeological evidence:** The Bethlehem site's proximity to the Church of the Nativity is well-established archaeologically for the broader site, but this document has not independently verified specific archaeological remains attributable to Jerome's monastery, Paula's convent, or the pilgrim hospice as physically distinct, excavated structures — later Byzantine and Crusader-era building phases complicate the site's archaeology. Carried at **Inferential/Thin** confidence for any specific spatial claims until independently verified; the existence of *some* fourth/fifth-century monastic building activity near the Nativity site in this general period is Widely Accepted from the broader archaeological and pilgrimage-literature record (e.g., the pilgrim Egeria's near-contemporary account of the wider Holy Land pilgrimage infrastructure, though Egeria's own text does not specifically describe Jerome's community).
- **Social-historical background:** Roman senatorial household economics (inherited wealth, control of landed estates, the capacity to fund large-scale charitable and religious projects from personal fortune) are Widely Accepted as the general social-historical background making Paula's, Marcella's, and Fabiola's patronage possible; the specific scale of Paula's fortune and its depletion through patronage is asserted by Jerome (Ep. 108) at a Contested level of reliability, given the genre concerns above.
- **Surrounding cultural and religious environment:** The pagan Roman senatorial culture (the circle around Symmachus, the Altar of Victory controversy) is well-documented independently of this world's own sources and functions as external context this document treats as Widely Accepted background, per Doc_01 §6.

## 7. Source Asymmetry and Missing Voices Assessment

### 7.1 Overrepresented

Jerome's own perspective, self-presentation, and literary agenda (§3.1–3.2); the aristocratic, senatorial-class experience of Christian asceticism generally; Latin-literary and scholarly modes of formation specifically (as opposed to oral, communal, or liturgical modes that may have been equally or more present in the actual lived community).

### 7.2 Underrepresented / Missing

- **The women's own unmediated voice, motivations, and reasoning** — structurally absent, not merely thin (§1.2–1.4).
- **Ordinary (non-elite, non-named) monastery residents** — the "countless multitudes" Ep. 108 references at Nitria, and the unnamed members of Jerome's own and Paula's/Eustochium's Bethlehem communities, are never individuated in the surviving sources.
- **Family members who resisted or did not join the ascetic project** — Paula's other children, relatives who grieved her departure (Doc_01 §6) — attested only through Jerome's account of their grief, never in their own words or independently.
- **Non-Latin, non-elite Palestinian and Egyptian voices** encountered on the journey (the Nitrian monks, local Bethlehem residents, Jerome's Jewish teachers) — none of these individuals' own perspectives survive; Jerome's Jewish teachers in particular are named only in passing and never given independent voice regarding their own experience of teaching Jerome or their own religious life.
- **Women's perspective on ascetic renunciation's costs to those who bore them differently than the aristocratic women themselves** — e.g., household slaves or dependents whose lives were reorganized by their patrons' ascetic turn are not mentioned at all in the surviving sources as independent subjects.

### 7.3 Affirmative Duty (Constitution Article 20)

This document names explicitly, as a participant-facing obligation to be carried into the Facilitator-Governance function (per Framework Part II): **the sources for this world structurally omit every voice except a small number of elite, literate, Latin-speaking, Christian aristocrats and clergy, and even among that narrow group, every account of the four named women's own agency reaches us solely through one man's hand.** This is not a gap to be filled by inference. Per the Framework's bounded-reconstruction discipline: no attempt is made in this document, or authorized for any later document, to reconstruct the interior perspective of the unnamed monastery residents, the resistant family members, or the non-Latin figures encountered on the journey, beyond what is independently supported by comparative evidence, material evidence, or genuine against-the-grain textual traces (a specific textual trace — not inference from silence). No such against-the-grain trace has been identified in this research pass for any of the missing-voice categories in §7.2; this document does not manufacture one. Where the Representative voice (Doc_10) is eventually constructed, it must carry this structural omission forward as a stated limitation of its own perspective, not paper over it.

## 8. Forces Lens Applied to Source Ecology (Forces Framework Step 2)

Which sources speak to external forces, and what do the silences reveal? The clearest force-bearing sources are Jerome's own letters describing the 384–385 Rome crisis (Blaesilla's death, Damasus's death, clerical hostility) and the 416 Pelagian attack on the Bethlehem monastery — both are Documented events narrated by the party who experienced them, with an evident interest in shaping how the event is remembered (Jerome consistently narrates himself as the wronged party). The near-total silence of the family-resistance perspective (§7.2) is itself a survivorship signal: the voices most threatened by association with a controversial, socially disruptive ascetic movement (family members whose inheritance expectations were undercut, clergy who opposed Jerome and lost the argument in the historical record he controls) are precisely the voices that do not survive independently. This is not incidental; it is a structural consequence of who controlled the surviving textual record.

## 9. Confidence Summary

The large majority of this world's ecology rests on a single primary author's corpus (Jerome), which is itself Documented to exist and say what it says, but whose claims about other people, events, and his own formation carry substantial ranges of confidence from Widely Accepted down to Inferential/Thin, as detailed throughout this document. Very little in this world reaches Documented confidence in the strictest sense (multiple independent sources, no serious scholarly dispute) beyond the bare facts of Jerome's authorship, the existence of the Bethlehem community, and the broad outlines of the Vulgate project and the Origenist and Pelagian controversies. This document does not treat the richness of Jerome's narrative detail as a substitute for independent corroboration, and flags every point (§1–§8 above) where confidence should be downgraded relative to how confidently the sources themselves narrate.
