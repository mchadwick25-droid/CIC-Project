## 1. Source Ecology Overview

This world's evidentiary base is thin in the way most non-elite antique evidence is thin, but unusually well-attested at its center: only three substantial fourth-century Syriac literary corpora survive from anywhere in this world's linguistic and geographic range at all — Ephrem's, Aphrahat's, and the anonymous *Liber Graduum* (Book of Steps, which falls partly outside this world's own 410 boundary in its final form and is not treated as a primary founding source here, though it is named for completeness). Of these, Ephrem's corpus overwhelms the other two in sheer volume. This concentration is itself the central Author Gravity fact governing this whole Ecology (Section 3).

Four source categories make up this world's evidentiary base:

- **Primary voices:** Ephrem's hymnic and prose corpus; Aphrahat's *Demonstrations*; the Diatessaron (indirectly attested); the Odes of Solomon (contested attribution to this world at all); the bnay/bnat qyama institution as attested in Aphrahat and Ephrem.
- **Secondary scholarship:** the modern critical apparatus this document and Doc_01 depend on — independently verified for actual expertise in Section 4, following this project's own established discipline of checking a scholar's real center of gravity before anchoring them to a world.
- **Formation-narrative and material sources:** the *Doctrina Addai*/Abgar legend (a formation myth, not a historical record — see Section 5), the *Chronicle of Edessa*, and the excavated Nisibis cathedral complex (Section 6).
- **Ordinary-participant evidence:** structurally almost absent, and named as such rather than papered over (Section 7).

No complete modern English translation exists for *Contra Haereses* or the *Carmina Nisibena* — a real limitation, since these are precisely the heresiological and Nisibis-siege texts most relevant to this world's boundary-work and its 363 transition.

**Aphrahat "the Persian Sage" (fl. c. 270–345).** Twenty-three *Demonstrations* (Syriac *taḥwyāṯā*), critical edition by Jean Parisot (*Patrologia Syriaca* I/1–2, 1894 and 1907), based on manuscripts from Deir al-Surian transferred to the British Museum. Standard complete English translation: Adam Lehto, *The Demonstrations of Aphrahat, the Persian Sage* (Gorgias Press, 2010); an older partial translation by John Gwynn covers only eight of the twenty-three (*NPNF*, 2nd series, vol. 13, 1898); a complete alternative translation exists by Kuriakose Valavanolickal (Gorgias, 2005). Composition in two phases: Demonstrations 1–10 (336/337 CE) and 11–22 (344 CE), each set following the 22-letter Syriac alphabetic acrostic; Demonstration 23 ("Concerning the Grape") added later (August 345), outside the acrostic scheme. Content spans practical/ascetic exhortation (including the *bnay qyama*, Demonstration 6), apocalyptic material addressing Rome-Persia tension (Demonstration 5, "On Wars"), and sustained anti-Jewish polemic (roughly four Demonstrations), which Naomi Koltun-Fromm's work treats as evidence of a live, localized polemical exchange rather than generic apologetic — see Section 3's Representativeness discussion.

Even Aphrahat's own name is uncertain: a 510 CE colophon calls him "Mar Jacob, the Persian sage"; the name "Aphrahat" appears only from the tenth century onward (Bar Bahlul, Elias of Nisibis). His biography outside the text is unknown.

**The Diatessaron (Tatian, c. 172 CE).** No complete Syriac manuscript survives. Its content is triangulated entirely from indirect and derivative witnesses: Ephrem's own *Commentary on the Diatessaron* (itself possibly a disciple's compilation rather than Ephrem's unmediated composition — Christian Lange, CSCO 616, 2005), the sixth-century Latin *Codex Fuldensis*, the later Arabic Diatessaron (Ciasca, 1888), and a contested third-century Greek fragment from Dura-Europos (P.Dura 10) whose actual identification as Diatessaronic is a live, unresolved scholarly dispute (Joosten defends the identification; Mills, Parker, David G.K. Taylor, and Goodacre reject it; Mina Monier and Joan E. Taylor — a different Taylor from the one just named — stake out a mediating position). William L. Petersen's *Tatian's Diatessaron* (Brill, 1994) remains the foundational survey; Matthew Crawford and Nicholas Zola's edited volume *The Gospel of Tatian* (T&T Clark, 2019) and James Barker's *Tatian's Diatessaron* (Oxford, 2022) are the current status-quaestionis. The original language of composition (Syriac vs. Greek) remains unresolved.

**The Odes of Solomon.** Forty-two odes. Manuscript witnesses: Papyrus Bodmer XI (Greek, Ode 11 only, late 3rd/early 4th c.); British Library Add. 14538, "Codex Nitriensis" (Syriac, c. 10th c.; standard manuscript descriptions give Codex N's contents as Odes 17:7–42:20, roughly 26 odes; the 40-ode figure belongs to the Harris manuscript below, not Codex N — do not cite a specific Codex N ode count until checked directly against Lattke's Hermeneia apparatus or Charlesworth's edition); the Harris manuscript (Syriac, most complete, but itself no earlier than the 15th c.); five odes quoted in Coptic within the *Pistis Sophia*. Dating proposals range from Charlesworth's c. 100 CE to Michael Lattke's c. 125 CE (Hermeneia commentary, 2009; "Dating the Odes of Solomon," *Antichthon* 27, 1993). Critically, Aaron Michael Butts's recent treatment (*The Cambridge Edition of Early Christian Writings*, vol. 3, 2022) states explicitly that "provenance, date, and original language are all disputed" — this document treats Edessene provenance as one proposed origin among several, not an established fact, and flags the Odes' Boundary Status accordingly (Section 8 and the Registry).

**The bnay qyama / bnat qyama ("sons/daughters of the covenant").** Within this world's own 200–410 window, only two sources are firmly datable: Aphrahat's Demonstration 6 (337 CE) — the earliest named source for the institution — and Ephrem's hymns, which depict the *bnat qyama* performing his madrashe as women's choirs. Malatius Malki Malki's 2024 article ("Revisiting the Institution of Bnay and Bnoth Qyōmo in the Syriac Tradition," *Religions* 15(6): 686) is the most recent scholarly treatment, but it treats fourth- through eighth-century material (including Rabbula's fifth-century Canons and the *Liber Graduum*) as one continuous stratum rather than separating what falls inside this world's own 410 boundary from what falls after it — this document uses Malki Malki's treatment cautiously and restricts its own Native claim to the Aphrahat/Ephrem-attested core, not the institution's later development.

## 3. Author Gravity Assessment (Constitution Article 16; five dimensions, per the Source Registry Template's instruction to treat this alongside the Boundary Check as one evidentiary act)

### Ephrem the Syrian

- **Visibility.** Ephrem's corpus so overwhelms Aphrahat's and the *Liber Graduum* that his own Nicene, anti-heretical, ascetic-catechetical voice risks standing in for the whole of this world's Christianity. No single scholarly study states this "dominance effect" as a discrete, named thesis; it is a reasonable and well-supported inference from the plain fact of relative corpus size (Brock's surveys; the *Cambridge History of Early Christian Literature*, ch. 33), flagged here as this document's own synthesis rather than a borrowed conclusion.
- **Representativeness.** Ephrem was a deacon (Sidney Griffith, "Ephraem, the Deacon of Edessa, and the Church of the Empire," 1986) — a clerical-ascetic institutional position, not a lay-householder one. This bullet previously also asserted Ephrem held the specific title *malpana* (teacher) and was "embedded in the bnay/bnat qyama choir-order," citing Griffith's *Faith Adoring the Mystery* (1997) for both. Doc_03, Section 3.1 found no direct textual attestation of the *malpana* title for Ephrem within this world's own 200–410 window — the association traces to later hagiographic and tertiary-source tradition — and Doc_03, Section 1.2 separately found that *Faith Adoring the Mystery* is a real Griffith work but is not itself the qyama-specific citation; the qyama-specific Griffith citations are his 1991 *The Harp* article and 1993 *Eulogema* chapter. Ephrem's own hymns do show the *bnat qyama* performing his madrashe as women's choirs (Section 2 above), but the specific claim that Ephrem himself organized or led the *bnat qyama* choirs is, per Doc_03 Section 1.2, a later hagiographic attribution rather than his own contemporary self-testimony. His voice remains well-documented as that of an institutionally embedded deacon-poet, not a neutral communal spokesman — but neither "malpana" nor "choir-organizer" should be carried forward as settled 200–410 fact.
- **Influence on this world's own boundaries.** This is the best-sourced dimension. Drijvers's *Bardaiṣan of Edessa* (1966) is the foundational study of how modern reconstruction of Bardaisan depends on hostile Ephrem material. Phil Botha ("The Textual Strategy of Ephrem's Hymn Contra Haereses I," *Acta Patristica et Byzantina* 15.1, 2004) shows Ephrem's rhetoric flattens Marcion, Bardaisan, and Mani into an interchangeable "satanic deception" triad rather than offering individuated doctrinal analysis. Flavia Ruani ("Books of Protection, Books of Perdition...", *Henoch* 39/2, 2017) calls Ephrem the founder of Syriac heresiology outright, treating his rhetoric as *constructive* of the orthodox/heretic boundary, not merely descriptive of a pre-existing one. This means this world's own boundary against Bardaisan, Marcion, and Mani (Doc_01, Section 5 and 7) is substantially Ephrem's own rhetorical achievement, and should be named as such rather than treated as a neutral historical given.
- **Limitations.** Susan Ashbrook Harvey ("Spoken Words, Voiced Silence," 2001; *Ministries of Song*, 2025) shows Ephrem gives the bnat qyama women prominent literary and performative voice through his hymns — but this is Ephrem's own construction of their voice, not their own unmediated speech. His corpus cannot show ordinary lay devotional practice directly; everything is filtered through a clerical-ascetic teaching register.
- **Transmission history.** The key early manuscript is British Library Add. 14571 (dated 519 CE), preserving major hymn cycles. Edmund Beck's CSCO editions (1955–1979) remain the modern authenticity baseline, using manuscript age and internal-anachronism tests — for example, Beck argued the "Hymns on Epiphany" are inauthentic because Nisibis had no separate Epiphany feast in Ephrem's own lifetime, only a combined Nativity-Baptism observance on January 6, and the Epiphany-specific material appears only in ninth-century, not sixth-century, manuscripts. A large body of Greek material attributed to "Ephraem Graecus" is now understood to be largely later pseudonymous composition, not translation of genuine Syriac originals, assessed case by case via language, meter, theology, and manuscript pedigree (Joseph P. Amar, *The Syriac Vita Tradition of Ephrem the Syrian*, CSCO 629–630, 2011).

### Aphrahat

- **Visibility.** Aphrahat is the earliest substantial literary witness to a distinctly Persian-side (non-Roman) Syriac Christianity: its scriptural exegesis (heavily indebted to Babylonian rabbinic hermeneutics, per multiple scholars), its ascetic *bnay/bnat qyama* institution, and its posture under an increasingly hostile Sasanian state.
- **Representativeness.** Genuinely contested, not settled. Naomi Koltun-Fromm's work (*Jewish-Christian Conversation in Fourth-Century Persian Mesopotamia: A Reconstructed Conversation*, Gorgias, 2011; *Hermeneutics of Holiness*, Oxford, 2010) treats Aphrahat's anti-Jewish material as evidence of a live, localized polemical exchange specific to his own community, not a generic literary topos — meaning he may speak for one particular argument within the Persian church rather than for it as a whole. Lehto's introduction (2010) frames Aphrahat as reflecting a Persian Christianity notably close to its Jewish roots, a distinctive rather than generic profile.
- **Influence.** Demonstrations 11–22 and 23 were composed during the height of Shapur II's persecution (from 339/344 onward); pastoral anxiety and apologetic pressure toward the Persian state visibly shape the text. "The Persian side" of this world, as reconstructed from Aphrahat, is filtered through a persecution-era defensive posture — a real limitation this document names rather than treats as neutral background (carried into the Forces lens, Section 9).
- **Limitations.** The text offers almost nothing on liturgical practice in concrete detail and minimal demographic information about the wider lay community.
- **Transmission history.** The earliest manuscript, British Library Add. 17182, survives in two parts (Demonstrations 1–10 copied 474 CE; 13–23 copied 510 CE), with Demonstrations 11–12 absent from the surviving witness — the cause of this gap is not established in the sources consulted, and this document does not speculate on it; the only complete manuscript is the sixth-century BL Add. 14619. The Demonstrations were not widely disseminated even within Syriac Christianity. Only Demonstration 6 was translated into Georgian, itself derived from an Armenian intermediary translation, and transmitted there under a misattribution to Hippolytus rather than Aphrahat; the fuller Armenian translation (misattributed to "Jacob of Nisibis," ed. Antonelli 1756, only 19 homilies) is separate from this single-Demonstration Georgian strand; the Demonstrations were also translated into Ge'ez and partially into Arabic (misattributed to Ephrem).

## 5. Formation Narrative Sources

**The *Doctrina Addai* / Abgar-Addai legend.** This is this world's own foundation myth — a text native to this world in the sense that it is this world's own self-understanding, produced and transmitted within it — but its historical claims (a 1st-century correspondence between King Abgar V and Jesus, conversion by the apostle Addai) are not historical, per Doc_01's own finding. Its Boundary Status here is Native, but its Licensed-For use is restricted to formation-narrative/self-understanding material (how this world told its own origin story) and explicitly not to historical-origin claims, which Doc_01 already resolved using the *Chronicle of Edessa* and Bardaisan's own attested career instead.

**The *Chronicle of Edessa*.** An anonymous sixth-century Syriac chronicle (composed c. 540s CE, in a single surviving abbreviated manuscript, Vatican Syriac 163), compiled roughly 340 years after the 201 CE flood/church-destruction event it reports. J.B. Segal treats it as substantially reliable, citing its comparative sobriety — it notably lacks the legendary Abgar/Addai material found elsewhere, which counts as internal evidence against wholesale later interpolation. This document treats the flood/church report as archivally-derived secondhand testimony (plausibly drawn from genuine earlier civic or church archives), not as a contemporary eyewitness record — a real, meaningful distinction Doc_01's beginning-point finding should be read alongside, not instead of.

## 6. Material Culture and Daily Life Sources

Physical evidence for this world is asymmetric between its two anchor cities, and even the Nisibis evidence should not be overstated. For Nisibis, Elif Keser Kayaalp and Nihat Erdoğan's excavation report ("The Cathedral Complex at Nisibis," *Anatolian Studies* 63, 2013) documents a securely dated, standing baptistery, datable to 313/320–359/360 CE and directly contemporary with Jacob of Nisibis and the Persian sieges Doc_01 discusses. The wider five-aisled cathedral plan is Kayaalp's own reconstruction — her own framing is explicitly an "attempt to reconstruct the possible cathedral" — inferred from partial surviving remains (part of one wall, several piers), not a fully excavated, confirmed structure; this document treats the baptistery as the secure, dated material fact and the five-aisled plan as a well-argued but hypothetical reconstruction, not settled fact. For Edessa, by contrast, no physical evidence survives from within this world's own window: modern Urfa sits atop ancient Edessa, blocking excavation, and a recent (2024–2025) dig at Urfa Castle uncovered a Christian mosaic/shrine dated 460–495 CE — after this world's own 410 boundary, and therefore Excluded here as Out-of-Boundary rather than treated as evidence for this world. This asymmetry (real, dated material evidence at Nisibis; none at Edessa within-window) should be named explicitly in any later document drawing on this world's material culture, not smoothed into a single undifferentiated "material evidence exists" claim.

## 7. Source Asymmetry and Missing Voices

This world's evidentiary base is structurally elite, clerical, and male, with four specific silences worth naming rather than leaving implicit:

- **Ordinary lay believers.** Susan Ashbrook Harvey's "Spoken Words, Voiced Silence" (*JECS* 9, 2001) argues directly that this corpus is structurally clerical: ordinary believers appear only as objects of address in Ephrem's and Aphrahat's texts, never as authors in their own right.
- **The bnat qyama women.** Harvey's "Revisiting the Daughters of the Covenant" (*Hugoye* 8.2, 2005) states plainly that no text composed by a bnat qyama woman survives from this period — their singing and communal life are attested only through Aphrahat, Ephrem, and a later memra on Ephrem by Jacob of Serug (falling after this world's own boundary). Harvey's own formulation — that Greek sources are as silent on these Syriac choirs as Syriac women were in their own liturgies — captures the asymmetry precisely and is quoted here as her own framing, not this document's paraphrase.
- **Jewish dialogue partners.** Aphrahat's anti-Jewish Demonstrations respond to what Koltun-Fromm's own scholarship frames explicitly as a "reconstructed conversation" — her book's title concedes this directly. No independent Jewish source confirms or corroborates his specific characterization of these exchanges; this world's record of Jewish-Christian contestation is entirely one-sided.
- **Enslaved and lower-status persons.** No dedicated scholarly treatment or surviving voice was located in this research at all. This is named as an unfilled gap, not glossed over with inference.

**Affirmative Duty (Constitution Article 20).** Per this world's own Forces Identification (Doc_01, Section 7), which frames a plural, contested Edessene religious milieu as this world's own generative condition, this document names explicitly that the source record structurally suppresses: lay/non-elite religious experience generally; the bnat qyama women's own unmediated voice specifically, despite their central liturgical role; independent Jewish testimony on a debate Aphrahat's text otherwise dominates entirely; and any trace at all of enslaved or lower-status community members. Later steps (Doc_05 Ecological Reconstruction, Doc_09 Story Inventory) should mark, wherever a claim rests on inferring these silenced voices' likely experience rather than their own attested words, that the inference is being made and on what basis — not present reconstructed color as if it were attested.

## 8. Boundary Cases (Bardaisan, Marcion, Mani) — Narrative Summary

Doc_01, Section 5, already reached and recorded the load-bearing determination on Bardaisan of Edessa: excluded as a native founding source (his theology does not confidently clear the Nicene-trajectory floor's resurrection clause), but preserved as a documented, contemporary, contested figure this world's own primary voice (Ephrem) explicitly defines against. This document does not re-litigate that determination; it formalizes it in the Registry (Boundary Status: Excluded; Exclusion Reason: Named Comparandum) and extends the same treatment, on the same evidentiary logic, to Marcion and Mani — both named by Doc_01's Preliminary Forces Identification as real contemporaries Ephrem's own corpus explicitly triangulates against (the Prose Refutations treat all three together). Marcion and Mani are Excluded/Named Comparandum in the Registry for the same reason Bardaisan is: their own systems are not native founding material for this world's voice, but they are exactly the kind of "real, undefeated-in-their-own-time neighbor" this project's own methodology requires a world to name rather than silently omit.

The Odes of Solomon receive a more qualified treatment: because Edessene provenance is only one proposed origin among several genuinely live scholarly positions (Section 2), this document does not assert unqualified Native status. The Registry records the Odes as Native/Contested rather than cleanly Native, at Confidence D, with the qualification carried forward rather than resolved by assertion.

## 9. Forces Lens Applied to Source Ecology (Forces Framework Step 2)

Which sources speak to external forces, and what do the silences reveal? The near-total loss of anything but Ephrem's, Aphrahat's, and the *Liber Graduum*'s corpora from an entire linguistic and geographic zone is itself a survivorship pattern worth naming: what endured is precisely the clerical-ascetic, doctrinally combative material produced by figures embedded in institutions (Ephrem's diaconate and qyama attachment; Aphrahat's synodal role, whatever its exact rank) with a stake in transmission and later liturgical use. Aphrahat's own text is additionally shaped, as Section 3 notes, by active persecution-era pressure — meaning the "Persian side" of this world as reconstructed here is not a neutral cross-section but a defensive, apologetic register produced under duress. On the Roman side, the near-total absence of Edessa's own physical evidence (Section 6), compared to Nisibis's excavated cathedral, means the Roman anchor city's material silence should not be read as absence of a substantial Christian community — Edessa's evidentiary gap is an artifact of a still-inhabited city sitting atop the ancient site, not evidence the community there was thin.

## 10. Confidence Map

Applying the fixed five-level vocabulary (Constitution Article 17) across this world's evidentiary base as a whole: Ephrem's and Aphrahat's own existence, dates (within the ranges given), and core corpora are **Documented**. Their precise biographical details beyond the bare textual record (Ephrem's specific ordination circumstances, Aphrahat's identity and rank) are **Widely Accepted** at best and in several places **Contested** (Aphrahat's episcopal status; see Section 11). The Diatessaron's content is a **Dominant Modern Reconstruction** built from indirect witnesses, with real open disputes (the Dura fragment, original language) at **Contested**. The Odes of Solomon's dating, provenance, and language are **Contested** across the board, per Butts (2022) — this document does not treat any single position as settled. The bnay/bnat qyama's basic existence and character in this period is **Widely Accepted**; its precise organizational structure remains **Contested/Inferential-Thin**. Material culture is **Documented** for the Nisibis baptistery (excavated, dated) and a **Dominant Modern Reconstruction** for the wider five-aisled Nisibis cathedral plan (Kayaalp's own inferential reconstruction from partial remains, not a fully confirmed excavated structure); it is simply absent (not merely low-confidence, but genuinely unavailable) for in-window Edessa. The Persian episcopal succession (Section 11) is **Contested/Inferential-Thin** at the level of exact dates (per the Simeon bar Sabbae redating dispute) though **Widely Accepted** at the level of a genuine multi-see structure existing.

## 11. Resolution of Doc_01's Carried-Forward Items

Doc_01 explicitly left several items open for Doc_02 to develop. This section reports what changed and what remains open, without reopening Doc_01 itself (none of the following overturns Doc_01's actual determinations; several confirm or refine them with better evidence, exactly as Doc_01 anticipated).

- **Sebastian Brock citation (Doc_01, Section 4):** resolved. Brock's actual argument (that Syriac *raza*, "symbol," is bound to *shrara*, "truth," and carries the truth's "hidden power" rather than merely representing it Platonic-allegory-style) is confirmed via two genuine, sourced quotations from *The Luminous Eye* (1992 ed., p. 41) and *Harp of the Spirit* (2013 ed., p. 17). The exact phrase "poetic/symbolic rather than philosophical-categorical" is standard secondary paraphrase of Brock's argument (used by several later scholars), not a verbatim Brock quotation — this document recommends any future citation of this point use the two genuine quotations directly, or cite the paraphrase explicitly as paraphrase, rather than implying it is Brock's own wording.
- **Persian-side institutional development (Doc_01, Section 6, deliberately left open):** substantially developed, though not fully resolved, and this document does not force a resolution Doc_01 itself declined to force. New evidence, drawn from the traditional Church-of-the-East chronicle record (Chronicle of Seert, Bar Hebraeus, as reconstructed by Fiey), not from contemporary attestation: beyond Papa bar Aggai and Simeon bar Sabbae, a fuller Persian episcopal succession is recorded — Shahdost (341–343, martyred), Barba'shmin (343–346, martyred, followed by a roughly twenty-year vacancy in the Catholicosate during the worst of Shapur II's persecution), Tomarsa (363–371, restored the church after the persecution), Qayyoma (377–399), and Isaac (399–410, presided over the 410 Synod). Additional martyred bishops are named in the martyrological record (Milles of Susa, Acepsimas, Mareas, Bicor, alongside roughly twenty other bishops and 250 clergy). Two caveats on this chain, both flagged rather than smoothed over: first, Simeon bar Sabbae's traditional martyrdom date (341, the anchor the rest of the chain's dates are built outward from) is itself actively disputed in current scholarship — Kosiński and Burgess argue for a redating to c. 344 — which would shift every subsequent date in this succession by two to three years; this document reports the traditional dates but flags them as chronicle-derived and hagiographically inflected, not contemporary attestation, and notes the live redating dispute rather than presenting the chain as settled. Second, the title "Catholicos"/"Catholicosate," used throughout the tradition this succession is drawn from, is itself anachronistic before the fifth century — the same kind of internal-anachronism problem Beck identifies in the Ephrem manuscript tradition (Section 3) — and its use here describes the office the tradition later called by that name, not a claim that the title was in contemporary use in the 340s–390s. With those two caveats attached, this still shows a genuinely multi-see Persian episcopal structure existed — more developed than a bare two-bishop (Papa, Simeon) picture suggested — but one severely disrupted by persecution, including a two-decade primatial vacancy. This is a third picture, distinct from both Round 2's overstated "stable parallel hierarchy" (rejected in Doc_01) and a hypothetical "no real structure at all": Persia had bishops, plural, but continuity was genuinely fragile and the precise dating chronicle-dependent. This is consistent with, and reinforces rather than overturns, Doc_01's decision to treat the authority-structure question as unresolved for Strand Determination purposes — an episcopal structure that exists but suffers a twenty-year primatial vacancy under persecution, recorded in a later and partly hagiographic chronicle tradition, is not the kind of stable, confidently-characterized structure Article 21 would need to establish a strand-distinguishing difference either way.
- **Aphrahat's own episcopal status:** still genuinely open. This document's research found the Wright argument (from Demonstration 14's synodal circular letter) is real but narrow, resting on a small, largely nineteenth-century citation web (Wright, George of the Arabs's own express disclaimer of certainty, a fourteenth-century marginal note calling Aphrahat "bishop of Mar Mattai" that is almost certainly anachronistic). No sustained, named 21st-century scholarly debate on this specific question was located. This document flags that any future claim of "a live current scholarly debate" on Aphrahat's episcopal status should be treated with caution; the more accurate characterization is a genuinely unresolved, thinly-argued 19th/early-20th-century question rather than a robust ongoing one.
- **Marutha of Maiperqat's role:** developed. Marutha, Roman bishop of Maiperqat/Martyropolis in Roman Mesopotamia, served as Emperor Arcadius's imperial envoy to Yazdegerd I, reportedly winning royal favor through medical skill; this access enabled the embassy that secured royal permission for Isaac to convene the 410 Synod. Bar Hebraeus credits Marutha with advising on organizing the Church of the East along Roman metropolitan lines; he later took relics of Shapur-II-era martyrs to found Martyropolis. This confirms substantive, sustained Roman-Persian ecclesiastical contact in this world's own final years, not an isolated diplomatic event.
- **373–410 window content:** inventoried, at least in outline. Tomarsa's post-persecution restoration (through 371); Qayyoma's patriarchate (377–399, a period of relative stability under Shapur III and Bahram IV); Yazdegerd I's accession (399) and the Church of the East's first formal state toleration; Isaac's patriarchate (399–410) culminating in the 410 Synod itself; and Marutha's dual embassies bracketing that synod. This document could not locate additional strongly-named individual figures specific to Edessa or Nisibis in this exact window beyond the continued diffusion of Ephrem's own corpus and school tradition — this is named as a genuine gap in what is currently locatable, not filled with invented content.
- **School of Nisibis founding date:** resolved. The commonly-cited 350 founding date cannot be tied to a specific named ancient primary source. Doc_03, Section 3.1, written later, found GEDSH's "Nisibis, School of" entry confirms the School of Nisibis was actually founded c. 489–496 CE — over a century after Ephrem's death, using titles *mhaggyana*, *maqryana*, *mpashshqana*, not "malpana" — which is what allowed Doc_03 to rule out a proposed "malpana of the School of Nisibis" title as chronologically impossible. 350 is not this institution's founding date; c. 489–496 CE, per GEDSH, is.
- **Jacob of Nisibis's death date:** still not resolved, and this document does not claim otherwise. This is a genuine primary-source conflict, not merely a secondary-literature discrepancy, and is a distinct question from the School of Nisibis's founding date above: the *Martyrologium Hieronymianum* implies 338 (during the first Persian siege), while the *Chronicon Paschale* records him defending Nisibis again in 350, which is incompatible with a 338 death. This document recommends both dates continue to be carried as an explicit, unresolved primary-source conflict in any later document, rather than either being silently adopted.
