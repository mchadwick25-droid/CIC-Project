THE TWO OPENS BOTH TURNED OUT TO BE ABOUT THE SAME THING, which was not
what the sweep expected. Both are outside texts in which this world can
watch itself being described from elsewhere, and in both the description
is more interesting than the report of it this world had been living on.

Basil credits his best reading of Genesis 1:2 to a Syrian whose learning
was in the Truth and not in Greek paideia, on the ground that the Syriac
word stands nearer the Hebrew than the Greek does. He does not name him.
This world's syr.story.basil-legend already said the legend of Ephrem
meeting Basil rests on a mistaken identification; it can now show the
unnamed sentence the mistake was made in, and it turns out that the
vendored volume's own Victorian apparatus disagrees with itself about who
the man was - Tillemont for Eusebius of Samosata, the Benedictines for
Ephrem Syrus - while this world's own record, on modern scholarship, says
Eusebius of Emesa. Three candidates and no name in the text. That is a
better thing to hold than any of the three answers.

The Syriac Paradise gives this world its own poet in its own language's
recension, and the divergences from the Greek are exactly where a Syriac
world would want them: two visions of Ephraim's teaching authority that
the Greek line does not carry at all, and a famine exchange whose
crucial line reads "O man, we know that thou art God" where the Greek
reads "a man of God".

FOUR OF THE SIX DECLINATIONS WERE ANSWERED BY READING ONE SENTENCE OF
THIS WORLD'S OWN RECORDS, per the rule desert's sweep proposed. Justin
appears as Tatian's teacher. Origen appears twice inside a caution about
a modern scholar's sympathies. Jerome appears as De Viris, which is
already open in another volume. In none of those four is an author being
used second-hand; in all four the observer is measuring a name in a
sentence that is about something else.

THE OTHER TWO WERE ANSWERED BY SEARCHING THE FILE, and those are the ones
worth recording as findings rather than rulings. anf06 was the sweep's
strongest candidate: Julius Africanus' Kestoi preserves a first-hand
notice of Bardaisan shooting at the court of Abgar, which would be a
primary witness on a question this world has a whole figure record for.
The volume contains no Bardaisan, no Bardesanes and no Abgar. The notice
is not in the ANF's Africanus. npnf214 was the second: this world claims
its own bishop sat at Nicaea and would be glad of the subscription list.
The volume carries neither Jacob nor James of Nisibis. Both declinations
are therefore about what is in a file, and both would be reversed by a
different file rather than by a better argument - which is the useful
kind of not-found, and both belong on a fetch list rather than in a
ruling.
