## 0. Purpose and Scope

Per Step 3 of the Construction Framework, this document's job is narrow and specific: identify candidate vocabulary *before* Gravity Discovery (Doc_04), so that gravities can be tested in this world's own language rather than in imported modern analytical categories. This is explicitly **a candidate list, not a full lexicon** — preliminary definitions only, with full three-level Tier 1 treatment deferred to Step 6 (Doc_06, Full Interpretive Lexicon), which occurs only after Ecological Reconstruction (Doc_05) is complete. Building full lexicon depth now, before the ecology that should inform it exists, would invert the Framework's own sequencing — this document deliberately does not do that.

Per the Framework's own instruction, candidate terms were identified from the **Source Registry's Native entries** (Doc_02's companion spreadsheet), not from Doc_02's narrative text directly — a safeguard against importing terminology tied to material the Registry's own Boundary Check might treat differently than the narrative summary implies. In practice, for this world, Doc_02's Native rows and narrative are closely aligned (nothing in Doc_02's narrative was excluded from the Registry), but the distinction is maintained procedurally rather than assumed.

Every candidate term below was independently verified via live research — not drawn from background knowledge — given this project's own documented history of catching invented or fabricated citation detail at exactly this level of specificity (per Doc_01's and Doc_02's own revision logs). Several claims could not be fully corroborated (a page number, a precise date); these are flagged explicitly rather than smoothed over.

**Strand attribution note:** Doc_01, Section 6, determined this world is strand-singular, with the authority-structure question left deliberately open rather than resolved into two strands. Accordingly, no term below carries a strand split; where a term is more heavily attested through one anchor city or author than the other, that is recorded as an Author Gravity consideration, not a strand distinction.

## 1. Candidate Terms

### 1.1 raza (ܐܪܙܐ) / shrara (truth)

- **Preliminary definition:** "Symbol/mystery/type" in a strong, non-representational sense — the *raza* is bound to the *shrara* (truth/reality) it signifies and conveys that reality's own "hidden power," rather than standing at an arbitrary representational remove from it. A deliberate contrast with Platonic/allegorical symbol-theory.
- **Attestation:** Primarily and most richly developed in **Ephrem**; also genuinely present in **Aphrahat**, chiefly as typological vocabulary for Old Testament persons/events as "types" (*raza*) of Christ (the "truth," *shrara*), without the same elaborated theological-method framing.
- **Author Gravity risk: Moderate-to-high.** The specific "hidden power" (*hayla kasya*) articulation of this term is substantially Brock's own synthesis of Ephrem specifically; Aphrahat's cognate usage is real but less developed, and should not be presented as co-equal attestation.
- **Sources:** Sebastian Brock, *The Luminous Eye* (Cistercian Studies 124, rev. ed. 1992) — two genuine quotations already located and verified in Doc_02, Section 11 (p. 41; and *The Harp of the Spirit*, 2013 ed., p. 17). Robert Murray, *Symbols of Church and Kingdom* (Cambridge, 1975; Gorgias repr. 2004).
- **Preliminary tier estimate: Tier 1.** This is Ephrem's central theological-method category and already named as this world's own doctrinal-method gravity candidate in Doc_01, Section 1 — needs full Tier 1 treatment at Step 6.
- **Note carried forward:** the phrase "poetic/symbolic rather than philosophical-categorical" remains standard secondary paraphrase, not verbatim Brock — continue using the two genuine quotations directly per Doc_02's own finding.

### 1.2 qyama / bnay qyama / bnat qyama

- **Preliminary definition:** A distinctive ascetic order of committed, celibate Syriac Christians — men (*bnay qyama*) and women (*bnat qyama*) — who took a vow ("stood" for the covenant, root *qwm*), typically remaining resident in towns rather than withdrawing to desert monasteries. The pre-monastic/proto-monastic institution of Syriac Christianity in this period.
- **Attestation:** **Aphrahat, Demonstration 6** ("On the Bnay Qyama," including the subsection some scholars call "Aphrahat's Rule," Dem. 6.8) is the direct, contemporary, firsthand primary source. The connection to **Ephrem** is real but runs chiefly through the *bnat qyama* women's-choir tradition, which is attested more directly by *later* sources (Jacob of Serugh's 6th-century panegyric on Ephrem; the 6th-century Syriac *Vita Ephraemi*) than by Ephrem's own surviving hymns using this technical term in so many words.
- **Author Gravity risk: Low for the Aphrahat/Demonstration 6 core; high for the specific claim that Ephrem himself organized or led the bnat qyama choirs**, which is a later hagiographic attribution, not Ephrem's own contemporary self-testimony. Any Tier 1 treatment must keep these two evidentiary tiers separate rather than blending them.
- **Sources:** Susan Ashbrook Harvey, "Revisiting the Daughters of the Covenant," *Hugoye* 8.2 (2005). Malatius Malki Malki, "Revisiting the Institution of Bnay and Bnoth Qyomo in the Syriac Tradition," *Religions* 15(6): 686 (2024). Sidney Griffith, "'Singles' in God's Service: Thoughts on the Ihidaye from the Works of Aphrahat and Ephraem the Syrian," *The Harp* 4 (1991): 145–59, and "Monks, 'Singles,' and the 'Sons of the Covenant'...," in *Eulogema* (Studia Anselmiana 110, 1993): 141–60 — note: *Faith Adoring the Mystery* (1997) is a real Griffith book but is not itself the qyama-specific citation.
- **Preliminary tier estimate: Tier 1.** Richly, independently, multiply-sourced institutional anchor already treated at length in Doc_02, Section 2 and Section 7 (bnat qyama as a missing-voices case). Needs full Tier 1 treatment at Step 6, explicitly separating the Aphrahat-firsthand core from the Ephrem-choir later-tradition layer.

### 1.3 taḥwyāṯā (Demonstrations)

- **Preliminary definition:** Aphrahat's own genre-term (singular *taḥwîṯâ*) underlying the conventional English title "Demonstrations" for his 23 doctrinal/exhortatory treatises, corresponding to the Greek *apodeixis*.
- **Attestation:** Exclusively **Aphrahat**. No evidence of Ephrem using this term. Note: Aphrahat himself also refers to his own works as "Letters" on occasion — *taḥwyāṯā* should not be presented as his sole self-designation.
- **Author Gravity risk: Low-moderate** — solidly Aphrahat-specific and not otherwise contested, but the "Letters" alternate designation should be carried alongside it rather than omitted.
- **Sources:** Jean Parisot, *Patrologia Syriaca* I/1–2 (1894/1907), already the Doc_02/Registry edition of record. Adam Lehto, *The Demonstrations of Aphrahat, the Persian Sage* (Gorgias Press, 2010).
- **Flag carried forward:** a "Valavanolickal 2005" translation date (already in the Registry, row 10) could not be independently reconfirmed in this pass — only a 2011 Gorgias edition was found. This should be reconciled with a direct check of the original Kottayam printing before Step 6, not silently left as-is.
- **Preliminary tier estimate: Tier 2.** Load-bearing as Aphrahat's own genre self-designation, but narrower in ecological reach than raza/shrara or qyama.

### 1.4 madrasha / madrashe (teaching-hymn)

- **Preliminary definition:** Syriac genre of sung, metered, stanzaic poetry with refrains (*ʿonyaṯa*), often acrostic — Ephrem's primary literary vehicle for theological argument.
- **Attestation:** Ephrem's dominant/signature genre, but **not his invention** — the genre pre-existed him, used earlier by Bardaisan (3rd c.) and by Mani, whom Ephrem was partly answering polemically by adopting and mastering their own literary form. Not linked to Aphrahat, whose Demonstrations are prose.
- **Author Gravity risk: Low-moderate.** Accurate to call it "Ephrem's genre" as his dominant vehicle, but its pre-Ephrem origin (Bardaisan, Mani) must be named rather than presenting Ephrem as having invented it — a direct continuity with Doc_01's own finding that Ephrem's rhetoric is partly constructed *against* these same figures (Doc_01, Section 5; Doc_02, Section 3).
- **Sources:** Sebastian Brock, "Ephrem and the Syriac Tradition," in *The Cambridge History of Early Christian Literature*, ed. Young, Ayres, Louth (Cambridge, 2004): 361–372. Jeffrey Wickes, *Bible and Poetry in Late Antique Mesopotamia: Ephrem's Hymns on Faith* (University of California Press, 2019), which deliberately transliterates rather than translates "madrasha" as a distinct genre category.
- **Preliminary tier estimate: Tier 1.** Ephrem's dominant literary genre and directly load-bearing for the "poetic/typological theological method" gravity already named in Doc_01, Section 1.

### 1.5 memra / memre (verse homily)

- **Preliminary definition:** Verse composition in couplets, single syllabic meter, apparently recited rather than sung — distinct from the sung, stanzaic, refrain-structured madrasha.
- **Attestation:** The bare metrical form is genuinely attested within Ephrem's own lifetime, in a small authenticated corpus: per Brock, only the six memre "On Faith" and the memra on the destruction of Nicomedia are "definitely" Ephrem's; "On the Repentance of Nineveh" is "probably genuine (though doubt has been expressed)"; "On the Sinful Woman" is "less certain." **However**, the fully developed, canonical "memra as verse-homily genre" that dominates later Syriac literary discussion is a 5th/6th-century crystallization — per Brock, it is Narsai and Jacob of Serugh who "adapt the narrative poem into a specifically homiletic context, producing the characteristically Syriac genre of the verse homily." Not attested for Aphrahat.
- **Author Gravity risk: Moderate-high for the developed genre category specifically** — heavily dependent on later authors (Narsai, Jacob of Serugh) even though the bare form exists in a handful of authenticated Ephrem texts.
- **Sources:** Same Brock Cambridge History chapter as above. A GEDSH "Memra" entry could not be directly accessed in this pass and remains unverified.
- **Preliminary tier estimate: Tier 3, with an explicit anachronism caveat carried forward.** Present at Step 6 as: narrowly attested for Ephrem himself in a small authenticated corpus, with explicit acknowledgment that the developed genre category postdates this world's own 410 boundary. Do not present memra as an already-settled, named literary category coequal with madrasha within 200–410 CE.

### 1.6 Ewangeliyon da-Mhallete ("Gospel of the Mixed" — the Diatessaron's Syriac name)

- **Preliminary definition:** The vernacular Syriac name (ܐܘܢܓܠܝܘܢ ܕܡܚܠܛܐ) distinguishing Tatian's harmonized Gospel text from the later four-discrete-Gospel ("separated," *da-Mepharreshe*) Syriac version.
- **Attestation — critical dating flag:** The Diatessaron's *content and use* are well attested within 200–410 CE (Aphrahat quotes/uses it; Ephrem wrote a full *Commentary on the Diatessaron*, per Doc_02 Section 2). But the specific vernacular **name** "da-Mhallete" is not independently confirmed as attested within this world's own 200–410 window, and the dating of its *earliest* attestation is itself unsettled, not a clean post-410 anchor point. Theodoret of Cyrrhus (bishop 423–457) describes confiscating "more than two hundred such books" in the 420s–430s, but his own account is in Greek and never actually uses the Syriac phrase "da-Mhallete" — it attests the Diatessaron's suppression, not the vernacular name specifically. Per Matthew Crawford's peer-reviewed work, the name's earliest secure Syriac witness may instead be an anonymous gloss in the Syriac translation of Eusebius's *Ecclesiastical History* — a witness roughly contemporary with, not clearly later than, Theodoret. Ephrem's own *Commentary* never uses "Mixed"/Diatessaron-type terminology at all, referring to the text simply as "the Gospel."
- **Author Gravity risk: N/A — this is a dating risk, not a voice-dependency risk.** The risk is conflating "the Diatessaron was used in this period" (true, well-attested) with "this specific vernacular name was in use in this period" (unconfirmed either way — the evidence does not clearly establish the name as pre-410 OR clearly establish it as definitively post-410; the honest position is unsettled, not "settled post-410").
- **Sources:** William L. Petersen, *Tatian's Diatessaron* (Brill, 1994) — standard reference, though its specific discussion of the Syriac terminology's own dating could not be directly re-verified in this pass. Petersen's "Diatessaron" entry, *Gorgias Encyclopedic Dictionary of the Syriac Heritage* (2011), confirmed to exist but not fully accessible in this pass. Matthew Crawford's peer-reviewed work on the name's earliest secure Syriac witness (Syriac Eusebius gloss) — not independently re-verified against the primary edition; flagged for direct check at Step 6.
- **Preliminary tier estimate: Tier 2, with an explicit dating caveat carried forward.** Any Step 6 treatment should read: "Diatessaron's use/content attested 337–390s CE via Aphrahat and Ephrem; the vernacular name 'da-Mhallete' has no confirmed pre-410 attestation, and its own earliest secure witness (Theodoret's Greek account vs. a possible anonymous Syriac-Eusebius gloss) is itself contested in the scholarship — do not present either as a settled anchor point."

### 1.6a Why this term is retained as a hedged Candidate rather than Excluded

A fair question: malpana and Peshitta were both Excluded (Section 3) specifically because their names are demonstrably absent from the 200–410 record, while Ewangeliyon da-Mhallete is retained here as a Candidate despite its own dating claim surviving fact-checking *less* cleanly than either excluded term's did (malpana and Peshitta both rest on a clean, verbatim-confirmed GEDSH statement of a specific later first-attestation; this term's dating is genuinely unresolved in the scholarship, not cleanly dated to a specific later point at all). The distinction this document draws, on reflection, is: malpana and Peshitta were excluded because independent research **positively confirmed** a specific later first-attestation, displacing any native-period claim. Ewangeliyon da-Mhallete's name-dating is not positively confirmed as either pre-410 or post-410 — the honest state of the evidence is unresolved, not resolved-against. Given the underlying institution/text (the Diatessaron itself) is unambiguously native and centrally important to this world (Doc_01, Section 4; Doc_02, Section 2), this document retains the term as a Candidate with the caveat strengthened above, rather than excluding it on the strength of an uncertainty that cuts both ways. This reasoning should be revisited at Step 6 if further primary-source work resolves the dating question in either direction.

### 1.7 Iḥidaya (ܝܚܝܕܝܐ) — "the Solitary/Single One"

- **Preliminary definition:** A double-duty term (root *yḥd*, "one"): (a) an ascetic/celibate designation for the Syriac church's consecrated elite — "single-minded," undivided in allegiance to God, living communally rather than in physical isolation, explicitly modeled on emulating Christ; and (b) simultaneously the Syriac christological title for Christ as "Only-Begotten" (Greek *monogenes*). In Aphrahat it functions as a near-synonym of *bar/bat qyama*.
- **Attestation: Both.** Aphrahat's Demonstrations (esp. Dem. 6:8, Dem. 7:20) and Ephrem's hymns, both squarely within this world's own 200–410 window and both in the authors' own surviving text — not a later retrojection, unlike the qyama/Ephrem-choir linkage above.
- **Author Gravity risk: Low-moderate.** Genuinely dual-attested; the main risk is over-collapsing it into "qyama" (double-counting the same institutional reality under two headwords) or over-reading later (5th/6th-century) semantic drift toward "monk" back onto the 4th century.
- **Sources:** *Gorgias Encyclopedic Dictionary of the Syriac Heritage*, s.v. "Iḥidaya" (Robert A. Kitchen). Sidney Griffith, "'Singles' in God's Service..." *The Harp* 4 (1991): 145–59. Sebastian Brock, *The Luminous Eye* (1985), pp. 131–141.
- **Preliminary tier estimate: Tier 1.** As strong a candidate as qyama itself — directly textually attested in both primary voices, double-duty (ascetic and christological), and already a standard headword in the field. Should be cross-referenced with, not merged into, the qyama entry at Step 6.

### 1.8 Mar (honorific, "lord/master")

- **Preliminary definition:** Honorific title-prefix (ܡܪܝ, "my lord") for bishops, saints, and revered teachers, parallel to "Saint."
- **Attestation:** Broad general use across Syriac Christianity in this period and beyond — Doc_02 (Section 2) already confirms Aphrahat himself was called "Mar Jacob, the Persian sage" in a 510 CE colophon. No source located pinning down a specific, datable attestation of the compound "Mar Ephrem" within Ephrem's own lifetime (d. 373) or immediately thereafter — this may be a later, veneration-driven convention.
- **Author Gravity risk: Low for the general honorific concept; moderate if a future document asserts contemporaries specifically called Ephrem "Mar Ephrem" during his own lifetime** — that specific claim is unconfirmed and should not be asserted without further check.
- **Sources:** Sebastian Brock, *An Introduction to Syriac Studies*: "Mor (or Mar) is an honorific title used both for bishops and for saints."
- **Preliminary tier estimate: Tier 3 (minor/supporting).** Real and low-controversy as a linguistic fact, but not itself world-organizing; useful mainly as a cross-reference note on other entries (e.g., noting Aphrahat's own attested honorific was "Mar Jacob," not "Mar Aphrahat").

## 2. Terms Flagged for Distortion-Risk-Only Treatment (not ordinary vocabulary candidates)

### 2.1 Catholicos / Catholicosate

Doc_02, Section 11, already determined this title is anachronistic before the fifth century for the Persian church hierarchy — describing the office the later tradition called by that name, not a title in contemporary use in the 340s–390s. Independent verification in this pass sharpens that finding: the early Seleucia-Ctesiphon succession claims, including "Papa bar Aggai took the title Catholicos c. 315," trace at least in part to the *Acts of Mari* (dated by scholarship anywhere from the 6th to as late as the 8th century — not a firmly settled 6th-century date), which retroactively organizes earlier, disorganized local leadership into a tidy succession with anachronistic titles. The characterization that this "ignores the gap of two and a half centuries" is this project's own paraphrase/synthesis of the underlying dating logic (Mari's legendary 1st-century mission versus Papa's actual c. 280 CE attestation), not a direct quotation from J.M. Fiey himself, and should not be cited as though it were his own wording. It is also worth noting that GEDSH's own Papa bar Aggai entry — arguably the most authoritative concise source on this specific succession question — sources its discussion primarily through the *Acts of Miles*, the *Synodicon Orientale*, Bar Ebroyo, and Aphrahat's Demonstration 14, rather than centering the *Acts of Mari* as the primary vector; the *Acts of Mari* is a real and relevant witness to the retrojection pattern, but not GEDSH's own preferred source for it.

This is not a normal vocabulary candidate — it is included here precisely because it is the kind of term likely to slip into later documents' prose as though it were this world's own contemporary self-designation, when it is a retrojection. **Carried forward as a standing distortion-risk flag, not a Tier candidate:** any later document using "Catholicos" for this world's own period must either avoid the term or explicitly flag it as later terminology applied to an earlier office.

## 3. Terms Considered and Excluded (named explicitly, not silently dropped)

Per this project's own transparency discipline (Constitution Article 20's spirit applied to methodology, not just missing voices), the following terms were researched and are explicitly excluded from candidacy, with the reason recorded — rather than simply omitted from this list without explanation.

### 3.1 malpana (teacher)

The claim that Ephrem held the specific title/office "malpana," reportedly appointed as such by Jacob of Nisibis, traces to later biographical/hagiographic and tertiary-source tradition, not to a direct textual attestation within this world's own period. Sebastian Brock's own GEDSH entry on Ephrem never uses the term, calling him instead "Deacon, poet, and theologian," and states his biography "written perhaps 200 years after his death, is full of unreliable information." GEDSH's "Nisibis, School of" entry confirms the School of Nisibis was founded c. 489–496 CE — over a century after Ephrem's death — with its own attested titles being *mhaggyana*, *maqryana*, *mpashshqana*, not "malpana." The "malpana of the School of Nisibis" claim commonly attached to Ephrem is therefore chronologically impossible as stated.

**Excluded from this list's candidacy.** If a future document wants a "teacher" self-vocabulary term for Ephrem specifically, it requires a dedicated primary-text check (Ephrem's own hymns, not tertiary biographical summary) before being reconsidered — this document does not perform that check and does not carry the term forward on the strength of unverified tradition.

### 3.2 Peshitta

Checked specifically because this world's boundary closes at 410 CE and Doc_01 already notes the Diatessaron-to-Peshitta transition happening in the early 5th century, under or after Rabbula (bishop 411–435), with Vööbus's contrary view (Peshitta predates Rabbula) already flagged as Contested in Doc_01. Independent verification confirms the *name* "Peshitta" is first attested with Moses bar Kepha (d. 903) — a roughly 500-year gap from this world's own closing date, a sharper anachronism than Catholicos. The underlying versional tradition existed and was in use by Aphrahat and Ephrem, but the name itself is not native-period vocabulary.

**Excluded from this list's candidacy as a native-vocabulary term.** Later documents should use descriptive language ("the emerging standard Syriac biblical text") rather than "Peshitta" when discussing this world's own period, and should flag the anachronism explicitly if the term appears at all.

### 3.3 Terms investigated but not spun into standalone entries (avoiding padding)

- ***kasyutha* / *hayla kasya*** ("hiddenness"/"hidden power") — genuinely relevant, but functions as a satellite concept explaining how *raza* operates, not an independently attested institution or office. Folded into the raza/shrara entry (1.1) rather than given a separate entry.
- ***qaddishutha*** ("holiness") — genuinely attested in Aphrahat (Dem. 7:20, tied to qyama and virginity), but no dedicated scholarly treatment was located establishing it as a standalone, heavily-textured, world-organizing term distinct from qyama/iḥidaya. Recommend a follow-up primary-text check before any future addition — not included now.
- ***dukrana*** ("commemoration") — a real Syriac liturgical term, but only generic/modern sourcing was found in this pass, not documentation of it functioning as load-bearing vocabulary specifically in Ephrem's or Aphrahat's own corpus. Not included without further primary-text verification.

## 4. Recurring Pattern Flagged for Step 6

Across this candidate list, one pattern recurs and should be watched for deliberately in Full Lexicon Development (Doc_06), not treated as a one-off: **later hagiographic or traditional material silently standing in for contemporary primary attestation.** The Ephrem/bnat-qyama-choir linkage (1.2), the malpana exclusion (3.1), and the Ewangeliyon da-Mhallete dating caveat (1.6) are three independent instances of the same underlying trap — a claim that reads as though it belongs to 200–410 CE actually resting on 5th/6th-century-or-later sources (Jacob of Serugh, the *Vita Ephraemi*, the *Acts of Mari*, Theodoret, Moses bar Kepha). Step 6's builder should treat any term's "native-period attestation" claim with this specific failure mode in mind.
