    text: >-
      Between 312 and 451 CE, the Christian church in Rome, Constantinople,
      and Milan went from a hunted community to a partner of imperial power.
      In 312 the emperor Constantine allied himself with a church he had
      once persecuted. Bishops now gathered in councils an emperor summoned and paid for. The church gained legal
      standing, wealth, and a seat near the throne. It also had to decide
      what that nearness would cost. Three claims competed for who held
      final authority in the church. Rome traced its standing back to the
      apostle Peter. Constantinople claimed rank because the emperor now
      reigned there. And Ambrose of Milan argued that the emperor stood
      inside the church, not above it. For roughly two decades, the
      empire's own church confessed that the Son is like the Father. That
      was the official faith, and bishops who refused it went into exile.
      This story closes at the Council of Chalcedon in 451, which settled
      who Christ is.

  relations_summary:
    text: >-
      This world argued with real rivals, not invented ones. Inside the
      church itself, for roughly two decades, the Homoian confession held
      the imperial throne. It was not a fringe opponent working from
      outside power. It was the establishment. Its rivals had to work
      against it from underneath. In Africa, a separate church called the
      Donatists claimed to be the true church. They held basilicas the
      Catholics claimed as their own. Constantine's own courts ruled
      against them twice. Even so, the emperor paid to build a second
      church, rather than send troops. On the eastern frontier, monks
      burned a meeting-house that belonged to a different Christian
      group, the Valentinians. A mob burned a synagogue on the very same
      day. And outside the church altogether, in 384, Rome's last great
      pagan senator made his own case in writing. He argued that the old
      gods' rites should be restored to the Senate house. This is a rare
      moment. This world's record keeps its own defeated opponent's case,
      in his own words, answered right beside it rather than erased.

    text: >-
      This world's record is richest in one shape: the paper trail of
      office. Council acts, church laws, imperial laws, and letters
      between bishops and emperors carry nearly everything that
      survives. Elsewhere, it runs thin, or falls silent. No ordinary
      believer's own words about any event survive. Only bishops,
      emperors, and their courts speak. Women show up as real political
      powers, not just as obstacles. But they never speak in their own
      words. Even the two who held real, proven power reach us only
      through men with their own stake in the story. And for roughly two
      decades, the empire's own church held a creed whose own fullest
      voice barely survives at all. We know it mostly through the side
      that later beat it.
