## 1. Primary Sources

This world's primary-source base is unusually rich by this project's own standards (Step 0 §3, B1) — its actors are the elite, literate, institutionally central figures whose writing survives best. The following are Native per the Source Registry's Boundary Check; full classification detail (Type, Confidence, Licensed-For target) lives in `Source_Registry.md`, not repeated in full here.

**The initiating alliance:** Eusebius of Caesarea's *Ecclesiastical History* (esp. Books 8–10) and *Life of Constantine* are this world's own foundational textual witnesses to its own beginning — a direct participant's own account, not a later reconstruction. Both carry serious Author Gravity (§2 below): Eusebius is a court panegyrist as much as a historian.

**The Roman/Apostolic-Primacy strand (Doc_01 Strand A):** Julius I's 341 letter to the Eusebian party, defending Rome's authority to review Alexandria's condemnation of Athanasius — preserved only through Athanasius's own quotation of it in his *Apologia contra Arianos* 21–35, and therefore doubly mediated (Julius's own words, filtered through a beneficiary's apologetic use of them) — is this strand's earliest attested data point, a generation before Damasus. Damasus's own epigraphic corpus (the Filocalian-script martyr inscriptions, collected in *Inscriptiones Christianae Urbis Romae*) is strong material-textual evidence of Rome's own self-presentation as guardian of apostolic memory; decretal material circulating under Damasus's name is used far more cautiously (§2, Author Gravity, and the Registry's own Confidence tiering — much of this corpus is of contested authenticity). Leo I's *Tome to Flavian* (Epistula 28) and his subsequent letters rejecting Canon 28 (to Marcian, Pulcheria, and Anatolius) are this strand's own closing self-statement.

**The Constantinopolitan/Imperial-Proximity strand (Doc_01 Strand B):** the Acts and Canons of the Council of Constantinople (381, esp. Canon 3) and of Chalcedon (451, esp. Canon 28) are this strand's own institutional self-assertion, in the conciliar record's own words.

**The Ambrosian/Sacramental-Independence strand (Doc_01 Strand C):** Ambrose's own letters (Epistula 51 on the Thessalonica massacre; the Callinicum-affair correspondence) and his *Sermo contra Auxentium* (preached during the 386 basilica standoff) are first-person accounts of his own confrontations — not neutral reportage (§2). Augustine's *Confessions* 9.7 is used here narrowly and specifically: as eyewitness testimony to Ambrose's introduction of antiphonal psalm-singing during the same standoff, not as evidence of Augustine's own formation, which belongs to World #8. This is a deliberate, disclosed Boundary Check judgment call, not an oversight — see the Registry's own Verification Note on this entry.

**Imperial legislation:** the *Theodosian Code*, Book 16 — especially 16.1.2, the Edict of Thessalonica ("Cunctos populos," 380) — is this world's own distinctive evidentiary layer: state law on religion, available to no other confirmed world in this project's portfolio in comparable depth.

**Connective narrative sources (later, secondary-primary):** Socrates Scholasticus, Sozomen, and Theodoret of Cyrrhus each wrote continuous ecclesiastical histories covering large stretches of this window, working a generation or more after the earliest events they narrate. They are Native and load-bearing — without them, this world's own narrative connective tissue across the Homoian-establishment decades (Constantius II, Valens) would be far thinner — but they are used at Widely Accepted rather than Documented confidence for specific narrative detail, and their own theological allegiances (all three are pro-Nicene, writing after the Nicene settlement had already won) are named as a source of bias, not neutral background (§2, §6).

## 2. Author Gravity Assessment

Per Constitution Article 26 and Construction Framework Part II, every major voice is assessed across all five dimensions: Visibility, Representativeness, Influence, Limitations, and Transmission History. This section covers the world's major primary voices; Narrative Source Author Gravity (for the secondary historians who narrate rather than participate) follows immediately after, per the Framework's own instruction that historians and hagiographers require the same five-dimension treatment.

**Eusebius of Caesarea.**
- *Visibility:* Eusebius is massively over-visible for the world's initiating decade (312–325) — he is a direct participant, an eyewitness at Nicaea, and the single most prolific literary voice of the entire alliance's founding moment.
- *Representativeness:* He is not a representative voice for ordinary Christian experience of the alliance, nor even for the full range of episcopal opinion about it — he represents a specific, court-adjacent, moderate-to-Origenist theological position that itself sits in genuine tension with what becomes Nicene orthodoxy (his own signature at Nicaea came with a hedging letter to his own congregation explaining his reservations about *homoousios* — a real, documented fact, not speculative).
- *Influence:* Enormous and lasting — his framing of Constantine's conversion and the alliance's providential character shapes nearly every later account (Socrates, Sozomen, Rufinus all write in his shadow).
- *Limitations:* A panegyrist writing about his own patron. The *Life of Constantine* in particular is not disinterested biography; its silences (notably its near-total silence on Constantine's execution of his son Crispus and wife Fausta in 326, a documented event Eusebius simply omits) are as informative as its content.
- *Transmission History:* The Greek text survives in a reasonably direct manuscript tradition (no adversarial-quotation problem of the kind affecting Damasus's decretals below), but selection and emphasis within Eusebius's own corpus were shaped by his own court proximity — the transmission problem here is authorial, not scribal.

**Ambrose of Milan.**
- *Visibility:* Over-visible for his own confrontations specifically — Letter 51 and the Callinicum correspondence are self-authored accounts of Ambrose's own conduct, which he had every reason to present favorably.
- *Representativeness:* Represents one bishop's own model of episcopal-imperial relations, not a settled Western consensus at the time — his contemporaries did not uniformly share his willingness to confront an emperor directly.
- *Influence:* Substantial and durable, cited by later Western political theology.
- *Limitations:* Self-reporting of one's own confrontations with power is a genre with an obvious incentive toward self-vindication; corroborating external testimony (Augustine's *Confessions* on the 386 standoff specifically) exists for some but not all of Ambrose's own claims about his conduct.
- *Transmission History:* Preserved through Ambrose's own episcopal archive and later collection; no major adversarial-quotation problem, though later medieval collectors selected and arranged the letters for their own purposes (some collections group them thematically rather than chronologically, affecting how confrontational a reader perceives the corpus to be).

**Leo I.**
- *Visibility:* Over-visible for the Chalcedon moment specifically and for the Roman-primacy claim generally — Leo is this strand's own most articulate self-advocate.
- *Representativeness:* Represents the Roman see's own claim about itself, not a claim the Constantinopolitan strand accepted (Canon 28's passage over Leo's own legate's objection is direct evidence Leo's view was contested even at the council he is remembered for dominating).
- *Influence:* Very high — the Tome to Flavian becomes a Chalcedonian Christological touchstone.
- *Limitations:* A papal document asserting papal authority is not a neutral source on the question of whether that authority was actually, universally recognized at the time — a real risk of the source and the claim it is used to support being the same voice.
- *Transmission History:* Well preserved through the Roman chancery's own record-keeping (part of the "Ongoing/Internal, Transmission dimension" pattern already flagged in Doc_01 Cell 2B — the institutions with the strongest archival culture are disproportionately the ones whose self-presentation survives).

**Damasus (via epigraphy and decretal material).**
- *Visibility:* Highly visible through his own deliberate, large-scale public program (the Filocalian inscriptions were commissioned specifically to be seen, at pilgrimage sites, by ordinary visitors — this is one of the few pieces of evidence in this world's ecology aimed at something other than an elite readership).
- *Representativeness:* Represents Damasus's own institutional project (linking the Roman see to martyr memory as a primacy-strengthening strategy), not necessarily broader Roman Christian sentiment about that project.
- *Influence:* High for the epigraphic program specifically; genuinely uncertain for much of the decretal corpus.
- *Limitations:* Real and specific — a significant portion of material later transmitted "under Damasus's name" is of contested or outright doubtful authenticity (later canonical collections had every incentive to backdate favorable rulings to an authoritative early pope). This document does not use contested decretal material to support any specific claim without flagging it as such in the Registry.
- *Transmission History:* The epigraphic material is directly preserved (stone, or early copies of stone before loss/damage) and carries comparatively low transmission risk. The decretal material's transmission history is precisely the problem — later compilers had a theological and institutional interest in expanding Rome's early authoritative record, which is the single most important Transmission History finding for this strand and is treated as a standing caution throughout this document, not a one-time footnote.

**Narrative Source Author Gravity.** Per the Framework's own instruction, historians and hagiographers who narrate this world rather than participate in it require their own full five-dimension entries, not a collective gesture. A shared structural bias is named once, up front, so it isn't repeated five times: Socrates, Sozomen, Theodoret, and Rufinus all write as continuators or independent historians one to several generations after the earliest events in this world's window, all are committed Nicene partisans writing after the Nicene position had already institutionally prevailed, and all are therefore structurally disposed to narrate the Homoian imperial establishment (§7 below) as a temporary aberration rather than as the actually-functioning imperial church of its own moment — exactly the distortion this world's own required disclosure obligation (§7) exists to correct for.

- **Socrates Scholasticus** (*Ecclesiastical History*, covering 305–439). *Visibility:* high for the whole of this world's mid-window narrative arc — the most-used continuous account for the Constantius II/Valens decades specifically. *Representativeness:* a Constantinopolitan lay lawyer's perspective, not an episcopal one — a genuinely different vantage from Eusebius's court-adjacent one, but still elite and literate. *Influence:* substantial; a primary model for Sozomen's own later, overlapping work. *Limitations:* openly corrects and revises his own earlier edition after further research (a real, documented instance of self-correction, unusual among ancient historians) — a mark of some care, but the shared post-Nicene retrospective bias (above) still applies throughout. *Transmission History:* continuous Greek manuscript tradition, not dependent on adversarial quotation — comparatively favorable relative to the Homoian material in §7.
- **Sozomen** (*Ecclesiastical History*, covering a similar span, with independent material). *Visibility:* high, overlapping with Socrates but not redundant — preserves some monastic and Palestinian material Socrates omits. *Representativeness:* a lawyer with monastic sympathies, writing for imperial (Theodosius II's court) rather than lay audience specifically — a distinct social position from Socrates's own. *Influence:* substantial, read alongside rather than instead of Socrates by later compilers. *Limitations:* more openly panegyrical toward the reigning imperial house than Socrates; the same structural post-Nicene bias applies. *Transmission History:* comparatively favorable, continuous manuscript tradition.
- **Theodoret of Cyrrhus** (*Ecclesiastical History*, with an additional Antiochene theological vantage). *Visibility:* high for the world's own Eastern, Antiochene-adjacent material specifically. *Representativeness:* an actively partisan bishop of the Antiochene theological tradition, not a neutral chronicler — his own later Christological controversies (post-451, outside this world's window) color how he narrates earlier disputes. *Influence:* substantial, particularly for material touching Antiochene figures. *Limitations:* the most openly polemical of the three historians toward Homoian material specifically (§7) — his hostility is a documented editorial stance, not merely inferred. *Transmission History:* comparatively favorable, continuous manuscript tradition for the *Ecclesiastical History* itself, though it should be distinguished from the fate of Theodoret's own later, more directly Christological writings — Theodoret was personally rehabilitated at Chalcedon (451, inside this world's own window) but his anti-Cyrilline works were condemned over a century later (553, well outside this world's window), which affected the complete transmission of that later corpus specifically, not of the *Ecclesiastical History* used here.
- **Rufinus of Aquileia** (Latin continuation of Eusebius's *History*, Books 10–11, extending the narrative to 395). *Visibility:* the primary bridge between Eusebius's own history (ending 325) and the later 4th century for Latin-reading audiences specifically — without Rufinus, this world's own Western narrative connective tissue for 325–395 would rest almost entirely on later, non-continuous sources. *Representativeness:* a Western monastic and translator's perspective, once a close associate of Jerome before their well-documented later estrangement (a fact relevant to how his own editorial choices have sometimes been read by later scholarship, though this document does not develop that further). *Influence:* significant as the standard Latin continuation for centuries. *Limitations:* translates and adapts rather than simply continues — his own additions to Eusebius's material are not always clearly distinguished from strict continuation, a real transmission-adjacent problem in its own right. *Transmission History:* continuous Latin manuscript tradition; the boundary between "Eusebius continued" and "Rufinus's own composition" is the specific point requiring care when this source is used.
- **Paulinus of Milan** (*Vita Ambrosii*, hagiography rather than history, written at Augustine's request some fifteen to twenty years after Ambrose's death (c. 412–413, per mainstream dating)). *Visibility:* the primary continuous narrative account of Ambrose's life as a whole — no comparably complete alternative exists. *Representativeness:* Paulinus served as Ambrose's own secretary (notarius) — a genuine insider's perspective, but one with an obvious personal and institutional stake in Ambrose's posthumous reputation. *Influence:* substantial; the direct source for later medieval Ambrose legend, including the bee-swarm topos (§4). *Limitations:* openly hagiographic in genre and purpose, written to edify rather than to chronicle neutrally — legendary material (§4) is not clearly marked off from documented biography within the text itself, which is why this document treats it at Tier 3 confidence rather than as corroborating evidence for anything not independently attested elsewhere (e.g., in Ambrose's own letters or Augustine's *Confessions*). *Transmission History:* reasonably direct, given its closeness to Ambrose's own circle, but the hagiographic genre itself is the dominant distorting factor, not scribal transmission.

## 4. Formation Narrative Sources

This world's own narrative/legendary material is thinner than its documentary record, and what exists skews toward founder-legend rather than communal memory:

- **Constantine's conversion narrative** exists in two materially different early versions — Eusebius's own account (a vision before the Milvian Bridge, reported at second hand from Constantine himself, appearing only in the *Life of Constantine*, written after Constantine's death) and Lactantius's earlier, differently-detailed account (*De Mortibus Persecutorum* 44, a dream the night before battle). The two do not agree in specifics. This divergence is named here explicitly, per this world's own Contested-confidence discipline, rather than harmonized into a single clean story — a live, unresolved case of exactly the kind Article 17 requires be carried at full strength.
- **Ambrose's childhood legend** (a swarm of bees settling on the infant Ambrose's mouth without harming him, portending his future eloquence) is preserved in Paulinus of Milan's *Vita Ambrosii*, written at Augustine's request some fifteen to twenty years after Ambrose's death (c. 412–413, per mainstream dating — corrected from this document's earlier, imprecise "shortly after"). Tier 3 (Attributed Tradition) material at best — a hagiographic topos (the same motif recurs in other late-antique saints' lives) rather than a documented biographical event, and used here, if at all, only with that tier and confidence marked.
- **Damasus's own martyr-cult program** (§2 above) functions as formation narrative in its own right, not merely as evidence about Damasus — it is itself an act of narrative construction, actively shaping how Roman Christians of the later 4th century understood their own community's relationship to its martyred past. This is a genuine formation-narrative source, not merely a primary-source data point, and is flagged for cross-reference at Doc_09 (Story Inventory).

## 5. Material Culture and Daily Life Sources

- **The Damasan inscriptions** (§1–2, §4 above) — physical, still-extant (in whole or in early copy) epigraphic evidence, among the strongest material evidence available to any world in this project's portfolio for a specific institutional program's actual public reception strategy.
- **Old St. Peter's Basilica**, begun under Constantine — archaeological and architectural evidence of the alliance's material, monumental expression, independent of any textual claim about it.
- **The Milan basilicas** (the Basilica Nova/Basilica Vetus complex and, at issue in 386, the Basilica Portiana) — architectural and archaeological evidence directly relevant to the Strand C standoff, corroborating (independent of Ambrose's own and Augustine's textual accounts) that a real, specific, contested physical space was at stake, not an abstraction.
- **Coinage:** Constantine's adoption of the chi-rho/labarum standard, visible on surviving numismatic evidence (notably a well-known solidus type depicting Constantine's upward gaze), is independent, non-textual, contemporary evidence of the alliance's public visual signaling — the category of evidence is well-established (Confidence B, per this build's own knowledge, not independently re-verified against a specific catalogued coin this session), though its *interpretation* (what Constantine himself believed it meant) remains properly in the Contested range, an important distinction this document maintains rather than collapses.

## 6. Source Asymmetries and Missing Voices

Per Step 0 §4.2 (binding on this document): this world's evidentiary strength and its subject matter select unusually strongly for exactly the skew Article 20 names project-wide — elite, literate, male, urban. Named specifically for this world, not merely gestured at via the general portfolio caveat. **A constitutional routing note, stated once here rather than left implicit:** Article 20's own affirmative duty is explicitly scoped to voices "marginalized within the community" and explicitly does not extend to "the community's external opponents, those it anathematized or combated" — that case is governed instead by the Writing-From-Inside Principle, Article 23, and the Constitution names these as distinct obligations that "must not be conflated." The bullets below sort accordingly: the first two are genuinely internal-marginalization cases (Article 20); Homoian Christianity, treated in §7, is this world's own anathematized doctrinal opponent and is therefore an Article 23 matter, not an Article 20 one, even though the practical posture (name the absence honestly, do not fabricate an interior voice) looks similar in both cases; the Jewish-communities item is neither — Jewish communities were never part of this world's own Christian community to begin with, so they fit cleanly under neither Article as strictly worded, and are named here as an honest boundary case rather than forced under either.

- **Elite and institutional exclusivity is not incidental here — it is close to total (Article 20).** Every Native primary source in §1 is authored by, or directly concerns the conduct of, a bishop, an emperor, or an imperial court. This world has no equivalent to, say, Desert Monasticism's ascetic sayings-literature or Post-Apostolic Christianity's community letters — genres that, whatever their own limitations, at least gesture toward a wider circle of participants. There is no comparably-attested "ordinary Roman Christian's" perspective on the Constantinian alliance, on Damasus's building program, or on Chalcedon anywhere in this world's own Native source base.
- **Gender (Article 20):** almost entirely male-authored and male-focused, with one significant, worth-naming exception rather than a uniform blank: Justina (regent for Valentinian II) is a documented political actor in the 386 Milan standoff, not merely a name — she is the standoff's other principal, acting from a Homoian theological commitment of her own, not merely as an obstacle in Ambrose's story. This world's own record, however, preserves her almost entirely through Ambrose's and Augustine's hostile framing; her own voice, on her own terms, is not independently attested. Similarly, Pulcheria's documented role in convening and shaping Chalcedon (451) is a second real instance of female political agency this world's record does preserve — again mediated through sources with their own stake in how her role is remembered, not through anything approaching her own testimony.
- **Homoian Christians' own voice (Article 23, not Article 20 — see routing note above):** treated at length in §7 below, as the disclosure obligation requires, rather than folded into this general section — the asymmetry here is severe enough, and specifically required to be addressed, that it needs its own treatment.

## 7. Required disclosure: Homoian ("Arian") Christianity, recentered

Per Step 0 §4.1 and Doc_01 §5 (Cell 2A), discharged here in full, not as a footnote to an already-settled Nicene victory.

**What Homoian Christianity actually was, stated plainly and without caricature.** Homoian theology is not "Arianism" in any simple sense — the label itself is a polemical simplification this document does not adopt uncritically. Homoian theologians deliberately avoided both *homoousios* ("of one substance," the Nicene formula) and *heteroousios* ("of a different substance," associated with the more radical Anomoian position) as unscriptural, metaphysically overreaching vocabulary, preferring instead to confess the Son as *homoios* — "like" the Father, per the scriptures, without further ontological specification. This was a coherent, considered theological position with its own internal logic and its own claim to biblical fidelity, not a placeholder or a simple denial of Christ's divinity (Hanson, *The Search for the Christian Doctrine of God*, is the standard modern treatment establishing this).

**It was, for real and substantial stretches of this exact world's own temporal window, the imperial church itself.** Constantius II became sole emperor in 353 (after defeating the usurper Magnentius, three years after his brother Constans's death in 350) and held that position until 361; across this reign Homoian formulae were pressed toward empire-wide acceptance, with the "Dated Creed" — the Fourth Sirmian formula, dated 22 May 359 — put before the twin councils of Rimini (West) and Seleucia (East) that same year, and a Homoian creed subscribed at Constantinople in 360. Under Valens (Eastern emperor, 364–378), Homoian Christianity was again the enforced imperial position in the East, and pro-Nicene bishops — including figures central to World #5's own Cappadocian tradition — were exiled or harassed under his authority. This is not a minor or contested historical point; it is Documented at the highest confidence this project's vocabulary allows. For a combined span of roughly two decades within this world's own 312–451 window, "the Roman imperial church" and "Homoian Christianity" were, at the level of state-recognized establishment, the same thing.

**Direct relevance to this world's own three strands.** Ambrose's own 386 confrontation (Doc_01 Strand C) was not a confrontation with paganism or with a marginal heresy — it was a direct confrontation with a still-functioning Homoian imperial court (Justina, regent for the young Valentinian II), demanding a Milan basilica for Homoian worship, within living memory of Homoian Christianity's own recent imperial establishment. Reading this confrontation as Ambrose defending settled orthodoxy against a fringe position misreads the actual balance of power at the moment it happened — Williams (1995) is the standard corrective here, and this document follows it rather than the more triumphalist framing available in, e.g., Paulinus's hagiographic *Vita Ambrosii*. Eusebius's own theological position (§2 above, his hedged subscription to Nicaea) sits closer to later Homoian-adjacent moderate positions than to the fully Nicene-Constantinopolitan settlement his own court theology (Strand B's seed) helps enable — a genuine complexity in the world's own initiating figure, not resolved by later hindsight into a cleaner story than the sources actually support.

**Source-asymmetry specific to Homoian Christianity, named rather than worked around.** This world's own narrative record of Homoian Christianity survives almost entirely through hostile, post-facto Nicene transmission — Socrates, Sozomen, Theodoret (§1, §2 above), all writing after the Nicene settlement had already prevailed. The one substantial piece of near-primary Homoian *self*-testimony available to this world's own Registry is not itself Nicene-transmitted: Auxentius of Durostorum's letter concerning Ulfila survives embedded within the *Dissertatio Maximini contra Ambrosium* — the work of Maximinus, a Homoian bishop, written *against* Ambrose (the same Maximinus who later debated Augustine, c. 427–428) — not within any hostile Nicene work. It survives because a fellow Homoian preserved it, inside a Homoian dossier, alongside Arian scholia on the Council of Aquileia, in a single Paris manuscript (Paris lat. 8907) — and even there, only as an erased, expunged lower layer of a manuscript — the Arian material was scraped out to suppress it as heretical content, not merely reused for ordinary economy of parchment — recovered only by modern paleographic work. The honest point this makes is sharper than a simple "hostile transmission" framing: Homoian self-testimony was precarious even in its *own* transmission, surviving this world's own window only as a single physically-erased trace within a text written by the losing side itself — not merely filtered through the winning side's later account, but nearly erased outright even where the losing side tried to preserve its own record. Confidence remains C, flagged accordingly in the Registry, and this material is still used here only to establish that a Homoian self-account exists at all and confirms Homoian theology's own considered, scripturally-grounded self-understanding — not to support any specific vivid claim beyond that.

## 8. Confidence Map
