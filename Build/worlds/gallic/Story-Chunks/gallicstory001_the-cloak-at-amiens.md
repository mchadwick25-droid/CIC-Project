## Story Text

This is how the tradition of Tours remembers the beginning of Martin — what it believed a life already turned toward God could look like inside a soldier's uniform, before any monastery existed in Gaul.

Sulpitius tells that Martin, the son of a military tribune, was seized on his father's information and put in chains at fifteen and made to take the military oath, and that even then he kept only one servant, and served that servant — drew off his boots and cleaned them with his own hand, and at their meals "the real master … generally acting the part of servant." He was, Sulpitius says, "regarded not so much as being a soldier as a monk." He was not yet baptized; he was a catechumen, and out of his pay he kept back only what he needed for the day, giving the rest away.

Then one winter — "a winter which had shown itself more severe than ordinary, so that the extreme cold was proving fatal to many" — Martin, "when he had nothing except his arms and his simple military dress," met at the gate of the city of Amiens a poor man with no clothes at all, begging from everyone who passed, and everyone passed. Martin "recognized that a being to whom others showed no pity, was, in that respect, left to him." He had nothing but the cloak he was wearing; the rest of his garments he had already given away in the same manner. So "taking … his sword with which he was girt, he divided his cloak into two equal parts, and gave one part to the poor man, while he again clothed himself with the remainder." Some of the bystanders laughed at the half-dressed soldier. Others, "of sounder understanding, groaned deeply because they themselves had done nothing similar," having more than Martin had and needing to strip no one to give.

That night, the tradition says, Martin saw Christ in his sleep, "arrayed in that part of his cloak with which he had clothed the poor man," and heard him say to the angels around him: "Martin, who is still but a catechumen, clothed me with this robe." The Lord who had said "Inasmuch as ye have done these things to one of the least of these, ye have done them unto me" showed himself in the beggar's half of a soldier's cloak. And this is the point the tradition presses: Martin "was not puffed up with human glory" by it; he went and was baptized, at twenty, and — because his tribune had promised to leave the world with him when his term was up — went on for nearly two more years wearing the uniform "although but in name."

## Formation Ecology Connection

This story is the tradition's own picture of **G8 — the soldier of Christ** (Supporting, cross-node) before the phrase is ever spoken: the soldier's sword is turned to an act of charity, the soldier's cloak becomes the beggar's, and the man is "regarded not so much as being a soldier as a monk" while still in the army. It is the story the discharge scene (gallicstory002) presupposes — Doc_08 Force 1A-2 names the late-Roman army as "the 'before' of every founding biography," and this is the Tours before in its own words.

It is also this world's earliest instance of **G5 — formation by named example** (Supporting, cross-node): Sulpitius writes so that readers "shall be roused" (*Vita* ch. I, Doc_04 G5), and the bystanders who "groaned deeply because they themselves had done nothing similar" are that rousing already happening inside the story. Its vision is an instance of **G6 — *virtus*** (Tensional, node-bound northern) in its earliest, gentlest form — not power exercised by the saint, but Christ's own attestation of him, which is what the tradition takes to make him worth imitating.

What it shows that a lexicon entry cannot: the world's founding charity is a *material* act (Doc_07 §2G — "objects carry power outward" at Tours) performed by a catechumen with a sword, and the tradition insists on the humility that follows it rather than on the wonder. A participant asking "what did formation look like before there were monasteries in Gaul" gets this story, and gets nothing comparable from the south.

## Tier Justification

Tier 3 — Attributed Tradition — is the honest classification, and it is worth stating why this story does not rise to Tier 1 even though its author is the same named, socially located, datable Sulpitius who anchors five Tier 1 stories in this repository. The governing text (CF V7.4) names "hagiographic narrative" as "a specific type within this tier," and states plainly of Tier 3 generally: "The formation ideal communicated is credible evidence; the specific events claimed are not" (corrected, Round 1 review finding H1 — the prior draft quoted a paraphrase from a sibling world's own Doc_09 as if it were the Framework's own wording). Every marker is present here: an idealized portrait of a saint's youth (the master who serves his servant; the soldier who is really a monk), a Christophany with angels, and an explicit Gospel proof-text supplied by the narrator to interpret the vision. Sulpitius did not witness any of this — he met Martin decades later (*Vita* ch. XXV: "I obtained my information partly from himself … and partly from those who had lived with him") — and the events are set in Martin's youth, decades before he wrote (the text gives no year; Sulpitius's "Julian Cæsar" in the next chapter is the only chronological anchor, and its identification is Roberts's editorial note). The tier's own confidence rule applies exactly: Contested for the portrait (that Martin was a charitable soldier-catechumen is the whole tradition's claim about him, corroborated by no independent witness inside this world's Native corpus), Inferential/Thin for the specific event and the dream.

Genuine uncertainty at construction, recorded rather than resolved: the *bare* claim that a soldier named Martin shared his cloak with a beggar is not miraculous and is the kind of thing a Tier 1 caveat could carry. What tips this story to Tier 3 is that Sulpitius himself makes the vision, not the act, the point — the act is confirmed *by* the Christophany — so that separating a "documented" cloak from a "hagiographic" vision would cut the story against its own author's grain. The tier is assigned to the story as told, not to a fragment of it.

## Usage Guidance

The Representative may offer this story as the tradition's own account of what a formed life looks like at its very beginning, with the hagiographic character made explicit: "This is how Tours remembers the young Martin — what it believed a soldier already given to God could do with a sword and a cloak." The Representative should not present the vision of Christ, or the exact exchange with the angels, as verified historical events; they are the tradition's own testimony to what it believed the act meant. The formation ideal — charity that leaves the giver half-naked, followed by humility rather than glory — is what the Representative carries.

Additional guidance specific to this story: if a participant knows this story from later art or from the medieval cult of the *cappa* (the "chapel"), the Representative should not confirm any of that later material — it lies outside this world's window and is not in this world's evidence. Sulpitius's text says nothing of a relic; it says Martin was baptized and stayed a soldier in name for two more years. If the participant asks whether the south told stories like this, the honest answer is that Cassian declined on principle to "weave a tale of God's miracles and signs" (*Inst.* Preface, Doc_04 G6), and no comparable vision-story is told of any Marseilles or Lérins figure in this world's Native corpus.
