## Story Text

Among the examples of the Egyptian fathers that Cassian set down for the monks of Gaul is this one, of two old men and forty years — received into Gallic cells as Egypt's own saying, not this world's own invention.

"When the aged John, who was superior of a large monastery and of a quantity of brethren, had come to visit the aged Pæsius, who was living in a vast desert, and had been asked of him as of a very old friend, what he had done in all the forty years in which he had been separated from him and had scarcely ever been disturbed in his solitude by the brethren: 'Never,' said he, 'has the sun seen me eating,' 'nor me angry,' said the other."

That is the whole of it as the tradition keeps it: the abbot of the many, asked by the hermit of the desert what forty years had yielded, answers with a fast; the hermit, asked the same, answers with a heart.

## Tier Justification

Tier 2 — Collected and Traditional Material. Cassian transmits this as one of a series of examples of the elders (*Inst.* V.24–28), naming the two abbots but not claiming to have been present at their exchange or to have heard it from either; it has the shape of an apophthegm — a remembered saying attached to named figures — which is exactly the material the governing text (CF V7.4) describes as "transmitted in collected form with identifiable collection history … traceable to the world's own community memory," where "academic consensus treats the tradition as authentic even where individual attribution or detail cannot be verified" (corrected, finding H1 — the prior draft quoted a sibling world's own paraphrase as if it were the Framework's own wording). The collection history is Cassian's *Institutes*, gathered from Egypt for a Gallic house; the community-memory link is the same as for every received southern story — Gallic monks received the fathers "into their cells" (*Conf.* Pref. III). Gibson's editorial note that Pæsius cannot securely be identified outside Cassian confirms the attribution's uncertainty without touching the tradition's authenticity.

Why not Tier 1: no eyewitness claim and no date — the adjacent examples in the same book (V.25, "one of the elders was pressing me to eat") *are* first-person, and are named in the main document's Candidates Considered as the reason this distinction was drawn text by text rather than by book. Why not Tier 3: it is not a hagiographic portrait of a saint's whole life but a two-line saying. Confidence: Widely Accepted for the tradition; Contested for the attribution.

Reception fact: Egyptian saying; Gallic reception; nothing here is Gaul's own except that Gaul was formed by reading it.

## Usage Guidance

The Representative may draw on this story as part of the community's living memory: "They say of two old men, Pæsius and John, as Cassian handed it on, that …" The Representative should not claim the exchange as a verified historical conversation; what is carried is the tradition's memory and the ranking it teaches.

Additional guidance specific to this story: name the origin — Egypt's fathers, received at Marseilles. If a participant asks which answer the tradition preferred, the Representative should say honestly that Cassian records both without comment in this chapter, and that the ranking of the heart over the fast is stated elsewhere in the same corpus (*Conf.* I.7; *Inst.* V.21 on anger as the soul's food); the story's economy is its own point. If asked whether Martin said anything like this, the honest answer is that Tours records Martin's fasts and "whole nights" of watching (*Vita* ch. XXVI; *Ep.* III) but no saying of this shape; the north's teaching is the master's example seen, not the elder's word remembered (Doc_05 §6A).
