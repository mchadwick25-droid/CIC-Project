## Story Text

Opening the second part of his *Conferences* — the part he dedicated to the "holy brothers" Honoratus and Eucherius, one of them "presiding as he does over a large monastery of the brethren" — Cassian records his own arrival, with Germanus, at Thennesus in Egypt, and the bishop God sent to meet them.

"God gratified our wishes," Cassian writes, "and had brought about the arrival of that most blessed and excellent man Bishop Archebius, who had been carried off from the assembly of anchorites and given as Bishop to the town of Panephysis." Archebius, Cassian says, "kept all his life long to his purpose of solitude with such strictness that he relaxed nothing of the character of his former humility, nor flattered himself on the honour that had been added to him" — for he "vowed that he had not been summoned to that office as fit for it, but complained that he had been expelled from the monastic system as unworthy of it because though he had spent thirty-seven years in it he had never been able to arrive at the purity so high a profession demands."

The business that had brought him to Thennesus was the election of another bishop. He received the two travellers "kindly and most graciously," and when he heard that they wished to seek out the holy fathers in still remoter parts of Egypt he said: "Come," said he, "see in the meanwhile the old men who live not far from our monastery, the length of whose service is shown by their bent bodies, as their holiness shines forth in their appearance, so that even the mere sight of them will give a great lesson to those who see them: and from them you can learn not so much by their words as by the actual example of their holy life, what I grieve that I have lost, and having lost cannot give to you. But I think that my poverty will be somewhat lessened by this zeal of mine, if when you are seeking that pearl of the Gospel which I have not, I at least provide where you can conveniently procure it." (Corrected, finding L1 — "said he" restored; the prior draft silently dropped it without an ellipsis.) And he took his staff and scrip, Cassian says, "as is there the custom for all monks starting on a journey," and led them himself.

## Formation Ecology Connection

This is the one Egyptian bishop Cassian chose to carry west, and Doc_04 G1 names him as "the Egyptian counter-case" of **G1 — the monk-bishop** (Primary, cross-node): a monk seized for a see, who counts the seizure a loss. Doc_07 §2C sets him beside Martin's guard and Honoratus's fillet — the "capture" shape every house's founding story shares — and Doc_08 Force 1B-1 cites him as the founding conviction's own Egyptian witness. He carries **G9 — ambivalence toward episcopal authority** (Tensional) in its Egyptian valence — the office as expulsion "from the monastic system" — the valence the [PV] tag on lexicon 013 keeps audible. And he carries **G5 — formation by named example**: the old men whose "mere sight … will give a great lesson," from whom one learns "not so much by their words as by the actual example of their holy life."

Why this story belongs here and how: Cassian placed it at the head of the part of his work he addressed to an abbot who, between this dedication and the next, himself became "a Bishop blessed in name as well as merits" (*Conf.* Pref. III, Doc_04 G1). The Gallic reader for whom Archebius was written was Honoratus — the story's afterlife is gallicstory014. What it does that no lexicon entry can: it shows a bishop, in his own reported words, treating his office as a poverty and pointing his visitors past himself to the elders he has "lost."

## Tier Justification

Tier 1 — Documented Historical Narrative — and the case is made rather than assumed, because this is the first of two southern stories this repository classifies above Tier 2, and the reception discipline this build applies to Cassian's Egyptian content must be squared with it. The governing criteria: direct textual attestation within the world's horizon (the *Conferences*, Part II, not later than 426 — Doc_01 §2.3); a named author with identifiable social location (Cassian, founder of the two Marseilles houses, writing to named Gallic recipients); datable with reasonable confidence; and a historically credible narrative claim — Cassian and Germanus did travel in Egypt, Panephysis and Thennesus are real places Cassian describes at length (*Conf.* XI.3, re-read), and a former anchorite serving as a town's bishop is an ordinary fact of the Egyptian church. Decisively, this is not received teaching transmitted from an unnamed elder: it is Cassian's own eyewitness report — "when we arrived there," "he … received us kindly" — of a man he met.

The caveats Tier 1 requires: the *Conferences* were composed in Gaul decades after the journey (the interval is not stated in this world's own documents and is not computed here), in a literary form whose verbatim speeches are reconstructions; Archebius's words are Cassian's rendering of what he remembered a man saying; no witness to Archebius survives outside Cassian (Gibson's note, editorial). Widely Accepted rather than Documented for that reason. Genuine uncertainty at construction, recorded: a reviewer could argue that the decades-long gap and the reported-speech form make this Tier 2 (Cassian's own collection of his own memories). The distinction this repository draws — and applies text by text, not book by book — is that Tier 2's own condition (CF V7.4: "academic consensus treats the tradition as authentic even where individual attribution or detail cannot be verified," corrected, finding H1) does not describe a named author's account of his own encounter with a named man; the uncertainty here is the ordinary uncertainty of memoir, which Tier 1's genre caveat exists to carry.

Reception fact, stated as this build requires: the event is Egyptian and the bishop is Egyptian; what is this world's own is that Cassian chose to open the Lérins-dedicated part of his book with him, and that Honoratus read it (Doc_04 G1; Doc_05 §2.5(c)).

## Usage Guidance

The Representative may draw on this story as remembered history, with Cassian named as its author and eyewitness: "Cassian tells that when he and Germanus reached Thennesus, Bishop Archebius — who had been carried off from the anchorites — received them and said …" Archebius's words may be offered as Cassian's memory of them; the Representative should not claim more precision than a memoir written decades later supports, and should say so if asked.

Additional guidance specific to this story: name the origin — an Egyptian bishop, in Cassian's Egyptian journey, written for Gaul. If a participant asks whether Gallic monks felt about their bishoprics as Archebius did, the Representative should point to what this world's own Lérins narrative says of Honoratus (gallicstory014: "he who had refused to go to the dignity — the dignity came to him") and to what Tours says of Martin (gallicstory004), while stating that the shared shape is this build's finding (Doc_07 §2C), not the world's own comparison — the nodes never cite each other. If asked whether Archebius contradicts Cassian's teaching that wanting office is vainglory, the honest answer is that he is its illustration: he did not want it, and counted it a loss.
