## Story Text

This is how the tradition of Tours remembers the first of Martin's *virtutes* — what it believed a man given wholly to God could do, and what it believed that doing made him.

"As Hilarius had already gone away, so Martin followed in his footsteps," and Martin "established for himself a monastery not far from the town." A catechumen joined him there, "desirous of becoming instructed in the doctrines and habits of the most holy man." Within a few days the catechumen fell into a fever; Martin was away for three days; and when he came back he found that "life had departed from the catechumen; and so suddenly had death occurred, that he had left this world without receiving baptism." The body was already laid out, and the brethren were mourning over it, "when Martin hurries up to them with tears and lamentations."

Then — the tradition's own words — "laying hold, as it were, of the Holy Spirit, with the whole powers of his mind, he orders the others to quit the cell in which the body was lying; and bolting the door, he stretches himself at full length on the dead limbs of the departed brother." He prayed. And "perceiving by means of the Spirit of God that power was present, he then rose up for a little, and gazing on the countenance of the deceased, he waited without misgiving for the result of his prayer and of the mercy of the Lord." Scarcely two hours passed before the dead man began to move, and to tremble, and to open his eyes. Martin cried out his thanks so loudly that the brethren at the door rushed in and "beheld the man alive whom they had formerly left dead." The catechumen was baptized at once and lived many years, and — the tradition is careful to say this — "he was the first who offered himself to us both as a subject that had experienced the virtues of Martin, and as a witness to their existence."

The man himself used to tell what had happened to him: that when he left the body he was brought before the tribunal of the Judge and assigned "to gloomy regions and vulgar crowds," and that two angels of the Judge said this was the man for whom Martin was praying, and he was ordered back by the same angels and "given up to Martin." From that day, the tradition says, Martin's name was illustrious, "so that, as being reckoned holy by all, he was also deemed powerful and truly apostolical."

## Formation Ecology Connection

This is **G6 — *virtus*: the saint as bearer of power** (Tensional, node-bound northern) at its founding instance — the episode Doc_04 G6 cites first, with Roberts's *adesse virtutem*, and the one Doc_07 §2I builds the "not I" grammar on for the north ("power is 'present,' not owned"). What the story shows that Doc_05 §5 and lexicon 010 cannot show as directly: the world's own theory of *how* the power operates — it is perceived as *present* before it is used, the door is bolted, the body is lain upon, and the result is waited for "without misgiving." Doc_05 §8 names the north's affective economy as "wonder, then imitation"; this is the wonder in its first form, and the tradition's own insistence that the catechumen "offered himself … as a witness" is G5's eyewitness principle inside the story.

## Tier Justification

Tier 3 — Attributed Tradition — by genuine genre analysis, not by author alone. Sulpitius is the same named, socially located, datable author who anchors this repository's Tier 1 Tours stories, and he is closer to this event than to the discharge (the catechumen "lived for many years afterwards" and "offered himself to us" as a witness — Sulpitius claims the man as a source). But the governing text (CF V7.4) names "the miracle sequence" as part of "recognizable hagiographic conventions" and states of Tier 3 generally: "The formation ideal communicated is credible evidence; the specific events claimed are not" (corrected, finding H1 — the prior draft quoted a sibling world's own paraphrase as if it were the Framework's own wording), and the core narrative claim here is a resurrection followed by the resurrected man's own report of a heavenly tribunal and two angels. There is no non-miraculous residue that could be carried as a Tier 1 narrative with the wonder caveated — the story *is* the wonder. Doc_04 G6 is explicit that "the historicity of the miracles is not what this gravity asserts and is not rated," and lexicon 010 carries Reported-Experience Status for the same reason; a story chunk on the same material belongs at the tier whose register keeps the formational and historical axes apart.

Confidence per the tier's own rule: Contested for the portrait — that Martin was known at Tours as a bearer of power is corroborated inside this world's corpus by Gennadius (ch. XIX, ancient text: "a man famous for signs and wonders and virtues," Doc_04 G6); Inferential/Thin for the specific event. The tier is assigned to the story as its own author tells it. Genuine uncertainty at construction: whether Sulpitius's naming of a living witness should pull this toward Tier 1. It should not — the witness is named only as "a certain catechumen," and the eyewitness claim is itself the hagiographic convention Doc_04 G5 identifies (a "convention risk" shared with the *Vita Antonii*, row 34, Named Comparandum, not drawn on). It is carried here as the tradition's own guarantee, which is what Tier 3 is for.

## Usage Guidance

The Representative may offer this story as the tradition's own account of what a formed life produces, with the hagiographic character made explicit: "This is how Tours remembers the first of Martin's powers — what it believed the Spirit of God could do through a man who had given everything." The resurrection, the tribunal, and the angels are not offered as historical fact; the formation ideal — power perceived as present and not one's own, used behind a bolted door, attested by the man it was used on — is what the Representative carries.

Additional guidance specific to this story: the Representative must not let this story speak for the south. Cassian's Egyptian fathers held that "our predecessors never reckoned those as good monks or free from the fault of vainglory, who professed themselves exorcists among men" (*Conf.* XV.7, Doc_04 G6), and Cassian himself refused to "weave a tale of God's miracles and signs" (*Inst.* Preface). A participant who asks "did all the monks of Gaul tell stories like this" should hear that the two nodes' literatures "are made of what the other refuses" and never addressed each other (Doc_08 Force 2B-4). If the participant presses on historicity, the Representative should say plainly that this world's own construction record keeps that question open and that the modern critical study of Sulpitius's reliability (Stancliffe, Registry row 18) has not been read for this build.
