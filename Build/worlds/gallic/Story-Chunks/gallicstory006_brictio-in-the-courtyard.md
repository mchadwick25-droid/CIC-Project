## Story Text

In the *Dialogues*, Sulpitius Severus has Gallus tell what happened one day at Martin's own dwelling, "after he had sat down on that wooden seat of his (which you all know), placed in the small open court which surrounded his abode."

Martin, Gallus says, "perceived two demons sitting on the lofty rock which overhangs the monastery," and heard them calling, "in eager and gladsome tones," *Come hither, Brictio, come hither, Brictio.* And Brictio came — "rushes in in absolute fury; and there, full of madness, he vomits forth a thousand reproaches against Martin."

The cause was a rebuke. The day before, Martin had reproved Brictio "because he who had possessed nothing before he entered the clerical office, having, in fact, been brought up in the monastery by Martin himself, was now keeping horses and purchasing slaves. For at that time, he was accused by many of not only having bought boys belonging to barbarous nations, but girls also of a comely appearance." Now, "with trembling lips, and a changing countenance, pale with rage," Brictio "rolled forth the words of sin, asserting that he was a holier man than Martin who had brought him up, inasmuch as from his earliest years he had grown up in the monastery amid the sacred institutions of the Church, while Martin had at first, as he could not deny, been tarnished with the life of a soldier, and had now entirely sunk into dotage by means of his baseless superstitions, and ridiculous fancies about visions." He said worse, Gallus adds, "which it is better not to mention," and nearly laid hands on him. Martin, "with a placid countenance and a tranquil mind, endeavored by gentle words to restrain the madness of the unhappy wretch."

Brictio went out as if he had vindicated himself — and "with rapid steps he rushed back by the way he had gone out," the demons, Gallus believes, "driven from his heart by the prayers of Martin." He threw himself at Martin's feet, begged pardon, confessed his error, and "acknowledges that he had been under the influence of a demon. It was no difficult business for Martin to forgive the suppliant." Martin then told him, and all of them, that he had seen him driven on by demons, "and declared that he was not moved by the reproaches which had been heaped upon him; for they had, in fact, rather injured the man who uttered them."

And afterwards, Gallus says, "when this same Brictio was often accused before him of many and great crimes, Martin could not be induced to remove him from the presbyterate, lest he should be suspected of revenging the injury done to himself, while he often repeated this saying: 'If Christ bore with Judas, why should not I bear with Brictio?'"

## Tier Justification

Tier 1 — Documented Historical Narrative — with caveats. Direct attestation within the horizon: the *Dialogues*, c. 404–406, by a named, socially located author, through a named disciple-narrator (Gallus) speaking of a scene in the household he belonged to, before an audience (Sulpitius, Postumianus) who knew the place — "that wooden seat of his (which you all know)." Datable to within Martin's episcopate (Brictio is a presbyter and Martin a bishop in the scene; the episcopate ends with the death of 397, Doc_01 §10). The narrative claim is historically credible and largely non-miraculous: a presbyter raised in the monastery, now acquiring property, rebuked by his bishop, losing his temper, repenting, and being kept in office despite repeated accusations. Brictio is a named, living, contemporary subject — a fact that weighs *for* the story's documentary character, since Sulpitius published it while the man could contradict it.

The caveats: the two demons on the rock, and Martin's later statement that he "had seen him driven on by demons," are Martin's own perception as Gallus transmits it, and the demonic explanation of Brictio's rage is Gallus's own ("as I believe"); these are carried here as the world's own self-understanding at Contested strength, exactly as the angel is carried in gallicstory005, not as fact. Sulpitius's and Gallus's hostility to Brictio is their perspective, and Brictio's own side is not heard — an Article 20-shaped limit inside a Tier 1 story, named rather than smoothed. The story is not corroborated by any independent witness in this world's Native corpus; Widely Accepted, not Documented.

Genuine uncertainty at construction: the demonic frame is not incidental — the story opens on it. It does not, however, constitute the narrative's *claim* the way the resurrection does in gallicstory003; the claim is the quarrel, the reproof, the repentance, and the saying. Tier 1 with the frame caveated is the honest classification; a reviewer who prefers Tier 3 for a story that opens with demons on a rock has a defensible position, and this chunk records the disagreement rather than hiding it.

## Usage Guidance

The Representative may draw on this story as remembered history, with Sulpitius and his narrator named: "In the *Dialogues*, Gallus tells that Brictio, whom Martin had brought up in the monastery …" The rebuke, Brictio's outburst, his repentance, and Martin's saying may be offered as this world's own memory. The demons on the rock should be presented as what Martin "perceived" and what Gallus believed, not asserted by the Representative as fact.

Additional guidance specific to this story: the Representative must not extend Brictio's story beyond the vendored text. This world's own evidence does not say what became of him; a participant who knows from elsewhere that a Brictius later held the see of Tours should be told plainly that this world's own record here ends at "Martin could not be induced to remove him from the presbyterate," and that the Representative will not supply what its evidence does not contain. If the participant asks whether the south told stories of disciples turning on masters, the honest answer is that Cassian's *exempla* tell of monks who fell by trusting their own judgment (Heron, *Conf.* II.5) and of one falsely accused (gallicstory011), but not of a Gallic disciple mocking his master's visions — and that the north's own failure-mode and the south's (cooling, Doc_08 Force 2B-5) are different failures.
