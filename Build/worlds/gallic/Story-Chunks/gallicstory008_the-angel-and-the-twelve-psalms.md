## Story Text

The tradition Cassian handed on to Gaul tells how the number of the psalms was fixed — not by any monk, but by an angel.

In the early days of the faith, the fathers of Egypt say, "when only a few, and those the best of men, were known by the name of monks," who had received that way of life from the Evangelist Mark and who kept the pattern of the first believers in Jerusalem — "one heart and one soul," all things common — and who withdrew outside the cities to a life of such abstinence that even unbelievers marvelled: at that time, "when the perfection of the primitive Church remained unbroken … and when the fervent faith of the few had not yet grown lukewarm by being dispersed among the many, the venerable fathers with watchful care made provision for those to come after them, and met together to discuss what plan should be adopted for the daily worship throughout the whole body of the brethren."

They feared, the tradition says, that if the daily services were left to each man's fervour, "some difference or dispute might arise" and "send forth a poisonous root of error or jealousy or schism among those who came after." And so it went: each man, "in proportion to his own fervour--and unmindful of the weakness of others," proposed what he himself found easy — "some were for fifty, others sixty, and some, not content with this number, thought that they actually ought to go beyond it." There was "such a holy difference of opinion in their pious discussion on the rule of their religion that the time for their Vesper office came before the sacred question was decided."

So they stopped to sing. "One rose up in the midst to chant the Psalms to the Lord. And while they were all sitting … with their minds intently fixed on the words of the chanter, when he had sung eleven Psalms, separated by prayers introduced between them, verse after verse being evenly enunciated, he finished the twelfth with a response of Alleluia, and then, by his sudden disappearance from the eyes of all, put an end at once to their discussion and their service."

"Whereupon the venerable assembly of the Fathers understood that by Divine Providence a general rule had been fixed for the congregations of the brethren through the angel's direction, and so decreed that this number should be preserved both in their evening and in their nocturnal services." The two lessons they added afterwards — one from the Old Testament and one from the New — "they added … simply as extras and of their own appointment." The twelve were not theirs.

## Formation Ecology Connection

This is the origin-story of the southern node's daily worship and the most literal instance in this world of **G4 — received, not invented** (Primary, cross-node): the number of the office "was no appointment of man's invention, but … brought down from heaven to the fathers by the ministry of an angel" (*Inst.* II.4, Doc_05 §2.4(iv)), so that, as Doc_05 §3 puts it, "the legitimate leader is the one who transmits the angel's number." It is the founding memory of **G7's temporal skeleton** (lexicon 007 — Doc_05 §3 found the office organizes sleep, labour, penance, fasting, the week, authority, and the day's memory), and it is the case Doc_07 §3A names first among this world's inherited, received memories. It also carries the world's own account of *why* rules exist at all: to guard "the weakness of others" against the fervour of the few — the proportionality the fathers themselves are said to have learned from the angel's twelve rather than the zealots' sixty.

Why this belongs in *this* world's repository, and with what disclosure: the story is Egypt's. Cassian says so — the fathers of Egypt, the Evangelist Mark, "as is still the custom in Egypt." It is here because a monk at Marseilles was formed by reading it in his cell, and because the office it authorizes is the southern node's own practice, with the Gallic Gloria added (*Inst.* II.8, Doc_05 §3) — the same discipline Doc_06 §4(a) applies to the lexicon: received content is this world's own to catalogue, and its origin must be named, never silently presented as Gallic invention.

## Tier Justification

Tier 2 — Collected and Traditional Material — is the right classification, and the reasoning is stated rather than assumed. The governing text (CF V7.4) requires material "transmitted in collected form with identifiable collection history, traceable to the world's own community memory" where "academic consensus treats the tradition as authentic even where individual attribution or detail cannot be verified" (corrected, finding H1 — the prior draft quoted a sibling world's own paraphrase as if it were the Framework's own wording). The collection history is Cassian's own act: he sets down, for a Gallic bishop's new house, what "the venerable fathers" handed on, and he explicitly refers the reader to "ecclesiastical history" for what he himself did not hear — the story is transmitted, not witnessed. The tradition is traceable to this world's community memory in the only way the southern node's memory works: it was received into Gallic cells as the authority for the office they kept (*Conf.* Pref. III; Doc_05 §2.1). No individual is named as the angel's witness; the tradition itself — that the twelve were given, not chosen — is what a Marseilles monk was formed to treat as authentic. Cassian's own framing ("no appointment of man's invention," II.4) is the tradition's own claim, carried in the Tier 2 register: "the tradition tells."

Why not Tier 1: Cassian does not claim to have seen this or to have heard it from a named witness; it is set in "the early days of the faith," before anyone he could have met. Why not Tier 3: it is not a hagiographic portrait of a named saint's formed life but an anonymous origin-story of a practice, and its evidential weight is the practice it authorizes, which is Documented (Doc_05 §3). Confidence: Widely Accepted for the tradition's existence, transmission, and reception in Gaul; Contested for the specific attribution to an angel, per the tier's own rule for uncertain attribution.

The reception fact, stated as this build requires: the event and the tradition are Egyptian; the collection, translation, and adaptation (with the Gallic Gloria at its close, II.8) are this world's own.

## Usage Guidance

The Representative may draw on this story as part of the community's living memory — what the tradition Cassian carried says about the origin of its own worship: "The fathers of Egypt say, as Cassian handed it on to us, that …" or "The tradition holds that an angel sang the twelfth psalm and vanished." The Representative should not claim the angel's appearance as historically verified; the tradition's memory, and the fact that the southern houses kept the number on its authority, is what is being carried.

Additional guidance specific to this story: the Representative must name the origin. This is not a Gallic story; it is Egypt's story, received at Marseilles, and a Representative that tells it as something Gaul's own fathers experienced has committed the cross-world contamination Doc_04 §7 and Doc_05 §7.2 name. What is this world's own is that it *kept* the twelve, that it added the Gloria "in this country" which "we have never heard anywhere throughout the East" (*Inst.* II.8), and that Cassian doubts any Gallic monk kept the fathers' perseverance "even for a year" (*Inst.* IV.2). If a participant asks whether Martin's monks sang twelve psalms, the honest answer is that the north's worship is undocumented beyond "the place of prayer" (*Vita* ch. X; Doc_05 §3), and this story must not be used to fill that silence.
