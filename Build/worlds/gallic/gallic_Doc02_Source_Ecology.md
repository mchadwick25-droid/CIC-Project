## 1. Primary Voices

### 1.1 Sulpitius Severus

`npnf211`'s introduction is **Alexander Roberts's** own nineteenth-century composition (named on the volume's own title page: "Translated, with preface, and notes, by Rev. Alexander Roberts, D.D., Professor of Humanity, University of St. Andrews"), not an ancient source. The specific dates sometimes attributed to "Gennadius's own chapter" — "born after 353, died about 410" — are **Ernest Cushing Richardson's** own 1892 bracketed editorial endnote, not Gennadius's ancient text. Gennadius's own chapter (`npnf203`, ch. XIX, read directly) dates nothing; it closes with no year at all, only a narrative account of Sulpitius's life and works.

This world's library holds **one ancient biographical source** on Sulpitius (Gennadius, c. 495) and **two modern editorial treatments** of him (Roberts's `npnf211` introduction; Richardson's `npnf203` endnotes) — not two ancient authorities. The two modern editors' own dating endnotes disagree (`npnf211`'s "about a.d. 363, and died, as is generally supposed, in a.d. 420" against Richardson's "after 353...about 410"; the "about" qualifier attaches only to the birth year in Roberts's own wording, not to both) — a real discrepancy, still worth disclosing, but a disagreement **between two nineteenth-century scholars**, not an ancient dispute. This document carries it as such.

**Independent ancient corroboration for the core biographical facts, correctly attributed:** Jerome (*Comm. Ezech.* 36); Augustine (*Ep.* 205); and Gennadius's own text, directly: "a man distinguished by his birth, by his excellent literary work, by his devotion to poverty and by his humility, beloved also of the sainted men Martin bishop of Tours and Paulinus Nolanus," and — a fact genuinely Gennadius's own — "He wrote to his sister **many** Letters exhorting to love of God and contempt of the world." **[Widely Accepted.]**

**The Pelagianism charge, at the correct evidentiary shape:** Gennadius's own text, read again directly, states the charge flatly and in his own voice, not as a report of rumor: *"In his old age, he was led astray by the Pelagians, and recognizing the guilt of much speaking, kept silent until his death, in order that by penitent silence he might atone for the sin which he had contracted by speaking."* This is one ancient author's own direct claim. The party who reports and rejects it in `npnf211` is **Roberts**, the Victorian translator, not a second ancient source — his own words are "it seems to us that there is no ground for any such conclusion," and his own phrase "some ancient writers" most plausibly refers to Gennadius himself, the very source Roberts is implicitly arguing against. **The correct shape of this tension is: one ancient source asserting the charge, and one modern translator denying it** — this still meets Article 17's *Contested* bar (a live disagreement about how to read the ancient record, not resolved), but the disagreement is not ancient-versus-ancient. Flagged for Doc_04 at this strength.

**Author Gravity Assessment (five dimensions):**

- **Visibility, Representativeness, Influence, Limitations:** the Gennadius material corroborates rather than alters these findings.
- **Transmission History:** the volume's own title page names Roberts directly, with his institutional position (Professor of Humanity, University of St. Andrews), exactly the kind of detail this dimension exists to capture. Roberts's own introduction names, as editions he consulted (not necessarily his single base text): "the editions of Sigonius (1609), of Hornius (1664), of Vorstius (1709), and of Halm (1866)." No single one is identified as the base text his translation follows; this document does not claim more precision than the source supplies.

### 1.2 John Cassian

A specific Cassian death date, "died 450," sometimes attributed to Gennadius, is actually Richardson's own bracketed endnote, not Gennadius's text — Gennadius's own chapter closes, as the chapters this document draws on mostly do (ch. XIX/Sulpitius is an exception), with a bare regnal formula ("in the reign of Theodosius and Valentinianus"). **What Gennadius's own text does independently corroborate:** the ordination as deacon "by bishop John the Great, at Constantinople," the presbyterate "at Marseilles," and — genuinely independent ancient confirmation — "founded two monasteries, that is to say one for men and one for women, **which are still standing**" (Gennadius wrote c. 495, within a lifetime of Cassian's own death, and confirms both houses' survival to his own day). Gennadius also independently confirms *De Incarnatione* was written "at the request of **Leo the archdeacon**, afterwards bishop of Rome" — corroborating Doc_01 §10's Leyser-sourced Leo detail from a source outside Leyser entirely — and lists all twenty-four Conferences by subject, in his own order (count verified: twenty-four, matching this world's own vendored structure).

**`npnf211`'s own Cassian prolegomena carry an extended, genuinely rich transmission-history discussion:**

- **Prosper's own polemical response to Cassian is named, described, and located inside a source this world already has in hand.** The prolegomena state directly: *"the former of them [Prosper] finally determined to put down, if possible, the upholders of the objectionable tenets by a direct criticism of Cassian's Conferences. This was the origin of Prosper's work 'Contra Collatorem,' against the author of the Conferences... The treatise is given in Gazet's edition of Cassian."* The prolegomena further describe its actual content: an examination of Conference XIII specifically, extracting twelve propositions, of which Prosper judges the first orthodox and the rest erroneous — and note, tellingly, that "his antagonist never once names him directly, but merely speaks of him as a man of priestly rank." This material is drawn from `npnf211`'s own already-vendored volume, not from Gennadius. See Source Registry row 32 (companion file).
- **A genuine doctrinally-motivated intermediary:** the same prolegomena describe **Dionysius Carthusianus** (a fifteenth-century Carthusian, "the doctor exstaticus"), who produced a free paraphrase of the *Institutes* and *Conferences* — and, specifically for Conference XIII, "in his endeavour to make Cassian orthodox, **omits all that savours of Semi-Pelagianism**; and from c. viii. onward there are large omissions and various suggestive alterations in the text." **This document states precisely what the source supports and no more:** Dionysius's paraphrase survives as a separate work, printed alongside the standard text in Gazet's 1616 edition (and thence Migne's); this document has not established, and does not claim, that Gibson's own NPNF translation follows Dionysius's redacted text rather than the standard one — the prolegomena discuss Dionysius as a documented historical instance of doctrinally-motivated editing of this exact Conference, not as the version this world's own English text is built from.
- **Also named, not previously in hand:** **Petschenig's** critical edition (the CSEL text) is cited by the prolegomena as a scholarly reference point, and a letter obtained by Prosper and Hilary from Pope Celestine (the prolegomena's own text gives no year for this letter) — addressed to the Gallican bishops including "**Venerius of Marseilles**" (Cassian's own bishop, named first in the salutation) — is described in detail, closing "with some words of eulogium on Augustine 'of holy memory'" despite the Massilians' continued resistance. This is a specific, textually-grounded instance of exactly the "forces" dynamic Doc_02 §11 discusses in general terms: papal authority invoked, named, and defied by name, inside a source this world already holds.

**Author Gravity Assessment:**

- **Transmission History:** translated by **Edgar C. S. Gibson** (title page: "Principal of the Theological College, Wells, Somerset"). This world's own vendored text is missing Conferences XII and XXII (Registry row 11). The prolegomena's own transmission discussion (above) supplies a fuller picture: Gazet's 1616 edition as the historical standard, Petschenig's later critical edition, and Dionysius Carthusianus's own doctrinally-motivated fifteenth-century paraphrase of Conference XIII specifically — three distinct editorial layers between Cassian's own hand and this world's own English text, now named rather than gestured at with a single footnote.

**Confidence:** authorship and the Institutes' addressee — Widely Accepted. **The Conferences' three-part chronology remains at Widely Accepted at most**, with the Chadwick/Casiday tension carried at full Contested strength (Doc_01 §7); this rating does not rest on a Gennadius death-date anchor, since no such anchor exists in the ancient text.

### 1.3 Vincent of Lérins

The claim that Vincent "was ordained presbyter specifically in 434 — the same year as the *Commonitory* itself" is **Richardson's own bracketed endnote** ("Presbyter 434, died before 450"), not Gennadius's text. Gennadius's own chapter, read again directly, says only that Vincentius was "presbyter in the Monastery on the Island of Lerins" — no year. A "doubly anchored... convergent independent evidence" reading for the 434 date does not hold: even if Richardson's endnote were ancient, it would not be independent — a one-line modern dating note for "presbyter 434" is overwhelmingly likely to be derived from the *Commonitory*'s own internal Ephesus-431-plus-three-years dating, the same datum already anchoring the composition date. Treating it as a second, separate anchor would convert one piece of evidence into an apparent convergence even if its source were reliable.

**The 434 composition date remains Documented on its own, sufficient grounds** — Doc_01 §2.4 already rates it so, on the *Commonitory*'s own internal statement that it was written three years after Ephesus (431), independently verified. This document does not add a second anchor that is not actually there.

**What Gennadius's own text does independently and genuinely supply:** the *Peregrinus* pseudonym, confirmed in his own words ("suppressing his name, he entitled *Peregrinus* against heretics"); and a real transmission-history fact — "the greater part of the second book of this work having been stolen, he composed a brief reproduction of the substance of the original work, and published in one [book]." Gennadius's own first line on Vincentius — "presbyter in the Monastery on the Island of **Lerins**" — is itself a genuine, near-contemporary, independent ancient attestation that Lérins existed and housed this world's own primary voice. This is the actual independent-ancient-corroboration this document has for the Lérins node's existence — see §3 below for why it does not answer the separate question it might seem to.

**Author Gravity Assessment:** Limitations and the Augustine-silence finding remain at the editor's-claim strength, correctly hedged. Transmission History reflects the Heurtley/Book-II-theft finding.

**Confidence:** the 434 composition date — **Documented**, on its own actual grounds (internal Ephesus dating) alone. The Vincentius attribution — Widely Accepted. Whether Vincent held or only sympathized with the Massilian position — Contested.

**A finding from Gennadius's own text:** ch. ## 3. Institutional Evidence

A claim that the Lérins–Marseilles link is "doubly evidenced" — by Gennadius's Faustus chapter "directly" and by the editorial apparatus — does not hold. Gennadius ch. LXXXVI concerns **Faustus**, whose own Lérins abbacy (433/4, per Richardson's endnote — Gennadius's own text says only "first abbot of the monastery at Lerins, **and then made bishop** of Riez," the "first... then" sequence stated here in full) postdates Cassian's own dedication by roughly a decade. **It says nothing about Honoratus, nothing about Cassian, and nothing about any Marseilles–Lérins relationship.** What it independently confirms is only that Lérins existed and had abbots — never in dispute.

**The link between Cassian's Marseilles and the institution at Lérins therefore rests on this world's own editorial apparatus** (Roberts's and Gibson's identification of Cassian's unnamed dedicatee as Honoratus, abbot of Lérins), not on Cassian's own words, which name two "holy brothers" and one unnamed presiding monastery.

**What does independently corroborate the Lérins node's own existence:** Gennadius's own opening line on **Vincentius** — "presbyter in the Monastery on the Island of **Lerins**" — is genuine, near-contemporary, ancient testimony that Lérins existed and housed one of this world's own three primary voices. This is real independent evidence for the institution's existence — it does not, however, answer the separate Cassian-dedication question.

**Saint-Victor and Saint-Sauveur, Marseilles:** Gennadius's own direct text ("two monasteries... which are still standing") is genuine independent corroboration.

**A further, small ancient transmission datum:** Gennadius describes the *Dialogues* as "a Conference between Postumianus and Gallus... in two divisions" — against `npnf211`'s own three-Dialogue structure. This document does not resolve the discrepancy (whether Gennadius is describing an earlier or differently-divided recension, or simply compressing loosely) and names it as an open item (§13) rather than building anything on it, particularly given Registry rows for the Dialogues split on the three-Dialogue structure this world's own vendored text actually has.

## 6. Ordinary Participant Evidence

**Claudia, Sulpitius's sister, checked directly, in three respects:**

1. **There are at least two letters to Claudia, not one** — Letter I ("Concerning the Last Judgment") and **Letter II, "Concerning Virginity"** (`npnf211`, Doubtful Letters), both read directly. Gennadius (ch. XIX) independently states Sulpitius "wrote to his sister **many** Letters."
2. **Claudia is not evidence of non-elite participation.** Sulpitius himself "belonged to an illustrious family" (`npnf211` line 366); his sister is a member of the same family. She is this world's own literate provincial aristocracy, not a counterexample to it.
3. **"Non-monastic" is not supported and is likely wrong.** Letter II, read directly this revision, opens with a treatise-length meditation on the special sanctity of consecrated virginity — "those who are virgins possess something above the rest, since ... they are chosen by the Holy Spirit, and are presented by the bishop ... at the altar of God, as if being more holy and pure sacrifices." A letter in this specific genre, addressed to a named woman, is the standard form for correspondence with a woman already under, or being urged toward, a vow of virginity — not neutral family correspondence to a laywoman outside any ascetic commitment.

Claudia is real, textually present, and worth keeping in view — but as a second data point on this world's own elite, and very possibly ascetically-committed, women (alongside the Saint-Sauveur community, Doc_01 §4), not as this library's one thread to ordinary, non-elite, non-monastic life. **That thread remains genuinely absent**, and §10 below restates the Missing Voices finding accordingly, without Claudia doing work she cannot do.

## 8. Formation Narrative Sources

Sulpitius's *Vita Martini*, *Dialogues*, and *Letters* are this world's own formation-narrative sources in the Framework's specific sense (§1.1 above covers authorship, proximity, and genre in full). One further note the Framework requires here specifically: **what formation ecology the narrative reveals.** Read together, Sulpitius's corpus reveals a formation pattern organized around direct, personally-witnessed contact with a single exemplary figure (Sulpitius insists throughout on his own acquaintance with Martin, and the *Dialogues*' frame narrative — a Gallic friend "who had been one of [Martin's] disciples," reunited with the returning traveller Postumianus — stages eyewitness testimony as a structural device, not incidental color) rather than through a written rule or codified curriculum. This is the same "named, direct testimony as the guarantee of a text's authority" pattern Doc_01 §6 flags as a candidate cross-node gravity for Doc_04 to test — this document supplies the Tours-side textual grounding for that candidate more fully than Doc_01 had space to.

## 10. Source Asymmetry and Missing Voices Assessment

**(a), (b), (c):** `npnf101`'s own Prefatory Note states the grounds for the `npnf101` letters gap by letter number: Letters 225 and 226 are omitted under the volume's own **"Some of the letters written by others to Augustin"** category — they are excluded *because they are not Augustine's own letters*, the same editorial policy that excludes seven other named letters (94, 109, 121, 160, 168, 230, 270) for the identical reason; Letters 221–224 fall under **"a large number of miscellaneous smaller letters."** The volume's own stated policy on Pelagian-controversy material generally is a **cross-reference**, not a suppression: "Almost all the letters relating to Pelagianism" are omitted specifically *because* "the series contains three volumes of Augustin's anti-Pelagian writings" elsewhere in the NPNF set — the opposite of excluding the topic. **This document states the finding at the strength the source actually supports:** the omission is real, checkable, and consequential for this world's own open questions (§1.4), but it is a declared, general editorial-selection policy, not evidence of a sympathy-driven suppression of one side's material.

**Salvian of Marseilles, *On the Government of God* — NATIVE, Documented.** Salvian is not merely a contemporary who happened to live in Cassian's own city. He personally taught at Lérins, and the evidence for this is primary and ancient, not a modern inference:

Verified by direct grep and read against the file, not taken from the translator's footnote alone.
- **Eucherius of Lyon's own letter to his son Salonius**, prefacing the *Instructiones* (CSEL XXXI.1, quoted in translation by Sanford, not yet independently vendored in Latin): Salonius and his brother Veranus were "reared up under our father Honoratus... there the teachings of the most blessed Hilarius, then a novice of the island... formed you in all branches of spiritual study; a work completed by **saints Salvian and Vincent**, preéminent alike in eloquence and knowledge."
