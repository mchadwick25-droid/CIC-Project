## Quick Meaning

For this world the cell is the monk's own dwelling and the place where everything happens to him — where Martin prays, receives saints, and is tempted by a purple-robed devil behind a closed door, and where Cassian's junior may not go out without leave, is bidden to stay all day at his work, and is assailed at noon by the disease that makes him hate the cell itself.

---

## World Meaning

At Tours the cell is what the master has and what the disciples make. Martin "possessed a cell constructed of wood. Many also of the brethren had, in the same manner, fashioned retreats for themselves, but most of them had formed these out of the rock of the overhanging mountain, hollowed into caves"; and "rarely did any one of them go beyond the cell, unless when they assembled at the place of prayer." What is seen and heard in the cell is the substance of the disciples' formation. Sulpitius and Gallus keep watch outside "the door of his cell being closed" and hear voices; Martin admits, "Agnes, Thecla, and Mary were there with me." The devil comes to it "as he was praying in his cell," "clothed in purple, and with a glittering crown," and is refused. Sulpitius, "sitting alone in my cell" after Martin's death, has the vision that becomes a letter. Even the counterfeit monk of the Dialogues builds "a cell for himself at a distance in the desert" — the cell is where a monk's claim is made and where it is tested.

At Marseilles the cell is a discipline before it is a place. The book Cassian writes for Gaul prescribes a cell "which he inhabits alone, or is allowed to share with only one other," and a rule of not leaving it: after the office "none of them dares to loiter ever so little or to gossip with another: nor does he presume even to leave his cell throughout the whole day"; the juniors, "without the knowledge and permission of their superior ... not only do not dare to leave their cell but on their own authority do not venture to satisfy their common and natural needs." Labour holds the monk there "like some strong and immoveable anchor, by which the changeableness and wanderings of the heart, fastened within the barriers of the cell, may be shut up in some perfectly secure harbour." And the cell has its own disease: accidie, "especially trying to solitaries," which "produces dislike of the place, disgust with the cell, and disdain and contempt of the brethren," so that the monk "frequently gazes up at the sun, as if it was too slow in setting." Piamun's warning closes the circle: patience must not rest "in the doors of our cell or the recesses of the desert," because "no one is more my enemy than my own heart." The cell keeps the body still so that the heart can be fought.

## Distortion Risk

**Modern Hearing:**
A prison cell, or a bare private bedroom; solitude as privacy; "hermitage" as retreat from stress.

**World Hearing:**
The one place a monk is: a wooden hut or a hollow in the rock that he rarely leaves except for prayer, in which saints and the devil appear, and — in the south — a harbour whose "barriers" hold the wandering heart still by labour and rule, with its own named affliction at noon.
