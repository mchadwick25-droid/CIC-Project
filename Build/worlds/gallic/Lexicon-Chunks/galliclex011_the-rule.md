## Quick Meaning

Vincent's test at Lérins for telling "the truth of Catholic faith from the falsehood of heretical pravity": hold "that faith which has been believed everywhere, always, by all" — and when a part, a novelty, or a few dissent, prefer the whole, antiquity, and the consent of an ancient council or of the ancients collated.

---

## World Meaning

The rule exists because Scripture alone, though complete, is read in more than one way. "Owing to the depth of Holy Scripture, all do not accept it in one and the same sense, but one understands its words in one way, another in another." Novatian, Photinus, Arius, Pelagius, Nestorius — each quotes the same book. So a Christian must "fortify our own belief in two ways; first, by the authority of the Divine Law, and then, by the Tradition of the Catholic Church." And within the Church, "all possible care must be taken, that we hold that faith which has been believed everywhere, always, by all. For that is truly and in the strictest sense 'Catholic,' which, as the name itself and the reason of the thing declare, comprehends all universally. This rule we shall observe if we follow universality, antiquity, consent."

It comes with a procedure for each way it can fail. If a part of the Church breaks off, prefer "the soundness of the whole body" to the corrupt member. If a novelty threatens all, hold to antiquity. If within antiquity a few err, "prefer the decrees, if such there be, of an ancient General Council to the rashness and ignorance of a few." And if no council has spoken, "collate and consult and interrogate the opinions of the ancients" — of those who, "though living in divers times and places," remained in the Church's communion — and hold what "all, or the more part, have supported and confirmed manifestly, frequently, persistently, in one and the same sense, forming, as it were, a consentient council of doctors." Against that consent no single teacher stands, "be he holy and learned, be he a bishop, be he a Confessor, be he a martyr": his singular opinion is "a private fancy of his own." The rule is not for every question; the pains are to be taken "not on every minor question of the Divine Law, but only, at all events especially, where the Rule of Faith is concerned."

Its cases are remembered. Pope Stephen, against rebaptism, "laid down this rule: 'Let there be no innovation — nothing but what has been handed down.'" The bishops at Ephesus, three years before, "innovated nothing, presumed nothing, arrogated to themselves absolutely nothing, but used all possible care to hand down nothing to posterity but what they had themselves received from their Fathers." Celestine's sentence is quoted: "let novelty cease to assail antiquity." And the rule answers the question "who ever before?" — "who ever before that profane Pelagius attributed so much antecedent strength to Free-will, as to deny the necessity of God's grace?" An error can always be dated; the faith cannot.

The same test governs what a monk wears. At Marseilles, on whether sackcloth is proper dress, allegiance is owed "not to those customs and rules which the will of a few have introduced, but to those which a long standing antiquity and numbers of the holy fathers have passed on by an unanimous decision to those that come after" — antiquity, numbers, unanimity — and the garments are judged by "the Catholic rule." The twelve psalms stand because their number "was no appointment of man's invention." And at Tours a bishop asked to venerate a tomb asks his elders whether any tradition "had come down from antiquity," and, finding none, will not lend the multitude his authority. None of the three cites the other; all three reach for the same instrument.

What the rule does to authority is the deepest thing about it. No office settles a question by itself — not a bishop's, not a confessor's, not a martyr's — and the fathers who settle it do so by agreeing, in writing, across times and places. A monk on an island, anxious about "new heretics" and his own failing memory, writes it down so as not to forget it.

## Ecological Function

This is the *test*-form of G4 (Primary — received, not invented): the fathers (3.3) are whom one receives from, novelty-versus-antiquity (7.3) is the axis, and this is the procedure. It is G9's southern valence (councils and the Apostolic See as guarantors — Doc_05 §4.2), the criterion of legitimacy that lets this ecology "produce bishops and distrust them in the same breath" (Doc_05 §4.3), and — at the editor's reading, Contested — the instrument by which the Massilian position was defended as antiquity (G3). Doc_05 §6C names Vincent's transfer of authority from persons seen to a consensus of texts "the interpretive ecology's most consequential single move," and §6B makes the *Commonitory* an institution against forgetting. Dependencies: Catholic (7.1), the deposit (7.4), progress vs. alteration (7.5), trial (7.6), council (7.8), heretic (7.10), Apostolic See (7.12), the customs of the monasteries (3.6, the monastic parallel). A participant who understands this term understands why a Lérins monk trusts a council of the dead over a living bishop.

## Distortion Risk

**Modern Hearing:**
"The Vincentian canon" as a slogan in Anglican, Roman Catholic, or Orthodox polemic about which church is the true one; a tidy three-part formula applied mechanically; or "rule" as a monastic Rule.

**World Hearing:**
A memory-aid written by a monk under "a certain awful expectation of the approach of the divine judgment," with a stepwise procedure whose hardest cases — great teachers erring as the Church's trial — sit inside it; a test that also governs what a monk wears and how many psalms he sings; and the one place in this world where authority passes from a face to a consensus of texts.

## CT Contest Type

**Historical scope:** whether the rule was ever applied as broadly or as uniformly as its later use claims — whether "everywhere, always, by all" ever described the actual state of any doctrine, and whether Vincent himself applied it consistently. The in-file instance of this contest is Heurtley's own Appendix III (editorial, row 17, not read in this build) on Vincent's handling of Celestine's letter, and Doc_04's forces notation for G4 records that the same rule that guarded the Massilian position was later turned against it (post-window; a Doc_08 item).

**Relationship to present-day traditions:** the rule's afterlife as "the Vincentian canon" in Anglican, Roman Catholic, and Orthodox argument over which communion holds the faith "everywhere, always, by all." A related but separate afterlife attaches to Vincent's own "progress ... not alteration" passage (ch. 23, see 057): Heurtley's editorial footnote at ch. 17 [44] (`iii.xviii`, row 17) quotes *Newman on Development* there, reading a fifth-century monk through a nineteenth-century theory — the footnote quotes Newman, not Vincent's progress passage itself (corrected, the spot-check — the prior fix-round wording, following the review's own phrasing, had this inverted). Present-day uses of the formula are not this world's own and must not enter the Representative's voice (Doc_05 §9B; Constitution Article 28).
