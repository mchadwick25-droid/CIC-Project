## Quick Meaning

For this world the elder is the man whose judgment stands in for the junior's own — the senior to whom every thought is laid bare and by whose "examination" a thing is "good or bad," the "Abbot" of every Egyptian father Cassian reports — while at Tours the same place is held by one master, Martin, who himself consults "his elders, whether among the presbyters or clerics" about what tradition allows.

---

## World Meaning

In Cassian's book the senior is the first fact of a young monk's life. The juniors are taught "not to conceal by a false shame any itching thoughts in their hearts, but, as soon as ever such arise, to lay them bare to the senior, and, in forming a judgment about them, not to trust anything to their own discretion, but to take it on trust that that is good or bad which is considered and pronounced so by the examination of the senior." Abbot Moses says the same: humility's "first proof is given by reserving everything (not only what you do but also what you think), for the scrutiny of the elders." The house is built of such men — an Elder at the gate "entrusted with the care of strangers and guests," another "placed over ten of the juniors ... whom he both teaches and governs in accordance with the arrangement which we read of in Exodus as made by Moses," the Abbot whose hands clothe the new brother "in the council of the brethren," and at the top the one who presides, who may not do so "before he has ... learnt by obedience what he ought to enjoin." Every father who speaks in the Conferences is "Abbot": Abbot Moses, Abbot Piamun, Abbot Chaeremon — the Egyptian title. Of the Gallic head of a house Cassian says only that he is "presiding as he does over a large monastery of the brethren." And not every grey head is an elder: "the true riches of old men are not to be measured by grey hairs but by their diligence in youth," for "there are some, and unhappily they form the majority, who pass their old age in a lukewarmness which they contracted in youth" — Cassian's own warning against those who "appoint ourselves Abbots before we have, as we ought, professed ourselves disciples."

At Tours the elder is one man and needs no title. Eighty disciples are "disciplined after the example of the saintly master"; Sulpitius, offered a place at Martin's table, "felt so overcome by the authority he unconsciously exerted, that I deemed it unlawful to do anything but acquiesce." But Martin too has elders. Asked to honour a tomb the crowd calls a martyr's, he "often asked from those who were his elders, whether among the presbyters or clerics, that the name of the martyr, or the time when he suffered, should be made known to him," and, finding "no steady tradition respecting them had come down from antiquity," he will not lend his authority "lest a mere superstition should obtain a firmer footing." The master who is obeyed without argument still submits what he does not know to those older in the Church.

## Distortion Risk

**Modern Hearing:**
"Abbot" as the canonical head of a Benedictine abbey, elected for life, with jurisdiction and a crozier; "elder" as a church officer or simply an old man; the senior's judgment of thoughts as spiritual direction or counselling one may take or leave.

**World Hearing:**
The man to whom one's thoughts are shown before they are acted on and whose "examination" replaces one's own discretion; "Abbot" as the honorific of an Egyptian father, never applied in what survives to a Gallic superior, who merely "presides"; a grey head that is no guarantee; and at Tours a master obeyed without a rule, who himself defers to "his elders" on what antiquity allows.
