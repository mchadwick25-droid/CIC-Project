## Quick Meaning

In Cassian's Marseilles, the junior is the newly received monk as Egypt receives him — ten days or more at the door "of set purpose repelled and scorned," searched for a hidden coin, stripped and reclothed "by the Abbot's hands," lodged a year under the guest-Elder, then handed to a dean over ten and taught "the alphabet, as it were, and first syllables in the direction of perfection" — with Cassian's own admission that "we cannot recollect any one who joined our monasteries keeping it up unbroken even for a year."

## World Meaning

The book for Castor's new house lays out the whole sequence as the Egyptians keep it. "One who seeks to be admitted to the discipline of the monastery is never received before he gives, by lying outside the doors for ten days or even longer, an evidence of his perseverance and desire, as well as of humility and patience. And when, prostrate at the feet of all the brethren that pass by, and of set purpose repelled and scorned by all of them," he has held on, he is searched "with the utmost care whether he is contaminated by a single coin from his former possessions clinging to him"; then "in the council of the brethren he is brought forward into the midst and stripped of his own clothes, and clad by the Abbot's hands in the dress of the monastery," so that he may know "that he has laid aside all worldly pride, and come down to the want and poverty of Christ." His old clothes go to the steward, to be given back "if they have seen him once grow cold in regard to the rule of their institution." He "is not allowed to mix at once with the congregation of the brethren, but is given into the charge of an Elder, who lodges apart not far from the entrance of the monastery, and is entrusted with the care of strangers and guests," and after a year "is handed over to another Elder, who is placed over ten of the juniors." That Elder's first lesson "will be to teach him first to conquer his own wishes; and ... he will of set purpose contrive to give him such orders as he knows to be contrary to his liking"; his second, "the alphabet, as it were, and first syllables in the direction of perfection" — to disclose every thought to the senior. The junior is a man being unmade and remade by a fixed order, and the order is not his own house's: Abbot Pinufius, a famous elder, "sought a distant monastery where he could be received as a novice," and was.

Then Cassian says what Gaul is. Of the Egyptians' "untiring perseverance and humility and subjection," he writes, "we cannot recollect any one who joined our monasteries keeping it up unbroken even for a year: so that when we have seen the beginning of their renunciation of the world, we shall understand how it came about that, starting from such a commencement, they reached such a height of perfection" — the sentence turns back at once to Egypt's beginnings, to explain Egypt's perfection; it does not say what a Gallic house does instead. The Gallic junior of the 420s is therefore told, in detail, what Egypt does to a novice, and told in the same book that no one here has borne it a year. What he actually undergoes is not written down. At Tours there is no grade at all: "the brethren of younger years" are the copyists, and the eighty are "disciples."

## Distortion Risk

**Modern Hearing:**
A Benedictine or later canonical novitiate — a defined year, a novice-master, vows at its end; "novice" as simply a beginner; the contrary orders and the scorning at the door as hazing or abuse.

**World Hearing:**
Egypt's own probation, prescribed for a Gallic house that cannot recall anyone keeping its perseverance a year: the door, the search, the stripping, the guest-house, the dean, the contrary command as the first lesson in conquering one's wishes, and disclosure as the alphabet — "the deed of Abraham," not cruelty, in the world's own reading; and no such grade at Tours.
