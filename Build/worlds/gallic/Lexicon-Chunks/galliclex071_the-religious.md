## Quick Meaning

In Salvian's Marseilles "the religious" is the ordinary name for those who have taken this world's way of life — "those who live and act according to their own determination and vows. Religious men are lowly — they wish to be so; poor — they delight in poverty; without ambition — they spurn it" — and "the monks — that is, ... the servants of God" is his gloss when he uses the other word once.

---

## World Meaning

Salvian, a presbyter addressing Romans who go to the games, calls the people of the monasteries "the religious," and describes them from outside as men who have chosen their lot: "Religious men are lowly — they wish to be so; poor — they delight in poverty; without ambition — they spurn it; unesteemed — they flee from honors; ... they are weak — nay, they rejoice in weakness." So "toil, fasting, poverty, humility and weakness are not burdensome to all who suffer them but only to those who are unwilling to bear them"; "the religious are happier than all others in this, that they have what they wish." When Carthage jeers at pallid, shaven "servants of God," he writes of "the hatred of the Africans for the monks — that is, for the servants of God," his one use of the monks' own word, glossed. The same class-noun names the lapsed: "the religious who have gained some reputation by a general repentance and now seek after new honors," who "repent of their conversion." What the cell calls a monk, the city calls a religious.

## Distortion Risk

**Modern Hearing:** "religious" heard as an adjective describing personal piety or devoutness in general.

**World Hearing:** at Marseilles it is Salvian's class-noun for a whole social category, spoken from outside by a non-monk addressing lapsed Romans — his ordinary word for "the monks," not a compliment about inner devotion, and available to describe the lapsed as readily as the faithful ("the religious who have gained some reputation by a general repentance").
