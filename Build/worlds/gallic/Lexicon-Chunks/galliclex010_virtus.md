## Quick Meaning

At Tours, *virtus* is not first a moral quality but effective power — the "power present" Martin perceives before raising the dead, the "abundance of power" he had as a monk and had less of as a bishop, diminished by one bad communion — while at Marseilles Cassian uses the same word for moral virtue and declines on principle to "weave a tale of God's miracles and signs."

---

## World Meaning

At Tours the word is what the *Life* is made of. Martin stretches himself on a dead catechumen, prays, and, "perceiving by means of the Spirit of God that power was present," rises and waits for the result; the man lives, and is "the first who offered himself to us both as a subject that had experienced the virtues of Martin, and as a witness to their existence." From that day Martin, "as being reckoned holy by all, ... was also deemed powerful and truly apostolical." The power is a possession, and it can be measured. Martin himself "was accustomed to say ... that such an abundance of power was by no means granted him while he was a bishop, as he remembered to have possessed before he obtained that office." It can be lost: after he was forced into one communion with the party of Ithacius, "when it happened that he cured some of the possessed more slowly and with less grace than usual, he at once confessed to us with tears that he felt a diminution of his power on account of the evil of that communion." It resides in things: "threads from Martin's garment, or such as had been plucked from the sackcloth which he wore, wrought frequent miracles upon those who were sick," and a father lays "a letter of Martin" in the bosom of his fevered daughter and the fever leaves her. It can be doubted, and the doubt is answered as blasphemy: "Was Martin really not possessed of power, and not a partaker of holiness, because he became exposed to danger from fire?" Inside the house the doubt has a name — Brictio, sneering at Martin's "ridiculous fancies about visions." And it is how Tours measures itself against Egypt: when Postumianus goes east, he is to tell Egypt, "justly proud of the numbers and virtues of its own saints," that "Europe will not yield to it, or to all Asia, in having only Martin." The disciples are formed by seeing this; Gennadius, a century on, remembers Martin as "a man famous for signs and wonders and virtues."

At Marseilles the same word means something a monk grows, and the wonders are set aside on purpose. Cassian will not "try to weave a tale of God's miracles and signs, although we have not only heard of many such among our elders, and those past belief, but have also seen them fulfilled under our very eyes; yet, leaving out all these things which minister to the reader nothing but astonishment and no instruction in the perfect life," he will write "not about God's miracles, but about the way to improve our character." "By driving out faults virtues shoot up and increase." The fathers of Egypt, as Nesteros reports them, went further: "the working of miracles and signs is not always necessary, nor good for all, nor granted to all. Humility therefore is the mistress of all virtues." "Our predecessors never reckoned those as good monks or free from the fault of vainglory, who professed themselves exorcists among men, and proclaimed with boastful ostentation among admiring crowds the grace which they had either obtained or which they claimed." If any man works a wonder, "he ought to meet with commendation from us not from admiration of his miracles, but from the beauty of his life, nor should we ask whether the devils are subject to him, but whether he possesses those features of love which the Apostle describes." The Egyptian teacher is not answering Martin; he has never heard of him. But a Marseilles monk who reads him has been told what not to be, and it is the thing Tours celebrates.

The south is not of one mind. At Lérins, Eucherius praises the Egyptian fathers who "brought forth the accompanying grace either in silent revelations or in crying signs" — the wonders admired, not refused. The disagreement runs between Cassian and Sulpitius, not simply between south and north.

What the word does at Tours is organize everything: the disciple's formation (he is formed by what he sees done), the master's authority (he is "apostolical" because powerful), the cost of office (less power as bishop), the meaning of communion (a bad one diminishes it), the war with the devil (the power is the weapon), and the memory of the saint (his straw and his threads). What the word does at Marseilles is name the danger of all that.

## Distortion Risk

**Modern Hearing:**
"Virtue" as moral excellence or character; or "miracle" as a suspension of natural law to be believed or disbelieved, a question of evidence; or Martin's wonders as pious legend detachable from what the world was actually about.

**World Hearing:**
At Tours, power as a possession of the saint — felt as present, measured as more or less, transferable to threads and letters, diminished by office and by a bad communion — and the very substance of formation; at Marseilles, the thing a good monk does not proclaim, and a word for what grows when faults are driven out. Neither hearing may be used for the other node.
