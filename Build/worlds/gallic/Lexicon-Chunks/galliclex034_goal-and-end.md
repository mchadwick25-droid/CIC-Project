## Quick Meaning

In Cassian's Marseilles, the structuring pair with which every teaching begins: every art "has some goal or mark; and end or aim of their own," and so does the monk's profession — its end "the kingdom of God or the kingdom of heaven," its immediate goal "purity of heart, without which no one can gain that end," to be aimed at "as if on a definite mark" like an archer's target.

---

## World Meaning

Abbot Moses will not begin a conference without it. "All the arts and sciences, said he, have some goal or mark; and end or aim of their own, on which the diligent pursuer of each art has his eye." The farmer bears heat and frost for the sake of a harvest; the merchant crosses seas for gain; the soldier serves for honour; "and our profession too has its own goal and end, for which we undergo all sorts of toils not merely without weariness but actually with delight." Then, "when we stood gaping at this remark," the two parts: "The end of our profession indeed, as I said, is the kingdom of God or the kingdom of heaven: but the immediate aim or goal, is purity of heart, without which no one can gain that end: fixing our gaze then steadily on this goal, as if on a definite mark, let us direct our course as straight towards it as possible." The picture is the archer's: those who compete "before a king of this world, try to shoot their arrows or darts into certain small targets which have the prizes painted on them" — without a target no one can tell whether his aim has improved. Everything a monk does is then placed on this line: fasting, vigils, reading, solitude "are not perfection, but aids to perfection," useful only as they bring him to the goal, and to be dropped the moment they hinder it. Abbot Moses closes his second conference having set out "the end and aim of the monastic life."

The pair is the shape of all the south's thinking. It tells a Gallic reader why the office exists (as a scaffold toward unceasing prayer), why the hardest faster may be the worst monk (he has mistaken an aid for the goal), and why the argument about grace matters (whether the goal can be reached by aiming at all). At Tours no one speaks of goal and end; Martin's whole hour is prayer, and no teacher there separates the kingdom from the purity that reaches it.

## Distortion Risk

**Modern Hearing:**
"Goals" as personal objectives or self-improvement targets; "end" as death or the end of the world; the pair as a philosopher's distinction rather than a monk's rule of thumb.

**World Hearing:**
A received Egyptian teaching that every practice is measured by a nearer mark (purity of heart) on the way to a farther one (the kingdom), so that the mark, not the effort, judges the fast — an archer's discipline, not a philosophy, and absent as a vocabulary at Tours.
