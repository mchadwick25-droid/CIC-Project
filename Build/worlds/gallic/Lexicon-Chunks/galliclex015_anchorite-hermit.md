## Quick Meaning

For this world an anchorite is the monk who, "first trained in the Cœnobium," withdraws to the desert "to fight with the devils in open conflict" — a grade no Gallic voice claims for itself: the boy Martin "desired to enter on the life of a hermit," Marmoutier gives its bishop "the solitude of a hermit," and Cassian hopes Gallic monks near their monasteries will "try to carry out the rule of anchorites."

---

## World Meaning

The word comes into Gaul with its order fixed. Abbot Piamun tells the two Gauls that "the second [kind] is that of the anchorites, who were first trained in the Cœnobium and then being made perfect in practical life chose the recesses of the desert: and in this order we also hope to gain a place." They "are rightly termed anchorites; i.e., withdrawers, because, being by no means satisfied with that victory whereby they had trodden under foot the hidden snares of the devil, while still living among men, they were eager to fight with the devils in open conflict, and a straightforward battle." Cassian sets the whole of the last Conferences under it — "in proportion as the Anchorite's life is grander than that of the Cœnobium, and the contemplation of God ... more sublime than ordinary practical life" — and then names the Gallic case exactly: the books are also for "those who retire to no great distance from your monasteries, and try to carry out the rule of anchorites." A Gallic anchorite is one who goes a short way out and tries. The unnamed fourth kind, the man who takes the name without the training, is the counterfeit the order exists to exclude.

At Tours the thing is older than the word's order. At twelve Martin "desired to enter on the life of a hermit; and he would have followed up that desire with the necessary vows, had not his as yet too youthful age prevented." As bishop he builds Marmoutier where "he enjoyed in it the solitude of a hermit," and the soldier-monk of the Dialogues erects "a cell for himself at a distance in the desert, as if with the purpose of leading the life of an eremite" — the "as if" is the story's warning, for he wants his wife there. When Postumianus returns from the East, Gallus tells him: "you have conquered all the eremites and anchorites. For no one of them, like your friend, or rather our friend, Martin, ruled over deaths of all kinds." The hermit's solitude at Tours is a bishop's retreat and a standard Martin is said to surpass; no one there says he went to the desert after the coenobium, and no one says he must.

## Distortion Risk

**Modern Hearing:**
A recluse or "hermit" as the purest or most original form of monk — the lone ascetic, escaped from institutions, whom community life dilutes; or a medieval anchorhold walled into a church.

**World Hearing:**
A grade reached *after* the coenobium, whose whole point is open battle with the devil, hoped for in Egypt's order and only "tried" a short way from a Gallic house; at Tours, a boy's wish, a bishop's retreat, and a standard the saint is said to have beaten.
