## Quick Meaning

At Tours the heathen are the unconverted countryside of Gaul, Martin's mission-field — "the Gallic rustics in their wretched folly" carrying "images of demons veiled with a white covering," villages where "before the times of Martin, very few, nay, almost none ... had received the name of Christ," temples burned and sacred pines felled, "for wherever he destroyed heathen temples, there he used immediately to build either churches or monasteries."

---

## World Meaning

Martin's Gaul is mostly not yet Christian, and the *Life* says so plainly of the country around Tours: "before the times of Martin, very few, nay, almost none, in those regions had received the name of Christ; but through his virtues and example that name has prevailed to such an extent, that now there is no place thereabouts which is not filled either with very crowded churches or monasteries." The rustics are met on the road with their rites — "it was the custom of the Gallic rustics in their wretched folly to carry about through the fields the images of demons veiled with a white covering" — and Martin, mistaking a funeral for such a procession, halts it with the cross. He sets fire to "a very ancient and celebrated temple," and when "the circle of flames was carried by the action of the wind upon a house which was very close to" it, he climbs the roof and "the fire" is "thrust back in a wonderful manner against the force of the wind"; he fells a sacred pine while "the chief priest of that place, and a crowd of other heathens began to oppose him," and the tree spins away from the sign of salvation; a heathen draws a sword on him and "offers his neck." The rustics are the ministry's object — converted in crowds, made catechumens "by placing his hand upon the whole of them" on the plain where martyrs once were consecrated, exorcised, catechized — and none of them speaks. Martin's own parents "were still involved in heathenism," and he "set free ... his mother ... from the errors of heathenism, though his father continued to cleave to its evils"; and he "could not visit the house of an unconverted heathen."

In the south the word has no countryside behind it. Cassian's "heathen" are the philosophers whose chastity was counterfeit, and the "ingrained heathen habits" of the Gentile converts the apostles made allowances for; nothing in Cassian, Vincent, or Salvian as read addresses a pagan village. The temples belong to Tours.

## Distortion Risk

**Modern Hearing:**
"Pagan" as a neutral religious identity to be respected; temple-burning as intolerance to be judged; Gaul as already Christian by the fourth century; "rustics" as a social class rather than a religious condition.

**World Hearing:**
The unconverted countryside as the field where the saint's power is shown and the name of Christ made to prevail; images of demons to be halted, temples to be replaced by churches and monasteries, a crowd to be made catechumens by one hand; and in the south, no such field at all.
