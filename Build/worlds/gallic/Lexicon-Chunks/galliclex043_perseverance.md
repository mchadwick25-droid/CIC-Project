## Quick Meaning

For this world perseverance is both the endurance a novice proves at the door and must keep "even to the end" — "not he who begins these things, but he who endures in them to the end, shall be saved" — and the third of Chaeremon's three stages of the Divine gift, "so that it may be held by the persistence of the goodness already acquired"; and it is what the Gallic brethren refused to hear "so preached as that it cannot be obtained by prayer or lost by obstinacy."

---

## World Meaning

In the house it is the first thing tested and the last thing required. The newcomer waits "outside the doors for ten days or even longer" to give "an evidence of his perseverance and desire, as well as of humility and patience"; and the charge given him when he is received warns that renunciation "is of no use if we are again entangled," "for not he who begins these things, but he who endures in them to the end, shall be saved." Abbot Nesteros adds that each should hold to the line he has chosen, "on perseverance in the line that has been chosen." Then Chaeremon sets it in the teaching on grace. God "puts into us the very beginnings of holy desire, and grants both the commencement of a good work and perseverance in it"; and "all the Catholic fathers" hold that "the first stage in the Divine gift is for each man to be inflamed with the desire of everything that is good," "the second stage in Divine grace is for the aforesaid practices of virtue to be able to be performed," and "the third stage also belongs to the gifts of God, so that it may be held by the persistence of the goodness already acquired, and in such a way that the liberty may not be surrendered." Perseverance is God's gift and the monk's holding, in one sentence, with the will kept free.

What the brethren of Marseilles would not have was a preaching of the gift that made prayer and effort pointless. Augustine's treatise on the subject exists because, as he quotes their letter, "these brethren will not have this perseverance so preached as that it cannot be obtained by prayer or lost by obstinacy" — and he asserts against them "that the perseverance by which we persevere in Christ even to the end is the gift of God." The Gallic monk hears the same word he learned at the door: a gift, and a thing he must not let go. At Tours the word is ordinary praise — "no one can adequately make known his perseverance and self-mastery in abstinence and fastings" — and no one argues about it.

## Distortion Risk

**Modern Hearing:**
"The perseverance of the saints" — a later Reformed doctrine of eternal security; or perseverance as sheer willpower; or the Gallic objection as a denial that perseverance is a gift.

**World Hearing:**
A gift of God that is also the monk's own holding — proved at the door, required to the end, the third stage of a Divine gift that leaves the will free — and, against a preaching that would make prayer pointless, the insistence that it can be "obtained by prayer or lost by obstinacy."
