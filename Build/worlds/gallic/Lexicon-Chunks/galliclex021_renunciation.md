## Quick Meaning

For this world renunciation is the act that makes a monk — "nothing but the evidence of the cross and of mortification," by which "you are dead to this world and its deeds and desires" — and, in the south's received teaching, an act in three stages, of which giving up one's goods, "of what is not our own," is only the first and "is not enough of itself to confer perfection on the renunciant."

---

## World Meaning

In the south the word is the title of a book — Cassian's fourth, "Of the Institutes of the Renunciants," "Of the training of those who renounce this world" — and its charge is given to the newly received by Abbot Pinufius: "Renunciation is nothing but the evidence of the cross and of mortification. And so you must know that to-day you are dead to this world and its deeds and desires." The chapter after warns "how our renunciation of the world is of no use if we are again entangled in those things which we have renounced," and the one after that names the danger of wanting one's money back "when he has cooled down." Abbot Paphnutius then counts the stages: three renunciations, keyed to the Lord's word to Abraham, "Get thee out from thy country and from thy kinsfolk and from thy father's house" — "the first is that by which as far as the body is concerned we make light of all the wealth and goods of this world," "the second, that by which we reject the fashions and vices and former affections of soul and flesh; the third, that by which we detach our soul from all present and visible things, and contemplate only things to come." And he ranks them: "The first renunciation then of which we spoke is of what is not our own, and therefore is not enough of itself to confer perfection on the renunciant, unless he advances to the second, which is really and truly a renunciation of what belongs to us." A man who has left everything and still boasts "of the mere name of monks" has done the easy third. Salvian, in the same city, sees the rich who "make a show of renouncing their wealth" and "do not appear to make their renunciation complete" — and he also uses the word for what every Christian said at the font: "I renounce the devil, his pomps and spectacles and his works." The monk's renunciation is the baptismal one carried further.

At Tours the thing is done and counselled without the term. Martin's talk with Sulpitius "was all directed to such points as the following: that the allurements of this world and secular burdens were to be abandoned in order that we might be free and unencumbered in following the Lord Jesus; and he pressed upon me as an admirable example" Paulinus, who had sold everything. Martin's own renunciation is a discharge: "I am the soldier of Christ: it is not lawful for me to fight." What Tours knows about renunciation is that its exemplars keep being drawn back into the Church as bishops — a renunciant is not a man the Church has lost.

## Distortion Risk

**Modern Hearing:**
Giving things up — poverty, celibacy, "detachment" — as the whole of the monastic act; or a heroic once-for-all rejection of society; or "renunciation" as self-denial for its own sake.

**World Hearing:**
A death "to this world and its deeds and desires" of which giving up goods is only the first and easiest stage; a state that can be lost by re-entanglement or faked "before the face of men"; an extension of what every baptized Christian renounced at the font; and, in this world specifically, an act after which the Church still comes to fetch the renunciant for its sees.
