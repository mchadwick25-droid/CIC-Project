## Quick Meaning

The office this world's monks keep being taken into: a monk "carried off," "escorted under guard," "bound to the long-avoided office," who then keeps "the objects and virtues of a monk" in a see — while the same world's own formation teaching names the desire for that office as vainglory's suggestion and the devil's pretext.

## World Meaning

At Tours the bishop is made by a trick and against bishops. Martin "could not easily be drawn forth from his monastery," so "a certain Ruricius, one of the citizens, pretending that his wife was ill," lures him out, and he is "under a kind of guard escorted to the city," where the crowd wants him. "A few persons, however, and among these some of the bishops, who had been summoned to appoint a chief priest, were impiously offering resistance, asserting forsooth that Martin's person was contemptible, that he was unworthy of the episcopate, that he was a man despicable in countenance, that his clothing was mean, and his hair disgusting." The psalter answers them: a bystander opens it at random on a verse about an "avenger," and "it was believed that this Psalm had been chosen by Divine ordination, that Defensor" — the bishop who had "specially offered opposition" — "might hear a testimony to his own work." And then the new bishop does not change: "he kept up the position of a bishop properly, yet in such a way as not to lay aside the objects and virtues of a monk." He keeps a cell, then builds Marmoutier, and from Marmoutier "we have seen numbers of these afterwards made bishops. For what city or church would there be that would not desire to have its priests from among those in the monastery of Martin?"

What the office costs him, Tours also says. Martin "was accustomed to say ... that such an abundance of power was by no means granted him while he was a bishop, as he remembered to have possessed before he obtained that office." His enemies are his own order: "some of his calumniators, although very few, some of his maligners, I say, were reported to be no others than bishops!" At the court of Maximus, "while the priestly dignity had, with degenerate submissiveness, taken a second place to the royal retinue, in Martin alone, apostolic authority continued to assert itself." After the Priscillianists are killed he holds that expulsion "from the churches" by "a sentence of the bishops" was punishment enough, and that "it was, besides, a foul and unheard-of indignity, that a secular ruler should be judge in an ecclesiastical cause"; forced into one communion with the Ithacian bishops, he feels his power diminished, and "never again did he attend a synod, and kept carefully aloof from all assemblies of bishops." As chief priest "he allowed himself to be wronged by the lowest clerics with impunity; nor did he either remove them from the office on account of such conduct, or ... repel them from a place in his affection." The Tours bishop uses his office chiefly by declining to use it.

At Marseilles the office is the addressee and the danger at once. Cassian writes every book for bishops or men about to be bishops: the *Institutes* "at the request of" "most blessed Pope Castor" for his "new monastery"; the second set of *Conferences* to "holy brothers Honoratus and Eucherius," one of them "presiding as he does over a large monastery of the brethren" — and by the third set that same man is "Honoratus, a Bishop blessed in name as well as merits." Between two prefaces, the monk has become the bishop. And the Egyptian fathers Cassian carries west say what a monk should think of that. The devil "incites a man to desire the holy office of the clergy under the pretext of edifying many people, and the love of spiritual gain, by which to draw us away from the humility and strictness of our life." Vainglory "creates a wish to take holy orders, and a desire for the priesthood or diaconate." The proud man "when a man has grown cold through pride ... wants to be put to rule other people." And the one Egyptian bishop Cassian chooses to describe, Archebius, "had been carried off from the assembly of anchorites and given as Bishop to the town of Panephysis," and "complained that he had been expelled from the monastic system as unworthy of it." Nobody may preside over the brethren "or even over himself" until "he has ... learnt the fact that he is not his own maker."

At Lérins the same thing happens to the founder, and his disciple tells it as a capture: Honoratus was "bound to the long-avoided office of the clergy; here the priestly fillet fastens on its fugitive; and he who had refused to go to the dignity — the dignity came to him"; and as bishop "he kept a monk's humility as entire in the priesthood as, when a monk, he had fully possessed the merits of the priesthood." His successor at Arles wrote his *Life*; a later abbot, Faustus, was "first abbot of the monastery at Lerins, and then made bishop of Riez." Vincent, who stays a presbyter on the island, allows the office no doctrinal privilege at all: a teacher's singular opinion is "a private fancy of his own," "be he a bishop, be he a Confessor, be he a martyr" — the whole priesthood, gathered in council, guards antiquity; no one bishop does.

The shape is the same in every house: the office is fled and it overtakes; the man who wants it has gone cold; the man who has it keeps the monk's humility inside it, loses some of his power to it, and forms the men who will be seized after him. To this world that is not a contradiction. It is what a monk is for, and what a monk must never seek.

## Distortion Risk

**Modern Hearing:**
A bishop as an administrator or prelate; "monk" and "bishop" as opposed careers, so that a monk-bishop is either a promotion or a sell-out; "Pope" as the bishop of Rome only; episcopal election as a formal procedure.

**World Hearing:**
An office one is seized into by a crowd, a trick, or a "fillet," that costs the saint his power, that the formation program names as vainglory's wish, and inside which a monk keeps "the objects and virtues of a monk"; a Gallic bishop is addressed as "Pope"; and a bishop's singular teaching counts for nothing against the consent of the ancients.
