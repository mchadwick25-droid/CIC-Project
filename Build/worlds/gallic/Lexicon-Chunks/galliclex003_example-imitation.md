## Quick Meaning

The mechanism by which formation passes from one person to another in this world: a disciple is made by watching — and, later, by reading — a named man whose life is held up as the thing to copy, and the world's books exist to be that example when the man himself is absent.

## World Meaning

The *Life of Martin* says why it exists in its first chapter: Sulpitius will "write the life of a most holy man, which shall serve in future as an example to others; by which, indeed, the readers shall be roused to the pursuit of true knowledge, and heavenly warfare, and divine virtue." At Marmoutier the same word is the whole method of the house: "eighty disciples, who were being disciplined after the example of the saintly master." Nothing more is said about how — no probation, no dean, no rule — because at Tours the example *is* the method. What the disciples see, they become; at Martin's funeral two thousand monks walk behind him, and "through his example so numerous plants had sprung up for the service of the Lord." The master knows it. Dying on ashes, asked to allow some straw beneath him, he answers: "It is not fitting that a Christian should die except among ashes; and I have sinned if I leave you a different example." And he points beyond himself: of Paulinus, who gave away his wealth, Martin "insisted strongly that that was the man who should be made the object of our imitation." The whole literature of Tours rests on having seen: "I myself am a witness to this latter occurrence," Gallus says, and the *Life* is defended against a skeptic on exactly that ground.

At Marseilles the word carries Egypt to Gaul. Cassian tells Castor that his "life alone is amply sufficient for an example to those who are seeking perfection," and explains why a book of institutes has to come from a man who was there: "these things cannot be taught save by one who has had experience of them." The junior is told where to look — "the examples of the perfect life of one dwelling in the congregation, which you may imitate, should be sought from a very few or indeed from one or two only and not from too many" — and, at the summit, told to look everywhere: "the monk who desires to gather spiritual honey, ought like a most careful bee, to suck out virtue from those who specially possess it," which is Antony's teaching. An old man will not relax his fast for guests, "lest the other's strictness should be relaxed owing to my example. For the authority of a teacher will never be strong unless he fixes it in the heart of his hearer by the actual performance of his duty." An example is a thing one is responsible for, not merely a thing one offers.

At Lérins the example moves from a face to a page. Vincent writes down "the things which I have truthfully received from the holy Fathers" — but these fathers are writings to be collated, and the *Commonitory* is a memory-aid, not a portrait. Yet the island keeps the older form too: Hilary of Arles, Honoratus's disciple and successor, writes "his Life of Saint Honoratus," a disciple's *Life* of his own master. Between them, Cassian's Gallic readers are told to receive the Egyptian fathers "into their cells" as books and to talk "with them after a fashion by daily questions and answers" — the example of an absent man, made present by a text.

What the word does, then, in every house of this world: it makes formation a matter of *who* rather than *what*. A monk is formed by a person, seen or read, whose life is the standard; the authority of every teaching rests on someone having seen it done; and the books of this world are written to stand in for the man when he is gone. The example points in one direction — from the master down — and at Tours it has a destination: the disciples formed by the example become bishops.

## Distortion Risk

**Modern Hearing:**
"Example" as an illustration in an argument, or a "role model" freely chosen by the individual and admired from a distance; "imitation" as derivative or inauthentic.

**World Hearing:**
A person assigned by the house or the master, to be copied in deed and body, with success measured by "plants" — new monks and bishops — and with real dangers: an example can mislead, an elder's laxity is his disciples' ruin, and the junior is told to imitate "one or two only." Authority to teach at all rests on having seen it done.
