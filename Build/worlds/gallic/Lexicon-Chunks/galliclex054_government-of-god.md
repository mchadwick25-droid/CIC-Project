## Quick Meaning

In Salvian's Marseilles, God is not "careless and neglectful of human actions" but a helmsman who "never takes his hand from the tiller" and "never turns his most gracious eyes from the whole extent of the world"; and his judgment is *present*, executed now in history through the barbarians — "we are judged by the ever-present judgment of God, and thus a most slothful race has been aroused to accomplish our destruction and shame" — a judgment-*now* that is the counterpart, not the twin, of the north's judgment-*soon*.

---

## World Meaning

Salvian begins with the objection he writes to answer: "By certain men God is said to be careless and neglectful of human actions, on the ground that he neither protects good men nor restrains the wicked." His answer is the pilot. Even the philosophers compared God with a helmsman, "for they clearly understood that as the helmsman never takes his hand from the tiller, so God" governs; "so our God never turns his most gracious eyes from the whole extent of the world, nor takes away the guiding power of his providence." God sees, governs, and judges — and judges now. Of a Roman who boasted of the sufferings he would inflict and then suffered them: "in addition to his actual misfortune, the present judgment of God was clearly shown." Of the provinces: "We are judged by the ever-present judgment of God, and thus a most slothful race has been aroused to accomplish our destruction and shame"; "the country of the Belgae burst into flames, then the rich estates of the luxurious Aquitanians, and after these the whole body of the Gallic provinces." The ruin of Gaul is not an accident God permitted but a sentence God is passing, on Christians, for the sins Salvian spends eight books naming — the rich whose taxes murder the poor, the spectacles, the lukewarm, the religious who repent of their conversion.

The founding voices have judgment, but not this one. Martin teaches that "the coming of Antichrist is at hand"; Sulpitius writes in "a terror of judgment, a fear of punishment"; Vincent under "a certain awful expectation of the approach of the divine judgment"; Cassian names "the fear of hell" as the beginner's restraint. Theirs is a judgment to come, an expectation or a motive; Salvian's is a judgment being carried out in the burning of the provinces, in the one voice this world has that admits the barbarians into its text at all.

## Distortion Risk

**Modern Hearing:**
"Judgment" as the last judgment only; "government of God" as politics or church-state theory; "providence" as a general comfort; Salvian as a historian reporting the fall of Rome.

**World Hearing:**
A God who never leaves the tiller and is judging *now*, in the flames of the provinces, for the sins of Roman Christians — a preacher's indictment, not a survey (Doc_05 §1.4's named tension), and a judgment-in-progress that the founding monastic voices, who expected judgment soon, do not have.
