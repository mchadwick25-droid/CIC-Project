## Quick Meaning

For this world obedience is the virtue the Egyptians "put not merely before manual labour and reading and silence and quietness in the cell, but even before all virtues" — proved by absurd and terrible commands, a dry stick watered for a year, a son carried to the river — and the first thing a junior learns, "to conquer his own wishes"; at Tours it is the disposition Sulpitius feels before Martin, "unlawful to do anything but acquiesce."

---

## World Meaning

The Egyptians, Cassian tells Gaul, "endeavour with the utmost earnestness and zeal to attain the virtue of obedience, which they put not merely before manual labour and reading and silence and quietness in the cell, but even before all virtues, so that they consider that everything should be postponed to it"; "the virtue of obedience ... holds the first place among other good qualities." It begins on the first day: the dean's "chief part of ... instruction ... will be to teach him first to conquer his own wishes; and, anxiously and diligently practising him in this, he will of set purpose contrive to give him such orders as he knows to be contrary to his liking." It is kept so strictly "that, without the knowledge and permission of their superior, the juniors not only do not dare to leave their cell but on their own authority do not venture to satisfy their common and natural needs." Then the stories. Abbot John, told to water a dry stick twice a day, "had continued for a whole year" until the old man came and asked, "has this tree put forth roots or no?"; the same John, told "Go up and take this cruse of oil" — "the only one in the desert" — "and throw it down out of window," "flew up stairs when summoned and threw it out of window and cast it down to the ground and broke it in pieces without any thought or consideration"; Patermucius, told to throw his little boy into the river, "as if this had been commanded him by the Lord, at once snatched him up and carried him in his arms to the river's bank to throw him in," and it was revealed to the Abbot "that he had done the deed of Abraham"; a count's son hawks ten baskets through the streets, "gaining through the grace of obedience that humility of Christ which is the true nobility." Obedience is how one's will is broken to the elders' judgment, and its reward is grace: John "was exalted even to the grace of prophecy for his admirable obedience."

At Tours the word is scarcely used, and the thing is felt. Invited to Martin's table and given the place of honour, Sulpitius writes: "I felt so overcome by the authority he unconsciously exerted, that I deemed it unlawful to do anything but acquiesce in his arrangements." No one at Marmoutier is given contrary orders in what was read; the master's authority is exerted "unconsciously," and obeyed the same way.

## Distortion Risk

**Modern Hearing:**
"Blind obedience" as the suppression of conscience; the dry stick and the river as abuse or cult control; obedience as institutional discipline enforced by rule.

**World Hearing:**
The first of all virtues, learned by orders "contrary to his liking," whose point is the conquest of one's own wishes and whose reward is grace; the river story read as Abraham's sacrifice; and at Tours an authority "unconsciously exerted" that makes disobedience feel "unlawful" without any command being given.
