## 1. Formation World Identity

**World Name:** The Society of Jesus.

**Short Description:** A Catholic order of priests that sent its men to teach, preach, hear confessions and go where the Pope sent them. Ignatius of Loyola was a soldier. At Pamplona, in the Autobiography's words, "a cannon ball of the enemy broke one of his legs and seriously injured the other" (row 5). He gathered companions at Paris, and they vowed together at Montmartre on 15 August 1534. Paul III approved the plan by word on 3 September 1539 and confirmed the Society by a bull in September 1540. Ignatius was elected General in April 1541 and died on 31 July 1556. His tool for forming men was the *Spiritual Exercises*, a structured month of prayer. At the start of 1555 Polanco counts eight provinces and sixty-one places of residence, from Rome to Brazil, India and Japan (row 30). Francis Xavier landed in Japan in 1549 and died in 1552 (rows 23, 24). Lainez, Borgia and Mercurian led the order in turn until 1580, and Aquaviva until 1615 (rows 25, 32, 33, 75). The window is 1540–1650, as in the census.

**Core Identity:** A world of men who are sent. Its texts say this in three registers. Ignatius gives the purpose of a human life: "Man is created to praise, reverence, and serve God our Lord, and by this means to save his soul" (row 1). His instruction for the road to Trent gives the work: "to preach, to hear confessions, to teach, at the same time instructing children, setting a good example, visiting the poor in the hospitals" (row 12). Nadal's commentary on the Constitutions says that the defence and the spread of the faith are joined to the Society's end (row 28). The men who did this work were shaped by one book, one rule and one General, and they kept in touch by letter. The Monumenta editors say that no founder of an order wrote more often to his own men (row 14, in Latin).

**Distinctive Contribution:** Step 0 (§3 B4) names the Society the only candidate of its batch whose core text is a practised spiritual discipline rather than a doctrinal or polemical body of writing. It is also a world of movement. The same order ran colleges in Europe and mission provinces in Asia and Brazil, under one General. The Tridentine Church (VI.22) is the council and its offices. The Society is one of the bodies that carried out the council's work, and this document does not draw its evidence from that world's shelf.

**Living Tradition Status:** Confirmed by the project lead on a simple basis that names the clear connection. The census records `living: true`. Its legacy field says: "The Society still exists and is the largest religious order of men in the Catholic Church". The connection is the Society of Jesus itself, which is the direct continuity. Article 29 makes confirmation a condition of freeze. The shelf is itself mediated by living hands. Its editors and translators are almost all Jesuits: Mullan, O'Conor, Goodier, Coleridge, Boero, Bouix, and the Jesuit fathers who edited the Monumenta Historica Societatis Jesu (rows 4, 5, 9, 14, 17, 20, 21, 25). Doc_02 §2 records this.

## 2. Historical Boundaries

**Approximate Time Horizon:** 1540–1650.

- **Beginning (1540).** The bull confirming the Society is dated 27 September 1540. Its own dating clause reads "quinto kal. Octobris", the fifth day before the Kalends of October, and the *Institutum*'s index gives "27 Sept. 1540" (row 74). The Lainez editors give "27 Septembris anni 1540" (row 25), and the Coleridge contents page reads "Sept 27, 1540" (row 21). Only Coleridge's text reads "Sept. 17" (row 21). Nadal's own note on Ignatius's death counts the Society's institution sixteen years before 1556, which gives the same year (row 27). Confidence: Documented for the day and the year. A spoken approval came earlier, on 3 September 1539 at Tivoli (Coleridge, row 21). The O'Leary table also lists an "Approbation of the Institute" under 1539 (row 9). This document starts the window at the bull, as the census and Step 0 do.
- **What lies before the window.** Ignatius's wound and conversion (1521–22), his study at Paris, the vows at Montmartre (1534), his ordination (1537) and the spoken approval (1539) all fall before 1540. They are inheritance, not window (§7). The Autobiography is a special case. Ignatius dictated it in 1553–55, inside the window, about events that are almost all before it. Its narrative ends with Ignatius's work in Rome around 1538 and does not reach the bull. It is evidence for how the founder told his own life, and it is not a record of the Society's life after 1540 (rows 7, 70).
- **Ending (1650).** 1650 is the census's window close. No source read for this document shows an event that closes the world then. Step 0 places later events after 1650. Doc_08's Ending cell must record this honestly. The shelf reaches 1650 in two places: the decrees of the eighth general congregation, of 1645–46 (row 75), and the last volume of the *Jesuit Relations*, which reports 1650 (row 92). Neither was read for an ending. For the order's letters the shelf stops in February 1585, with Salmeron's second volume (row 33). After that date the order speaks on the shelf as a body, in the decrees and ordinances of the *Institutum*, and New France speaks in the Relations (Doc_02 §6).

**Developments internal to the window:** Ignatius is elected General (April 1541). The day is not settled on the shelf. Coleridge dates the ballots 4–5 April and says Ignatius took up his duties on 19 April. The O'Leary table gives 13 April (rows 9, 21). Paul III names Broët and Salmeron nuncios to Ireland on 3 July 1541 (row 87), and Ignatius instructs them in September (row 11). Their group reports to Ignatius on 9 April 1542, after 34 days inside Ireland (row 68). Xavier sails from Lisbon on 7 April 1541 (row 21). The Council of Trent meets (1545–63). Lainez and Salmeron speak there as the Pope's theologians (1546, rows 25, 32). Faber dies on 1 August 1546 (row 19). The Holy See approves the Latin text of the *Exercises* (1548, row 4). Xavier lands in Japan on the feast of the Assumption in 1549 (row 23). He dies on Friday 2 December 1552, on Coleridge's dating (row 24). Nadal carries the Constitutions to Spain and Portugal (1553–54, row 27). Ignatius dies on 31 July 1556. Lainez governs as vicar and is elected General on 2 July 1558 (row 75). Pope Paul IV imposes choir prayer and a three-year term on the General, and Lainez has both removed under Pius IV (row 25). Lainez takes part in the Poissy colloquy (1561) and dies on 19 January 1565. Borgia is elected General on 2 July 1565 and dies on 1 October 1572 (rows 33, 75). Mercurian is elected in April 1573 and dies on 1 August 1580. The *Institutum* gives 23 April for his election, and the Salmeron preface gives 29 April (rows 75, 33). Nadal dies on 3 April 1580 (row 27). Aquaviva is elected on 19 February 1581 and dies at the end of January 1615. Vitelleschi is elected on 15 November 1615 and dies on 9 February 1645. Carafa is elected on 7 January 1646 (row 75). The events of the Chinese and Malabar Rites controversy fall inside the window: Ricci's method in China (from the 1580s, row 51), de Nobili's Madurai mission (from 1606, row 52), Gregory XV's ruling of 1623, the Jiading conference (1627–28) and the Propaganda Fide decree of 1645. These dates come from Step 0 §2 A3, which names no source. No vendored source confirms them. Confidence: Inferential/Thin for each date (row 53).

**Developments that indicate transition:** the condemnations of the rites after 1650 and the suppression of 1773, both named in Step 0 and both after the window.

**Geographic Centers:**

- **Rome.** The seat of the General and of the Roman College, and the city of the Pope who sent the men out. Ignatius governed from Rome until his death.
- **The Iberian kingdoms.** Portugal sent the first missionaries east. In March 1540 the Portuguese ambassador wrote to King John III about sending men of the new company to India (row 15). Spain gave the order many of its first men, among them Lainez and Salmeron, who came from Alcalá to Paris (row 25).
- **Italy, the German lands and France.** Colleges and preaching, and the meeting ground with Protestants. Salmeron, Le Jay and Canisius went to Ingolstadt in 1549 (row 32). Lainez went to the Poissy colloquy (row 25).
- **India, Japan, Brazil, Ethiopia and the Congo.** The editors' note to Polanco's year 1555 lists the places where the Society lived. It names Goa, Malacca and Amboina, and in Japan two places, "Amangucium" and "Bungum", which read as Yamaguchi and Bungo. It names five places in Brazil (row 30). A consistory of January 1555 discussed sending thirteen priests to Ethiopia (row 30).
- **China.** Xavier died in 1552 on the island of San Chan, waiting to enter China (row 24). The census names Ricci for China. He is on the shelf only in Trigault's reworking of 1615, a second witness (rows 51, 89).
- **New France.** Acadia, Quebec and the Huron country. The *Jesuit Relations*, volumes 1 to 35, report the mission from 1610 to 1650 (rows 90–92).

**Cultural Environment:** Catholic Europe was in open conflict with the Reformation. Latin was the language of learning and of the editors' printed record. The letters themselves were written in Latin, Spanish, Italian and Portuguese (rows 14, 15), and the reports from Canada in French (rows 90–92). The Portuguese King asked for men for India (row 15). On Coleridge's account, the papal brief that made Xavier Apostolic Nuncio was granted at the King's request (row 24). Xavier, at Malacca in 1552, used the brief against a Portuguese captain who blocked his voyage to China. The captain gave way in part. On Coleridge's account he let Xavier sail, without the embassy that was to go with him (row 24). The first companions came from several kingdoms. Faber was from Savoy, Lainez from Castile and Nadal from Majorca (rows 19, 25, 27). Outside Europe, the people they met appear in the sources only through the missionaries' own letters and biographies (Doc_02 §6).

**Historical Pressures:**

- **The Protestant challenge and the Council.** Ignatius told the men going to Trent: "In preaching I should not touch upon any of the points in which Protestants differ from Catholics" (row 12).
- **Suspicion.** The Autobiography's chapter on Alcalá and Salamanca describes prison and inquiry (row 7). In 1554 Nadal defended the *Exercises*, which the Latin says had been "acriter impetitum", sharply attacked (row 27).
- **The Pope's hand on the order.** Paul IV imposed choir prayer and a three-year term for the General, and Lainez had both removed under Pius IV (row 25). A decree of Pius V allowed ordination only to men with solemn vows. Salmeron advised Borgia to answer with plain obedience. Polanco and Nadal then pressed Gregory XIII to lift it, and the Nadal Preface says Nadal's plea for the Society's institutes succeeded (rows 27, 33).
- **Quarrels inside the Society.** The Lainez editors write that he steered the Society through "domesticas dissensiones, a nonnullis ambitiosis suscitatas", domestic dissensions stirred up by some ambitious men (1556–58, row 25).
- **Colonial officials and rivals.** Xavier's dispute with Don Alvaro de Ataide at Malacca (row 24).
- **The rites question.** A quarrel over whether missionaries might adapt to local custom. It is inside the window and unsourced on the shelf (row 53).

**Historical Catalysts:** the wound at Pamplona (1521); the vows at Montmartre (1534); the failed plan to sail to Palestine and the companions' offer to go where the Pope sent them (rows 15, 21); the bull of 1540; Trent; Xavier's letters, which drew recruits. Nadal joined after reading a letter of Xavier's. The Monumenta editors say he felt "novos, vehementesque stimulos", new and strong spurs, from it (row 27).

## 3. Distinct World Criteria

- **What recurring formation ecology exists?** The *Exercises* are given one to one over about thirty days (row 1). Weekly confession and communion, daily meditation and daily examination of conscience were already the habit of Xavier's Paris circle (row 21). The Scholia's editor cites the rules for a novitiate and a third probation for priests (rows 27, 28). There are colleges for the young (row 8). The mission field is also a school. Xavier tells the Goa College to open their faults "to men who can help you by counsel" (row 23).
- **What recurring gravities exist (preliminary, subject to Doc_04)?** These are candidates for testing, not findings. Each carries an Author Gravity risk. The evidence comes mostly from the Society's own hands.
  1. Praise, reverence and service of God as the end of human life (row 1). Risk: it comes from Ignatius alone.
  2. The discernment of interior movements, consolation and desolation (row 2). Risk: the *Exercises* are a manual, not a record of how men used them.
  3. Obedience to the hierarchical Church. Ignatius writes: "we ought always to hold that the white which I see, is black, if the Hierarchical Church so decides it" (row 1). Risk: this is his rule for the exercitant. It does not show how the Society held to it under pressure from Popes (§2).
  4. Help to souls through ministries: preaching, confession, teaching, works of mercy (row 12). Risk: the Trent instruction is one document.
  5. Being sent. Risk: the mission voice on the shelf is Xavier's, in Coleridge's English and in the original (rows 22, 23, 85), and the reports of New France, which were written for a printed audience (rows 90–92).
  6. Education through colleges. Risk: the shelf holds the *Ratio Studiorum* and the earlier school documents (rows 76, 77). They prescribe, and they do not show a college's life. The Constitutions are in a clean Latin text (row 75). The Autobiography's list of early colleges is a later editor's (row 8).
  7. As a tension: adaptation to other cultures against uniformity. It rests on the rites controversy, which has no vendored source (row 53). The instruction of 1637 for the fathers sent to the Hurons bears on it (row 91).
- **What recurring patterns of worship, formation, interpretation, belonging, and authority exist?** Worship: the Mass and the sacraments. In 1540 Ignatius asked the citizens of Azpeitia to form a confraternity of the Blessed Sacrament and to confess and communicate once a month (row 10). Formation: as above. Interpretation: Gospel scenes meditated in order (row 3), and Nadal's later Gospel notes (row 29). Belonging: letters. The Regesta are the registers of letters sent from Rome. They begin in 1547 with Polanco (row 16). Authority: a General, provincials and the Constitutions (row 30).
- **What distinguishes this world from adjacent worlds?** See §7.

## 4. World Separation Criteria

The Framework asks when one world becomes another. The six questions are run below against the world's own periods: the founders' generation (1540–56), the years of Lainez, Borgia and Mercurian (1558–1580), and the years after. The evidence for the third period is thin, apart from the decrees of the general congregations and the reports of New France.

- **Has a new gravity emerged?** Education grows: Coimbra in 1542, Gandia in 1546, the Roman College by 1553 (an editor's list, row 8). Adaptation to other cultures is a candidate for the later period, but nothing vendored shows it (row 53). Nothing else is new on the shelf.
- **Has a major gravity disappeared?** No evidence on the shelf shows one gone.
- **Has formation changed substantially?** The forms grow, and the core stays. Nadal set up the first probation house (row 27). The ministries Ignatius lists in 1546 are the ones Coleridge describes for Xavier at Bologna in 1537: preaching, confessions, prison and hospital visits, and catechizing children (rows 12, 21).
- **Has worship changed substantially?** Not lastingly. Paul IV's imposed choir was set aside under Pius IV at Lainez's request (row 25).
- **Has authority changed substantially?** Not lastingly. The three-year term was also set aside (row 25). Ignatius, Lainez, Borgia, Mercurian, Aquaviva and Vitelleschi each served as General until death, on the shelf's dates (rows 9, 25, 33, 75). Ignatius delegated his authority for distant missions. Polanco reports that Father Joannes Nugnez, named Patriarch of Ethiopia, was to act as Ignatius's own deputy over the Jesuits there (row 30).
- **Has interpretation changed substantially?** No evidence shows it. After 1585 the shelf has the decrees of four general congregations and the reports of New France, and they were not read for this question (Doc_02 §6).

**Finding.** The evidence supports one world. The same rule, the same General and the same formation run through all periods and all places on the shelf. The evidence is thin after 1585, apart from the decrees and New France, and thin for the mission provinces of Asia after 1552. That thinness limits the finding. It is not a reason to split the world.

## 5. Strand Determination

Article 21 defines a strand as "a meaningfully distinct pattern of formation emphasis, practice, authority structure, or ecological orientation within a single world, not merely a variation in detail." It adds that strand determination "is never assigned to satisfy an architectural preference for plurality". Strand is a finding that must answer to evidence. Article 21 also gives the default: "A world without established strands is treated as strand-singular throughout construction."

**Finding (the builder's, for the project lead to confirm or change).** The Society is treated as strand-singular. This is a recommendation. It is not a ruling, and no later document should rest on it as settled until the project lead has confirmed it. Doc_04 tests it. The recommendation rests on Article 21's default, because the shelf shows no established second strand. It does not rest on proof that the Society had only one.

**The case for strand-singular.**

- One order, one General, one rule. The editors' note to Polanco's year 1555 tables the provinces of the order. Brazil, and India and Japan, stand in the same table as Italy, Sicily, Aragon, Castile, Andalusia and Portugal, each with a named provincial (row 30).
- One formation. Xavier tells the Goa College to open their faults to a guide, as the Paris circle practised confession and examination of conscience (rows 21, 23).
- One end. Nadal's first scholion joins the defence and the spread of the faith to the Society's end, and cites the founding bull for it (row 28).
- One chain of authority. In Ethiopia the Patriarch was to act as Ignatius's own deputy over the Jesuits there (row 30). This is the same use of the Patriarch as in §4.
- Letters connect the two. Nadal joined the Society after reading a letter of Xavier's (row 27).

**The case for two strands, a European one and a mission one, at full strength.**

- Practice differs. In Japan Xavier wrote that "we are like so many dumb statues in the midst of the people" and prayed to master the language soon. He relied on a Japanese convert, Paul of the Holy Faith, for the first teaching in Japanese (rows 23, 85). In New France the Society's instruction of 1637 for the fathers sent to the Hurons begins: "You must have sincere affection for the Savages" (row 91). The Relations also show the missionaries setting out to learn the language (row 90).
- Method follows the audience. The census says that de Nobili "took up the life of a Hindu ascetic in order to be heard". It says that Ricci "learned classical Chinese and wrote for Confucian scholars". These are strong data for a mission strand, because a method built around the audience is more than a variation in detail. The words are the census's. De Nobili's own writing is not on the shelf (row 52), and Ricci is there only in Trigault's second witness (row 89).
- Authority differs in form. In Asia Xavier acted as Apostolic Nuncio under a papal brief, granted at the request of the King of Portugal, and used it against a Portuguese captain (row 24). In Ethiopia, if the Patriarch and the others disagreed on who should go with him, the King was to hear both sides and decide (row 30). The Patriarch's own place in the chain is counted above, as continuity.
- Orientation differs. The European work faced Protestants. The mission work faced peoples who had not heard of Christ.
- The rites controversy, named in Step 0, may be the mark of a distinct mission strand. Its documents are not on the shelf (row 53).

**What weighs against two strands.**

- For Asia the evidence for a mission strand stops with Xavier's death in 1552 and Polanco's notes for 1554–56. A strand there that cannot be tested with its own voice would rest on a census sentence. New France is a mission voice from 1610 to 1650, and this pass read it only at the loci named in the Registry (rows 90–92).
- On the shelf, the same rule and the same chain of authority reach every province (row 30). The instruction for the Hurons is addressed to "the Fathers of our Society" (row 91), so the mission of New France runs inside the order.

**Why the recommendation is one strand.** The differences above are differences in circumstance on the evidence we hold. Article 21's default applies unless a strand is established, and none is. The one place where the evidence might show a real difference in ecological orientation is the method of de Nobili and Ricci, and the rites controversy that followed. The shelf has no source for de Nobili or for the rites, and Ricci is there only as a second witness. The Relations of New France may show one, and they have not been read for it. If a source shows the missionaries acting on a different logic from the European colleges, Doc_04 should reopen the finding, and it should test the Relations first. Confidence: Inferential/Thin. The finding rests on a few loci, on Article 21's default, and on the fact that the mission voices after 1552 were read only at a few loci.

**What holds across the whole world.** The *Exercises* as the shared formation (row 1). The Society's letters as the bond (rows 14, 16). The aim of defending and spreading the faith (row 28). These are candidates for cross-strand testing at Doc_04 if the project lead orders two strands.

**What this document does not decide.** Whether a mission strand exists is not decided here. Nor is Representative identity, which is decided later in the build, at Doc_10.

## 6. Preliminary Forces Identification

Constitution Article 22 requires that a world be "situated against the forces that shaped it across both external and internal dimensions". It adds that where "no internal fracture is attested" the finding is recorded honestly. The sketch below is Layer 1 (Historical Event) only. The complete analysis is Doc_08.

**What generated this world:** a group of students at Paris who first vowed to go to Palestine. Coleridge, a Jesuit writing in 1872, reports that when they could not go, Ignatius told them that "in Italy, the centre of Christendom", God had opened "how vast a field" for their apostolic labours (row 21). In 1539 and 1540 the companions put themselves at the Pope's disposal, and Paul III approved the plan (rows 15, 21). Step 0 (§2 A3) frames the Society as founded within the Reformation crisis and as a response to it. The shelf's own account of the founding does not name the Reformation as the motive. This document therefore carries both: the order was founded amid the crisis, and its founders' stated reason was a vow of service. Confidence: Contested whether the crisis was the cause. The census's own source note, on O'Malley (row 54), says that book was written "to counter mythologized readings of the order's founding".

**What larger world it was embedded in:** four settings. The first was the Catholic Church at the time of Trent. The second was the Iberian kingdoms, whose kings asked for missionaries (row 15). The King of Portugal also asked the Pope for the brief that made Xavier Nuncio (row 24). The third was the courts and cities of Italy, the German lands and France, where the order found its patrons and correspondents (row 33). The fourth was the world outside Europe, where the order met peoples with their own religions.

**What it was responding to:** three things. The first was the Protestant break. The shelf shows the Society sending men to the Council and to the colloquy at Poissy (rows 12, 25). The second was the need for priests who could move: the vow of service to the Pope led to missions such as Ireland (1541, row 11) and Ethiopia (1555, row 30). The third was ignorance of the faith among ordinary people. Xavier's catechetical forms are addressed to catechists teaching "these ignorant nations" (row 22).

**What it was refusing:** the shelf does not state a refusal of the cloister in so many words. It shows a life of pilgrimage, hospitals, prisons and teaching (rows 5, 7), and a choir imposed by Paul IV that the order later had removed (row 25). Ignatius told his men to avoid arguing the disputed points with Protestants in their preaching (row 12).

## 7. World Continuity & Distinction

**What this world inherits:**

- **The Latin Church's creed and sacraments.** §8 shows how the Society's own voices affirm the creed's substance. The Council's Session III recites the whole Niceno-Constantinopolitan Creed (row 35, context only).
- **Devotio Moderna reading.** Coleridge reports that Xavier's Paris circle read "the Bible and the Imitation of Christ" (row 21). A footnote citing Alcázar (row 61) adds that Ignatius gave each monk at Monte Cassino a copy of *De Contemptu Mundi*, which is the *Imitation* (row 21). The Devotio Moderna world (V.7) is a separate world. This document names the *Imitation* only as a book the companions read.
- **The books behind the Exercises.** The Autobiography says that Ignatius, while recovering, was given "The Life of Christ," by Rudolph, the Carthusian, and the "Flowers of the Saints". It links his later teaching on the discernment of spirits to this time (row 5).

**What this world transmits:**

- To **the Tridentine Church's implementing work.** The Society's men sit at Trent as papal theologians (row 25).
- To **the mission-born traditions.** The census names this transmission ("parent of the mission-born traditions"). Nothing vendored was read for it. The Relations of New France may bear on it (rows 90–92).
- To **the later Society and the schools.** The census names colleges and the *Exercises* as its lasting bequest. The shelf is silent on later use.

**Continuity and change.** The Framework asks three further questions. *What shared material functions differently here than in adjacent ecologies?* The Trent creed and decrees are the Church's rule. In the Society they are the frame that its preachers use, and Ignatius tells them to avoid arguing the disputed points. *Where does continuity mask change?* The name "Society of Jesus" covers the college teacher in Europe. On the census's account it also covers de Nobili, who "took up the life of a Hindu ascetic in order to be heard", and Ricci, who wrote for Confucian scholars. The same rule may hide different lives. *Where does discontinuity mask continuity?* The quarrels of 1556–58 and the papal decrees under Paul IV read like breaks. Yet the order came out of them without the imposed choir and the three-year term (row 25).

**Distinctness from adjacent worlds:**

- **The Tridentine Church (VI.22).** The council and its offices. The two worlds may cite the same Trent text with different weight, and the corpus map settles this: tradition for that world, context for this one (row 35).
- **Lutheran Wittenberg (VI.1) and the Reformed cities.** The opposing side of the same crisis. No text is shared.
- **Devotio Moderna (V.7).** An older lay and clerical devotion. Step 0 (§3 B2) names it as a two-way ancestor of the Jesuit schools. The Society's own evidence is in row 21.
- **Desert Monasticism and the Cappadocian world.** Step 0 (§2 A4) reads a thematic echo, not a textual link. Doc_01 records it as the builder's reading only.
- **The St Thomas Christians of India.** The rites controversy touches the Malabar coast, where the census keeps a separate world. Nothing from that world's shelf is used here.
- **The Kyiv Mohyla and Brest worlds and Orthodoxy under the Ottomans.** The Society's activity there is not covered by any vendored source of this world.

## 8. The Article 4 check (Step 0 §4 item 1)

Step 0 found that Ignatius's *Exercises* and *Autobiography* contain no deviation from any of Article 4's five commitments. This section extends the check across the shelf. The five commitments are quoted from Constitution V2.3, Article 4 ("On the Scope of 'Movement'"), in the same words as the Step 0 header. Article 4 also says: "A movement need not have recited the Creed, formally adopted it, or existed at a date when doing so was possible." So the test is plain sense. It asks whether a commitment is affirmed. It does not ask whether the Nicene words appear. Article 4 also says that the floor "is never a standard imposed on any selected world's own voice".

Mediation matters here. Three kinds of evidence are used, and the strength of each is stated.

- **The Society's founding and early voices, in translation or in Latin:** the *Exercises* (Mullan's English from the Spanish autograph), the Autobiography (an English edition of a text dictated in 1553–55), the O'Leary letters (English) and Faber's *Memoriale* (Latin).
- **Xavier's catechetical documents in Coleridge's notes** (row 22). Coleridge attributes to Xavier a "Method of catechizing the Ignorant" and an "Explanation of the Creed", the second "written by St. Francis for the people of the Moluccas". He gives the transmission of the first as copies gathered in India by Philippucci, a Latin translation, and the Menchaca and Poussines editions (row 62). He names no source for the second. The attribution to Xavier personally therefore rests on Coleridge. Confidence: Inferential/Thin for the attribution to Xavier himself. The text is at least a Jesuit catechetical voice of the first missions, in English of 1872.
- **Trent, Session III** (row 35). This is context, not the Society's voice.

**1.** "One God, the Father, the Almighty, maker of heaven and earth, of all that is, seen and unseen."
   - *Shown.* The *Exercises*: "Man is created to praise, reverence, and serve God our Lord". The Contemplation to Gain Love asks the exercitant to recall "the benefits received, of Creation, Redemption and particular gifts" (row 1). The Autobiography: "God permitted him to understand how He had created this world" (row 6). Faber addresses "Patrem cælestem" (row 18), and in April 1543 writes of Christ "sedens ad dexteram Dei Patris omnipotentis", seated at the right hand of God the Father almighty (row 18). The Explanation of the Creed, in Coleridge's English: "I believe in God, the Father Almighty, Creator of heaven and earth" (row 22).
   - *Not shown in the Society's own voice.* "Of all that is, seen and unseen", in those words. Trent's recital has "of all things visible and invisible" (row 35, context).

**2.** "Jesus Christ as the only Son of God, eternally begotten of the Father, God from God, Light from Light, true God from true God, begotten not made, of one Being with the Father."
   - *Shown.* Ignatius, Rome, 1540: "His only begotten Son Christ Our Lord in this great work of the most Holy Sacrament, in which His Divine Majesty is present, in His Divinity and in His Humanity" (row 10). The *Exercises*: the Three Divine Persons decide that "the Second Person shall become man" (row 1). Faber, April 1543: the humility of the Son of God, who from eternity willed to become man and to be under him "qui ipsum ab æterno genuit sibi æqualem", who begot him from eternity equal to himself (row 18). The Explanation of the Creed, in Coleridge's English: "God the Son, Who is begotten of God the Father, not made, nor created" (row 22).
   - *Not shown in the Society's own words.* "Light from Light", "true God from true God" and "of one Being with the Father". Faber's "sibi æqualem", equal to himself, is close in sense and is not the same word. Coleridge's catechist form adds "the only-begotten Son of God" (row 22).

**3.** "Jesus Christ as truly human — incarnate of the Holy Spirit and the Virgin Mary, 'became truly human.'"
   - *Shown.* The *Exercises* name "the Eternal Word incarnate" (row 1). Ignatius: "in His Divinity and in His Humanity" (row 10). Faber: "Deum et hominem quem colimus ut Deum nostrum", the God and man we worship as our God (row 18). Coleridge's Explanation: "the Second Person of the holy Trinity, God the Son, became Incarnate in the womb of the Virgin Mary" (row 22). The catechist form: "conceived by the Holy Ghost, was made flesh in the womb of the most pure Virgin Mary" (row 22).
   - *Shown only in Coleridge's rendering of Xavier's material.* "Conceived by the Holy Ghost". In Ignatius's and Faber's own words the phrase is not found.

**4.** "Christ's death under Pontius Pilate, burial, bodily resurrection on the third day, ascension, and his return in glory to judge the living and the dead."
   - *Shown in Ignatius's own compilation.* The *Exercises* narrate Pilate as judge, the burial, and the resurrection appearances. They state that Christ appeared "also in soul to the Holy Fathers of Limbo, and after taking them out and having taken His Body again" (row 3).
   - *Shown by Faber.* In the same April 1543 notes Christ reigns "sedens ad dexteram Dei Patris omnipotentis". Faber also writes of Christ "qui judicaturus est vivos et mortuos ... in Humanitate illa in qua ascendens in cœlum visus est", who will judge the living and the dead in the same humanity in which he was seen to go up into heaven (row 18).
   - *Shown in Coleridge's Explanation.* "who suffered under Pontius Pilate, was crucified, dead, and buried"; "on the third day, which was Sunday, He rose from the dead, reuniting His most holy soul to the body which it had quitted"; "who will come to judge the living and the dead" (row 22).
   - *Partly shown.* In the Ascension contemplation Ignatius quotes Acts: "This Jesus, Who is taken from your eyes to heaven, shall so come as you saw Him go into heaven" (row 3). This is Scripture with no gloss of his own, so it is not counted. "In glory" is not found in his words.

**5.** "The Holy Spirit as Lord and giver of life, worshiped and glorified together with the Father and the Son."
   - *Shown.* Faber: "Adora personam tui glorificatoris, Spiritus Sancti Paracleti", in English: adore the person of your glorifier, the Holy Spirit, the Paraclete (row 18). Ignatius, Trent instructions, 1546: "the Holy Ghost may come down upon the Council with the greatest abundance of gifts and graces" (row 12). Coleridge's Explanation: "and glorifying the Holy Ghost, consubstantial with the Father and the Son" (row 22).
   - *Not shown in the Society's own words.* "Lord and giver of life". Note also that the Explanation says the Spirit "proceeds from the Father and the Son". Article 4's own text is filioque-free, and it asks only that the Spirit be worshipped with Father and Son. The addition is ordinary Latin usage and is not counted as a departure from the commitment.

**Corroboration from the Church's creed at Trent.** Session III recites the creed "of heaven and earth ... the Lord, and the giver of life" (row 35). It states that the Society's own age held these clauses. It is context only.

**Canisius.** His *Summe of Christian Doctrine* (1622) comments article by article on the Creed. Its commentary on the second article calls Christ the only Son of God, begotten before all worlds, and consubstantial. Its commentary on the Holy Ghost calls him equal and consubstantial with both. The scan is heavily damaged, and marginal citations are interleaved with the text. No passage is quoted here. This is paraphrase from a read locus, and quotability waits on a ruling by the Library (row 34, Open_Gaps).

**The other voices.**

- *The mission voice.* Xavier's own letters are on the shelf in Coleridge's compilation and in the original (rows 23, 24, 85). Nothing in them denies a commitment. The Relations of New France (rows 90–92) were not read for this check. The rites controversy is where a floor question might arise. No document of it is on the shelf, and this check cannot reach it.
- *The order after 1585.* The decrees of the later general congregations, the *Ratio Studiorum* and Trigault's Latin are on the shelf (rows 75, 76, 89). They were not read for this check, so it does not reach the years 1585–1650.

**Result.** Nothing on the shelf denies any of the five commitments. Clause by clause: commitment 4 is largely shown, in Ignatius's compilation, in Faber, and in Coleridge's Explanation. Commitments 1, 2, 3 and 5 are shown in substance, and some clauses are shown only in Coleridge's rendering of material attributed to Xavier. The clauses listed as "not shown" are silent, not denied. The floor holds on the evidence for the founders' generation and the first mission. It is not a completed check of the mission's later voice or of the order from 1585 to 1650, because that evidence was not read.
